R2.1.1 – Chemical Equations

Guiding questionHow are chemical equations used to calculate reacting ratios?

Stoichiometry (from the Greek stoicheion, element, and metron, measure) describes the reacting ratios during chemical reactions. Because matter is conserved during chemical change (the law of conservation of mass, established by Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier, often called the ‘father of chemistry’), stoichiometry is a form of book-keeping at the atomic level: it enables chemists to determine what amounts of substances should be reacted together, and to predict how much product will be obtained.

A chemical equation is a representation, using chemical symbols, of the simplest ratio of atoms, as elements or in compounds, undergoing chemical change. The left-hand side shows the reactants and the right-hand side shows the products, separated by an arrow showing the direction of change.

reactants → products

As atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction, the total number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the equation. This is achieved by balancing the equation using stoichiometric coefficients: numbers placed in front of each formula to denote the number of units of that species reacting. A coefficient of 1 is never written explicitly. It is usually easiest to start balancing with the most complex species in the equation, leaving terms that involve only a single element until last.

Balancing ruleWhen balancing an equation, only the stoichiometric coefficient in front of a formula may be changed — the subscripts within a chemical formula must never be altered, since that would change the identity of the substance itself.
Worked example

Write a balanced equation for the thermal decomposition of sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO3, into sodium carbonate, Na2CO3, water and carbon dioxide.

NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2

Checking atoms on each side shows sodium (1 vs 2), hydrogen (1 vs 2) and carbon (1 vs 2) are all unbalanced, so a coefficient of 2 is introduced on the left:

2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2

Checking again: sodium (2 = 2), hydrogen (2 = 2), carbon (2 = 2) and oxygen (6 = 3 + 1 + 2 = 6) all balance, so the equation is complete.

State symbols

State symbols are commonly added to chemical equations to indicate the physical state of each substance: solid (s), liquid (l), gas (g), or a substance dissolved in water, an aqueous solution (aq). An equation is, by definition, always balanced, so this will never be stated explicitly in a question — after writing an equation, the number of atoms of each element on both sides should always be checked.

‘Heating’ versus ‘burning’When a question refers to heating a reactant or to thermal decomposition, this means only that heat is the energy source for the reaction, not that oxygen is involved. When a question refers to burning or combustion, this indicates that oxygen is a reactant.

Some redox reactions are more conveniently presented as two separate half-equations, one identifying the element that loses electrons (oxidation) and one identifying the element that gains electrons (reduction); species not involved in the electron transfer are omitted. This approach, and the processes of oxidation and reduction, are covered in more detail in Reactivity 3.2.

R2.1.2 – Reacting Masses

The mole ratio given by the coefficients of a balanced equation can be used to determine the masses of reactants and products. Moles provide the essential link between the number of particles that react (fixed by the equation) and the mass that can actually be measured in the laboratory.

n = m / M   or   m = nM

where n is the amount in moles, m is the mass in grams, and M is the molar mass in g mol−1. Values of the relative atomic mass, Ar, given in the data booklet to two decimal places, should be used throughout these calculations.

General strategy for reacting-mass questions
  • Write the balanced equation.
  • Identify the mole ratio for the two species named in the question (ignore all others — converting every species to moles and grams wastes time).
  • Convert this mole ratio into a reacting ratio by mass, using m = nM.
  • Insert the data given in the question and solve the resulting ratio.
Worked example

Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced from the complete combustion of 1.00 g of methane.

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)

Only CH4 and CO2 are needed. The mole ratio is 1 : 1, so the reacting ratio by mass is M(CH4) : M(CO2) = 16.05 : 44.01.

g CH4 / g CO2 = 16.05 / 44.01 = 1.00 / x

x = (1.00 × 44.01) / 16.05 = 2.74 g CO2.

Worked example

Iodine chloride, ICl, is made by 2I2 + KIO3 + 6HCl → 5ICl + KCl + 3H2O. Calculate the mass of I2 needed to prepare 28.60 g of ICl.

The relevant mole ratio is I2 : ICl = 2 : 5, so the mass ratio is (2 × 253.80) : (5 × 162.35) = 507.60 : 811.75.

g I2 / g ICl = 507.60 / 811.75 = x / 28.60

x = (507.60 × 28.60) / 811.75 = 17.9 g I2.

R2.1.2 – Reacting Volumes of Gases

In the laboratory, gas volumes are often more convenient to measure than masses. Avogadro's law states:

Avogadro's lawEqual volumes of all gases, measured under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules: V ∝ n.

This means the ratio of reacting volumes of gases (all measured under the same conditions) is identical to the mole ratio given by the coefficients of the balanced equation — no conversion to mass is needed.

Worked example

10 cm3 of nitrogen reacts with excess hydrogen: N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). What volume of ammonia is produced (same temperature and pressure throughout)?

‘Excess’ hydrogen means enough is present to react with all the nitrogen. The mole ratio N2 : NH3 = 1 : 2, so the volume ratio is also 1 : 2.

10 cm3 N2 produces 20 cm3 NH3 (and would consume 30 cm3 H2).

Worked example

10 cm3 of a gaseous hydrocarbon, CxHy, is burnt completely in excess oxygen to give 30 cm3 CO2 and 30 cm3 H2O vapour (same conditions throughout). Determine its molecular formula.

The volume (mole) ratio hydrocarbon : CO2 : H2O is 10 : 30 : 30, or 1 : 3 : 3. Each mole of hydrocarbon therefore gives 3 mol CO2 (3 C atoms) and 3 mol H2O (6 H atoms), so the molecular formula is C3H6.

Molar volume and standard temperature and pressure (STP)

Since equal numbers of moles of any gas occupy equal volumes under the same conditions, all gases share the same molar volume, Vm, at a given temperature and pressure. At standard temperature and pressure (STP) — 0 °C (273 K) and 100 kPa — the molar volume of any gas is 22.7 dm3 mol−1 (2.27 × 10−2 m3 mol−1), a value given in the data booklet. Increasing temperature increases the molar volume; increasing pressure decreases it.

n = V / Vm
STP is not the ‘standard state’Standard temperature and pressure (STP), used for gas volume calculations, is a different convention from the thermodynamic ‘standard state’ used for enthalpy and other thermodynamic data (Reactivity 1.1).
Worked example

Calculate the volume occupied by 0.0200 g of He at STP.

n = m/M = 0.0200 / 4.00 = 5.00 × 10−3 mol.

V = n × Vm = (5.00 × 10−3 mol) × 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 0.114 dm3.

Worked example

What volume of oxygen at STP is needed to completely burn 1.00 mol of butane, C4H10?

2C4H10(g) + 13O2(g) → 8CO2(g) + 10H2O(g)

The mole ratio C4H10 : O2 is 2 : 13, so 1.00 mol butane reacts with 6.50 mol O2.

V = 6.50 mol × 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 148 dm3.

Units of volumeThe SI unit of volume is m3, but dm3 and cm3 are far more convenient in the laboratory: 1 m3 = 1000 dm3, and 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3. The litre (l or L) is widely used in place of dm3, and the millilitre (ml or mL) in place of cm3, although only m3, dm3 and cm3 appear in examination questions.

R2.1.2 – Concentrations and Titration

The mole ratio of an equation can also be used to determine the concentration of a reactant or product in solution.

n = cV

where n is the amount in moles, c is the concentration in mol dm−3, and V is the volume in dm3. A standard solution is a solution of accurately known concentration.

Acid–base titration apparatusA known volume of one solution is measured accurately using a pipette and transferred into a conical flask. The other solution is placed in a burette, a calibrated glass tube with a tap at the bottom, clamped vertically above the flask so it can deliver precise, small volumes into the conical flask. A few drops of a suitable indicator are added to the flask; the indicator changes colour sharply at its end point, which is used to signal the equivalence point — the point at which the two solutions have reacted exactly, in the ratio given by the balanced equation. The volume of solution released from the burette to reach the end point is called the titre. Multiple titrations are usually performed, and concordant titres (typically agreeing to within 0.05 cm3 of each other) are averaged for a reliable result.
Burette uncertaintyA burette is read to a precision of ±0.05 cm3. Because a titre is calculated from an initial and a final burette reading, the absolute uncertainty in a single titre is 0.05 cm3 + 0.05 cm3 = ±0.10 cm3. Readings should always be recorded to this precision (for example 12.00 or 3.50 cm3, not 12.0 or 3.5 cm3).
Worked example

25.00 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 NaHCO3(aq) is titrated with dilute H2SO4(aq): 2NaHCO3(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l) + 2CO2(g). 15.20 cm3 of acid is required. Calculate the concentration of the acid.

n(NaHCO3) = cV = 0.100 mol dm−3 × (25.00/1000) dm3 = 2.50 × 10−3 mol.

From the mole ratio, 2n(NaHCO3) = n(H2SO4), so n(H2SO4) = 1.25 × 10−3 mol.

c = n/V = (1.25 × 10−3 mol) / (15.20/1000 dm3) = 0.0822 mol dm−3.

Volumetric-analysis strategy
  • Write a balanced equation for the reaction, if not already given.
  • Identify the reactant whose volume and concentration are both known, and use n = cV to find its moles.
  • Use the mole ratio in the equation to find the moles of the other reactant.
  • Use c = n/V to find the unknown concentration.

Back titration

A back titration is done in reverse: it is used when the end point of a direct titration is hard to identify, or when a reactant is impure or its exact amount is otherwise uncertain. A known excess of a standard reagent is added to react completely with the sample, and the unreacted excess is then determined by titration against a second standard solution. Subtracting the unreacted amount from the total amount originally added gives the amount that reacted with the sample.

Worked example

A 0.300 g antacid tablet containing NaHCO3 is added to 25.00 cm3 of 0.125 mol dm−3 HCl. The excess HCl requires 3.50 cm3 of 0.200 mol dm−3 NaOH to reach the equivalence point. Calculate the percentage by mass of NaHCO3 in the tablet.

NaHCO3(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g); HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) (both 1 : 1 ratios).

n(HCl total) = (25.00/1000) dm3 × 0.125 mol dm−3 = 3.13 × 10−3 mol.

n(NaOH) = (3.50/1000) dm3 × 0.200 mol dm−3 = 7.00 × 10−4 mol = n(HCl unreacted).

n(HCl reacted with tablet) = 3.13 × 10−3 − 7.00 × 10−4 = 2.43 × 10−3 mol = n(NaHCO3).

M(NaHCO3) = 22.99 + 1.01 + 12.01 + (3 × 16.00) = 84.01 g mol−1. m(NaHCO3) = 2.43 × 10−3 × 84.01 = 0.204 g.

% by mass = (0.204 / 0.300) × 100 = 68.0%. (This assumes that all the NaHCO3 reacted with the acid, and that no other component of the tablet reacted with HCl.)

R2.1.3 – The Limiting Reactant and Theoretical Yield

In most reactions, reactants are not supplied in exactly the ratio given by the balanced equation. The reactant that is completely used up first, and so determines the quantity of product formed, is called the limiting reactant. Any other reactant that is not fully used is said to be in excess.

Theoretical yieldThe theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product obtainable, in grams or moles, assuming 100% of the limiting reactant is converted into product.

Deliberately supplying one reactant in excess is a common laboratory and industrial strategy: it guarantees that the other, limiting, reactant is used up completely. For example, adding excess sodium carbonate, Na2CO3, to water contaminated with lead(II) ions ensures that essentially all the lead is precipitated and removed as insoluble lead carbonate:

Pb(NO3)2(aq) + Na2CO3(aq) → PbCO3(s) + 2NaNO3(aq)

Identifying the limiting reactant always depends on comparing mole ratios, so if reactant quantities are given in grams, they must first be converted to moles.

Worked example

N2 is prepared by 2NH3(g) + 3CuO(s) → N2(g) + 3Cu(s) + 3H2O(g). If 18.1 g NH3 reacts with 90.4 g CuO, determine the mass of N2 formed.

n(NH3) = 18.1 / 17.04 = 1.06 mol. n(CuO) = 90.4 / 79.55 = 1.14 mol.

Required mole ratio (from equation): NH3 : CuO = 2 : 3 = 0.667. Given mole ratio: 1.06 : 1.14 = 0.93.

Since the given ratio (0.93) is larger than the required ratio (0.667), there is relatively more NH3 than the equation needs, so NH3 is in excess and CuO is the limiting reactant.

Using the mole ratio CuO : N2 = 3 : 1: n(N2) = 1.14 / 3 = 0.380 mol.

m(N2) = n × M = 0.380 mol × 28.02 g mol−1 = 10.6 g N2.

Steps for limiting reactant problems
  • Write the balanced equation and note the mole ratio of the reactants.
  • Convert the given mass (or volume, or concentration and volume) of each reactant to moles.
  • Compare the given mole ratio of reactants with the ratio of their coefficients in the equation.
  • Identify the limiting reactant: it is the one present in a smaller ratio than the equation requires.
  • Use the moles of the limiting reactant, and the mole ratio in the equation, to calculate the moles (and then mass or volume) of product.

Calculating the amount of excess reactant remaining

It is sometimes useful to calculate how much of the excess reactant is left over once the reaction is complete — back titration, described above, is one indirect method of doing this.

Worked example

1 mol of CH4 and 1 mol of O2 are mixed: CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g). How much CH4 remains unreacted?

The required mole ratio CH4 : O2 is 1 : 2, but the given ratio is 1 : 1, so O2 is the limiting reactant. Only 0.5 mol CH4 can react with the 1 mol O2 available (ratio 1 : 2), leaving 0.5 mol CH4 unreacted at the end of the reaction.

R2.1.4 – Percentage Yield

Real reactions rarely proceed with no loss of product, no impurities, no wastage and no incomplete reaction, so the actual, experimental yield obtained is usually different from the theoretical yield calculated from the limiting reactant.

Causes of experimental yield differing from theoretical yield
Experimental yield lower than theoreticalExperimental yield higher than theoretical
Side reactions occurringImpurities present in the product
Loss of product during purificationProduct not fully dried
Reversible reactions preventing completion (Reactivity 2.3)—
An incomplete reaction (could affect the yield in either direction, depending on the type of reaction)

Comparing the experimental and theoretical yields gives a measure of the efficiency of converting reactants to products, expressed as the percentage yield:

percentage yield = (experimental yield / theoretical yield) × 100
Worked example

The theoretical yield of N2 from a reaction of NH3 and CuO is 10.65 g. An experiment produces 8.35 g N2. Calculate the percentage yield.

percentage yield = (8.35 / 10.65) × 100 = 78.4%.

Percentage yield is important both in evaluating laboratory experiments and in industry, where it is used to assess and improve the efficiency of large-scale processes such as pharmaceutical drug synthesis. Green Chemistry places great importance on increasing yield in order to reduce wastage.

R2.1.5 – Atom Economy

Green Chemistry is the sustainable design of chemical products and processes, aiming to minimize the use and generation of substances that are hazardous to human health and the environment. Percentage yield alone is a limited measure of efficiency, since it gives no indication of how much waste is produced — a reaction can have a high percentage yield while still generating large amounts of unwanted by-products. The atom economy instead measures what proportion of the total mass of the reactants ends up in the desired product.

% atom economy = (molar mass of desired product / molar mass of all reactants) × 100

(This equation is given in Section 1 of the data booklet.) Reactions with a high atom economy make more efficient use of natural resources and generate less waste, both key aims of sustainable chemical design.

Addition polymerizationAddition polymerization reactions have an atom economy of 100%, since every atom in the monomer reactants ends up in the polymer product, with no side products formed.
Worked example

Nickel is produced by NiO(s) + C(s) → Ni(s) + CO(g). Calculate the atom economy for the production of nickel, to 3 significant figures.

Molar mass of desired product, Ni = 58.69 g mol−1. Molar mass of all reactants = (58.69 + 16.00) + 12.01 = 86.70 g mol−1.

% atom economy = (58.69 / 86.70) × 100 = 67.7%.

Percentage yield vs atom economyPercentage yield and atom economy are different, complementary measures of the efficiency of a chemical process. A reaction can have a high percentage yield (little of the desired product is lost during the process actually carried out) yet a low atom economy (most of the mass of the reactants was always going to end up as waste by-products, not in the desired product), or vice versa. Many real-world syntheses, for example in drug manufacture, deliberately use an excess of one reactant to increase the percentage yield, at the cost of a lower atom economy.

Other factors used to gauge the overall efficiency of a chemical process include its rate (Reactivity 2.2), the quantities of reagents such as solvents and catalysts that are not themselves consumed, and its energy usage and economic efficiency.

Guiding Question Revisited

How are chemical equations used to calculate reacting ratios?
  • A balanced chemical equation gives the mole ratio of reactants and products directly from its stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Mole ratios can be converted to reacting masses using m = nM, to reacting gas volumes using Avogadro's law (or n = V/Vm at STP), and to reacting solution volumes and concentrations, via titration, using n = cV.
  • The limiting reactant is identified by comparing the given mole ratio of reactants with the ratio of coefficients in the equation; it determines the theoretical yield of product, while any other reactant is left in excess.
  • Percentage yield = (experimental yield / theoretical yield) × 100 measures how much of the theoretical maximum was actually obtained in practice.
  • Atom economy = (molar mass of desired product / molar mass of all reactants) × 100 measures how efficiently the mass of the reactants is converted into the desired product, a key concept in Green Chemistry.
