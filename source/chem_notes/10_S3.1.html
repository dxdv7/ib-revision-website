Guiding Question

How does the periodic table help us to predict patterns and trends in the properties of elements?

Chemistry is not a random collection of facts about 118 elements memorized in isolation; it is the study of patterns and trends. The periodic table is the single most powerful tool for this task, because it arranges the elements so that similar elements sit close together and predictable trends run across and down the table. First proposed by the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev in 1869, the periodic table groups elements by their chemical and physical properties — a pattern later shown to be a direct consequence of the periodic repetition of electron configurations. Once the position of an element in the table is known, a great deal about its electron configuration, reactivity, bonding and physical properties can be predicted without ever testing it in a laboratory.

Periods, Groups and Blocks

The periodic law and the development of the table

The periodic law states that, at regular but varying intervals, the chemical elements show an approximate repetition of their properties. Mendeleev arranged the 62 elements known in 1869 into rows and columns by their properties, and — crucially — left gaps for elements that had not yet been discovered, predicting their properties from the surrounding pattern. When elements such as gallium, scandium, germanium and technetium were later isolated, their measured properties matched Mendeleev’s predictions closely, which gave the table immediate scientific credibility. Mendeleev had no knowledge of atomic structure; only after the discovery of the electron (Thomson, 1897) and the nuclear model of the atom (Rutherford and Bohr) could the periodic law be explained. We now know that the periodicity in the elements’ properties arises directly from the periodicity of their electron configurations.

PeriodicityPeriodicity is the regular repetition of the properties of the elements, arising from the pattern in which their electrons fill successive energy levels and sublevels.

Periods and groups

In the modern periodic table, elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number, Z (the number of protons in the nucleus), rather than by relative atomic mass as Mendeleev originally used. Because there are no gaps left in the sequence of atomic numbers up to 118, chemists are confident that the search for new naturally occurring elements is essentially complete; any further elements can only be made artificially.

  • The horizontal rows of the table are called periods, numbered 1 to 7. The period number equals the number of occupied principal electron energy levels (shells) in atoms of that element.
  • The vertical columns are called groups, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in the same group share the same number of valence (outer-shell) electrons and consequently have similar chemical properties.
The layout of the periodic tablePicture a grid seven rows deep (periods 1–7) and eighteen columns wide (groups 1–18). Two short rows — the lanthanides and actinides — are lifted out from between groups 3 and 4 of periods 6 and 7 and printed below the main body of the table purely to keep it a manageable width. Reading left to right and top to bottom, atomic number increases by exactly one at every step, with no gaps anywhere in the sequence from hydrogen (Z = 1) to oganesson (Z = 118).

Metals, non-metals and metalloids

A bold zig-zag “staircase” line running diagonally from boron (group 13) down to astatine (group 17) divides the periodic table into two great territories.

Metals vs non-metals vs metalloidsMetals occupy the whole left and centre of the table: the s block, the d block, the f block “island”, and a handful of elements at the lower-left of the p block such as aluminium, tin and lead. Metals are shiny, malleable, good conductors of heat and electricity, and tend to lose electrons when they react. Non-metals are confined to the upper right-hand corner of the p block (plus hydrogen). They are typically dull, brittle if solid, poor conductors, and tend to gain electrons when they react. Metalloids — boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony, tellurium and polonium — sit along the staircase itself. They look and behave physically like metals but react chemically more like non-metals; several, such as silicon and germanium, are semiconductors and form the basis of the electronics industry.
The metal/non-metal staircaseStarting at boron in period 2, the diagonal boundary steps down and to the right one element at a time: boron, then between aluminium and silicon, then between germanium and its neighbours, continuing down through arsenic, antimony, tellurium and polonium, finally reaching astatine at the bottom right of the p block. Every element to the lower-left of this staircase is a metal, every element to the upper-right of it is a non-metal, and the seven elements sitting directly on the staircase are the metalloids.

Named groups and blocks

  • Group 1 — the alkali metals (lithium to francium), each with a single valence electron in an s sublevel.
  • Group 17 — the halogens (fluorine to astatine), each with seven valence electrons.
  • Group 18 — the noble gases (helium to oganesson), each with a complete outer energy level (an octet, except helium with a full first shell of two).
  • The d block elements (scandium to zinc in period 4, and their equivalents in periods 5 and 6) are called transition elements.

Periodicity and Electron Configuration

An element’s position in the periodic table is a direct readout of its ground-state electron configuration.

Reading the tableThe period number gives the number of occupied principal energy levels (shells) in an atom. The group number (1–18) is related to the number of valence electrons: for the main-group elements (s and p blocks), groups 1–2 have 1–2 valence electrons, and groups 13–18 have 3–8 valence electrons respectively (group 18 has 8, except helium with 2).

For example, sodium (Z = 11) has the configuration 1s22s22p63s1. It sits in period 3 because its highest occupied shell is the third, and in group 1 because it has one electron in that outer shell. Bromine (Z = 35) has the configuration 1s22s22p63s23p63d104s24p5, usually condensed to [Ar]3d104s24p5; it is in period 4 (its highest shell is the fourth) and group 17 (its outer 4s and 4p electrons together give 7 valence electrons).

Worked example

How many electrons are in the valence energy level of iodine (Z = 53), and what does this tell us about its group?
Locate iodine in the periodic table: it is in period 5, so it has the noble-gas core of krypton, [Kr], followed by electrons filling 5s, then 4d, then 5p.
Its full configuration is [Kr]4d105s25p5.
Counting the outer (n = 5) electrons: 5s25p5 gives 2 + 5 = 7 valence electrons, confirming that iodine belongs to group 17, the halogens.

Worked example

Deduce the period and group of the element with atomic number 20, and hence classify it.
Its configuration is 1s22s22p63s23p64s2, or [Ar]4s2.
The highest occupied shell is n = 4, so the element is in period 4.
It has 2 valence electrons in an s sublevel, so it is in group 2 of the s block.
This is calcium, an alkaline earth metal.

This relationship works in both directions: given an atomic number up to Z = 36, the electron configuration can be built up by filling sublevels in order of increasing energy (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p …), and given a position in the table, the configuration can be deduced without any calculation at all.

Group vs period, at a glanceIt is easy to muddle these two terms. A period is a row (reads across); its number tells you how many shells are occupied. A group is a column (reads down); its number is linked to the number of valence electrons and therefore to chemical behaviour — elements in the same group react in chemically similar ways.

Periodicity in Physical Properties

Many of the trends in atomic and physical properties across the periodic table can be traced back to a single underlying idea: effective nuclear charge, the net positive pull felt by an atom’s outer (valence) electrons once the shielding effect of inner electrons is accounted for. Effective nuclear charge increases across a period (protons are added to the nucleus one at a time while the same inner shells do the shielding) but stays roughly constant down a group (extra protons are added, but so are extra complete inner shells that shield the new outer electron just as effectively).

Atomic radius

Atomic radiusThe (covalent) atomic radius, r, is taken as half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond. It is not a sharply defined boundary — electrons occupy orbitals that describe a probability distribution with no fixed edge — but this operational definition allows radii to be compared consistently.

Down a group, atomic radius increases. Each successive element has one more occupied principal energy level than the one above it, so its outer electrons sit further from the nucleus, even though the nuclear charge is also greater. In group 1, atomic radius increases steadily from lithium (130 pm) to sodium (160 pm) to potassium (200 pm) to rubidium (215 pm) to caesium (238 pm), tracking the number of occupied shells (2, 3, 4, 5, 6 respectively).

Across a period, atomic radius decreases. All the elements in a period have the same number of occupied shells, but the nuclear charge (and hence the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer shell) increases steadily from left to right, pulling the outer electrons progressively closer to the nucleus. Across period 3, radius falls from 160 pm (Na) to 140 pm (Mg), 124 pm (Al), 114 pm (Si), 109 pm (P), 104 pm (S), 100 pm (Cl). The decrease is substantial: the chlorine atom is only about 60% the size of the sodium atom, even though chlorine has 7 more protons and 7 more electrons.

Atomic radius vs atomic numberPlot atomic radius (pm) on the vertical axis against atomic number on the horizontal axis for the first 20 elements. The line is a repeating saw-tooth: it falls fairly steadily across each period (a steep drop from each group 1 element to the following noble gas) and then jumps sharply back up at the start of the next period, because the newly added principal energy level outweighs the reset in nuclear charge. The overall envelope of the peaks (Li, Na, K …, all group 1) rises steadily left to right along the graph, showing the increase in atomic radius down group 1.

Ionic radius

Forming an ion changes an atom’s radius in a predictable way.

  • Positive ions are smaller than their parent atom, because forming a cation removes the entire outer occupied shell (or empties it substantially), so the remaining electrons are held by a now higher effective charge per remaining electron.
  • Negative ions are larger than their parent atom, because extra electrons added to the same outer shell increase electron–electron repulsion, forcing the electron cloud to expand.

Across period 3, the ionic radii of the common ions fall into two separate decreasing sequences: Na+ (102 pm) > Mg2+ (72 pm) > Al3+ (54 pm) > Si4+ (40 pm) for the cations (groups 1–14), each smaller than the last because increasing nuclear charge pulls the same two-shell electron arrangement in tighter; and Si4− (271 pm) > P3− (212 pm) > S2− (184 pm) > Cl− (181 pm) for the anions (groups 14–17), again shrinking as nuclear charge rises across the period. There is a large jump between the Si4+ and Si4− ions because the cation has lost its outer (n = 3) shell entirely and is measured by its n = 2 shell, while the anion still has a filled n = 3 shell — it is the difference in the number of occupied shells, not just nuclear charge, that produces this discontinuity. Down a group, ionic radius increases for the same reason as atomic radius: successive ions have progressively more occupied shells.

Worked example

Explain the trend in radius across the isoelectronic species O2−, F−, Ne, Na+ and Mg2+.
All five species have exactly 10 electrons and the same electron configuration, 1s22s22p6.
Their nuclear charges, however, increase steadily: O (+8), F (+9), Ne (+10), Na (+11), Mg (+12).
Since the number of electrons (and hence the amount of electron–electron repulsion) is fixed, a larger nuclear charge pulls the same electron cloud in more tightly.
So the radius decreases steadily along the series: O2− is the largest and Mg2+ is the smallest, even though Mg has more protons and more mass.

Ionization energy

First ionization energyThe first ionization energy of an element is the energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms of that element in their ground state: X(g) → X+(g) + e−. It is always endothermic, since energy must be supplied to pull a negatively charged electron away from a positively charged nucleus.

Down a group, first ionization energy decreases. The electron being removed occupies a shell further from the nucleus in each successive element; although the actual nuclear charge increases, the effective nuclear charge stays roughly constant because the extra protons are matched by extra inner shielding shells, so the dominant effect is the increased distance, which weakens the attraction and makes the electron easier to remove.

Across a period, first ionization energy generally increases. Effective nuclear charge rises steadily left to right (electrons added at the same shell shield each other poorly), so outer electrons are held more tightly and become harder to remove.

First ionization energy vs atomic number (first 20 elements)Plot first ionization energy (kJ mol−1) against atomic number. Each noble gas (He, Ne, Ar) sits at a sharp peak, since a complete outer shell is exceptionally hard to ionize; each alkali metal (Li, Na, K) sits at a deep trough immediately afterward, since its single, well-shielded outer electron is the easiest of all to remove. Between each trough and peak the line climbs in a slightly irregular staircase rather than a smooth diagonal: within period 2, for instance, the value dips slightly at boron (group 13) compared with beryllium (group 2), because boron’s outer electron occupies a slightly higher-energy 2p orbital rather than the filled, extra-stable 2s sublevel; it dips again at oxygen (group 16) compared with nitrogen (group 15), because nitrogen’s three 2p electrons are each alone in their own orbital (an extra-stable, half-filled sublevel) whereas oxygen’s fourth 2p electron must pair up, increasing electron–electron repulsion and making it slightly easier to remove. The same two small dips, after groups 2 and 15, recur in every period.

The trend in ionization energy is essentially the mirror image of the trend in atomic radius: both measure the strength of attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons, so whatever makes an atom smaller also makes it harder to ionize.

Electron affinity

First electron affinityThe first electron affinity of an element is the energy change when one mole of electrons is added to one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions: X(g) + e− → X−(g). Because the added electron is attracted to the positive nucleus, this process is usually exothermic.

Electron affinity becomes generally more exothermic (a larger magnitude, negative value) across a period, and less exothermic down a group, following the same effective-nuclear-charge argument as ionization energy: a small atom with a high effective nuclear charge attracts an incoming electron more strongly. The group 17 halogens, with an effective nuclear charge of roughly +7 acting on an almost-complete outer shell, have the most exothermic electron affinities of any group. The noble gases do not generally form stable negative ions and so are not assigned electron affinity values, and a few individual elements (beryllium, nitrogen and magnesium among them) are exceptions to the general trend because an incoming electron must enter a new, higher-energy sublevel, or pair up in an already half-filled one, either of which reduces the energy released.

Electronegativity

ElectronegativityElectronegativity is a measure of the ability of an atom to attract the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond towards itself. Unlike ionization energy, it is not a directly measurable property of an isolated atom — it describes an atom’s behaviour specifically within a bond, and the most widely used scale (devised by Linus Pauling in 1932) is based on bond energies. The concept is not applied to the group 18 elements, which do not generally form covalent bonds.

The trends mirror those of ionization energy, for the same underlying reason (attraction of the nucleus for outer electrons, this time bonding electrons): electronegativity increases across a period as effective nuclear charge rises, and decreases down a group as the bonding electrons sit further from the nucleus. Fluorine, at the top right of the table, is the most electronegative element of all (electronegativity 4.0 on the Pauling scale); francium, at the bottom left, has one of the lowest values (about 0.6). Because ionization energy and electronegativity share the same general trend, an element such as sodium, with a low ionization energy, also has a low electronegativity, and elements within the same group tend to have similar electronegativities to one another.

Electronegativity across period 3Plot electronegativity on the vertical axis against the period 3 elements (Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl) on the horizontal axis. The line rises in a fairly smooth, steady staircase from about 0.9 at sodium up to about 3.0 at chlorine, climbing towards — but never quite reaching — the value of 3.4 assigned to oxygen. This steady climb is the same underlying trend that causes the period 3 oxides to become progressively less ionic and more covalent from left to right, since a bond is more ionic the greater the electronegativity difference between its two atoms.
Ionization energy vs electronegativityThese two properties follow the same general trends but are conceptually distinct. Ionization energy is a measurable property of an isolated gaseous atom (removing an electron completely). Electronegativity is a relative, dimensionless measure of an atom’s pulling power on electrons it is already sharing in a covalent bond with another atom — it cannot be measured for an isolated atom on its own.

Melting point

Melting point periodicity is governed less by a single smooth trend and more by changes in structure and bonding across a period. Within group 1, melting point decreases down the group (from lithium, 181°C, to caesium, 28°C) because the metallic bonds weaken as the atoms get larger: the delocalized outer electrons sit further from the positive ion cores, so the electrostatic attraction holding the metallic lattice together is reduced. Within group 17, melting point increases down the group (from fluorine, a gas, to iodine, a solid) because the molecules get larger and more polarizable, strengthening the London (van der Waals) forces between them.

Across period 3, melting point does not simply rise or fall — it tracks the change in bonding and structure described in the next section. It climbs from sodium through magnesium to aluminium as metallic bonding strengthens (more delocalized electrons per atom and a higher positive charge on each ion core), rockets to a maximum at silicon (a giant covalent, macromolecular network held together throughout by strong covalent bonds), and then collapses to much lower values for phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine, which exist as small, discrete, molecular substances (P4, S8, Cl2) held together in the solid state only by weak London forces between molecules, before reaching its lowest point at argon, a monatomic gas with the weakest London forces of all.

Melting point across period 3Plot melting point on the vertical axis against the period 3 elements (Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar) on the horizontal axis. The line rises steadily through the three metals, Na < Mg < Al, then shoots up to a tall, sharp spike at silicon — by far the highest point on the graph, reflecting its giant covalent network. Immediately after silicon the line plunges to a very low value at phosphorus, rises slightly at sulfur (whose S8 rings are larger and more polarizable than P4 molecules), drops again at chlorine (Cl2, a smaller molecule with weaker London forces), and reaches its lowest point of all at argon, a single, small, monatomic gas.

Periodicity in Reactivity

The chemical reactivity of an element is governed by how easily its atoms can lose or gain electrons to reach the stable, complete outer-shell arrangement of the nearest noble gas.

Group 18: the noble gases

The noble gases (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon, oganesson) are the least reactive elements in the periodic table because their atoms already have a complete outer energy level (a full octet, or a full first shell of 2 for helium), so there is no thermodynamic driving force to lose or gain electrons. Historically called the “inert gases”, this name is now considered inaccurate: xenon, which has the lowest ionization energy in the group, was made to react (forming a compound with fluorine) as early as 1962, showing that even “noble” gases can, under the right conditions, be persuaded to react.

Group 1: the alkali metals

The alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium) are soft, low-density, silvery metals that are good conductors of heat and electricity. They are far too reactive to occur naturally as free elements and must be stored under oil to keep them away from air and moisture.

Group 1 reactivity trendReactivity of the alkali metals increases down the group. Each element forms a singly charged M+ ion by losing its one valence electron, and first ionization energy decreases down the group (the valence electron is progressively further from the nucleus and no better shielded), so the outer electron is lost more and more readily from lithium down to francium.

All the alkali metals react with water to give hydrogen gas and a metal hydroxide, which dissolves to make the solution alkaline (hence the group’s name):

2M(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H2(g)

For potassium specifically: 2K(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H2(g), or, showing the ionic species that actually form in solution, 2K(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2K+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) + H2(g). The vigour of this reaction increases visibly down the group: lithium floats and fizzes gently, keeping its shape; sodium reacts vigorously enough that the heat released melts the unreacted metal into a small silvery ball that darts across the surface; potassium reacts so vigorously that the heat ignites the hydrogen gas itself, producing a lilac flame; and caesium reacts explosively.

Group 17: the halogens

The halogens (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine) exist as coloured diatomic molecules, X2, and show a clear gradation in physical state and appearance down the group.

Physical state and colour down group 17Reading down the group: fluorine, F2, is a very pale yellow gas; chlorine, Cl2, is a denser, greenish-yellow gas; bromine, Br2, is one of only two elements that is liquid at room temperature, a dark red-brown liquid that readily gives off an orange-brown vapour; and iodine, I2, is a dark grey-black crystalline solid that sublimes to a violet vapour on gentle heating. This steady progression from gas to liquid to solid, and the deepening of colour, reflects the steadily increasing strength of the London (van der Waals) forces between the larger, more polarizable molecules further down the group.
Group 17 reactivity trendReactivity of the halogens (as oxidizing agents) decreases down the group. Each halogen atom reacts by gaining one electron to complete its outer octet, forming a singly charged X− ion; this pull on an incoming electron is strongest for the smallest atom, fluorine, whose high effective nuclear charge (about +7) acts over the shortest distance. Further down the group, the increasing atomic radius places the incoming electron further from the nucleus, so the attraction — and hence the reactivity — weakens. Fluorine is accordingly the most reactive non-metal in the entire periodic table.

Displacement reactions

The relative reactivity of the halogens can be demonstrated directly by placing two halogens in competition for the same electron. A more reactive (higher) halogen will displace a less reactive (lower) halogen from a solution of its halide salt:

Cl2(aq) + 2NaBr(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + Br2(aq)
or, as an ionic equation: Cl2(aq) + 2Br−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + Br2(aq)
Worked example

Chlorine water is added to a colourless solution of potassium bromide. Describe and explain what happens.
Chlorine is above bromine in group 17, so it is the more reactive (more strongly oxidizing) halogen: its smaller atomic radius and higher effective nuclear charge give it a stronger pull on the electron it needs to complete its outer shell.
Chlorine therefore takes an electron from each bromide ion, so Cl2 is reduced to Cl− while Br− is oxidized to Br2.
The observation is a colour change from colourless to orange, as free bromine, Br2(aq), is formed: Cl2(aq) + 2Br−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + Br2(aq). No visible reaction occurs if chlorine water is instead added to a fluoride solution, because fluorine is above chlorine and so cannot be displaced by it.

Halide ions can also be identified by precipitation with silver ions: Ag+(aq) + X−(aq) → AgX(s). Silver chloride is a white precipitate, silver bromide is cream/pale yellow, and silver iodide is a distinctly darker yellow — a visual trend that itself follows the increasing size and polarizability of the halide ion down the group.

Metal and Non-metal Oxides

Metallic and non-metallic character is not a sharp switch but a continuum, and nowhere is this clearer than in the bonding, structure and acid–base behaviour of the period 3 oxides.

Bonding and structure of the period 3 oxides

Whether an oxide is ionic or covalent depends on the electronegativity difference between the element and oxygen (electronegativity 3.4). Because electronegativity increases across period 3, the ionic character of the oxides steadily decreases from left to right as each element’s own electronegativity gets closer to that of oxygen.

The maximum oxidation state shown by each period 3 element in its oxide equals its group number for groups 1–17 (using the 1–18 numbering: group 1 gives +1, group 2 gives +2, group 13 gives +3, and so on, up to +7 for chlorine in group 17) — a direct consequence of each atom using all of its valence electrons to bond with the highly electronegative oxygen. Molten conductivity gives direct experimental evidence for this bonding pattern: an oxide only conducts electricity when charged ions are free to move, so the high conductivity of molten Na2O, MgO and Al2O3 confirms their ionic lattices, while the total absence of conductivity in molten P4O10, SO3 and Cl2O7 confirms that they are simple covalent molecules with no free ions. High melting points are generally associated with giant (ionic or covalent) structures, and low melting points with small, simple molecular structures — exactly the pattern already described for the period 3 elements themselves.

Acid–base character of the period 3 oxides

The metal-to-non-metal oxide continuumIonic, metallic oxides are generally basic; covalent, non-metallic oxides are generally acidic; and oxides with a mixture of ionic and covalent character, such as aluminium oxide, can be amphoteric — capable of reacting as both an acid and a base.
Acid–base gradient across period 3Imagine a strip of universal indicator paper dipped into aqueous solutions (or suspensions) of each period 3 oxide, arranged left to right in the order Na2O, MgO, Al2O3, SiO2, P4O10, SO3, Cl2O7. The strip starts deep purple/blue at sodium oxide (strongly alkaline, pH well above 7), fades to a slightly less intense blue at magnesium oxide, sits at a neutral green in the middle for aluminium oxide (amphoteric, and in any case nearly insoluble, so it barely shifts the pH) and for silicon dioxide (insoluble and only very weakly acidic), then shifts decisively through yellow and into deep red by phosphorus and sulfur oxides, ending at the most intense red of all for the chlorine oxides (pH well below 7). The overall gradient runs smoothly from strongly basic on the left to strongly acidic on the right, mirroring the change from ionic to covalent bonding.

Basic oxides

Group 1 and group 2 metal oxides dissolve in water to give alkaline solutions containing hydroxide ions:

Na2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq)
MgO(s) + H2O(l) → Mg(OH)2(aq)

A basic oxide also reacts directly with an acid to form a salt and water, since the oxide ion combines with two H+ ions to form water: O2−(s) + 2H+(aq) → H2O(l); for example, MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l).

Acidic oxides

Non-metal oxides react with water to give acidic solutions. Carbon dioxide dissolves to form the weak acid carbonic acid: H2O(l) + CO2(g) ⇌ H2CO3(aq). Sulfur trioxide and sulfur dioxide react with water to give two different sulfur oxoacids:

SO3(l) + H2O(l) → H2SO4(aq)  (sulfuric(VI) acid)
SO2(g) + H2O(l) → H2SO3(aq)  (sulfuric(IV) acid)

Amphoteric oxides

Aluminium oxide barely dissolves in pure water (so it has little effect on pH on its own), but it demonstrates its amphoteric character by reacting with both acids and bases. It behaves as a base towards a strong acid:

Al2O3(s) + 6H+(aq) → 2Al3+(aq) + 3H2O(l)

and as an acid towards a strong alkali:

Al2O3(s) + 3H2O(l) + 2OH−(aq) → 2Al(OH)4−(aq)

Acid rain and ocean acidification

All rainwater is naturally slightly acidic (pH around 5.6) because atmospheric carbon dioxide continually dissolves in it to form carbonic acid. Acid rain refers specifically to precipitation that is more acidic than this natural background level, owing to dissolved sulfur and nitrogen oxides from human activity.

Sulfur dioxide is released mainly by burning fossil fuels (coal in particular) and by metal-ore smelting: S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g). It dissolves directly in rainwater to give sulfuric(IV) (sulfurous) acid, or is first oxidized in the atmosphere to sulfur trioxide, which forms the much stronger sulfuric(VI) acid:

H2O(l) + SO2(g) → H2SO3(aq)
2SO2(g) + O2(g) → 2SO3(g)
H2O(l) + SO3(g) → H2SO4(aq)

Nitrogen oxides form mainly in the high-temperature environment of internal combustion engines, where nitrogen and oxygen from the air are forced to combine: N2(g) + O2(g) → 2NO(g). Nitrogen monoxide is then oxidized further to brown nitrogen dioxide, which dissolves in rainwater to give a mixture of nitric(III) (nitrous) acid and nitric(V) acid: H2O(l) + 2NO2(g) → HNO2(aq) + HNO3(aq).

Ocean acidificationAbout half of the carbon dioxide released by burning fossil fuels dissolves into the oceans, where it forms carbonic acid, which partly dissociates into hydrogencarbonate and carbonate ions: CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2CO3(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq) ⇌ 2H+(aq) + CO32−(aq). The resulting rise in ocean surface acidity inhibits shell and skeleton growth in many marine organisms and is linked to reproductive problems in some fish species.

Oxidation States

Oxidation stateThe oxidation state (or oxidation number) of an atom is the charge that atom would have if the compound it is part of were composed entirely of ions. It is a book-keeping tool for tracking how electrons are distributed between atoms, whether the actual bonding is ionic or covalent.

Oxidation was originally defined narrowly as a reaction with oxygen, but the concept is far more general. Consider magnesium burning in oxygen, and separately reacting with chlorine:

Mg(s) + ½O2(g) → MgO(s)
Mg(s) + Cl2(g) → MgCl2(s)

In both reactions magnesium is converted from a neutral atom into a 2+ ion, Mg → Mg2+ + 2e−, losing two electrons regardless of which non-metal it reacts with. Because oxidation state tracks exactly this kind of electron loss or gain, chemists now define oxidation more broadly, so that it applies to reactions that do not involve oxygen at all.

Four equivalent definitions of oxidationOxidation is: the addition of oxygen; the removal of hydrogen; the loss of electrons; and an increase in oxidation state. (Reduction is the reverse of each: removal of oxygen, addition of hydrogen, gain of electrons, and a decrease in oxidation state.) Magnesium’s oxidation state rises from 0 (in the free element) to +2 (in MgO or MgCl2): magnesium has been oxidized.

Rules for assigning oxidation states

The oxidation state of every atom in any species can be worked out from a short list of rules, applied in order:

  1. An atom in a free, uncombined element has an oxidation state of 0 (e.g. Mg, O2, N2 and Ar are all 0), because its electrons are shared equally, or not at all, among identical atoms.
  2. In a simple (monatomic) ion, the oxidation state equals the charge on the ion (Mg in Mg2+ is +2; O in O2− is −2; N in N3− is −3).
  3. The oxidation states of all atoms in a neutral compound must sum to zero.
  4. The oxidation states of all atoms in a polyatomic ion must sum to the overall charge on that ion.
  5. In compounds, group 1 metals are always +1 and group 2 metals are always +2 (matching the charge on their most common ion).
  6. Fluorine is always −1 in its compounds, because it is the most electronegative element of all.
  7. Oxygen is usually −2, except in peroxides such as H2O2 (where it is −1, since the O–O bond splits the usual electron count between two oxygens) and in compounds with fluorine (where it is positive, since fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen, e.g. +2 in OF2).
  8. Hydrogen is usually +1, except in metal hydrides such as NaH and MgH2, where it is −1 (hydrogen is less electronegative than a group 1 or 2 metal, so it is hydrogen that gains control of the shared electrons).
Neutral compound: sum of oxidation states = 0
Polyatomic ion: sum of oxidation states = charge on the ion
Worked example

Assign the oxidation state of sulfur in (a) H2SO4 and (b) SO32−.
(a) H2SO4 is a neutral molecule, so all the oxidation states must sum to zero. Using H = +1 (rule 8) and O = −2 (rule 7): 2(+1) + S + 4(−2) = 0, so S = 8 − 2 = +6.
(b) SO32− is a polyatomic ion, so the oxidation states must sum to −2, its overall charge. Using O = −2: S + 3(−2) = −2, so S = −2 + 6 = +4.
Sulfur can clearly exist in more than one oxidation state depending on the compound — here +6 in sulfuric(VI) acid and +4 in the sulfate(IV), or sulfite, ion.

Worked example: identifying oxidation and reduction

Identify what is oxidized and what is reduced when hydrogen reacts with chlorine: H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g).
In the elements H2 and Cl2, both oxidation states are 0 (rule 1).
In the product HCl, hydrogen is bonded to the more electronegative chlorine, so H = +1 and Cl = −1 (their sum is zero, as required for a neutral compound).
Hydrogen’s oxidation state has increased, 0 → +1: hydrogen is oxidized.
Chlorine’s oxidation state has decreased, 0 → −1: chlorine is reduced.

Worked example: disproportionation

Nitrogen dioxide dissolves in rainwater: H2O(l) + 2NO2(g) → HNO2(aq) + HNO3(aq). Show that nitrogen is both oxidized and reduced in this reaction.
In NO2, using O = −2: N + 2(−2) = 0, so N = +4.
In HNO2, using H = +1 and O = −2: +1 + N + 2(−2) = 0, so N = +3.
In HNO3, using H = +1 and O = −2: +1 + N + 3(−2) = 0, so N = +5.
Nitrogen starts at +4 in NO2 and ends up at both +3 (reduced, in HNO2) and +5 (oxidized, in HNO3).

DisproportionationA disproportionation reaction is a redox reaction in which atoms of the same element, starting in a single oxidation state, end up simultaneously oxidized and reduced into two different oxidation states in the products.

Naming compounds with oxidation numbers

Because many elements (particularly transition metals and non-metals such as nitrogen, sulfur and chlorine) form more than one oxidation state, IUPAC nomenclature inserts the oxidation number, written as a Roman numeral in brackets directly after the element’s name (with no space), to remove any ambiguity. For example, the S = +6 species H2SO4 is sulfuric(VI) acid, and its oxoanion SO42− is the sulfate(VI) ion, while the S = +4 species H2SO3 is sulfuric(IV) acid and SO32− is the sulfate(IV) ion. The everyday names sulfuric acid, sulfurous acid, nitric acid and nitrous acid remain acceptable, but the oxidation-number system stays unambiguous even for less familiar species.

Worked example

Deduce the systematic (oxidation-number) name of (a) N2O5, (b) NO2, and (c) HNO2.
(a) N2O5 is neutral. Using O = −2: 2N + 5(−2) = 0, so 2N = +10, giving N = +5. Name: nitrogen(V) oxide.
(b) NO2 is neutral. Using O = −2: N + 2(−2) = 0, so N = +4. Name: nitrogen(IV) oxide.
(c) HNO2 is neutral. Using H = +1 and O = −2: 1 + N + 2(−2) = 0, so N = 4 − 1 = +3. Name: nitric(III) acid.

Common mistake: averaged oxidation statesSome species contain the same element in more than one distinct chemical environment, so the oxidation state calculated from the rules is only an average. In the tetrathionate ion, S4O62− (found in Na2S4O6), applying the rules gives 4S + 6(−2) = −2, so 4S = +10 and S = +2.5. A non-integer oxidation state like this is a signal that the four sulfur atoms are not all chemically equivalent (two are bonded only to other sulfur atoms, and two are also bonded to oxygen) rather than an error in applying the rules.

Guiding Question Revisited

How does the periodic table help us to predict patterns and trends in the properties of elements?

  • Elements are arranged by increasing atomic number into periods (rows, giving the number of occupied shells) and groups (columns, giving the number of valence electrons and hence similar chemical behaviour), and can be divided into s, p, d and f blocks according to which sublevel is being filled.
  • Metals occupy the left and centre of the table, non-metals the upper right, and metalloids form a diagonal staircase between them.
  • Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group; ionic radius follows the same pattern once account is taken of whether the ion is a cation or an anion.
  • First ionization energy, electron affinity (in magnitude) and electronegativity all increase across a period and decrease down a group, since each tracks the strength of attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons.
  • Melting point across a period rises through the metals, peaks sharply at the giant covalent structure (silicon in period 3), then falls to low values for the simple molecular non-metal elements.
  • Reactivity increases down group 1 (electrons are lost more easily) but decreases down group 17 (electrons are gained less easily), because the two groups react in opposite directions.
  • Period 3 oxides shift from giant ionic and basic (Na, Mg), through amphoteric (Al), to giant covalent (Si) and simple molecular, acidic (P, S, Cl), tracking the steady increase in electronegativity across the period.
  • The oxidation state of an atom is the charge it would carry if the compound were fully ionic, assigned using a consistent set of rules; oxidation is an increase in oxidation state, and IUPAC names use Roman numerals to show oxidation number unambiguously.
