Guiding Question

What determines the metallic nature and properties of an element?

Metals have played a defining role in human history — the Bronze Age and Iron Age are named after them — and remain essential to modern technology, from electrical wiring to spacecraft mirrors. Metals have a set of characteristic physical properties (electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability, ductility, lustre, generally high melting points) that are clearly different from those of ionic or covalent substances. This sub-topic introduces the metallic model of bonding, which explains those properties, and looks at how the strength of metallic bonding — and hence properties such as melting point and reactivity — varies across the periodic table.

The Metallic Bond

Metals are found on the left-hand side and centre of the periodic table. Their atoms have relatively few electrons in their outer (valence) shell and low ionization energies, so they readily lose those valence electrons rather than sharing or fully gaining electrons. When many metal atoms pack together in the solid or liquid state, with no other element present to accept the lost electrons, those valence electrons do not stay attached to any single nucleus. Instead they spread out, or delocalize, throughout the whole structure, while the metal atoms themselves become positively charged ions (cations) arranged in a regular, repeating lattice.

Metallic bondA metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positively charged metal cations and a “sea” of delocalized electrons that are free to move throughout the whole structure, belonging to no single ion in particular.
The metallic model: lattice of cations and sea of electronsA regular, repeating grid of positively charged circles (each labelled with a + charge, representing metal cations) arranged in neat rows and columns, like a cross-section through a crystal. Scattered freely throughout the gaps between the cations are many small, unlabelled dots representing delocalized electrons, drawn without any fixed position or association with a particular cation, suggesting they can move freely in any direction throughout the entire lattice, much like a fluid or “sea” surrounding and holding together the fixed grid of positive ions.

A helpful analogy is a close-knit neighbourhood in which children move freely between houses rather than staying only with their own parents; the constant mixing between households creates a strong overall bond between all the families. In the same way, delocalized electrons are shared among all the cations in the lattice rather than belonging to any one of them, and the resulting electrostatic attraction between the whole sea of electrons and the whole lattice of cations holds the metal structure together.

Explaining the Properties of Metals

Each characteristic physical property of metals can be traced directly back to the presence of delocalized electrons moving freely through a lattice of cations.

Malleable vs ductileBoth terms describe a metal’s ability to change shape without breaking, but malleable specifically refers to being hammered, rolled or pressed into flat sheets or other shapes, while ductile refers to being stretched or drawn out into a thin wire. Both properties have the same underlying cause: the non-directional nature of metallic bonding allows the cation lattice to deform while the delocalized electron sea keeps the structure bonded together throughout.

Alloys

Because metallic bonding is non-directional (the delocalized electrons are not associated with any particular pair of ions, unlike a covalent bond), a metal’s properties can be modified by mixing it, in the molten state, with other metallic or non-metallic elements. The resulting solid mixture is called an alloy.

AlloyA mixture, formed by combining a molten metal with one or more other elements (metallic or non-metallic), that solidifies with the added atoms sitting within (or substituting into) the original metal’s cation lattice. The metallic bonding is retained throughout the mixture, since the delocalized electrons continue to attract the cations from every direction even with foreign atoms present.

Steel is a widely used example: it is made by adding small amounts of carbon into molten iron. Even though carbon atoms occupy positions within the iron lattice, the electrostatic attraction between the iron cations and the delocalized electrons continues to act in all directions, so the metallic bonding is preserved. Because the carbon atoms disrupt the even layers of the pure iron lattice, they make it harder for layers of ions to slide past each other, giving steel a greater tensile strength (resistance to being pulled apart) than pure iron, which is one reason steel is so widely used in construction.

Factors Affecting the Strength of a Metallic Bond

Not all metals bond equally strongly. The strength of a metallic bond — and hence properties such as melting point — depends on three related factors.

Factors determining metallic bond strengthA metallic bond is stronger (and the metal has a higher melting point) when: (1) the cation carries a greater positive charge; (2) there are more delocalized electrons per atom contributed to the electron sea; and (3) the cation has a smaller ionic radius. All three factors increase the electrostatic force of attraction between the cations and the delocalized electrons.

These factors can be compared directly for two period 3 elements: sodium (group 1, electron configuration 1s22s22p63s1) and magnesium (group 2, electron configuration 1s22s22p63s2). Sodium contributes one delocalized electron per atom and forms the singly charged Na+ ion, while magnesium contributes two delocalized electrons per atom and forms the doubly charged Mg2+ ion, which also has a slightly smaller ionic radius than Na+. All three factors therefore predict that magnesium should have stronger metallic bonding than sodium.

The left-hand lattice is labelled sodium: a grid of ions each marked Na+, with a moderate scattering of delocalized electron dots between them. The right-hand lattice is labelled magnesium: a grid of ions each marked Mg2+, drawn slightly smaller than the Na+ ions and packed slightly closer together, with roughly twice as many delocalized electron dots filling the spaces between them, representing the two electrons per atom that magnesium contributes to the electron sea compared to sodium’s one.
Worked example: using melting point data to compare bond strength

Sodium melts at 98 °C and magnesium melts at 650 °C. Explain this difference in terms of the metallic model.
Melting point is a direct experimental measure of how much thermal energy is needed to overcome the electrostatic attraction holding a metallic lattice together, so a much higher melting point indicates much stronger metallic bonding. Magnesium’s far higher melting point (650 °C, compared with sodium’s 98 °C) is consistent with the prediction above: Mg2+ has double the ionic charge of Na+, contributes twice as many delocalized electrons per atom, and has a smaller ionic radius, so the electrostatic attraction between its cations and the electron sea is considerably stronger, and considerably more energy is required to separate the lattice.

Trends in Metallic Bond Strength Across the Periodic Table

Trend down a groupMetallic bond strength generally decreases down a group. Although the cation’s charge stays the same down a group, its ionic radius increases (extra occupied energy levels), which increases the distance between the delocalized electrons and the nucleus and weakens the electrostatic attraction between them.
Worked example: melting points down group 1

Sodium melts at 98 °C, potassium at 63 °C, and rubidium at 39 °C. Explain this trend.
Moving down group 1 from Na to K to Rb, each successive element has one more occupied main energy level, so its cation has a larger ionic radius, even though every group 1 cation carries the same +1 charge and contributes only one delocalized electron per atom. The larger cation radius places the outer delocalized electrons further, on average, from the positively charged nucleus, weakening the electrostatic attraction and giving progressively weaker metallic bonding — consistent with the steadily falling melting points (98 → 63 → 39 °C) observed down the group.

Trend across a periodMetallic bond strength generally increases from left to right across a period of s- and p-block metals. Moving across a period, the cation charge increases (e.g. +1 for group 1, +2 for group 2, +3 for group 13) and the number of delocalized electrons contributed per atom increases, while ionic radius decreases slightly, so the electrostatic attraction between the cations and the electron sea becomes stronger.

Because metals react by losing their valence electrons, the same factors that determine metallic bond strength can be used to predict relative reactivity: a metal with weaker metallic bonding (i.e. one that holds on to its delocalized/valence electrons less tightly) tends to react more readily.

Melting point trend vs reactivity trendMelting point and metallic bond strength rise and fall together (a stronger metallic bond needs more energy, hence a higher temperature, to break apart the lattice into a liquid). Reactivity moves in the opposite direction to bond strength: a metal whose valence electrons are held only loosely (weak metallic bonding, e.g. low melting point, large cation, low charge) loses those electrons more easily in a chemical reaction, and so is more reactive, not less.

Metal Extraction and Environmental Considerations

Extracting metals from their ores is a major industrial activity worldwide, but it carries significant environmental costs, including the destruction of landscapes through strip mining, the build-up of waste rock and processing residue (“tailings”) in spoil tips, and the release of toxic substances that can leach out of waste material and pollute soil and water supplies. Because metals are a valuable but finite resource, maximizing the reuse and recycling of metal objects, and avoiding unnecessary disposal of metal waste, are important ways of reducing these environmental impacts.
