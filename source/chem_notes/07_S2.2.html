Guiding Question

What determines the covalent nature and properties of a substance?

The word “covalence” comes from the idea that atoms can combine by sharing pairs of valence electrons, rather than transferring them outright as in ionic bonding. This idea dates back to the early 20th century and the American chemist Gilbert Lewis, who first proposed the covalent bond and devised a notation — the Lewis formula — for showing how electrons are arranged so that each atom gains a full valence shell. This chapter builds a complete toolkit for predicting the structure of covalent substances: how to draw Lewis formulas, how to use them to predict three-dimensional molecular shapes (the VSEPR model), how uneven sharing of electrons creates polar bonds and polar molecules, why some covalent substances form giant network lattices instead of small molecules, how molecules attract each other through intermolecular forces, and how those same forces are exploited in chromatography to separate mixtures.

Roadmap of this sub-topicCovalent bonding & the octet rule → Lewis formulas → single/double/triple bonds → coordination bonds → VSEPR shapes → bond polarity → molecular polarity → covalent network structures → intermolecular forces → chromatography. Each stage builds on the one before it — you cannot predict a molecule’s shape without first drawing its Lewis formula, and you cannot predict its polarity without first knowing its shape.

Covalent Bonding and the Octet Rule

How a covalent bond forms

When two non-metal atoms react, each is trying to gain a full valence shell like the nearest noble gas. Instead of transferring electrons completely, non-metals achieve this by sharing a pair of electrons. The shared pair sits in the region of space between the two nuclei, and because it is a region of negative charge, it is attracted to both of the positively charged nuclei at once. This mutual, simultaneous attraction of two nuclei to one shared electron pair is what holds the atoms together.

Covalent bond (definition)A covalent bond is the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the positively charged nuclei of the two bonded atoms. A group of atoms held together entirely by covalent bonds is called a molecule.
Diagram: energy profile of covalent bond formationA graph with “potential energy” on the vertical axis and “distance of separation between two hydrogen nuclei” on the horizontal axis, plotted for two approaching hydrogen atoms. Far apart (right-hand side), the energy is defined as zero for two isolated atoms. As the atoms approach, energy drops because the nucleus of each atom starts to attract the electron of the other — the system becomes more stable. The curve reaches a minimum (lowest energy, most stable point) at a separation of 75 pm, releasing 435 kJ mol−1 of energy — this defines the H–H covalent bond length and bond enthalpy. If the atoms are pushed closer together than this minimum, the energy rises steeply again because the two positively charged nuclei begin to repel each other directly. The covalent bond therefore represents a balance point: the distance at which the attractive pull of nuclei on the shared electrons is exactly balanced by internuclear repulsion.

For example, two separate hydrogen atoms, each with one electron, come together and share their electrons to form the diatomic molecule H2. Each hydrogen atom now has a share of two electrons, matching the stable electron arrangement of helium.

The octet rule

Octet rule (definition)The octet rule refers to the tendency of atoms to gain a valence shell containing a total of eight electrons (through a combination of their own electrons and shared electrons), matching the stable electron arrangement of a noble gas.

Two chlorine atoms illustrate the octet rule nicely. Each Cl atom starts with seven valence electrons. By sharing one pair between them, each chlorine ends up with a share of eight electrons overall — one bonding pair plus three of its own unshared pairs. The unshared pairs are called non-bonding pairs or lone pairs; they take no part in the bond itself but, as we will see later in this chapter, they strongly influence the shape of more complex molecules.

Hydrogen and chlorine can also react with each other to form hydrogen chloride, HCl: by sharing one electron pair, hydrogen completes the two-electron arrangement of helium and chlorine completes its octet. Note that hydrogen is a built-in exception to the octet rule — because its valence shell is the first shell, it can only ever hold a maximum of two electrons, not eight.

The octet rule also explains why the noble gases (group 18) are so unreactive. With the exception of helium, every noble gas atom already possesses a complete octet of its own electrons, so there is no tendency to share electrons with another atom and form covalent bonds.

Why covalent bonds form between similar atoms but ionic bonds do notTwo atoms of the same or similar element (e.g. two chlorine atoms) attract valence electrons with very similar strength, so neither can pull an electron completely away from the other — sharing (a covalent bond) is the only option. Ionic bonding, by contrast, requires a large difference in electron-attracting power between a metal and a non-metal, so that electrons can be transferred completely rather than shared.
Nature of science — limitations of the octet ruleScientific rules are descriptive generalizations that work most of the time but are not universal laws, so it is just as important to know their exceptions as to know the rule itself. The octet rule has several recognized exceptions: hydrogen (limited to 2 electrons), small central atoms such as beryllium and boron that form an incomplete octet, some transition elements, free radicals (species with an unpaired, odd number of electrons), and some larger central atoms (period 3 and below) that can accommodate an expanded octet of more than eight electrons because they have accessible d-orbitals.

Drawing Lewis Formulas

A Lewis formula (also called an electron dot formula or Lewis structure) shows every valence electron — both bonding and non-bonding pairs — in a covalently bonded species. Electron pairs can be shown as two dots, two crosses, a mixture of dots and crosses (useful for distinguishing which atom each electron came from), or as a single dash representing a bonding pair.

Step 1: total valence electrons = Σ (number of valence electrons per element × number of atoms of that element)
Step 2: draw the skeletal structure, joining the central atom to each outer atom with one shared pair
Step 3: complete the octet on the outer atoms first, then the central atom, using leftover pairs (forming double/triple bonds if there are not enough electrons for single bonds alone)
Step 4: check that the total number of electrons drawn equals the number calculated in Step 1

Plain-language explanation: First you work out exactly how many valence electrons the whole species has to share out. Then you sketch which atoms are bonded to which (usually the atom that appears once, or the least electronegative non-hydrogen atom, sits in the centre). Then you distribute the remaining electrons as lone pairs, starting on the outer atoms, until every atom (except recognized exceptions) has eight electrons. Finally, you always double check your total electron count matches the number you calculated at the very start — this catches almost every mistake.

Worked exampleDraw the Lewis formula of CCl4.
Step 1: valence electrons = (4 × 1) for carbon + (7 × 4) for the four chlorines = 4 + 28 = 32.
Step 2: carbon sits at the centre, singly bonded to each of the four chlorine atoms (4 bonding pairs = 8 electrons used so far).
Step 3: each chlorine already has one bonding pair; add three lone pairs to each chlorine to complete its octet (4 chlorines × 3 lone pairs × 2 electrons = 24 electrons). Carbon now has four bonding pairs around it (8 electrons) and no lone pairs of its own.
Step 4: total electrons shown = 8 (bonding) + 24 (lone pairs on Cl) = 32, which matches Step 1. The structure is confirmed correct.

Lewis formulas of ions

When drawing the Lewis formula of an ion, first calculate the valence electrons exactly as for a molecule, then adjust the total: add one electron for every unit of negative charge, or subtract one electron for every unit of positive charge. The finished structure is then enclosed in square brackets with the overall charge written outside, at the top right.

Worked exampleDraw the Lewis formula of the hydroxide ion, OH−.
Valence electrons = 6 (from O) + 1 (from H) + 1 (for the single negative charge) = 8.
Skeleton: O–H, a single bonding pair (2 electrons) between them.
Remaining electrons = 8 − 2 = 6, drawn as three lone pairs on the oxygen atom.
The whole structure [O–H, with three lone pairs on O] is placed inside square brackets with a − charge shown outside — giving oxygen a complete octet (1 bonding pair + 3 lone pairs = 8 electrons) while hydrogen keeps its 2-electron duet.

The sulfate ion, SO42−, can be drawn in the same way. Valence electrons = 6 (S) + (6 × 4) (four O atoms) + 2 (for the 2− charge) = 32. A simple, fully octet-satisfying structure places sulfur at the centre with four single bonds to four separate oxygen atoms; each oxygen then carries three lone pairs to complete its own octet, using up all 32 electrons (4 bonding pairs = 8 electrons, plus 4 × 3 lone pairs × 2 electrons = 24 electrons, giving 32 in total). This gives sulfur four electron domains around it — consistent with the real tetrahedral shape of the sulfate ion.

The octet rule is not always followed: the incomplete octet

Small central atoms with very few valence electrons of their own — especially beryllium and boron — are simply too small, and have too few electrons, to form four bonds and complete a full octet. They instead form stable molecules with fewer than eight electrons around the central atom, called an incomplete octet.

Lewis formulas of BeCl2 and BF3BeCl2: a central beryllium atom joined by two single bond lines to two chlorine atoms (one on each side, in a straight line), with no lone pairs and no additional dots on beryllium at all — beryllium ends up with only 4 electrons around it (2 bonding pairs), not 8. Each chlorine atom carries three lone pairs to complete its own octet. BF3: a central boron atom joined by three single bond lines to three fluorine atoms arranged symmetrically (like a three-pointed star), with no lone pairs on boron — boron ends up with only 6 electrons around it (3 bonding pairs). Each fluorine atom carries three lone pairs to complete its own octet.

Valence electron counts confirm this: BeCl2 has 2 + (7 × 2) = 16 valence electrons in total (all accounted for by 2 bonding pairs and 3 lone pairs on each Cl, with none left over for Be), and BF3 has 3 + (7 × 3) = 24 valence electrons (3 bonding pairs and 3 lone pairs on each F, again none left over for B). Common examples of elements that form an incomplete octet are hydrogen, beryllium, boron, and aluminium.

Single, Double and Triple Covalent Bonds

Sometimes there are not enough electrons available to complete an octet on every atom using single bonds alone. In these cases, atoms share more than one electron pair, forming a multiple bond. A double bond consists of two shared electron pairs (four electrons total); a triple bond consists of three shared electron pairs (six electrons total).

Worked exampleDraw the Lewis formula of O2.
Step 1: valence electrons = 6 × 2 = 12.
Step 2: skeleton O–O, one bonding pair (2 electrons) used so far.
Step 3: distributing the remaining 10 electrons as three lone pairs on each oxygen (12 electrons) would leave only a single bond, giving each oxygen just 6 electrons — short of an octet. Instead, one lone pair from each oxygen is converted into a second shared pair, forming a double bond. Each oxygen now has one double bond (4 electrons, 2 of which “belong” to each O) plus two lone pairs (4 electrons) = 8 electrons total.
Step 4: total electrons = 4 (double bond) + 4 (lone pairs on left O) + 4 (lone pairs on right O) = 12, matching Step 1.

Nitrogen, N2, needs a triple bond to satisfy the octet rule on both atoms: each nitrogen ends up with one lone pair plus a share in three bonding pairs (2 + 6 = 8 electrons). This triple bond is very difficult to break, which is why atmospheric nitrogen gas, despite making up about 78% of the air we breathe, is so chemically unreactive that living organisms almost never use it directly as a nitrogen source.

Lewis formulas of CO2 and HCNCarbon dioxide: a central carbon atom joined by two double-bond lines (each drawn as two parallel lines), one to an oxygen on the left and one to an oxygen on the right; each oxygen carries two lone pairs, and carbon carries none. Hydrogen cyanide, HCN: a hydrogen atom singly bonded to a central carbon atom, which is in turn triple-bonded (three parallel lines) to a nitrogen atom on the far right; the nitrogen carries one lone pair, and carbon carries none.

Bond length and bond strength

Every covalent bond can be characterized by its bond length (the distance between the two bonded nuclei) and its bond strength (usually expressed as bond enthalpy, the energy needed to break the bond). Multiple bonds involve a greater number of shared electrons, producing a stronger electrostatic pull between the bonded nuclei. This pulls the atoms closer together and makes the bond harder to break.

Key relationshipFor bonds between the same pair of elements, as the number of shared electron pairs increases: bond length decreases and bond strength (bond enthalpy) increases. Triple bonds are shorter and stronger than double bonds, which are shorter and stronger than single bonds — but note a double bond is not exactly twice as strong as a single bond, nor is a triple bond exactly three times as strong.

The same pattern appears when comparing the two different types of carbon–oxygen bond within a single molecule of ethanoic acid, CH3COOH: the C–O single bond has a length of 143 pm and an enthalpy of 358 kJ mol−1, while the C=O double bond is shorter, at 122 pm, and stronger, at 804 kJ mol−1.

Down a group, atomic radius increases, so bonds between larger atoms of the same group (for example the halogens F–F, Cl–Cl, Br–Br, I–I) generally become longer and weaker, because the shared electron pair sits further away from the pull of both nuclei. Fluorine, F2, is a notable outlier to this trend because of unusually strong repulsion between its non-bonding pairs, which are forced very close together by its small atomic size.

Coordination (Dative) Bonds

Coordination bond (definition)A coordination bond (also called a dative covalent bond) is a covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair originate from the same atom, rather than one electron from each bonded atom. The atom donating the pair is often called the donor, and the atom accepting it the acceptor.

In diagrams, a coordination bond is sometimes drawn as an arrow along the bond axis (rather than a plain line) to show the direction the electron pair came from — pointing from the donor atom towards the acceptor atom. Crucially, once a coordination bond has formed, it is completely indistinguishable from any other covalent bond: the electrons are shared just as in a normal bond, and the bond has the same length and strength as any other bond of that type between those two elements. The distinction only matters when explaining how the bond initially formed.

Coordination bonding in H3O+ and NH4+In the hydronium ion H3O+: a water molecule (O bonded to two H atoms, with two lone pairs) donates one of its lone pairs to an incoming H+ ion (a bare proton with no electrons of its own). The new O–H bond is drawn with an arrow pointing from the oxygen’s lone pair towards the new hydrogen, showing both bonding electrons came from oxygen. The resulting ion has a central oxygen bonded to three hydrogens with one remaining lone pair, and an overall 1+ charge. In the ammonium ion NH4+: ammonia (N bonded to three H atoms, with one lone pair) donates its lone pair to an incoming H+ ion, forming a fourth N–H bond shown with an arrow from nitrogen towards the new hydrogen. The resulting ion has nitrogen bonded to four hydrogens with no lone pairs left, and an overall 1+ charge. In both cases, once formed, all the N–H (or O–H) bonds in the ion are completely identical — there is no way to tell, from the finished molecule, which bond was originally coordinate.
Coordination bonding in carbon monoxide, COCarbon monoxide has a triple bond between carbon and oxygen. Two of the three shared pairs are formed in the ordinary way, one electron contributed by each atom. The third shared pair, however, is a coordination bond: both electrons are donated by the oxygen atom (which starts with a lone pair to spare), drawn with an arrow pointing from oxygen to carbon. This coordination bond is what allows carbon — which only has 4 valence electrons of its own — to still complete a full octet (a lone pair plus three shared pairs = 8 electrons) in this molecule. Oxygen also ends with one lone pair plus three shared pairs = 8 electrons. All three C–O bonds in the finished molecule are identical to one another.

Coordination bonds also explain how two separate, stable molecules can combine into a larger one. Ammonia, NH3, can react with boron trifluoride, BF3, because ammonia’s nitrogen lone pair can be donated directly into the empty space around boron (which, as we saw above, has only an incomplete octet of 6 electrons in BF3). The nitrogen lone pair forms a new coordination bond to boron, giving both atoms a complete octet in the product, H3N→BF3. In a similar way, gaseous aluminium chloride, which is covalent and sublimes at 180 °C, exists as pairs of AlCl3 molecules joined together by two coordination bonds to form the larger molecule Al2Cl6: two chlorine atoms each donate one of their lone pairs to the aluminium atom of the neighbouring molecule, bridging the two halves and giving every atom (both aluminium and all six chlorine atoms) a complete octet.

The VSEPR Model: Predicting Molecular Shape

Once we have a molecule’s Lewis formula, we can predict its three-dimensional shape — often a crucial factor in a substance’s reactivity. Predictions of molecular shape are based on the Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) model, built on the simple idea that because all electron pairs in the valence shell carry the same negative charge, they repel each other and so arrange themselves as far apart in space as possible.

Electron domainThe term “electron pair” in VSEPR is really a simplification. A double or triple bond behaves as a single repelling unit, because all the bonding pairs within it point in the same direction. A more accurate term is electron domain: any region of electron density around a central atom, whether it is a single bond, a double bond, a triple bond, or a non-bonding (lone) pair. It is the total number of electron domains — not the total number of electron pairs — that determines the geometry.
Worked exampleHow many electron domains surround the central atom in (a) NH3, (b) CO2, (c) O3 (ozone)?
(a) NH3 has 3 N–H single bonds plus 1 lone pair on nitrogen = 4 electron domains total (3 bonding, 1 non-bonding).
(b) CO2 has 2 C=O double bonds and no lone pairs on carbon; because each double bond counts as a single domain, this is 2 electron domains total (2 bonding, 0 non-bonding).
(c) Ozone has a central oxygen with one O–O single bond, one O=O double bond, and one lone pair = 3 electron domains total (2 bonding, 1 non-bonding).
The VSEPR model, summarized• Electron domains (single bonds, double bonds, triple bonds, or lone pairs) repel each other and spread out as far apart as geometrically possible.
• The total number of electron domains around the central atom fixes the electron domain geometry.
• The molecular geometry (the shape actually described when naming the molecule) depends only on the positions of the bonded atoms — a lone pair is invisible in the final named shape, even though it still occupies space and pushes the other domains around.
• Lone pairs and multiple bonds cause slightly more repulsion than an ordinary single bonding pair, because their electron density is either not shared between two nuclei (lone pairs) or contains extra electron pairs (multiple bonds). The order of repulsive strength is: non-bonding pair > multiple bond > single bond.

Because of this repulsion order, whenever a lone pair or a multiple bond is present on the central atom, the bond angles are compressed slightly below the “ideal” angle predicted from electron domain geometry alone. You are not expected to memorize the exact size of every such distortion, but you must recognize when an angle will be slightly larger or smaller than the basic VSEPR prediction.

VSEPR Shapes: Two and Three Electron Domains

Two electron domains — linear

With only two electron domains, the repulsion is minimized by placing them at exactly 180° to each other, giving a straight-line, linear arrangement.

Linear molecules: BeCl2, CO2, C2H2BeCl2: two chlorine atoms positioned on exactly opposite sides of a central beryllium atom in a single straight line, Cl–Be–Cl, bond angle 180°. CO2: two oxygen atoms double-bonded on exactly opposite sides of a central carbon atom in a single straight line, O=C=O, bond angle 180°. Ethyne, C2H2: all four atoms lie on one straight line, H–C≡C–H (triple bond between the two carbons), with both H–C–C bond angles equal to 180°.

Three electron domains — trigonal planar (all bonding) or bent (with a lone pair)

With three electron domains, repulsion is minimized by spreading them out at approximately 120° to each other, all in the same flat plane — a trigonal planar electron domain geometry. If all three domains are bonding pairs, the molecular shape is also trigonal planar.

Trigonal planar molecules: BH3, AlCl3, methanal HCOHBH3: three hydrogen atoms spread symmetrically around a central boron atom, all lying flat in one plane, each H–B–H angle exactly 120°. AlCl3: three chlorine atoms spread symmetrically around a central aluminium atom in one plane, each Cl–Al–Cl angle exactly 120°. Methanal, HCOH: a central carbon double-bonded to an oxygen and singly bonded to two hydrogens, all four atoms lying in one plane; because the C=O double bond causes slightly more repulsion than the two C–H single bonds, the O=C–H angle opens up to slightly more than 120° while the H–C–H angle closes to slightly less than 120° — the VSEPR model correctly predicts the overall trigonal planar shape but needs refining to explain these small distortions.

If one of the three electron domains on the central atom is a lone pair instead of a bonding pair, the electron domain geometry remains trigonal planar, but the molecular geometry (defined only by the positions of atoms) becomes bent or V-shaped, and the bond angle closes up slightly below 120° because of the extra repulsion from the lone pair.

Ozone, O3 — a bent molecule from three electron domainsA central oxygen atom has three electron domains around it: one O–O single bond, one O=O double bond, and one lone pair, arranged in a rough triangle (trigonal planar electron domain geometry). But because one of those three positions is a lone pair (not an atom), the visible shape traced out by the three atoms is bent, or V-shaped, not a straight triangle. The O–O–O bond angle is compressed to approximately 117°, below the 120° expected for a perfect trigonal planar arrangement, because both the lone pair and the double bond push extra hard on the remaining single bond.

VSEPR Shapes: Four Electron Domains

With four electron domains, repulsion is minimized by pointing them towards the four corners of a regular tetrahedron, each domain separated from its neighbours by approximately 109.5°. This is the tetrahedral electron domain geometry. If all four domains are bonding pairs, the molecular geometry is also tetrahedral.

If one or more of the four domains is a lone pair, we again focus only on the positions of the bonded atoms to name the molecular shape, while remembering that the lone pair(s) are still there, pushing the bonded atoms closer together than the ideal tetrahedral angle.

CH4, NH3 and H2O comparedMethane, CH4: a central carbon bonded to four hydrogen atoms pointing towards the four corners of a tetrahedron — if you picture the carbon at the centre of a pyramid-like shape with a triangular base, one hydrogen points straight up while the other three point outward and downward, each separated by 109.5°. No lone pairs distort this shape, so it is a perfectly symmetrical tetrahedron. Ammonia, NH3: the same tetrahedral arrangement of four electron domains around nitrogen, but one corner of the tetrahedron is occupied by a lone pair instead of a hydrogen atom. Looking only at the three N–H bonds, the molecule looks like a short, three-sided pyramid with nitrogen at the apex and the three hydrogens forming a triangular base — this shape is called trigonal pyramidal. The single lone pair pushes down on the three N–H bonds, compressing the H–N–H angle from 109.5° to about 107°. Water, H2O: again four electron domains around oxygen arranged tetrahedrally, but now two corners are occupied by lone pairs and only two by hydrogen atoms. Looking only at the two O–H bonds, the molecule is bent, or V-shaped, like a wide letter V. The two lone pairs together push even harder on the remaining two bonds than the single lone pair did in ammonia, compressing the H–O–H angle further, down to about 104.5°.

Summary method for determining molecular shape

Step-by-step procedure1. Draw the full Lewis formula of the species.
2. Count the total number of electron domains on the central atom (bonding pairs, treating each multiple bond as one domain, plus lone pairs).
3. Use the domain count to find the electron domain geometry: 2 domains → linear; 3 domains → trigonal planar; 4 domains → tetrahedral.
4. Use the number of bonded domains (ignoring lone pairs) to name the molecular geometry.
5. Adjust the expected bond angle slightly downward wherever a lone pair or a multiple bond is present on the central atom, since these cause more repulsion than an ordinary single bonding pair.
Nature of science — how useful is the VSEPR model?VSEPR reliably predicts the correct general shape and approximate bond angle of almost any simple molecule from nothing more than a Lewis formula, which is remarkable given how simple its underlying assumption is (that electron domains just repel each other like similarly charged balloons). Its main limitation is that it only explains bond-angle distortions qualitatively (larger or smaller than the ideal angle) rather than calculating their exact size — for that, more advanced quantum mechanical models of bonding are required.

Bond Polarity and Electronegativity

Not every covalent bond shares its electrons equally. Whenever the two bonded atoms have different electronegativities, the shared pair spends more time nearer the more electronegative atom.

Electronegativity (definition)Electronegativity is a measure of the ability of an atom to attract a shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond. It is expressed on the dimensionless Pauling scale, running from about 0.7 (for francium, the least electronegative element with a normally assigned value) up to 4.0 (for fluorine, the most electronegative element). Electronegativity generally increases across a period (as nuclear charge increases) and decreases down a group (as atomic radius increases and outer electrons are shielded from the nucleus by more inner shells), so it increases towards the top right of the periodic table — towards fluorine.

When two bonded atoms differ in electronegativity, the bond has a bond dipole: two partially separated opposite charges. The more electronegative atom becomes partially negative, δ−, having gained more “possession” of the shared electrons, while the less electronegative atom becomes partially positive, δ+. This can be shown either by writing δ+ and δ− directly over the relevant atoms, or by drawing a small arrow along the bond, pointing towards the more electronegative atom, to represent the pull on the electrons as a vector.

The polar H–Cl bondA single H–Cl molecule is drawn with the label δ+ over the hydrogen atom and δ− over the chlorine atom, plus a small arrow drawn along the bond pointing from H towards Cl, showing that the shared electron density is skewed towards the chlorine end of the molecule (chlorine being more electronegative than hydrogen). This uneven distribution of electron density is what makes the H–Cl bond polar.
ΔEN = |EN of more electronegative atom − EN of less electronegative atom|

Plain-language explanation: ΔEN, the electronegativity difference, is simply the (always positive) gap between the Pauling electronegativity values of the two bonded atoms. The bigger this gap, the more unevenly the electrons are shared, and the more polar the bond. As a rough, widely used guideline for classifying a bond (there is no single official cut-off, since electronegativity difference is really a continuous spectrum): ΔEN below about 0.5 is considered essentially non-polar covalent (also called “pure” covalent); ΔEN of roughly 0.5–1.8 is considered polar covalent; and ΔEN above about 1.8 is considered predominantly ionic.

Worked exampleClassify the bonds in H–F (EN of H = 2.2, EN of F = 4.0), H–Cl (EN of H = 2.2, EN of Cl = 3.2), and Na–Cl (EN of Na = 0.9, EN of Cl = 3.2).
H–F: ΔEN = |4.0 − 2.2| = 1.8, which sits right at the polar covalent/ionic boundary, so H–F is classified as a strongly polar covalent bond.
H–Cl: ΔEN = |3.2 − 2.2| = 1.0, comfortably within the polar covalent range, so H–Cl is a polar covalent bond — less polar than H–F.
Na–Cl: ΔEN = |3.2 − 0.9| = 2.3, above 1.8, so the Na–Cl bond is classified as ionic rather than covalent.

Comparing the hydrogen halides shows how bond polarity decreases as the halogen’s electronegativity falls (moving down group 17): H–F (ΔEN = 1.8) is the most polar, followed by H–Cl (ΔEN = 1.0), H–Br (ΔEN = 0.8), and H–I (ΔEN = 0.5), which is the least polar of the four.

The only bonds that are truly non-polar (ΔEN exactly zero) are bonds between two atoms of the same element, such as those in F2, H2, O2, and Cl2 — sometimes called pure covalent bonds. Every other covalent bond has at least some degree of polarity, even if it is very slight. The C–H bond that is universal throughout organic chemistry is a good example: carbon (EN = 2.6) is only very slightly more electronegative than hydrogen (EN = 2.2), so the bond is usually treated as essentially non-polar in practice, even though it is not perfectly so.

The bonding continuumPolar covalent bonds sit on a continuum between pure covalent bonding (equal sharing, ΔEN = 0) and pure ionic bonding (complete electron transfer, very large ΔEN). The more polar a covalent bond becomes, the more it starts to take on some ionic character — which is why hydrogen chloride, HCl, a covalent molecule made of discrete molecules in the gas phase, ionizes completely when dissolved in water to give H+ and Cl− ions and conducts electricity in that aqueous state. There is no sharp dividing line between “covalent” and “ionic” substances; the boundary is genuinely fuzzy, so chemists often describe a bond as “predominantly” covalent or ionic rather than absolutely one or the other.

Molecular Polarity

Whether a bond is polar tells us about the charge separation across just that one bond. Whether the whole molecule is polar depends on two things together: (1) which polar bonds it contains, and (2) how those bonds are arranged in three-dimensional space (the molecular geometry).

The cancellation principleIf a molecule’s bond dipoles are all of equal size (i.e. they involve identical elements) and are arranged symmetrically around the central atom, they point outward in perfectly opposing directions and their effects cancel out exactly — like a balanced tug-of-war where equally strong teams are pulling in exactly opposite directions. In this case, the molecule as a whole is non-polar, even though it clearly contains individually polar bonds. If the bond dipoles are not equal, or not arranged symmetrically, they do not fully cancel, leaving a net dipole across the molecule, and the molecule is polar.
Non-polar molecules despite polar bonds: CO2, BF3, CCl4CO2: two identical, equally polar C=O bond dipoles point outward from carbon in exactly opposite directions (the molecule is linear, 180° apart), so the two dipole arrows cancel perfectly — net molecular dipole is zero. BF3: three identical, equally polar B–F bond dipoles point outward from boron symmetrically at 120° to each other in a flat triangle; by vector addition, three equal arrows spaced evenly around a point sum to zero — net molecular dipole is zero. CCl4: four identical, equally polar C–Cl bond dipoles point outward from carbon symmetrically towards the four corners of a tetrahedron; again, by symmetry, the four vectors cancel exactly — net molecular dipole is zero.
Polar molecules: CH3Cl, NH3, H2OCH3Cl (chloromethane): the single, strongly polar C–Cl bond dipole is not balanced by an equal and opposite dipole anywhere else on the tetrahedral carbon (the other three bonds are to hydrogen, which forms a much weaker C–H dipole) — the dipoles do not cancel, leaving a clear net dipole pointing from carbon towards the chlorine end of the molecule. NH3: the three N–H bond dipoles all point somewhat towards nitrogen, but because the molecule is trigonal pyramidal (not perfectly symmetrical like a flat triangle, due to the lone pair occupying the fourth tetrahedral position), the three dipoles do not cancel — they add up to leave a net dipole pointing from the base of hydrogens up towards the nitrogen lone pair. H2O: the two O–H bond dipoles both point somewhat towards oxygen, but because the molecule is bent (not linear), the two dipole vectors do not point in exactly opposite directions and so cannot fully cancel — they add up to leave a substantial net dipole pointing from between the two hydrogens towards the oxygen.

Bond dipoles behave mathematically like vectors, so the overall molecular polarity is the vector sum (resultant) of every individual bond dipole. This is a useful mental shortcut: a molecule can contain several very polar bonds and still be completely non-polar overall, purely because of a highly symmetric shape.

Worked comparison: cis and trans 1,2-dichloroetheneThe two C–Cl bonds in this molecule are identical in strength. In the trans isomer, the two chlorine atoms are positioned on opposite sides of the C=C double bond (diagonally across from each other), so their bond dipoles point in nearly opposite directions and largely cancel — the trans isomer is non-polar (or only very weakly polar). In the cis isomer, both chlorine atoms are on the same side of the double bond, so their bond dipoles point in a similar direction and reinforce each other rather than cancelling — the cis isomer has a clear net dipole moment and is polar. This shows that molecular geometry, not just the presence of polar bonds, determines overall polarity.

Covalent Network Structures: Carbon and Silicon

Most covalent substances exist as small, discrete molecules with a fixed, finite number of atoms. A small number of elements, however, form a covalent network structure (also called a giant molecular or macromolecular structure): a crystalline lattice in which every atom is linked to its neighbours by covalent bonds that repeat indefinitely in a regular pattern, so the whole crystal is effectively one single, enormous molecule with no fixed size.

Allotropes (definition)Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element, existing in the same physical state, that consequently have different chemical and physical properties. Carbon has several well-known allotropes: diamond, graphite, graphene, and the fullerenes.

Silicon and silicon dioxide

Silicon, directly below carbon in group 14, also has four valence electrons and forms an analogous giant covalent lattice: each silicon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement, just like diamond.

Silicon dioxide, SiO2 (also known as silica or quartz), forms a related giant covalent structure, but here silicon and oxygen alternate: each silicon atom is bonded to four oxygen atoms, and each oxygen atom bridges two silicon atoms.

The structure of quartz, SiO2A three-dimensional lattice in which each silicon atom sits at the centre of a tetrahedron of four oxygen atoms (Si–O bond angles of 109.5° around each silicon), and each oxygen atom in turn bridges to a second silicon atom, linking the tetrahedra together into one continuous network extending in all directions. The chemical formula SiO2 is only an empirical (simplest ratio) formula — it describes the 1:2 ratio of silicon to oxygen atoms, not a finite, countable molecule, since the true structure is one enormous lattice.

Because all four of silicon’s valence electrons are locked into strong, direction-specific covalent bonds throughout the lattice, silicon dioxide is strong, insoluble in water, has a very high melting point, and does not conduct electricity — the properties we associate with everyday forms of silica such as sand and glass.

Why carbon forms C–C chains but silicon bonds mainly to oxygenCarbon’s much smaller atomic radius allows a stronger electrostatic attraction between its nucleus and a shared electron pair, giving the C–C bond a much higher bond enthalpy (approximately 346 kJ mol−1) than the corresponding Si–Si bond (approximately 226 kJ mol−1). This ability of carbon to form strong, stable chains and rings with itself is called catenation, and underlies the entire diversity of organic chemistry. Silicon, in contrast, forms an especially strong bond with oxygen (Si–O bond enthalpy approximately 466 kJ mol−1), so silicon is found overwhelmingly in silicate minerals rather than in long silicon-only chains.

Pure silicon is a poor conductor of electricity at low temperature, but conductivity increases sharply as temperature rises, since thermal energy frees some electrons from the covalent lattice (leaving behind a positive “hole” that other electrons can move into). This semiconductor behaviour, further enhanced by deliberately adding trace impurities such as arsenic or boron (a process called doping), is the basis of the entire modern electronics industry — the origin of the name “Silicon Valley.”

Intermolecular Forces

Covalent bonds hold the atoms within a single molecule together, but molecules also attract each other, through weaker forces of attraction called intermolecular forces (note the prefix inter-, meaning “between,” as opposed to intra-, meaning “within”). The type and strength of intermolecular force present depends on the size and polarity of the molecules involved, and largely determines a covalent substance’s physical properties.

Key relative strength orderingFor molecules of comparable molar mass, intermolecular forces generally increase in strength in this order: London (dispersion) forces < dipole–dipole forces < hydrogen bonding. All intermolecular forces, however, are considerably weaker (roughly 15–20 times weaker) than covalent or ionic bonds.

London (dispersion) forces

Non-polar molecules, such as Cl2, have no permanent separation of charge, because their shared electrons are pulled equally by both atoms. However, electrons behave like a constantly shifting cloud of negative charge, and at any given instant the electron density might happen to be slightly greater over one end of the molecule than the other. This creates a very brief, weak, instantaneous (temporary) dipole.

London dispersion forces between two Cl2 moleculesStep 1: two Cl2 molecules drawn side by side, electron clouds evenly spread, no dipole in either. Step 2: at some instant, the electron cloud in the left-hand molecule randomly shifts to be denser on its right-hand chlorine atom, giving that molecule an instantaneous dipole labelled δ− on the right chlorine and δ+ on the left chlorine. Step 3: this instantaneous dipole repels the electron cloud of the nearby right-hand molecule, pushing its electron density towards its far side and inducing a matching dipole in it (δ+ on its near chlorine, δ− on its far chlorine). Step 4: the resulting attraction between the δ− end of the left molecule and the newly induced δ+ end of the right molecule is a London dispersion force; because electron clouds are constantly reshuffling, these instantaneous dipoles and their induced partners appear and disappear continuously all over the sample, in every possible orientation.

London dispersion forces are the weakest type of intermolecular force, but they are the only intermolecular force present between non-polar molecules, and they are also present, to some extent, between every pair of molecules regardless of polarity. Their strength increases with increasing molecular size (and therefore increasing number of electrons), because more electrons make a larger, more easily distorted electron cloud, increasing both the probability and the size of temporary dipoles.

Dipole–dipole attraction

Polar molecules, such as HCl, have a permanent dipole because of the fixed electronegativity difference between their bonded atoms. Neighbouring polar molecules line up so that the δ+ end of one molecule is attracted to the δ− end of the next, a force called dipole–dipole attraction.

Dipole–dipole attraction between HCl moleculesTwo separate HCl molecules are drawn one above the other, each with δ+ labelled over its hydrogen and δ− labelled over its chlorine. The molecules orient themselves so the δ+ hydrogen end of the lower molecule sits close to the δ− chlorine end of the molecule above it, with a dotted line connecting these two ends to represent the electrostatic attraction between the permanent, fixed dipoles.

Because these dipoles are permanent (unlike the fleeting, instantaneous dipoles responsible for London forces), dipole–dipole attractions are consistently stronger, and cause polar substances to have higher melting and boiling points than non-polar substances of similar molar mass. For example, propane (C3H8, M = 44 g mol−1, non-polar, only London forces) boils at 229 K, while methoxymethane (CH3OCH3, M = 46 g mol−1, polar, London forces plus dipole–dipole) boils at the higher temperature of 249 K, despite having an almost identical molar mass.

Comparing intermolecular forces fairlyTo isolate the effect of polarity on boiling point, you must compare substances of similar molar mass. Otherwise a higher boiling point might simply be caused by stronger London dispersion forces in the heavier, more electron-rich molecule, rather than by any difference in polarity.

Dipole–induced dipole attraction

When a mixture contains both polar and non-polar molecules, the permanent dipole of the polar molecule can temporarily distort (induce a dipole in) the electron cloud of a nearby non-polar molecule, creating a weaker attraction called a dipole–induced dipole force.

Dipole–induced dipole attraction between HCl and Cl2A polar HCl molecule, labelled δ+ on H and δ− on Cl, sits close to a non-polar Cl2 molecule. The permanent δ− charge on the HCl chlorine repels the nearby electron cloud of the Cl2 molecule, pushing its electron density to the far side and inducing a temporary dipole in it (δ+ on the near chlorine, δ− on the far chlorine). This creates a weak attraction between the δ− of HCl and the newly induced δ+ of Cl2.
Van der Waals’ forces (umbrella term)“Van der Waals’ forces” is an inclusive term covering all three of the forces described so far: London dispersion forces, dipole–induced dipole forces, and dipole–dipole forces. In other words, van der Waals’ forces refer to any intermolecular attraction that does not involve full ionic charges or the formation of an actual covalent/hydrogen bond.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bond (definition)A hydrogen bond is a particularly strong type of dipole–dipole attraction that occurs when a hydrogen atom, covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (only fluorine, oxygen, or nitrogen), is also attracted to a lone pair on an electronegative atom (F, O, or N) of a neighbouring molecule. Because hydrogen is so small and has no other inner electrons to shield its nucleus, the large electronegativity difference leaves it with an unusually concentrated, exposed partial positive charge, letting it interact very strongly with a neighbouring lone pair.
Hydrogen bonding between water moleculesThree water molecules are drawn near one another. Each oxygen atom carries two lone pairs and is bonded to two hydrogen atoms, with δ− over the oxygen and δ+ over each hydrogen. A dotted line, labelled “hydrogen bond,” connects a δ+ hydrogen atom of one water molecule directly to a lone pair on the δ− oxygen atom of a neighbouring water molecule. This particular type of dipole–dipole interaction, involving an exposed hydrogen and a lone pair on F, O, or N, is drawn as a dotted (not solid) line to show it is much weaker than an ordinary covalent bond, even though it is the strongest of the intermolecular forces.

Hydrogen bonds are strong enough that substances containing them have significantly higher boiling points than their molar mass alone would predict. Comparing the group 14–17 hydrides down each group, boiling point generally rises with increasing molar mass, but NH3, HF, and H2O are all striking anomalies with far higher boiling points than the trend predicts — explained entirely by hydrogen bonding, which the heavier hydrides in each group (which lack H bonded directly to F, O, or N) cannot form.

Isomers provide another clean comparison: methoxymethane, CH3OCH3, and ethanol, CH3CH2OH, are both C2H6O with an identical molar mass of 46 g mol−1. Methoxymethane has no O–H bond (its hydrogens are all bonded to carbon) and so cannot hydrogen bond with itself, giving it a boiling point of only −23 °C. Ethanol has an O–H bond and forms hydrogen bonds between its molecules, giving it a much higher boiling point of +79 °C, despite the identical molar mass.

Why ice floats: hydrogen bonding in water and iceEach water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and two lone pairs on its oxygen, so it can, in principle, form up to four hydrogen bonds with neighbouring water molecules (two as the hydrogen-bond donor, using its own hydrogens, and two as the acceptor, using its lone pairs). In liquid water, molecules form and break fewer than four hydrogen bonds on average as they tumble past each other. In solid ice, however, every water molecule is locked into the maximum of four hydrogen bonds, holding each molecule a fixed distance from four neighbours in a rigid, open, tetrahedral lattice with empty space at its centre. This open lattice is actually less densely packed than liquid water, so ice is less dense than liquid water and floats — the opposite of almost every other substance, where the solid form is denser than the liquid.

Hydrogen bonding is not limited to being an intermolecular force between separate molecules; within very large molecules it can also occur intramolecularly, holding different parts of the same molecule together. This is essential to the structure of DNA, where hydrogen bonds link the two complementary strands of the double helix together strongly enough to be stable, yet weakly enough (compared to a covalent bond) to be broken and re-formed whenever the molecule needs to replicate.

Physical Properties from Intermolecular Forces

Knowing which intermolecular force(s) act between the molecules of a substance allows us to predict and explain a whole range of physical properties.

Melting point, boiling point, and volatility

Melting and boiling both require overcoming the forces of attraction between particles (never breaking the covalent bonds within a molecule). The stronger the intermolecular force, the more energy is needed to separate the molecules, and so the higher the melting and boiling point.

Common misconceptionWhen a covalent molecular substance melts or boils, only the weak intermolecular forces between separate molecules are broken — the strong covalent bonds holding each molecule together stay completely intact. Boiling a kettle of water does not split the O–H bonds and release separate hydrogen and oxygen atoms; it simply separates whole, intact H2O molecules from one another as a gas.
Worked exampleRank CH3CH2CH3 (propane), CH3CHO (ethanal), and CH3CH2OH (ethanol) in order of increasing boiling point.
Step 1 — check molar mass: propane M = 44, ethanal M = 44, ethanol M = 46. All three are almost identical, so London dispersion forces should be very similar in strength for all three, and the difference in boiling point must come from other intermolecular forces.
Step 2 — identify additional forces: propane is non-polar (C and H have very similar electronegativity), so only London dispersion forces act. Ethanal contains a polar C=O bond, so dipole–dipole forces act in addition to London forces. Ethanol contains an O–H bond, so hydrogen bonding acts in addition to London forces.
Step 3 — apply the strength order (London < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding): propane (London only) has the lowest boiling point, ethanal (plus dipole–dipole) is next, and ethanol (plus hydrogen bonding) has the highest boiling point. Order: CH3CH2CH3 < CH3CHO < CH3CH2OH.

Giant covalent (network) structures behave completely differently: because changing state means breaking actual covalent bonds throughout the lattice, not just weak intermolecular forces, they have extremely high melting and boiling points, often comparable to or higher than ionic compounds. Diamond, for instance, remains solid until roughly 4000 °C.

Volatility (the tendency of a substance to evaporate/vaporize) follows directly from this: the weaker a substance’s intermolecular forces, the more volatile it is. Butane, C4H10 (London forces only), is considerably more volatile than propan-1-ol, C3H7OH (which also hydrogen bonds), of similar molar mass.

Solubility

The guiding principle for solubility is “like dissolves like”: a solute dissolves most readily in a solvent whose intermolecular forces are similar in type and strength to its own.

  • Non-polar solutes (e.g. the halogens, or grease, wax, and oils) dissolve well in non-polar solvents (e.g. hexane, paraffin oil), because both can interact only through London dispersion forces.
  • Polar and ionic solutes generally dissolve well in polar solvents such as water, because both can interact through dipole–dipole attractions and/or hydrogen bonding. This is reduced, however, in larger molecules where the polar bond is only a small part of a mostly non-polar structure — small alcohols like ethanol mix completely with water, but much larger alcohols like heptanol do not, because the large non-polar hydrocarbon “tail” cannot interact well with water.
  • Polar and non-polar substances generally do not dissolve well in each other, since neither can interact effectively with the other’s intermolecular forces (this is why sugar does not dissolve in cooking oil).
  • Giant covalent (network) substances are essentially insoluble in any solvent, because dissolving them would require breaking strong covalent bonds throughout the lattice rather than just overcoming weak intermolecular forces.

Electrical conductivity

Covalent substances contain no free ions and generally cannot conduct electricity as solids or pure liquids. A polar covalent molecule can conduct electricity only if it ionizes in solution (for example, hydrochloric acid, HCl dissolved in water, conducts because it splits into H+ and Cl− ions). Among the giant covalent structures, graphite and graphene conduct well because of their mobile delocalized electrons, silicon and the fullerenes show only weak semiconductor behaviour, and diamond, having no delocalized or mobile electrons at all, does not conduct electricity.

Chromatography and Rf Values

Chromatography is a technique used to separate and identify the components of a mixture, based on the relative attraction of each component (through intermolecular forces) to two different phases: a stationary phase that stays fixed in place, and a mobile phase that moves through or over it. Components with a stronger attraction to the stationary phase move more slowly; components with a stronger attraction to the mobile phase move faster.

Paper chromatography

In paper chromatography, the stationary phase is chromatography paper, which contains roughly 10% water held in place by hydrogen bonds to the –OH groups of the paper’s cellulose fibres. A suitable solvent acts as the mobile phase, rising up the paper by capillary action and carrying the dissolved sample components with it at different speeds, according to how strongly each is attracted to the water-soaked paper versus the moving solvent.

Paper chromatography setup and resultA rectangular strip of chromatography paper hangs down inside a sealed tank, its lower edge dipping into a shallow pool of solvent at the bottom of the tank. A small spot of the sample mixture has been applied in pencil near the bottom of the paper, above the level of the solvent pool — this position is called the origin. As the experiment runs, solvent is drawn up the paper by capillary action; the point it eventually reaches, marked before the paper dries, is the solvent front. On the finished chromatogram, the original single spot has separated into several distinct, isolated spots at different heights up the paper, each corresponding to one component of the original mixture — components that travelled furthest were more strongly attracted to the moving solvent (mobile phase), while components that stayed closer to the origin were more strongly attracted to the paper (stationary phase).

Each separated spot can be described by its retardation factor, or Rf value, which is characteristic for a given compound under fixed conditions (same solvent, same paper, same temperature) and can be compared against known reference values to help identify an unknown component.

Rf = distance moved by the component ÷ distance moved by the solvent

Plain-language explanation: Both distances are measured from the same starting point, the origin (never from the bottom edge of the paper). The “distance moved by the component” is measured from the origin to the centre of that component’s spot; the “distance moved by the solvent” is measured from the origin all the way to the solvent front. Because the solvent always travels at least as far as any dissolved component, Rf is always a value between 0 (the component never moved at all) and 1 (the component travelled exactly as far as the solvent front) — it has no units.

Worked exampleOn a chromatogram, the origin to solvent front distance is 10.0 cm. Spot A has travelled 7.5 cm from the origin, and spot B has travelled 3.0 cm from the origin. Calculate the Rf value of each spot.
Rf(A) = 7.5 cm ÷ 10.0 cm = 0.75.
Rf(B) = 3.0 cm ÷ 10.0 cm = 0.30.
Spot A has the higher Rf value, showing it was more strongly attracted to the mobile solvent phase (or less strongly attracted to the stationary paper phase) than spot B.

Thin layer chromatography (TLC)

Thin layer chromatography works on the same underlying principle as paper chromatography, but the stationary phase is instead a thin, uniform layer of silica (silicon dioxide) or alumina (aluminium oxide) coated onto a rigid backing of glass, metal, or plastic. Both silica and alumina surfaces contain polar hydroxyl (–OH) groups, allowing them to form hydrogen bonds with suitable components of the sample mixture, in exactly the same way that the water held in paper does.

Why the same compound can give different Rf valuesAn Rf value is only meaningful when compared to reference data collected under the same conditions, because changing the solvent (or the stationary phase) changes the balance of intermolecular attractions for every component, and therefore changes how far each one travels relative to the solvent front. A component that is strongly attracted to one solvent (giving a high Rf) might be only weakly attracted to a different solvent (giving a much lower Rf for the very same compound).

Guiding Question Revisited

What determines the covalent nature and properties of a substance?

  • A covalent bond is the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the positively charged nuclei of the bonded atoms; sharing one, two, or three pairs of electrons forms a single, double, or triple bond respectively, and more shared pairs mean a shorter, stronger bond.
  • A coordination (dative) bond is a covalent bond in which both shared electrons originate from the same atom; once formed, it is indistinguishable from any other covalent bond.
  • The octet rule — the tendency of atoms to gain a valence shell of eight electrons — is a very useful guideline for predicting Lewis formulas, but has recognized exceptions (hydrogen, incomplete octets on small atoms like Be and B, expanded octets, radicals, and some transition metals).
  • Lewis formulas represent every valence electron in a covalently bonded species and are the essential first step before predicting a molecule’s shape.
  • The VSEPR model predicts molecular shape from the repulsion between electron domains (bonding pairs, multiple bonds, and lone pairs) around a central atom: 2 domains give a linear electron domain geometry, 3 give trigonal planar, and 4 give tetrahedral; the molecular geometry then depends only on how many of those domains are bonded atoms, with lone pairs and multiple bonds slightly compressing the ideal bond angle.
  • Bond polarity arises from a difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms, producing a bond dipole that can be shown with partial charges (δ+ / δ−) or a vector arrow; molecular polarity then depends on both the bond dipoles present and the molecular geometry, since symmetrically arranged, equal dipoles cancel to give a non-polar molecule.
  • Carbon and silicon can form covalent network (giant molecular) structures — diamond, graphite, graphene, the fullerenes, elemental silicon, and silicon dioxide — whose properties (extremely high melting points, hardness, variable conductivity) arise from their extended lattices of covalent bonds rather than from weak intermolecular forces.
  • Intermolecular forces — London dispersion, dipole–induced dipole, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding, increasing in that order of strength — determine key physical properties of covalent substances, including melting and boiling point, volatility, solubility, and electrical conductivity.
  • Chromatography separates mixture components based on their differing attractions, through intermolecular forces, to a stationary phase and a mobile phase; each component’s position can be quantified by its Rf value (distance moved by the component ÷ distance moved by the solvent), which can be compared to reference data to help identify it.
