Guiding Question

Guiding QuestionWhat role do bonding and structure have in the design of materials?

Throughout history, humans have transformed raw materials into more useful ones: smelting copper ore in the Bronze Age, extracting iron in the blast furnaces of the Industrial Revolution. As our models of chemical bonding have become more sophisticated, our ability to predict — and design — the properties of materials has advanced with them. Innovations such as superconductors, breathable fabrics and liquid crystals only exist because scientists could apply detailed bonding theory to their synthesis.

Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding were introduced in earlier chapters as three separate categories, but this chapter builds a single, unified picture: the bonding triangle, which treats these three models as extremes of one continuum and can be used to predict the properties of a material from its position within it. The chapter then looks at two important applications of bonding theory to real materials: alloys (mixtures of metals with improved properties) and polymers (very large molecules built from repeating units, which include essentially all plastics).

Bonding as a Continuum: The Bonding Triangle

Real materials rarely show pure ionic, pure covalent or pure metallic bonding. Most substances lie somewhere between these three extremes and show a mixture of characteristics — we already saw this in Structure 2.1 with polar covalent bonds, which show partial ionic character. A more realistic model represents bonding as a continuum using a triangle, with each corner (vertex) representing 100% of one bonding type: ionic, covalent or metallic. Any element or compound can then be plotted at a position inside (or on an edge of) the triangle according to how much of each bonding type it shows.

The bonding triangleAn equilateral triangle is drawn with three labelled vertices: "ionic" at the top, "metallic" at the bottom left, and "covalent" at the bottom right. The horizontal axis along the base of the triangle represents the average electronegativity of the two bonded elements, increasing from left (low, metallic) to right (high, covalent). The vertical axis (height up the triangle) represents the electronegativity difference between the two elements, increasing from the base (zero difference) to the top vertex (large difference, ionic). Substances plotted near the top of the triangle behave as ionic compounds; those near the bottom left behave as metals or alloys; those near the bottom right behave as covalent (usually molecular) substances; and those in between show blended, intermediate properties, e.g. "polar covalent" sits partway between the covalent and ionic corners.

The position of a substance in the triangle is calculated from electronegativity values (given in the data booklet) using two combined quantities:

x-axis: average electronegativity, Σχ = (χA + χB) ÷ 2
y-axis: electronegativity difference, Δχ = χA − χB

where χA and χB are the Pauling electronegativity values of the two bonded elements (the more electronegative element is normally taken as A, so Δχ is positive). Elements have zero electronegativity difference (since both "bonded" atoms are identical), so they always lie on the horizontal base of the triangle: metals sit towards the low-electronegativity (left) end and non-metals towards the high-electronegativity (right) end. Compounds with a large electronegativity difference plot near the top of the triangle (ionic); compounds with a small electronegativity difference and high average electronegativity plot in the bottom right corner (covalent).

Worked exampleLocate caesium (Cs), fluorine (F2) and caesium fluoride (CsF) in the bonding triangle, given χ(Cs) = 0.8 and χ(F) = 4.0.
Cs: average = 0.8, difference = 0.8 − 0.8 = 0.0 → sits at the extreme left of the base (100% metallic; caesium has the lowest electronegativity of any element).
F2: average = 4.0, difference = 4.0 − 4.0 = 0.0 → sits at the extreme right of the base (100% covalent).
CsF: average = (4.0 + 0.8) ÷ 2 = 2.4, difference = 4.0 − 0.8 = 3.2 → sits at the very top of the triangle (100% ionic) — this is in fact the reference point usually taken as the most ionic compound possible, since it pairs the most electropositive metal with the most electronegative non-metal.
Worked exampleLocate diamond (carbon, χ = 2.6), silicon dioxide (Si χ = 1.9, O χ = 3.4) and bronze (an alloy of Cu χ = 1.9 and Sn χ = 2.0) in the bonding triangle.
Diamond: average = 2.6, difference = 0.0 (an element, so it lies on the base, well into the covalent region).
SiO2: average = (1.9 + 3.4) ÷ 2 = 2.65, difference = 3.4 − 1.9 = 1.5 → a position with substantial covalent character but also some ionic character (consistent with SiO2 being a giant covalent, rather than simple molecular, structure).
Bronze (Cu/Sn): average = (1.9 + 2.0) ÷ 2 = 1.95, difference = 2.0 − 1.9 = 0.1 → very close to the base, deep in the metallic corner (consistent with bronze being an alloy).

Using the Bonding Triangle to Predict Properties

Once a substance's position is known, its likely properties can be predicted from which corner it lies closest to. Substances near the ionic vertex are expected to have high melting points and to conduct electricity only when molten or dissolved. Substances near the metallic vertex are expected to conduct electricity as solids and to be malleable. Substances near the covalent vertex are expected to be non-conductors, and (if simple molecular) to have low melting and boiling points.

The period 3 oxides illustrate this well. Moving across period 3, the electronegativity of each element increases and gets closer to that of oxygen (χ = 3.4), so Δχ between the element and oxygen steadily falls, and the oxides become progressively less ionic and more covalent.

OxidesBondingProperties
Na2O, MgO, Al2O3ionic (metallic oxides)high melting/boiling points; conduct electricity when molten
SiO2giant covalent (oxide of a metalloid)high melting/boiling point; does not conduct electricity
P4O10, SO2, Cl2Ocovalent (non-metallic oxides)low melting/boiling points; do not conduct electricity

The bonding triangle also exposes the limits of treating "metal + non-metal = ionic" as a hard rule. Aluminium chloride, AlCl3, contains a metal bonded to a non-metal, which might suggest a typically ionic, high-melting solid, yet its melting point (192°C) is far below that of a genuinely ionic chloride such as NaCl (801°C).

Worked exampleCompare the bonding character of NaCl and AlCl3 using electronegativity data (χ(Na) = 0.9, χ(Al) = 1.6, χ(Cl) = 3.2).
NaCl: average = (0.9 + 3.2) ÷ 2 = 2.05, difference = 3.2 − 0.9 = 2.3 → upper-centre of the triangle, approximately 75% ionic.
AlCl3: average = (1.6 + 3.2) ÷ 2 = 2.4, difference = 3.2 − 1.6 = 1.6 → close to the ionic/covalent border, approximately 50% covalent.
This large covalent contribution in AlCl3 explains its much lower melting point and its poor electrical conductivity even in the liquid state, compared with the strongly ionic NaCl.
Composite materials vs a position in the bonding triangleA composite (e.g. reinforced concrete or fibreglass) is a heterogeneous mixture of at least two separate materials, each keeping its own individual properties and its own separate position in the bonding triangle (e.g. ionic concrete strengthened with metallic steel rebar). This is different from a single compound with an intermediate bonding character, which has one blended position in the triangle because its electrons are genuinely shared between ionic and covalent character throughout the structure.

Alloys

AlloyAn alloy is a homogeneous mixture containing at least one metal, combined with one or more other metals or non-metals (such as carbon), held together throughout by metallic bonding.

Alloys are made by mixing metals together while molten, so that the different types of atom can mix freely, and then allowing the mixture to solidify as a single, uniform solid solution. Because metallic bonding relies on a "sea" of delocalized electrons attracting a lattice of metal cations in all directions (non-directional bonding), the lattice can readily accommodate cations of different sizes without the strict directional requirements found in ionic or covalent bonding — this is precisely what makes alloying possible.

Structure of an alloyA regular grid of large, identically sized positive metal ions (representing a pure metal) is compared side-by-side with a second grid where smaller cations of a different metal are inserted into some of the gaps between the larger cations. In both grids, a shaded background of "−" symbols represents the sea of delocalized electrons flowing freely around all the positive ions and holding the whole structure together, regardless of which size of cation each electron happens to be near.

Introducing atoms of a different size into the regular lattice of a pure metal disrupts the orderly rows of identical ions. In a pure metal, these regular rows/layers can slide over each other relatively easily when a force is applied, which is why pure metals are often soft and easily bent (malleable). In an alloy, the irregular packing caused by differently sized ions makes it much harder for layers to slide past each other, so alloys are typically harder, stronger, and often more resistant to corrosion than the pure metals they are made from. Steel, for example (iron alloyed with under 2% carbon plus small amounts of manganese and other elements), can be around 1000 times stronger than pure iron.

Why alloys resist deformationTwo side-by-side diagrams show a metal lattice under an applied force. In the pure metal, uniform rows of identical circles slide smoothly past one another when the force is applied, changing the metal's shape easily. In the alloy, larger and smaller circles of two different elements are mixed within the same layers; when the same force is applied, the irregular sizes jam against each other and resist sliding, so the alloy holds its shape and resists deformation far more strongly.

Because alloys are mixtures rather than compounds, they have no fixed composition and cannot be written with a single chemical formula — "steel" describes a whole family of iron-carbon(-and other element) mixtures of varying proportions, not one substance. Since no new chemical bonds are broken or formed between the different metal elements during mixing (only physical mixing occurs), each component of an alloy keeps typical metallic properties such as electrical and thermal conductivity, and, where iron is present, magnetism.

Polymers

Polymer, monomerA polymer (or macromolecule) is a very large molecule built from many repeating subunits, called monomers, joined together by covalent bonds. Polymers vary enormously in the identity of their monomer, and in the length and branching of the resulting chain, giving a huge range of possible properties.

Because a polymer chain can contain many thousands of repeat units, its structure is normally drawn as a single repeating unit in brackets, with open bonds shown at each end to indicate that the chain continues, and a subscript n outside the brackets representing the (large, variable) number of repeat units in an actual chain.

General repeating-unit notationA single monomer unit is drawn with a central carbon atom bonded to two identical side groups labelled X. This same unit is then shown enclosed in large square brackets, with a short bond line extending outward from each side of the bracket to show that the chain continues indefinitely in both directions, and a small subscript n is placed just outside the bottom-right corner of the bracket to represent the large, repeating number of these units making up the full polymer chain.

Polymers occur throughout the living world: proteins, starch and DNA are all natural polymers, and understanding how their structure relates to their biological function has driven huge advances in medicine, from vaccines to new drugs. Human-made (synthetic) polymers first became widely available in the 1930s once raw materials from the petroleum industry were accessible, rapidly giving rise to modern plastics.

Plastics and the Environment

Plastics are valued for being lightweight, unreactive, water-resistant, and in some cases very strong, which is exactly why they are used everywhere in packaging, construction, clothing, transport and machinery. Unfortunately, the same unreactivity and water resistance that make plastics useful also make most of them non-biodegradable: they cannot be broken down by natural biological processes and so persist in the environment, in some cases for centuries, contributing heavily to pollution on land and in the oceans.

Biodegradable plasticsA biodegradable compound can be broken down by microorganisms into simple products already found in nature (typically carbon dioxide and water) and so does not accumulate as pollution. Biodegradable plastics are often plant-based (e.g. made from corn starch, lignin or cellulose); others are petroleum-based but contain catalysts that assist their breakdown. Compostable plastics break down under the specific warm, moist, microbially active conditions of a compost pile, alongside food and garden waste.

Reducing plastic pollution is not solved by any single strategy: better recycling, greater use of biodegradable materials, and (most importantly) reducing the overall production and single-use consumption of plastic all play complementary roles.

Addition Polymers

Addition polymerAn addition polymer forms when the double bond in each monomer molecule breaks, creating two new bonding positions that link the monomer to its neighbours on either side, building up a long chain. No other product is formed — the polymer is the only product of an addition polymerization reaction.

Alkenes (which contain a carbon–carbon double bond) are reactive at that double bond and so readily act as monomers for addition polymerization. As the double bond in each monomer opens up, its two carbon atoms each gain a new single bond, one to each of its neighbouring monomer units in the growing chain.

Forming an addition polymer from etheneThree separate ethene molecules are drawn side by side, each shown with its carbon–carbon double bond turned to point vertically (up-down) so that the two hydrogen atoms on each carbon point out horizontally to the left and right, like a person standing with arms out to the sides. Curved arrows show each double bond opening up into a single bond, with a new single bond then forming between the right-hand carbon of one monomer and the left-hand carbon of its neighbour, so the three units join end-to-end into a single continuous zig-zag carbon chain, extending open bonds off each end to show that the process continues indefinitely.

Drawing the monomer with its double bond in the centre and its two substituent groups pointing outward at right angles (like a person releasing their outstretched arms to link hands with the next person in a human chain) makes it much easier to see how the repeating unit is formed and how neighbouring monomers connect.

MonomerPolymerRepeating unit

To find the repeating unit from a monomer, simply change the C=C double bond into a C–C single bond and place the resulting unit in brackets with a subscript n; to find the monomer from a given repeating unit, do the reverse — identify the two carbon atoms that connect to the neighbouring units and convert that single bond back into a double bond.

Worked exampleDeduce the repeating unit formed from propene, CH2=CHCH3.
1. Draw the monomer with the double bond in the centre, the two hydrogens on one carbon pointing outward, and the H and CH3 groups on the other carbon pointing outward.
2. Open the double bond into a single bond, extending a new bonding position from each of the two carbons.
3. Enclose the resulting unit in brackets with subscript n: (–CH2–CH(CH3)–)n.

Atom Economy of Addition Polymerization

Atom economyAtom economy measures what proportion of the total mass of reactants ends up in the desired product, based on the reaction's stoichiometry.
% atom economy = (molar mass of desired product ÷ molar mass of all reactants) × 100

In an addition polymerization reaction, the only product is the polymer itself — there is no by-product, because every atom present in the monomers ends up in the polymer chain. This means addition polymerization always has an atom economy of exactly 100%.

Worked exampleShow that the polymerization of ethene (C2H4, M = 28 g mol−1) to form poly(ethene), (–C2H4–)n, has 100% atom economy.
Reactant mass: n monomers of C2H4, total molar mass = 28n g mol−1.
Product mass: the polymer chain (–C2H4–)n also has molar mass 28n g mol−1, since every atom from the monomers is incorporated with nothing left over.
% atom economy = (28n ÷ 28n) × 100 = 100%.
Common mistakeA 100% atom economy does not mean an addition polymerization process has no environmental impact. A reaction yield below 100% still leaves unreacted monomer, and the solvents, catalysts, and high temperatures/pressures often used in industrial polymerization all carry their own environmental costs that must be assessed separately from atom economy.

Guiding Question Revisited

  • Bonding in real materials is best described as a continuum between ionic, covalent and metallic extremes, represented visually by the bonding triangle.
  • A substance's position in the bonding triangle is found from the average electronegativity and the electronegativity difference of its constituent elements, and this position allows the properties of the substance to be predicted and explained.
  • Classifying a compound as simply "ionic" or "covalent" from its component elements alone can be misleading (e.g. AlCl3 behaves far more covalently than its metal-plus-non-metal composition would suggest).
  • Alloys are homogeneous mixtures containing at least one metal, held together by non-directional metallic bonding, which allows differently sized cations to be incorporated into a single lattice.
  • Disrupting the regular packing of a pure metal's lattice with atoms of different sizes typically makes an alloy stronger, harder and more corrosion-resistant than the pure metal.
  • Polymers are macromolecules built from repeating monomer units joined by covalent bonds; they include both natural polymers (proteins, starch, DNA) and synthetic polymers (plastics).
  • Addition polymers form when the double bond in an alkene monomer opens to create new bonding positions, joining monomers into a chain with no other product formed, giving 100% atom economy.
  • The widespread use of plastics brings major practical benefits but also significant environmental costs, since most plastics are not biodegradable and accumulate in the environment.
