R3.3.1 – Radicals

Guiding questionWhat happens when a species possesses an unpaired electron?

A radical is a chemical species that contains a single unpaired electron. The presence of this unpaired electron gives radicals a high enthalpy compared with their non-radical counterparts, which makes them highly reactive: it is energetically favourable for a radical to take an electron from another species, or to combine with a second radical, sharing both unpaired electrons to form a new covalent bond, since either process leads to products of lower enthalpy.

Representing radicalsA radical is shown in a chemical formula by adding a dot (•), representing the unpaired electron, for example a chlorine radical, Cl•, or a methyl radical, CH3•.

The only formal requirement of a radical is the presence of an unpaired electron, so a radical can be an atom, a neutral molecule, or an ion:

  • Atomic radicals: single atoms with an unpaired electron, e.g. a chlorine radical, Cl•, a bromine radical, Br•, or a hydrogen radical, H•.
  • Molecular radicals: neutral molecules with an odd number of electrons overall, e.g. nitric oxide, NO•, the hydroxyl radical, OH•, and the methyl radical, CH3•.
  • Radical anions: species that have gained an electron and so are both a radical and a negatively charged ion, e.g. the superoxide radical, O2•−.
  • Radical cations: species that have lost an electron and so are both a radical and a positively charged ion, e.g. the propane radical cation, C3H8•+.
Radical versus ionA radical has an unpaired electron but does not necessarily carry a net charge; an ion carries a net charge but does not necessarily have an unpaired electron. A species that is both a radical and an ion, such as O2•−, is called a radical anion or radical cation.

Because they are highly reactive, most radicals have very short lifetimes. An important exception occurs in the upper atmosphere, where radicals can persist for a long time, because the low density of gas molecules there makes collisions with other reactive species relatively rare.

Radicals in the body and the environmentRadical reactions can be beneficial, for example in some enzyme-catalysed reactions, in the destruction of invading pathogens, in wastewater treatment, and in the industrial manufacture of addition polymers. They can also be harmful: the free-radical theory of ageing proposes that oxidative radical reactions damage cell membranes and DNA over time, and radicals are similarly implicated in smog formation and in the depletion of atmospheric ozone, covered later in this chapter. Antioxidants, abundant in fresh fruit and vegetables, are thought to help protect cells against this kind of oxidative damage.

R3.3.2 – Homolytic Fission

Radicals are generated by breaking a covalent bond. Homolytic fission occurs when a covalent bond breaks symmetrically, with each atom keeping one electron from the original shared pair, so that two radicals are formed. Because a bond is being broken, homolytic fission is always an endothermic process, requiring an input of energy.

X–X → X• + X•

The amount of energy needed to bring about homolytic fission depends on the strength (bond enthalpy) of the covalent bond being broken:

Thermolytic versus photolytic fission
  • Thermolytic fission is homolytic fission brought about by heat. Sufficient energy can be supplied thermally to break relatively weak covalent bonds.
  • Photolytic fission is homolytic fission brought about by the absorption of high-energy ultraviolet (UV) light. This is needed to break stronger covalent bonds.
Worked example

The Cl–Cl bond in chlorine, Cl2, undergoes photolytic fission when it absorbs UV light, producing two chlorine radicals:

Cl2(g) → 2Cl•(g)  (UV light)

Each of the two chlorine atoms formed keeps one electron from the original shared pair, so both products are radicals.

Fish-hook (single-barbed) arrowsThe movement of a single electron during homolytic fission, or during a termination step (its reverse), is shown using a single-barbed curly arrow, sometimes called a ‘fish hook’. Two of these half-headed arrows are drawn, one starting from each of the two electrons that made up the original shared pair, each curving outward to show that electron ending up on a separate atom. This is different from the full, double-barbed curly arrows used elsewhere in organic mechanisms to show the movement of an electron pair, which represent heterolytic, not homolytic, bond breaking.

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and ozone depletion

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) were once widely used as refrigerants, solvents and aerosol propellants because they are chemically unreactive and non-toxic in the lower atmosphere (the troposphere). On reaching the stratosphere, however, they are exposed to higher-energy UV radiation than at the Earth's surface, which is able to break them down.

A CFC such as Freon-12, CCl2F2, contains both C–Cl and C–F bonds. The C–Cl bond (bond enthalpy 324 kJ mol−1) is considerably weaker than the C–F bond (bond enthalpy 492 kJ mol−1), so photolytic fission of the C–Cl bond occurs in preference, releasing a reactive chlorine radical:

CCl2F2(g) → CClF2•(g) + Cl•(g)  (UV light)
Worked example

The energy, E, of a photon is related to its wavelength, λ, by E = hc/λ, where h is the Planck constant and c is the speed of light. Calculate the maximum wavelength of UV radiation able to break a C–Cl bond, given a bond enthalpy of 324 kJ mol−1 and the Avogadro constant, NA = 6.02 × 1023 mol−1.

E = hc / λ

First convert the bond enthalpy from a per-mole quantity into an energy per bond, by dividing by the Avogadro constant:

E(per bond) = (324 × 103 J mol−1) / (6.02 × 1023 mol−1) = 5.38 × 10−19 J.

Rearranging E = hc/λ gives λ = hc/E, so, using h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s and c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1:

λ = (6.63 × 10−34 J s × 3.00 × 108 m s−1) / (5.38 × 10−19 J) = 3.70 × 10−7 m (370 nm).

Repeating this calculation for the stronger C–F bond (492 kJ mol−1) gives a required wavelength of 2.43 × 10−7 m (243 nm), a shorter, higher-energy wavelength than for the C–Cl bond. Since breaking the C–F bond needs more energetic radiation than breaking the C–Cl bond, the C–Cl bond breaks in preference, which is why CFCs release chlorine radicals but not fluorine radicals.

Once formed, chlorine radicals catalyse the breakdown of ozone, O3, through a two-step cycle:

Cl•(g) + O3(g) → ClO•(g) + O2(g)
ClO•(g) + O•(g) → Cl•(g) + O2(g)

Adding these two steps together, the chlorine radical cancels out, giving the overall reaction:

O3(g) + O•(g) → 2O2(g)

Because the chlorine radical is regenerated at the end of the cycle, it acts as a catalyst and is not consumed overall, so a single chlorine radical can go on to break down many thousands of ozone molecules before it is eventually removed in a termination step. Chlorine radicals do not react significantly with ordinary oxygen, O2, present in the stratosphere, since the O=O double bond in oxygen is stronger than the bonds within ozone.

R3.3.3 – Radical Substitution Reactions of Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons containing only strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds. Because they are non-polar, alkanes do not attract charged or partially charged reactive species such as electrophiles or nucleophiles, and because their bonds are strong, reactions with alkanes have high activation energies.

Kinetically stable but thermodynamically unstableAt room temperature, most alkane molecules do not have enough kinetic energy to overcome the high activation energy of a reaction, so alkanes are described as kinetically stable (unreactive in practice). However, reactions of alkanes such as combustion are strongly exothermic, meaning the alkane has a higher enthalpy than the products: alkanes are therefore thermodynamically unstable relative to their reaction products, even though they react only slowly, if at all, under ordinary conditions.
Reaction profile for an alkane reactionOn a plot of potential energy (vertical axis) against reaction coordinate (horizontal axis), the alkane reactants start at a relatively low potential energy. The curve then rises steeply to a high peak, representing the large activation energy that must be overcome, before falling to a lower potential energy for the products than for the reactants. The height of the initial rise reflects the kinetic stability of the alkane; the overall drop in potential energy from reactants to products reflects its thermodynamic instability relative to those products.

Because alkanes are saturated, the main reaction they undergo is substitution: another atom or group takes the place of a hydrogen atom. When an alkane reacts with a halogen, the products are a halogenoalkane and the corresponding hydrogen halide. For example, methane reacts with chlorine to give chloromethane and hydrogen chloride:

CH4(g) + Cl2(g) → CH3Cl(g) + HCl(g)  (UV light)

This reaction does not occur in the dark: UV light is essential because its energy is needed to break the Cl–Cl bond and generate chlorine radicals, which then start a chain reaction. The full mechanism is described as a sequence of three stages: initiation, propagation and termination.

The three stages of a radical substitution mechanism
  • Initiation: radicals are first generated, usually by the homolytic fission of a halogen molecule under UV light.
  • Propagation: each radical reacts with a stable molecule to generate a new radical, which can then go on to react further; because a radical is used up but a new one is always produced, this stage can repeat itself many times as a chain reaction.
  • Termination: two radicals react with each other, combining their unpaired electrons to form a new covalent bond and removing both radicals from the reaction mixture. Termination is, in effect, the reverse of homolytic fission.
Worked example

Give the initiation, propagation and termination steps that lead to the formation of chloromethane, CH3Cl, when methane reacts with chlorine.

Initiation (photolytic fission of chlorine):

Cl2 → 2Cl•  (UV light)

Propagation (a chlorine radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from methane, forming a methyl radical, which then reacts with another chlorine molecule to regenerate a chlorine radical):

Cl• + CH4 → CH3• + HCl
CH3• + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•

Termination (two radicals combine):

CH3• + Cl• → CH3Cl

Adding the initiation step and the two propagation steps together, so that the intermediate radicals cancel, reproduces the overall equation CH4(g) + Cl2(g) → CH3Cl(g) + HCl(g).

In practice, many different propagation and termination steps are possible at the same time, so the reaction mixture contains a mixture of products, not just chloromethane. Further propagation steps can substitute additional hydrogen atoms:

CH3Cl + Cl• → CH2Cl• + HCl
CH2Cl• + Cl2 → CH2Cl2 + Cl•

Other termination steps are also possible, including two radicals of the same type combining with each other:

Cl• + Cl• → Cl2
CH3• + CH3• → C2H6
CH2Cl• + Cl• → CH2Cl2
Evidence for a chain mechanismThe formation of small amounts of ethane, C2H6, alongside the expected chloromethane is important evidence for the radical chain mechanism: ethane can only be formed by two methyl radicals combining in a termination step (CH3• + CH3• → C2H6), so its presence confirms that methyl radicals really are intermediates in the reaction, and that the mechanism proceeds through repeated propagation and termination steps rather than in a single step.
Worked example

Ethane, C2H6, reacts with bromine, Br2, under UV light to form mainly bromoethane and, as a minor by-product, butane. Outline the three stages of the mechanism.

Initiation:

Br2 → 2Br•  (UV light)

Propagation:

Br• + C2H6 → C2H5• + HBr
C2H5• + Br2 → C2H5Br + Br•

Termination (giving the main product, bromoethane, and the minor product, butane):

C2H5• + Br• → C2H5Br
C2H5• + C2H5• → C4H10

The formation of butane (C4H10), rather than only bromoethane, is further evidence that the reaction proceeds via free ethyl radicals, C2H5•, which can combine with each other as well as with bromine radicals.

Distinguishing alkanes and alkenes with bromine water

Bromine water: alkanes versus alkenesBromine water is decolorized rapidly by an alkene, even in the dark, through electrophilic addition. With an alkane, however, decolorization only occurs when the mixture is exposed to UV light, since a radical substitution mechanism first requires the photolytic fission of a halogen molecule; in the dark, no reaction is observed. This difference in behaviour can be used as a simple chemical test to distinguish an alkane from an alkene.

Guiding Question Revisited

What happens when a species possesses an unpaired electron?
  • A radical is a chemical species containing an unpaired electron, represented by a dot (•); radicals may be atoms, molecules, or ions, and are highly reactive.
  • Radicals are generated by homolytic fission of a covalent bond, in which each product keeps one electron from the original shared pair; this endothermic process can be brought about by heat (thermolytic fission) or by UV light (photolytic fission).
  • Chlorine radicals released from CFCs by photolytic fission catalyse the breakdown of stratospheric ozone through a two-step cycle in which the chlorine radical is regenerated.
  • Alkanes are kinetically stable, due to strong, non-polar bonds and high activation energies, but react with halogens under UV light via a radical substitution mechanism.
  • A radical substitution mechanism has three stages: initiation (radicals are generated), propagation (a chain of steps in which each radical reacts with a stable molecule to regenerate a new radical), and termination (two radicals combine and are removed from the mixture).
