Rate of Reaction: Definition and Units

Guiding questionHow can the rate of a reaction be controlled?

The rate, or kinetics, of a chemical reaction refers to the speed at which reactants are converted into products during a chemical process. Studies of rates of reaction are of prime importance in industry because they give information on how quickly products form and on the conditions that give the most efficient and economic yield. They can also be useful in situations where we want to slow reactions down — for example, the reactions that destroy ozone in the stratosphere, or the reactions where pollutants in the air combine to produce smog.

Rate takes the reciprocal value of time, so it is expressed per unit time, or in SI units, per second.

rate = 1 / time, units = s−1

As a reaction proceeds, reactants are converted into products, so the concentration of reactants decreases while the concentration of products increases. The rate of a reaction depends on how quickly the concentration of either reactant or product changes with respect to time.

rate of reaction = Δ[P] / Δt or −Δ[R] / Δt

Δ means “change in”, [P] is the concentration of a product, and [R] is the concentration of a reactant, both usually in mol dm−3. By convention, rate is always expressed as a positive value. Because [R] decreases over time, Δ[R] is naturally negative, so a negative sign is placed in front of the reactant expression to keep the calculated rate positive. As rate is a change in concentration per unit time, its units are mol dm−3 s−1.

Rate of reactionThe rate of a chemical reaction is the increase in concentration of products, or the decrease in concentration of reactants, per unit time.
Figure R2.2.1 and R2.2.2: Concentration against timeTwo simple sketch graphs with time on the horizontal axis. The first shows product concentration, [P], starting at zero and rising as a curve that gets less steep over time, levelling off as the reaction finishes. The second shows reactant concentration, [R], starting at a maximum value and falling as a curve that gets less steep over time, levelling off toward zero (or toward whatever concentration remains) as the reaction finishes. Both curves illustrate that the rate of change is greatest at the start of the reaction and decreases as the reaction proceeds.

Measuring Rate from a Graph: Gradients and Tangents

Figure R2.2.3: Two reactions comparedA sketch graph of reactant concentration, [R], against time for two different reactions, A and B, both starting from the same initial concentration and decreasing. Curve A is steeper (drops more sharply) than curve B, showing that the concentration of reactant is decreasing more quickly in reaction A than in reaction B.

The steepness, or gradient, of a concentration–time curve is a measure of the rate of the reaction. Because these graphs are curves rather than straight lines, the gradient is not constant, so a rate can only be given for a particular value of time. This is done by drawing a tangent to the curve at the chosen time point and measuring the gradient of that tangent. Note that the gradient of a graph of [R] against time is negative, since [R] is decreasing, but rate itself is still reported as a positive value.

Worked example

A tangent is drawn to a curve of [R] against time at t = 120 s. Reading from the tangent, the change in concentration is −2.40 mol dm−3 over a change in time of 283 s. Calculate the reaction rate at t = 120 s.

rate = −Δ[R] / Δt = 2.40 mol dm−3 / 283 s = 8.48 × 10−3 mol dm−3 s−1

The measured rate of the reaction is 8.48 × 10−3 mol dm−3 s−1 at t = 120 s. By convention, this is expressed as a positive value even though the tangent itself has a negative gradient.

The shape of a typical [R]-against-time curve shows that the rate of reaction is not constant, but is greatest at the start of the reaction and slows down as the reaction proceeds. This is because the rate is greatest at the start, when the reactant concentration is highest.

Worked example

Tangents are drawn to a concentration–time curve at t = 0 s (the start of the reaction) and at t = 90 s. At t = 0 s, the tangent gives a change of −1.92 mol dm−3 over 67 s. At t = 90 s, the tangent gives a change of −1.18 mol dm−3 over 164 s. Calculate the rate at each time.

rate at t = 0 s = 1.92 / 67 = 2.9 × 10−2 mol dm−3 s−1
rate at t = 90 s = 1.18 / 164 = 7.2 × 10−3 mol dm−3 s−1

This confirms that the rate decreases sharply as the reaction proceeds: the rate at 90 s (7.2 × 10−3 mol dm−3 s−1) is roughly four times smaller than the initial rate at 0 s (2.9 × 10−2 mol dm−3 s−1).

Because of this variation in rate as a reaction proceeds, it is common practice to compare the initial rate of different reactions by taking the tangent to the curve at t = 0. Initial rate data are especially useful when analyzing the effect of concentration on rate.

Rate changes during a reactionThe rate of a reaction is not constant during a reaction, but is greatest at the start and decreases as the reaction proceeds.
“Draw a graph” versus “sketch a graph”Drawing a graph is based on actual data, so scales must be chosen appropriately and data points and units must be clearly marked. Sketching a graph is a way of showing a trend in the variables without reference to specific data. In both cases, the axes must be clearly labelled, with the dependent variable on the y-axis and the independent variable on the x-axis. A more accurate tangent gradient is obtained by drawing the tangent line as long as reasonably possible, so that both the concentration change and the time change used in the calculation are as large as possible.

Experimental Techniques for Measuring Rate

In most cases, concentration is not measured directly. Instead it is measured by means of a signal that is related to the changing concentration, such as a change in volume, mass, colour, or conductivity. Many of these experiments can be carried out using data-logging devices.

Change in volume of gas produced

This is convenient if one of the products is a gas. A gas syringe, or displacement of water from an inverted burette, can be used to collect the gas and measure the change in volume at regular time intervals, giving a graph of volume against time. The displacement method can only be used if the gas has low solubility in water. The reaction of a metal with dilute acid to release hydrogen gas can be followed this way:

Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)

Change in mass

If a reaction gives off a gas, the resulting decrease in mass can be measured directly by standing the reaction mixture on a balance, allowing continuous readings and a graph of mass against time. This method is unlikely to work well when the evolved gas is hydrogen, as it is too light to give significant changes in mass. The release of carbon dioxide from a carbonate reacting with dilute acid can be followed this way:

CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + CO2(g) + H2O(l)

Change in transmission of light: colorimetry/spectrophotometry

This technique can be used if a reactant or product is coloured and shows characteristic absorption in the visible region (wavelengths of about 320–800 nm). A colorimeter or spectrophotometer passes light of a selected wavelength through the solution and measures the intensity of light transmitted; as the concentration of the coloured compound increases, more light is absorbed and less is transmitted. This allows a graph of absorbance (or transmittance) against time to be plotted continuously, and absorbance can be converted into concentration using a calibration curve. For example, the reaction between the dye crystal violet and sodium hydroxide can be followed by the decrease in absorbance as the coloured reactant is used up:

CV+ + OH− → CVOH

Change in concentration measured using titration

A sample of the reaction mixture may be withdrawn at regular time intervals and analyzed by titration against a known standard. Because titration itself takes time, during which the sample would continue to react, a technique called quenching is used: a substance is added that effectively stops the reaction in that sample at the moment it is withdrawn, giving a “freeze-frame” snapshot of the reaction at that instant. For example, the reaction between H2O2 and acidified KI yields I2, which can be titrated against sodium thiosulfate, Na2S2O3, with sodium carbonate, Na2CO3, used to quench the reaction by neutralizing the acid:

H2O2(aq) + 2H+(aq) + 2I−(aq) → I2(aq) + 2H2O(l)

Change in concentration measured using conductivity

The total electrical conductivity of a solution depends on the total concentration and charge of its ions. If this changes as reactants are converted to products, a conductivity meter with immersed inert electrodes can be used to follow the reaction, calibrated with solutions of known concentration. For example, in the reaction

BrO3−(aq) + 5Br−(aq) + 6H+(aq) → 3Br2(aq) + 3H2O(l)

the sharp decrease in the total concentration of ions (12 on the reactants side, 0 on the products side) gives a corresponding decrease in electrical conductivity as the reaction proceeds.

Non-continuous methods: “clock reactions”

When it is difficult to record a continuous change, it may be more convenient to measure the time taken to reach a chosen, observable “end point” that stops the clock. The limitation of this method is that it gives only an average rate over the time interval measured. Examples include the time taken for a piece of magnesium ribbon to react completely and disappear, and the classic thiosulfate “disappearing cross” experiment, in which a solution of sodium thiosulfate becomes opaque with precipitated sulfur so that a cross viewed through the solution is no longer visible:

Na2S2O3(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + SO2(aq) + H2O(l) + S(s)
Time as independent versus dependent variableIn the first five techniques described above (gas volume, mass, colorimetry, titration, conductivity), time is the independent variable and the signal (volume, mass, absorbance, and so on) is measured continuously as it changes. In a clock reaction, by contrast, the signal is a fixed, chosen end point, and the time taken to reach it becomes the dependent variable.
Balanced equations give no rate informationThe balanced equation of a reaction gives no information about its rate. Rate information can only be obtained from experimental (empirical) data.

Collision Theory

The kinetic molecular theory states that particles in a substance move randomly as a result of the kinetic energy they possess. Because of the random nature of these movements and collisions, not all particles in a substance have the same kinetic energy at any one time; instead they have a range of values. A convenient way to describe the kinetic energy of a substance is to take the average of these values, and this average is directly related to the substance's absolute temperature, that is, its temperature measured in kelvin.

Temperature and kinetic energyTemperature in kelvin (K) is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.

Increasing temperature means an increase in the average kinetic energy of the particles of a substance. When we compare the behaviour of particles in the three states of matter, from solid through liquid to gas, the differences arise from this increase in average kinetic energy.

The Maxwell–Boltzmann energy distribution curve

Figure R2.2.4: The Maxwell–Boltzmann energy distribution curveA graph with kinetic energy, E, on the horizontal axis and the number of particles with that kinetic energy on the vertical axis. The curve starts at the origin, rises steeply to a single peak at a relatively low energy value, and then falls away more gradually, tailing off toward the higher-energy end of the axis without ever quite reaching zero. The curve is asymmetric: it is not a bell shape, but is skewed with a long tail extending toward high energies. The area enclosed under the whole curve represents the total number of particles in the sample.

This curve shows the number of particles having a specific value of kinetic energy (or the probability of that value occurring) plotted against the values for kinetic energy.

The nature of collisions between particles

When reactants are placed together, the kinetic energy of their particles causes them to collide. The energy of these collisions may break some bonds within the reactants and form some new bonds, so that products form and the reaction happens. This explanation of chemical reactions is called collision theory. Not all collisions are “successful”, that is, not all collisions lead to the formation of products. Two main factors influence this: the energy of collision, and the geometry of collision.

Collision theorySpecies react as a result of collisions of sufficient energy and proper orientation. In order to react, particles must collide with kinetic energy greater than the activation energy and with the correct collision geometry.

Energy of collision: activation energy

In order for a collision to lead to a reaction, the particles must have a certain minimum value of kinetic energy, known as the activation energy, Ea. This energy is necessary to overcome repulsion between molecules and, often, to break some bonds in the reactants before they can react. When this energy is supplied, the reactants reach a high-energy transition state from which products can form.

Activation energyActivation energy (Ea) is defined as the minimum value of kinetic energy that particles must have before they are able to react.

Activation energy can be thought of as a threshold value, like a pass mark in an examination: values greater than the mark achieve a “pass” (a successful, reacting collision), while lower values do not. Particles with kinetic energy below the activation energy may still collide, but these collisions will not be successful in the sense of causing a reaction. It therefore follows that the rate of a reaction depends on the proportion of particles that have kinetic energy greater than the activation energy.

Figure R2.2.5: Activation energy on the Maxwell–Boltzmann curveThe same Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curve as before, with a vertical line marked at a value of kinetic energy labelled Ea (activation energy), positioned some way along the tail of the curve, to the right of the peak. The area under the curve to the left of this line (from zero up to Ea) is shaded to represent the proportion of particles that do not have enough energy to react. The area under the curve to the right of the line, under the tail of the distribution, is shaded a different colour to represent the (much smaller) proportion of particles in the sample that do have enough energy to react.

The magnitude of the activation energy varies greatly from one reaction to another and is an important factor in determining the overall rate. In general, reactions with a high activation energy proceed more slowly than those with a low activation energy, because fewer particles have the required energy for a successful collision.

Reaction energy profiles

Figure R2.2.6: Energy profiles for endothermic and exothermic reactionsTwo potential-energy-against-reaction-coordinate diagrams placed side by side. In diagram (a), an endothermic reaction, the reactants are drawn at a lower potential energy on the left, the curve rises to a peak (the transition state) in the middle, and the products are drawn at a higher potential energy than the reactants on the right; the height from reactants up to the peak is labelled Ea, and the smaller height from reactants up to products (lower than the peak) is labelled ΔH, which is positive since products sit above reactants. In diagram (b), an exothermic reaction, the reactants are drawn at a higher potential energy on the left, the curve again rises to a peak labelled Ea above the reactants, then falls to products drawn at a lower potential energy than the reactants on the right; ΔH is again the vertical distance between reactants and products, but here it is negative, since products sit below reactants.

Geometry of collision

Because collisions between particles are random, they occur with particles in many different orientations. In some reactions this is crucial in determining whether a collision is successful.

Figure R2.2.7: Effect of collision geometryTwo small diagrams of reactant molecules approaching each other. In (a), the molecules approach with the wrong orientation for their reactive parts to meet; after the collision, the reactant molecules simply separate again with no product formed — an ineffective collision. In (b), the molecules approach with the correct orientation so that the reactive parts of each molecule meet directly; after the collision, product molecules are formed — an effective collision.

Collision theory can be summarized as follows: the rate of a reaction depends on the frequency of collisions that occur between particles possessing both (1) kinetic energy greater than the activation energy, and (2) appropriate collision geometry.

Factors That Influence the Rate of Reaction

From collision theory, any factor that increases the number of successful collisions will increase the rate of a reaction. Five such factors are considered here: temperature, concentration, pressure, surface area, and the use of a catalyst.

Temperature

Increasing the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the particles.

Figure R2.2.8: Maxwell–Boltzmann curves at two temperaturesTwo Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curves plotted on the same axes for the same sample of gas at 300 K and at 310 K. The 310 K curve has a lower, broader peak that is shifted to the right (toward higher kinetic energy) compared to the 300 K curve, which has a taller, narrower peak further to the left. The area enclosed under each curve is equal, since both represent the same total number of particles. A vertical line marking the activation energy, Ea, is drawn further along the tail of both curves; the area under the 310 K curve beyond this line (representing particles with energy greater than Ea) is visibly larger than the corresponding area under the 300 K curve.

At the higher temperature, more particles have higher values of kinetic energy, and the proportion of particles with energy greater than the activation energy increases. As temperature increases, there is an increase in collision frequency due to the higher kinetic energy, but more importantly, there is an increase in the number of collisions involving particles with the necessary activation energy. Consequently, the frequency of successful collisions increases, and so the rate of reaction increases. Many reactions approximately double their reaction rate for every 10°C increase in temperature.

Concentration

Increasing the concentration of reactants increases the rate of reaction, because as concentration increases, the frequency of collisions between reactant particles increases, and so the frequency of successful collisions increases too.

Figure R2.2.9: Effect of concentration on collision frequencyTwo boxes containing the same small number of particles of each of two reactant types, drawn as dots of two different colours. In the low-concentration box, the particles are spread far apart with lots of empty space between them. In the higher-concentration box (drawn the same size), more particles of each colour are packed into the same volume, so that they are much closer together on average, illustrating that particles that are closer together have a greater chance of colliding.

The effect of concentration can be seen by following the rate of a reaction as it progresses: as reactants are used up, their concentration falls and the rate decreases, giving the typical curved rate graphs described earlier in this chapter.

Pressure

For reactions involving gases, increasing the pressure increases the rate of reaction. This is because higher pressure compresses the gas, effectively increasing its concentration, which increases the frequency of collisions.

Figure R2.2.10: Effect of pressure on collision frequencyTwo identical boxes each containing the same number of gas particles, drawn as dots. In the low-pressure box, the container is large so the particles are spread out. In the high-pressure box, the container has been made smaller (compressed) while holding the same number of particles, so the particles are packed more closely together, illustrating a greater collision frequency at the same temperature.

Surface area

Increasing the surface area of reactants increases the rate of reaction. This is important in heterogeneous reactions, where the reactants are in different phases, such as a solid reacting with a solution. Subdividing a large particle into smaller parts increases the total surface area and allows more contact and a higher probability of collision between reactants. In reactions involving solids in solutions, stirring may also help to increase effective surface area by ensuring individual particles are spread throughout the solution rather than aggregating at the bottom of the container.

The effect of particle size can be demonstrated in the reaction between marble (CaCO3) and hydrochloric acid: when marble chips are replaced with powder, the effervescence caused by carbon dioxide release is much more vigorous. This effect can be dramatic on an industrial scale and has been responsible for explosions of flammable dust powders, such as coal dust in mines and flour in mills, where the enormous surface area of fine dust particles allows an extremely rapid reaction with oxygen.

Catalyst

CatalystA catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without itself undergoing permanent chemical change. Most catalysts work by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
Figure R2.2.11: Reaction profile with and without a catalystA potential-energy-against-reaction-coordinate diagram showing reactants on the left and products on the right, with ΔH marked as the (unchanged) vertical distance between them. Two separate curves are drawn connecting reactants to products: the uncatalyzed reaction follows a path that rises to a high peak, labelled with a large activation energy; the catalyzed reaction follows a lower path that rises to a noticeably lower peak, labelled with a smaller activation energy. Both curves start and end at the same reactant and product energy levels, showing that ΔH is identical for the catalyzed and uncatalyzed routes; only the height of the peak (Ea) differs.

Because the catalyzed pathway has a lower activation energy, a larger number of particles will have kinetic energy greater than this lower activation energy, without any increase in temperature, so more particles are able to undergo successful collisions.

Figure R2.2.12: Maxwell–Boltzmann curve showing the effect of a catalystA single Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curve at one fixed temperature. Two vertical lines are drawn on the tail of the curve: one further to the right, labelled Ea uncatalyzed, and one closer to the peak, labelled Ea catalyzed (a lower energy value than the uncatalyzed line). The area under the curve beyond the catalyzed line (representing particles with enough energy to react via the catalyzed pathway) is visibly larger than the area beyond the uncatalyzed line, even though the curve itself has not changed shape or shifted — only the position of the energy threshold has moved to a lower value.

Catalysts bring about an equal reduction in the activation energy of both the forward and the reverse reactions, so they do not change the position of equilibrium or the yield of a reaction; they simply allow both the forward and reverse reactions to reach that same equilibrium position more quickly. Without catalysts, many industrial reactions would proceed too slowly, or would need to be run at such high temperatures that they would not be economically worthwhile.

Homogeneous versus heterogeneous catalystsA homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants: for example, gaseous chlorine radicals catalyzing the breakdown of ozone in the stratosphere. A heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase to the reactants: for example, solid vanadium(V) oxide catalyzing the reaction between gaseous sulfur dioxide and oxygen. The specific mechanisms of homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis are not required in this course.

Biological catalysts are called enzymes. Every biological reaction is controlled by a specific enzyme, and enzymes are increasingly used in domestic and industrial processes, from biological detergents to biosensors in medical studies. Catalytic converters in cars use transition metal catalysts such as platinum, palladium and rhodium to convert toxic exhaust emissions, including carbon monoxide and nitrogen monoxide, into less harmful gases:

2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO2(g) + N2(g)

It is estimated that catalytic converters can reduce pollution emissions by 90% without loss of engine performance or fuel economy. Catalysis is also central to Green Chemistry, which seeks to reduce the environmental impact of chemical processes: catalysts can replace stoichiometric reagents, enhance the selectivity of a process, and, because they are effective in very small quantities and can often be reused, they do not contribute to chemical waste, thereby increasing atom economy.

Investigating Rate of Reaction: Variables and Errors

Identifying controlled variables

Given the range of factors that affect rate of reaction, it is essential to identify the relevant controlled variables when planning an investigation. For example, when investigating the effect of temperature on the reaction between hydrochloric acid and magnesium, the same concentration of acid must be used in every trial, and the same size of magnesium strip must be used to control surface area. It is also important to control the specific details of the measurement technique itself — for example, keeping the position of a light sensor and light source the same in every trial when following a precipitation reaction by light absorbance.

Evidence for systematic and random errors in graphs

Figure R2.2.13: Systematic and random error on a graphThree small scatter-plots of a dependent variable against an independent variable, each with a straight trend line drawn through the data. In the first, labelled “perfect”, every data point sits exactly on the trend line. In the second, labelled “systematic”, all of the data points sit consistently to one side of (either entirely above or entirely below) the expected trend line, which is itself displaced from where a perfect data set would sit. In the third, labelled “random”, the data points are scattered on both sides of the trend line, some above and some below, without a consistent pattern.

Systematic errors occur when all measurements are consistently higher, or consistently lower, than the expected value, leading to inaccuracy in the final results. Random errors occur in individual measurements due to limitations in measurement equipment (uncertainty), changes in the surroundings, misinterpreted readings, or an insufficient number of repeat trials, leading to imprecision in the collected data. The fit of a trend line or curve can be evaluated using the coefficient of determination, R2, which falls between 0 and 1, with 1 indicating that the trend line passes perfectly through every data point.

Guiding Question Revisited

How can the rate of a reaction be controlled?
  • The rate of reaction is expressed as the change in concentration of a particular reactant or product per unit time. It has units of mol dm−3 s−1.
  • Rate of reaction can be measured indirectly using different experimental techniques (gas volume, mass, colorimetry, titration, conductivity, clock reactions). The choice of technique depends on the changes that occur during a specific reaction.
  • The rate at a specific point in a reaction can be calculated from the gradient of a tangent on a graph of concentration, volume, absorbance or mass against time.
  • For a reaction to occur, species must collide with sufficient kinetic energy and proper orientation. These are known as “successful” collisions.
  • The minimum energy required for a successful collision is the activation energy, Ea.
  • Increasing pressure, concentration, surface area or temperature, or using a catalyst, all increase the frequency of successful collisions per unit time and so increase the rate of a reaction.
  • Catalysts increase the rate of reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower Ea. This can be represented in a reaction energy profile, and does not affect ΔH or the yield of the reaction.
  • Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curves can be used to explain the effect of changing temperature and the use of a catalyst on the probability of successful collisions.
