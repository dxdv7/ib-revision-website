Dynamic Equilibrium

Guiding questionHow can the extent of a reversible reaction be influenced?

Many chemical reactions are reversible: they can proceed in both the forward direction (reactants forming products) and the backward, or reverse, direction (products re-forming reactants). The symbol ↔ is used in place of a single arrow to show that a reaction is an equilibrium reaction, occurring in both directions at once.

Dynamic equilibriumA state of dynamic equilibrium is reached in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction becomes equal to the rate of the backward reaction. The reaction has not stopped – both the forward and backward reactions are still occurring, but because they occur at the same rate there is no net (overall) change in the amounts of reactants and products.

A physical system reaching equilibrium: evaporation and condensation of bromine

Consider liquid bromine, Br2(l), placed in a sealed container at room temperature. Because bromine is volatile, molecules escape from the liquid surface and form vapour by evaporation. As the container is sealed, this vapour cannot escape, so its concentration builds up and some vapour molecules collide with the liquid surface, lose energy, and condense back to liquid.

Br2(l) ↔ Br2(g)

Initially the rate of evaporation is much greater than the rate of condensation. As vapour builds up, the rate of condensation increases (more vapour particles are available to collide with the liquid surface), while the rate of evaporation stays roughly constant, since it depends only on the surface area of the liquid. Eventually the rate of condensation becomes equal to the rate of evaporation, and from that point onward there is no further net change in the amount of liquid or vapour present: the system has reached equilibrium.

Rate–time graph for the Br2(l) ↔ Br2(g) equilibriumA graph with rate on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. The rate of evaporation is drawn as a horizontal line starting high and staying constant throughout, since it depends only on the constant surface area of the liquid. The rate of condensation is drawn as a curve starting at zero (no vapour present initially) and rising steeply at first, then levelling off as it approaches the constant evaporation rate from below. The point where the two curves meet and become a single horizontal line marks the establishment of equilibrium: from that time onward, rate of evaporation equals rate of condensation.

A chemical system reaching equilibrium: hydrogen iodide dissociation

Consider the reaction between colourless hydrogen iodide gas and its elements, colourless hydrogen gas and purple iodine gas:

2HI(g) ↔ H2(g) + I2(g)

Starting with pure HI(g) in a sealed container, the purple colour of I2(g) at first increases as the forward (dissociation) reaction proceeds. The rate of this forward reaction is fastest at the start, when [HI] is greatest, and falls as HI is used up. Meanwhile the reverse (association) reaction, which starts with a zero rate since no H2 or I2 is present initially, speeds up as H2 and I2 accumulate. Eventually the rate of the forward reaction becomes equal to the rate of the reverse reaction, so the concentrations of HI, H2 and I2 all become constant – the purple colour stops changing, and equilibrium has been reached. Analysis of the flask at this point would show that HI, H2 and I2 are all simultaneously present.

Importantly, the same equilibrium mixture is reached whether the reaction is started from pure HI, or from a mixture of H2 and I2, or from any other combination of the three species, provided the overall proportions of H, I and total moles are consistent and the temperature is the same.

Concentration–time graph approaching equilibrium from two different starting mixturesTwo side-by-side concentration-versus-time graphs. In the left-hand graph, starting from pure HI, the [HI] curve begins high and falls to a constant equilibrium value, while the [H2] and [I2] curves (identical to each other, since they are produced in a 1:1 ratio) begin at zero and rise to a constant, lower equilibrium value. In the right-hand graph, starting from a mixture of H2 and I2 only, the [H2] and [I2] curves begin high and fall, while [HI] begins at zero and rises. Both graphs level off at the same final equilibrium concentrations of HI, H2 and I2, with [HI] noticeably higher than [H2] and [I2] in each case, illustrating that the same equilibrium state is reached regardless of the starting mixture.
Equal concentrations is not the same as equilibriumAt equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products are constant, but this does not mean they are equal. Most commonly one side of the reaction is present in much higher concentration than the other, depending on the particular reaction and the conditions used.

Characteristics of a system at equilibrium

CharacteristicExplanation
Equilibrium is dynamicThe reaction has not stopped; both forward and backward reactions are still occurring, at the same rate.
Equilibrium is achieved in a closed systemA closed system has no exchange of matter with the surroundings, allowing reactants and products to react and recombine with each other.
Concentrations of reactants and products remain constantThey are being produced and destroyed at an equal rate.
No change in macroscopic propertiesObservable properties such as colour and density do not change, since they depend on the concentrations of the mixture, which are constant.
Equilibrium can be reached from either directionThe same equilibrium mixture results under the same conditions, whether the reaction starts from all reactants, all products, or any mixture of both.

Strictly speaking, essentially all reactions are reversible to some extent, but in many cases the equilibrium mixture consists almost entirely of products, so the reaction is considered to have gone virtually to completion. By convention, a single arrow, →, is used rather than the equilibrium sign in these cases – combustion reactions, for example, are treated as irreversible because the products are so much more stable than the reactants.

Describing the position of equilibrium

The proportion of reactant and product present in the equilibrium mixture is called its equilibrium position. A reaction whose equilibrium mixture contains predominantly products is said to lie to the right; one whose mixture contains predominantly reactants is said to lie to the left.

The Equilibrium Law and the Equilibrium Constant, K

The equilibrium lawThe equilibrium constant, K, has a fixed value for a particular reaction at a specified temperature. The only thing that changes the value of K for a given reaction is a change in temperature.

Consider the reaction H2(g) + I2(g) ↔ 2HI(g), studied at 440°C starting from three different initial mixtures.

Although the three experiments started from very different initial concentrations, processing the equilibrium concentrations as [HI]2/([H2][I2]) always gives the same value, within experimental accuracy: about 49.5. This constant value, obtained by raising each equilibrium concentration to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation, is called the equilibrium constant, K (sometimes written Kc, where the subscript ‘c’ stands for ‘concentration’).

Writing the equilibrium constant expressionFor the general homogeneous reaction aA + bB ↔ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant expression is:
K = ([C]eqmc[D]eqmd) / ([A]eqma[B]eqmb)

Products appear in the numerator and reactants in the denominator; each concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation (a power of 1 is not shown); and where more than one reactant or product appears, the terms are multiplied together. Square brackets [ ] denote concentration in mol dm−3. Only equilibrium concentrations may be substituted into this expression – using initial or non-equilibrium concentrations gives an incorrect value. The expression applies to homogeneous reactions, where all reactants and products are in the same phase (all gases, or all in solution); state symbols should always be included in the equation to confirm this. By IB convention, no units are given for K.

Worked example

Write the equilibrium constant expressions for: (i) 2H2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2H2O(g); (ii) Cu2+(aq) + 4NH3(aq) ↔ [Cu(NH3)4]2+(aq).

(i) K = [H2O]2 / ([H2]2[O2])

(ii) K = [[Cu(NH3)4]2+] / ([Cu2+][NH3]4)

Worked example

At 298 K, the equilibrium mixture for N2O4(g) ↔ 2NO2(g) has [N2O4] = 1.62 mol dm−3 and [NO2] = 1.80 mol dm−3. Determine the value of K.

K = [NO2]2 / [N2O4] = (1.80)2 / 1.62 = 3.24 / 1.62 = 2.00

The Magnitude of K and the Extent of Reaction

Because the equilibrium expression places products in the numerator and reactants in the denominator, a large value of K means that at equilibrium there are proportionately far more products than reactants: the equilibrium mixture lies to the right and the reaction goes almost to completion. A small value of K means there are proportionately far more reactants than products: the equilibrium mixture lies to the left and the reaction has barely taken place. Note that the magnitude of K tells us nothing about the rate at which equilibrium is reached – only about the composition of the mixture once it is reached.

Value of KExtent of reaction
K ≪ 1Reaction hardly proceeds
K < 1Equilibrium lies towards the reactants
K = 1Significant amounts of both reactants and products at equilibrium
K > 1Equilibrium lies towards the products
K ≫ 1Reaction goes almost to completion

For example, at 550 K, H2(g) + I2(g) ↔ 2HI(g) has K = 2, H2(g) + Br2(g) ↔ 2HBr(g) has K = 1010, and H2(g) + Cl2(g) ↔ 2HCl(g) has K = 1018. The huge range of these K values shows that at this temperature the reaction of H2 with Cl2 has proceeded the most fully, while H2 with I2 has reacted the least.

The value of K is also useful for comparing the strength of weak acids in aqueous solution, where K is given the symbol Ka. At 298 K, methanoic acid has Ka = 1.78 × 10−4 for HCOOH(aq) ↔ HCOO−(aq) + H+(aq), while ethanoic acid has the smaller value Ka = 1.74 × 10−5 for CH3COOH(aq) ↔ CH3COO−(aq) + H+(aq). Although the equilibrium lies to the left for both weak acids, methanoic acid dissociates to a greater extent, since it has the larger Ka value.

K for the Reverse Reaction, and for Multiples of a Reaction

For the general reaction aA + bB ↔ cC + dD, with equilibrium constant K = [C]c[D]d / ([A]a[B]b), the reverse reaction cC + dD ↔ aA + bB has equilibrium constant K′ = [A]a[B]b / ([C]c[D]d).

K′ = 1/K , or equivalently K′ = K−1

The equilibrium constant for a reaction is the reciprocal of the equilibrium constant for its reverse reaction, at the same temperature.

Worked example

The equilibrium constant for 2HI(g) ↔ H2(g) + I2(g) is 0.040 at a certain temperature. Find K′ for the reverse reaction, H2(g) + I2(g) ↔ 2HI(g), at the same temperature.

K′ = 1/K = 1/0.040 = 25

If instead the coefficients of a reaction equation are all multiplied by some factor n, the new equilibrium constant is the original K raised to the power n. Doubling all the coefficients squares K (K′ = K2); halving all the coefficients gives the square root of K (K′ = √K).

Worked example

At a given temperature, 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2SO3(g) has K = 278. Determine K for: (a) 4SO2(g) + 2O2(g) ↔ 4SO3(g); (b) SO3(g) ↔ SO2(g) + ½O2(g); (c) 6SO3(g) ↔ 6SO2(g) + 3O2(g).

(a) This equation is the original reaction with every coefficient doubled, so K = 2782 = 77 284.

(b) This equation is the reverse of the original reaction with every coefficient halved. First halve: SO2(g) + ½O2(g) ↔ SO3(g) has K = √278 = 16.7. Then reverse: SO3(g) ↔ SO2(g) + ½O2(g) has K = 1/16.7 = 0.0600.

(c) This equation is the reverse of the original reaction with every coefficient tripled. First triple: K = 2783 = 21 484 952. Then reverse: K = 1/21 484 952 = 4.65 × 10−8.

Le Châtelier's Principle

Le Châtelier's principleWhen a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change, the system responds in such a way as to minimize the effect of that change. Add something, and the system reacts to remove it; remove something, and the system reacts to replace it. After a while, a new equilibrium is established, with a different composition from the original equilibrium mixture.

Changes in concentration

Increasing the concentration of a reactant increases the rate of the forward reaction without immediately affecting the rate of the backward reaction, so the equilibrium shifts to the right (towards products) as a new equilibrium is established; the addition of reactant is partly countered by consuming some of it. Similarly, decreasing the concentration of a product (removing it) decreases the rate of the backward reaction, so the equilibrium again shifts to the right, replacing some of the product removed. In industrial processes, continuously removing product as it forms is a common way of pulling the equilibrium to the right and increasing yield.

Conversely, the equilibrium shifts to the left (towards reactants) if the concentration of a product is increased, or if the concentration of a reactant is decreased.

Effect on KChanging concentration shifts the position of equilibrium but does not change the value of K, provided the temperature stays constant. A new equilibrium mixture is reached in which the same expression for K, evaluated with the new equilibrium concentrations, gives the same numerical value as before.

Changes in pressure (or volume)

A change in pressure only affects the position of equilibrium for a gaseous reaction if the number of gas molecules differs between the two sides of the equation, because the number of gas molecules in a fixed volume is directly related to pressure. Increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with the smaller number of gas molecules, reducing the total number of gas molecules and so partly countering the pressure increase; decreasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with the larger number of gas molecules.

Worked example

For CO(g) + 2H2(g) ↔ CH3OH(g), there are 3 mol of gas on the left and 1 mol of gas on the right. Predict the effect of increasing the pressure.

High pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules, so the equilibrium shifts to the right, increasing the yield of CH3OH(g).

By contrast, for H2(g) + I2(g) ↔ 2HI(g), there are 2 mol of gas molecules on both sides, so changing the pressure changes the rate of the reaction but has no effect on the position of equilibrium or the value of K.

Effect on KChanging pressure (or volume) may shift the position of equilibrium for a reaction that involves a change in the number of gas molecules, but it never changes the value of K, provided the temperature stays constant.

Changes in temperature

Unlike concentration and pressure, a change in temperature does change the value of K, because it affects the rates of the forward and backward reactions unequally (they have different activation energies). To predict the direction of the shift, the enthalpy change of the forward reaction must be considered: the forward and backward reactions have equal and opposite enthalpy changes.

Worked example

2NO2(g) ↔ N2O4(g), ΔH = −57 kJ mol−1 (brown ↔ colourless). Predict the effect of decreasing the temperature.

The forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH is negative). Decreasing the temperature favours the exothermic direction, so the system responds by producing more heat: the equilibrium shifts to the right, in favour of N2O4, and K increases. This is confirmed by data: K = 170 at 298 K but K = 1300 at the lower temperature of 273 K.

Conversely, for the endothermic reaction N2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2NO(g), ΔH = +181 kJ mol−1, increasing the temperature favours the forward, endothermic, direction: the equilibrium shifts right and K increases (K = 4.5 × 10−31 at 298 K, rising to K = 1.7 × 10−3 at 2300 K). Decreasing the temperature would shift this equilibrium to the left, favouring the exothermic backward reaction, and K would decrease.

Effect on KAn increase in temperature increases the value of K for an endothermic (forward) reaction and decreases the value of K for an exothermic (forward) reaction. This is the only one of the four factors covered here that changes the actual value of K.

Addition of a catalyst

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, Ea, increasing the proportion of particles with enough energy to react without any change in temperature. Because the forward and backward reactions of an equilibrium pass through the same transition state, a catalyst lowers the activation energy of both reactions by the same amount, so the rates of both the forward and backward reactions increase by the same factor.

Effect of a catalyst on the activation energy of forward and backward reactionsTwo potential energy profiles, drawn side by side, each with reaction coordinate on the horizontal axis and potential energy on the vertical axis. In the uncatalyzed profile, reactants sit at one energy level and products at a lower level, connected by a peak (the transition state); the height from reactants up to the peak is labelled as the activation energy of the forward reaction, and the height from products up to the same peak is labelled as the activation energy of the backward reaction. In the catalyzed profile, drawn immediately below or beside it, the same reactant and product energy levels are shown, but the peak connecting them is noticeably lower, with a dashed line showing the size of this lowering. Both the forward and backward activation energies in the catalyzed profile are smaller than in the uncatalyzed profile by the same amount, illustrating that a catalyst lowers the energy barrier for both directions equally, without changing the relative energies of reactants and products.
Effect on KA catalyst has no effect on the position of equilibrium and no effect on the value of K, because it speeds up the forward and backward reactions equally. It does not increase the equilibrium yield of product – it only speeds up how quickly the equilibrium state is reached. Catalysts are therefore not included in the equilibrium constant expression.

Le Châtelier's principle also applies to heterogeneous equilibria, involving reactants and products in different phases, not just to the homogeneous gas-phase examples above. Consider the equilibrium between dissolved and gaseous carbon dioxide in a sealed, carbonated drinks bottle:

CO2(g) ↔ CO2(aq)

Opening the bottle releases some gaseous CO2, so the equilibrium shifts to the left (fizzing, as more CO2(aq) converts to CO2(g)). Once the cap is replaced, a new equilibrium composition forms, but the value of K remains the same. If the bottle is left open, the equilibrium keeps shifting left until essentially no dissolved CO2 remains and the drink goes flat.

Applying Le Châtelier's Principle to Industrial Processes

Choosing conditions to maximize the equilibrium yield of an industrial reaction is only part of the story: the rate of reaction is equally important, since a process with a high equilibrium yield that takes years to reach it would have little economic value. The best industrial conditions are usually a compromise between the position of equilibrium (how far the reaction goes) and the rate of reaction (how fast it gets there).

Worked example: the Haber process

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ↔ 2NH3(g), ΔH = −93 kJ mol−1. Deduce the conditions that would maximize the conversion of reactants to products.

Concentration: increasing the concentrations of N2 and H2 (in their 1:3 stoichiometric ratio) and continuously removing NH3 as it forms both shift the equilibrium to the right.

Pressure: the forward reaction reduces the number of gas molecules (4 mol of gas on the left, 2 mol on the right), so high pressure favours the forward reaction.

Temperature: the forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature favours a higher equilibrium yield; however, too low a temperature makes the rate uneconomically slow, so a moderate temperature is used as a compromise.

Catalyst: an iron-based catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium but speeds up attainment of equilibrium, compensating for the moderate temperature used.

In practice, the Haber process converts only about 10–20% of N2 and H2 to NH3 per single pass through the reactor; unconverted reactants are separated from the NH3 product and recycled, giving an overall yield of about 95%.

The industrial production of methanol, CO(g) + 2H2(g) ↔ CH3OH(g), ΔH = −90 kJ mol−1, follows the same logic: the forward reaction reduces the number of gas molecules from three to one, so high pressure is used to shift the equilibrium right, while the exothermic forward reaction means a moderate temperature is chosen as a compromise between equilibrium yield and reaction rate. A catalyst is again used to increase the rate without affecting the position of equilibrium.

The Contact process, used to manufacture sulfuric acid, includes the step 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2SO3(g), ΔH = −198 kJ mol−1. As with the Haber process, high pressure and an exothermic forward reaction both favour the forward direction, so a moderate temperature and a catalyst are again used in practice as a compromise between equilibrium yield and reaction rate.

Guiding Question Revisited

How can the extent of a reversible reaction be influenced?
  • A dynamic equilibrium is reached in a closed physical or chemical system when the rates of the forward and backward reactions are equal, so the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant.
  • The equilibrium law states that every reaction has a particular value of the equilibrium constant, K, at a given temperature; K can be determined from the stoichiometry of the reaction and the equilibrium concentrations of reactants and products.
  • The magnitude of K indicates the extent of reaction: K ≫ 1 means the reaction goes almost to completion, while K ≪ 1 means the reaction hardly proceeds.
  • Le Châtelier's principle predicts how an equilibrium responds to a change: it shifts in the direction that minimizes the effect of the change.
  • An increase in the concentration of a reactant, or a decrease in the concentration of a product, shifts the equilibrium towards the products, and vice versa; K is unchanged.
  • An increase in pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with fewer gas molecules, and vice versa; K is unchanged.
  • An increase in temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic direction, and vice versa; K changes with temperature.
  • A catalyst has no effect on the position of equilibrium or on K, since it speeds up the forward and backward reactions equally – it only allows equilibrium to be reached more quickly.
