Guiding Question

How do we quantify matter on the atomic scale?

An individual atom is far too small to weigh directly — even the best analytical balances can measure down to only about 1 × 10−8 kg, while a single carbon atom has a mass of about 1.99 × 10−26 kg. Chemistry solves this problem with a counting unit called the mole, which links the number of particles reacting (far too many to count one by one) to a mass we can actually measure on a balance, in grams. This sub-topic builds the complete toolkit of mole calculations used throughout the rest of the course: converting between number of particles, moles and mass; finding the formula of a compound from experimental data; and applying the mole concept to solutions and to gases.

The Mole as the Unit of Amount

Atoms of different elements have different masses, but if we take a large, fixed number of atoms of each element, the ratio of their masses always matches the ratio of the mass of one atom of each. For example, one atom of oxygen has roughly 16 times the mass of one atom of hydrogen, and this exact ratio holds for any equal number of atoms of each — 100 atoms, a million atoms, or any other fixed amount. Chemists chose a specific, enormous number of particles so that this abstract ratio becomes a mass we can weigh in grams: 6.02 × 1023 atoms of hydrogen have a mass of 1 g, while the same number of atoms of oxygen have a mass of 16 g.

The mole (mol)The mole is the SI unit of amount of substance. One mole of a substance contains exactly the number of elementary entities given by the Avogadro constant, NA ≈ 6.02 × 1023 mol−1. An elementary entity can be an atom, a molecule, an ion, an electron, or any other specified particle or group of particles — the mole is simply a very large counting number, in the same way that “dozen” means 12 and “couple” means 2.

The number 6.02 × 1023 is called the Avogadro number; the corresponding physical constant, the Avogadro constant (NA), has units of mol−1 and is listed in the data booklet. Since 2019, the mole has been formally defined by fixing the numerical value of the Avogadro constant, which shifts the emphasis of “amount of substance” toward counting entities rather than measuring mass.

number of particles = number of moles (n) × Avogadro constant (NA)
N = n × NA   or, rearranged,   n = N ÷ NA

Here N is the number of elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, etc.), n is the amount in moles, and NA is the Avogadro constant, 6.02 × 1023 mol−1. To go from an amount in moles to a count of particles, multiply by NA; to go from a count of particles to an amount in moles, divide by NA.

Worked example

A tablespoon of water contains 0.500 mol of H2O molecules. How many molecules of water is this?
Using N = n × NA: N = 0.500 mol × 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 = 3.01 × 1023 molecules of water.

Worked example

A solution contains 2.10 × 1023 molecules of H2O and 8.00 × 1021 molecules of NH3. How many moles of hydrogen atoms are present in total?
Each H2O molecule contributes 2 H atoms and each NH3 molecule contributes 3 H atoms, so first find the total number of H atoms:
from H2O: 2 × (2.10 × 1023) = 4.20 × 1023 H atoms
from NH3: 3 × (8.00 × 1021) = 0.240 × 1023 H atoms
total H atoms = 4.20 × 1023 + 0.240 × 1023 = 4.44 × 1023
Now convert this count to moles by dividing by NA: n = N ÷ NA = (4.44 × 1023) ÷ (6.02 × 1023 mol−1) = 0.738 mol of H atoms.

The Avogadro constant is so large that it is impossible to picture directly — for instance, 6.02 × 1023 pencil erasers would cover the entire surface of the Earth to a depth of about 500 m. This is exactly why chemists never try to count particles individually and instead rely on the mole, and ultimately on mass, as the practical unit of amount.

Relative Atomic Mass and Relative Formula Mass

Since individual atoms cannot be weighed on a laboratory balance, their masses are compared on a relative scale instead. The internationally agreed reference point is the isotope carbon–12: one twelfth of the mass of one atom of 12C is defined as exactly 1 unit.

relative atomic mass, Ar = (weighted average mass of one atom of the element) ÷ (1/12 × mass of one atom of carbon–12)

Ar compares the average mass of atoms of an element (averaged over all of its naturally occurring isotopes, weighted by their relative abundance) against one twelfth of the mass of a carbon–12 atom. Because it is a ratio of two masses, Ar has no units. Values are not whole numbers, because most elements exist as a mixture of isotopes of slightly different mass; the data booklet gives Ar values to two decimal places, and these more precise values should be used in calculations unless a question says otherwise. For example, Ar(C) = 12.01, Ar(O) = 16.00, Ar(H) = 1.01, Ar(Na) = 22.99.

Relative formula mass, MrThe relative formula mass of a compound (or of an element that exists as molecules) is the sum of the relative atomic masses of every atom or ion shown in its formula. Like Ar, Mr has no units. The older term “relative molecular mass” is only strictly correct for substances that exist as discrete molecules, so “relative formula mass” is preferred because it also covers ionic compounds.
Worked example

Calculate the Mr of (a) chlorine, Cl2, (b) ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3, and (c) aluminium sulfate, Al2(SO4)3, using Ar(Cl) = 35.45, Ar(N) = 14.01, Ar(H) = 1.01, Ar(O) = 16.00, Ar(Al) = 26.98, Ar(S) = 32.07.
(a) Mr(Cl2) = 35.45 × 2 = 70.90
(b) Mr(NH4NO3) = 14.01 + (1.01 × 4) + 14.01 + (16.00 × 3) = 14.01 + 4.04 + 14.01 + 48.00 = 80.06
(c) Mr(Al2(SO4)3) = (26.98 × 2) + 3 × [32.07 + (16.00 × 4)] = 53.96 + 3 × 96.07 = 53.96 + 288.21 = 342.17

Weighted-average isotope calculationsBecause Ar is a weighted average, an element with two isotopes of different abundance is calculated as: Ar = (mass of isotope 1 × % abundance of isotope 1 + mass of isotope 2 × % abundance of isotope 2) ÷ 100. For example, chlorine consists of about 76% 35Cl and 24% 37Cl: Ar(Cl) = (35 × 76 + 37 × 24) ÷ 100 = (2660 + 888) ÷ 100 = 3548 ÷ 100 = 35.48, close to the data booklet value of 35.45. This confirms that chlorine’s Ar is not a whole number specifically because the lighter isotope, 35Cl, is more abundant than the heavier 37Cl, pulling the weighted average below 36.

Molar Mass

The Avogadro constant is defined precisely so that the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams, is numerically equal to its relative atomic mass (for an element) or relative formula mass (for a compound). This quantity is called the molar mass.

Molar mass, MThe molar mass of a substance is its Ar or Mr expressed in grams per mole. It is given the symbol M and has units g mol−1. For example, M(H) = 1.01 g mol−1, M(Cl2) = 70.90 g mol−1, M(NH4NO3) = 80.06 g mol−1.

Molar mass is the key that links the amount of a substance in moles (n) to its mass in grams (m). The relationship, given in the data booklet, is:

n = m ÷ M

where n is the amount in moles (mol), m is the mass of the sample (g), and M is the molar mass of the substance (g mol−1). Rearranged, m = n × M lets you find the mass of a known number of moles, and dividing a given mass by the molar mass gives the number of moles.

Worked example (moles to mass)

What is the mass of 6.50 mol of NaCl? (Ar(Na) = 22.99, Ar(Cl) = 35.45)
First find the molar mass: M(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g mol−1.
Then m = n × M = 6.50 mol × 58.44 g mol−1 = 380 g (to 3 s.f.).

Worked example (mass to moles)

How many moles are in 32.50 g of ammonium sulfate, (NH4)2SO4? (Ar(N) = 14.01, Ar(H) = 1.01, Ar(S) = 32.07, Ar(O) = 16.00)
M((NH4)2SO4) = 2 × [14.01 + (1.01 × 4)] + 32.07 + (16.00 × 4) = 2 × 18.05 + 32.07 + 64.00 = 36.10 + 32.07 + 64.00 = 132.17 g mol−1.
n = m ÷ M = 32.50 g ÷ 132.17 g mol−1 = 0.2459 mol.

Because a chemical equation shows the ratio of moles of each reactant and product, molar masses also let us calculate the masses that react together or are produced. For example, in 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l), 2 mol of H2 (2 × 2.02 g = 4.04 g) reacts with 1 mol of O2 (32.00 g) to give 2 mol of H2O (2 × 18.02 g = 36.04 g); this same reasoning, applied to any balanced equation, underlies all calculations of reacting masses and percentage yield, covered further in Reactivity 2.1.

The central role of the moleA three-box flow diagram with “number of particles” on the left, “number of moles, n” in the centre, and “mass in grams” on the right. The arrow from particles to moles is labelled “divide by Avogadro constant, NA” (and the reverse arrow “multiply by NA”); the arrow from moles to mass is labelled “multiply by molar mass, M” (and the reverse arrow “divide by M”). The number of moles, n, sits in the middle of every mole calculation, acting as the essential stepping-stone between a countable number of particles and a measurable mass.

Empirical and Molecular Formulas

Empirical vs molecular formulaThe empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element present in one molecule of the compound. The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula (the multiple can be 1, in which case the two formulas are identical).

The formulas of all ionic compounds (a metal combined with a non-metal) are always empirical formulas. For covalent (molecular) compounds, this is not guaranteed: benzene, C6H6, has the empirical formula CH, and dinitrogen tetroxide, N2O4, has the empirical formula NO2, because in each case the atom ratio can be simplified further. Propane, C3H8, and lead nitrate, Pb(NO3)2, are both already in their simplest ratio, so for these compounds the empirical and molecular formulas are the same.

Finding an empirical formula from masses or percentage composition

The method is the same whether you start from measured masses of each element or from percentage composition by mass (percentages are simply the mass, in grams, present in a 100 g sample):

  • Convert the mass (or percentage mass) of each element to moles by dividing by its molar mass, M.
  • Divide every mole value by the smallest of the mole values, to give a ratio starting from 1.
  • If necessary, multiply every value in the ratio by a small whole number (2, 3, …) until all values are close enough to whole numbers to round sensibly.
moles of element = mass of element (g) ÷ molar mass of element (g mol−1)
Worked example

A sample of urea contains 1.120 g N, 0.161 g H, 0.480 g C and 0.640 g O. Find its empirical formula. (Ar(N) = 14.01, Ar(H) = 1.01, Ar(C) = 12.01, Ar(O) = 16.00)
Moles: n(N) = 1.120 ÷ 14.01 = 0.0800 mol; n(H) = 0.161 ÷ 1.01 = 0.159 mol; n(C) = 0.480 ÷ 12.01 = 0.0400 mol; n(O) = 0.640 ÷ 16.00 = 0.0400 mol.
Divide every value by the smallest (0.0400): N: 0.0800 ÷ 0.0400 = 2.00; H: 0.159 ÷ 0.0400 = 3.98 ≈ 4.00; C: 0.0400 ÷ 0.0400 = 1.00; O: 0.0400 ÷ 0.0400 = 1.00.
Ratio N : H : C : O = 2 : 4 : 1 : 1, so the empirical formula of urea is N2H4CO, usually written CO(NH2)2.

Worked example (percentage composition, with a subtraction step)

The mineral celestine is 47.70% Sr and 17.46% S by mass, with the remainder oxygen. Find its empirical formula. (Ar(Sr) = 87.62, Ar(S) = 32.07, Ar(O) = 16.00)
First find % O by subtracting from 100: % O = 100 − (47.70 + 17.46) = 34.84%.
Treat the percentages as grams in a 100 g sample and convert to moles: n(Sr) = 47.70 ÷ 87.62 = 0.5443 mol; n(S) = 17.46 ÷ 32.07 = 0.5444 mol; n(O) = 34.84 ÷ 16.00 = 2.178 mol.
Divide by the smallest (0.5443): Sr = 1.000; S = 1.000; O = 2.178 ÷ 0.5443 = 4.001 ≈ 4.
The empirical formula is SrSO4.

When the ratio isn’t close to a whole numberSometimes dividing by the smallest value gives a ratio like 1 : 1.5, which is not close enough to a whole number to round directly. In that case, multiply every term in the ratio by the smallest integer that clears the fraction: 1 : 1.5, multiplied by 2, becomes 2 : 3, giving an empirical formula such as Fe2O3. Common fractions to watch for are 0.5 (×2), 0.33 or 0.67 (×3), and 0.25 or 0.75 (×4).

From empirical formula to percentage composition

The reverse calculation — finding the percentage by mass of each element from a known formula — is useful for interpreting labels on fertilizers, foods and medicines.

% by mass of element = (total mass of that element’s atoms in the formula ÷ Mr of the compound) × 100
Worked example

Find the percentage by mass of N, H and O in ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3. (Mr = 80.06, calculated earlier)
% N = (14.01 × 2 ÷ 80.06) × 100 = (28.02 ÷ 80.06) × 100 = 35.00%
% H = (1.01 × 4 ÷ 80.06) × 100 = (4.04 ÷ 80.06) × 100 = 5.05%
% O = (16.00 × 3 ÷ 80.06) × 100 = (48.00 ÷ 80.06) × 100 = 59.96% (or, more simply, by subtraction: 100 − 35.00 − 5.05 = 59.95%, matching within rounding)
Checking, the three percentages add up to almost exactly 100%, which is always a useful check of this type of calculation.

Molecular formula from empirical formula and molar mass

The empirical formula alone does not tell us how many atoms are in an actual molecule — only their simplest ratio. If the molar mass of the compound is also known, the molecular formula can be found because it must be a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

(mass of the empirical formula) × x = M,  where x is a positive integer

Here, the “mass of the empirical formula” means the Mr calculated from the empirical formula itself, and M is the actual (experimentally measured) molar mass of the compound. Solving for x tells you how many empirical-formula units make up one molecule.

Worked example

Calomel has the empirical formula HgCl and a molar mass of 472.08 g mol−1. Find its molecular formula. (Ar(Hg) = 200.59, Ar(Cl) = 35.45)
Mass of empirical formula = 200.59 + 35.45 = 236.04 g mol−1.
(236.04) × x = 472.08, so x = 472.08 ÷ 236.04 = 2.
The molecular formula is Hg2Cl2.

Combustion analysis

Combustion analysis rarely gives the mass of each element directly. Instead, a compound containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen is burned completely, converting all the carbon to CO2 and all the hydrogen to H2O; the masses of these products are measured, and the mass of carbon and hydrogen in the original sample is worked backward from them. Any oxygen in the original compound is then found by subtracting the mass of C and H from the total sample mass (since all of the sample’s mass must be accounted for by C, H and O).

Worked example

A 0.5438 g sample containing only C, H and O is burned completely, producing 1.0390 g of CO2 and 0.6369 g of H2O. Its molar mass is 46.08 g mol−1. Find its empirical and molecular formulas. (Ar(C) = 12.01, Ar(H) = 1.01, Ar(O) = 16.00)
First find the moles of CO2 and H2O produced: M(CO2) = 12.01 + (16.00 × 2) = 44.01 g mol−1, so n(CO2) = 1.0390 ÷ 44.01 = 0.02361 mol ⇒ 0.02361 mol C atoms (one C per CO2). M(H2O) = (1.01 × 2) + 16.00 = 18.02 g mol−1, so n(H2O) = 0.6369 ÷ 18.02 = 0.03534 mol ⇒ 0.03534 × 2 = 0.07068 mol H atoms (two H per H2O).
Convert these moles of atoms back to mass: mass C = 0.02361 mol × 12.01 g mol−1 = 0.2836 g; mass H = 0.07068 mol × 1.01 g mol−1 = 0.07139 g.
Mass of O by subtraction: 0.5438 − (0.2836 + 0.07139) = 0.5438 − 0.3550 = 0.1888 g, so n(O) = 0.1888 ÷ 16.00 = 0.01180 mol.
Divide every mole value by the smallest (0.01180): C: 0.02361 ÷ 0.01180 = 2.00; H: 0.07068 ÷ 0.01180 = 5.99 ≈ 6.00; O: 0.01180 ÷ 0.01180 = 1.00.
Empirical formula = C2H6O, with mass of empirical formula = (12.01 × 2) + (1.01 × 6) + 16.00 = 24.02 + 6.06 + 16.00 = 46.08 g mol−1. Since this already equals the given molar mass, x = 46.08 ÷ 46.08 = 1, so the molecular formula is also C2H6O.

Molar Concentration

A solution is a homogeneous mixture in which a solute is dissolved in a solvent (the substance present in the greatest quantity). For a solution, the “amount” of dissolved substance is most usefully expressed as its concentration.

Molar concentration, cThe molar concentration of a solution is the amount of solute (in moles) per unit volume of solution (in dm3). It has the unit mol dm−3, and is conventionally written using square brackets, e.g. [HCl] = 1.0 mol dm−3. Concentration is always defined relative to the volume of the final solution, not the volume of solvent added, since dissolving a solute can itself change the total volume.
c = n ÷ V   or, rearranged,   n = c × V

Here c is the molar concentration (mol dm−3), n is the amount of solute (mol), and V is the volume of solution (dm3, remembering that 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3, so a volume in cm3 must be divided by 1000 before use in this equation).

Worked example

What volume of 0.400 mol dm−3 NaCl(aq) contains 0.250 mol of NaCl?
Rearranging n = cV: V = n ÷ c = 0.250 mol ÷ 0.400 mol dm−3 = 0.625 dm3.
Converting to cm3: 0.625 dm3 × 1000 = 625 cm3.

Concentration can also be expressed in grams per dm3 of solution rather than moles per dm3. Molar mass converts between the two.

concentration (g dm−3) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm3)
Worked example

A solution of sodium carbonate, Na2CO3, has a concentration of 4.24 g dm−3. Find its molar concentration. (Ar(Na) = 22.99, Ar(C) = 12.01, Ar(O) = 16.00)
M(Na2CO3) = (22.99 × 2) + 12.01 + (16.00 × 3) = 45.98 + 12.01 + 48.00 = 105.99 g mol−1.
A 4.24 g dm−3 solution contains 4.24 g of solute per dm3, so n per dm3 = m ÷ M = 4.24 ÷ 105.99 = 0.0400 mol.
So [Na2CO3] = 0.0400 mol dm−3.

To prepare a standard solution (one of accurately known concentration), a precisely measured mass of solute is dissolved in some solvent, transferred to a volumetric flask, and the solvent (always distilled water, never tap water) is added carefully until the liquid level reaches the calibration mark on the flask.

Worked example

Describe how to prepare 100 cm3 of 0.10 mol dm−3 NaCl(aq), and state the mass of NaCl needed. (Ar(Na) = 22.99, Ar(Cl) = 35.45)
First convert the volume to dm3: 100 cm3 ÷ 1000 = 0.100 dm3.
n = c × V = 0.10 mol dm−3 × 0.100 dm3 = 0.0100 mol.
M(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g mol−1, so mass = n × M = 0.0100 mol × 58.44 g mol−1 = 0.584 g.
Method: weigh out 0.584 g of solid NaCl, transfer it into a 100 cm3 volumetric flask, add distilled water and swirl to dissolve, then carefully add more distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus sits exactly on the calibration mark.

Dilution

Diluting a solution adds more solvent without adding or removing any solute, so the number of moles of solute stays the same before and after dilution — only the volume (and therefore the concentration) changes.

c1V1 = c2V2

c1 and V1 are the concentration and volume before dilution; c2 and V2 are the concentration and volume after dilution. Because volume appears on both sides, any consistent unit of volume (cm3 or dm3) can be used without conversion, as the units cancel.

Worked example

75 cm3 of 0.40 mol dm−3 HCl is diluted to a total volume of 300 cm3. Find the new concentration.
c1V1 = c2V2: (0.40 mol dm−3)(75 cm3) = c2(300 cm3).
c2 = (0.40 × 75) ÷ 300 = 30 ÷ 300 = 0.10 mol dm−3.
The volume increased by a factor of 4 (75 to 300 cm3), so the concentration correctly decreased by the same factor of 4 (0.40 to 0.10 mol dm−3).

Repeating a dilution by the same factor several times in a row is called a serial dilution, and is often used to generate a set of standard solutions (for example 1.00, 0.100, 0.0100, 0.00100 mol dm−3) for building a calibration curve, where a measured property such as absorbance is plotted against known concentration and then used to find the concentration of an unknown sample.

Serial dilution by pipetteA stock solution in a flask, from which a pipette transfers exactly 10 cm3 into an empty 100 cm3 volumetric flask; the flask is then filled to the mark with distilled water to make solution 1, which is ten times more dilute than the stock. Repeating this process — taking 10 cm3 of solution 1 and diluting it again to 100 cm3 — produces solution 2, a further ten times more dilute, and so on, generating a chain of solutions each exactly one tenth the concentration of the one before it.

Avogadro’s Law

Avogadro’s lawEqual volumes of all gases, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of particles (molecules). Equivalently, equal numbers of gas particles, at the same temperature and pressure, occupy equal volumes. Using V for volume and n for amount in moles, this means V is directly proportional to n: V ∝ n.

This might seem surprising — a bromine molecule, Br2, is far heavier and larger than a hydrogen molecule, H2 — but in a gas the particles are spread far apart with negligible attractive forces between them, so almost all of a gas’s volume is empty space. Since the identity of the gas barely matters to how much space it occupies, gas volume depends only on the number of particles present (and on temperature and pressure), not on what the gas actually is. This is one of the basic assumptions of the ideal gas model, explored further in Structure 1.5.

Because equal numbers of moles of any gas occupy equal volumes under the same conditions, the mole ratios in a balanced chemical equation translate directly into gas volume ratios — without needing to know the actual number of moles at all.

Worked example

40 cm3 of carbon monoxide reacts completely with excess oxygen: 2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g). What volume of oxygen is used, and what volume of carbon dioxide is produced (all volumes at the same temperature and pressure)?
The mole ratio CO : O2 : CO2 is 2 : 1 : 2, so the volume ratio must be the same: 2 : 1 : 2.
Scaling so that 2 parts corresponds to 40 cm3 (1 part = 20 cm3): volume of O2 used = 1 × 20 = 20 cm3; volume of CO2 produced = 2 × 20 = 40 cm3.

Worked example (finding a molecular formula from gas volumes)

10 cm3 of a gaseous hydrocarbon CxHy is burned completely in excess oxygen, producing 30 cm3 of CO2 and 30 cm3 of water vapour, all measured at the same temperature and pressure. Find the molecular formula of the hydrocarbon.
The volume ratio hydrocarbon : CO2 : H2O is 10 : 30 : 30, which simplifies (divide by 10) to 1 : 3 : 3. This is also the mole ratio, so 1 molecule of hydrocarbon produces 3 molecules of CO2 (giving 3 C atoms per hydrocarbon molecule) and 3 molecules of H2O (giving 3 × 2 = 6 H atoms per hydrocarbon molecule).
The molecular formula is C3H6.

Because gas volume depends only on the number of moles present (at fixed temperature and pressure), and not on molar mass, two flasks of equal volume filled with different gases under the same conditions always contain the same number of moles — but not the same mass, since mass also depends on the molar mass of each gas.

Guiding Question Revisited

How do we quantify matter on the atomic scale?

  • One mole of any substance contains the Avogadro number, 6.02 × 1023, of elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, or any other specified particle).
  • The Avogadro constant, NA, interconverts the number of moles and the number of entities: N = n × NA.
  • The molar mass, M, of a substance is its Ar or Mr expressed in g mol−1, and interconverts moles and mass: n = m ÷ M.
  • The empirical formula (simplest whole-number ratio of atoms) can be found from percentage composition or masses by converting to moles and dividing by the smallest value; the molecular formula (actual number of atoms per molecule) is then found using the molar mass.
  • Molar concentration expresses moles of solute per volume of solution: c = n ÷ V, with dilutions following c1V1 = c2V2.
  • Avogadro’s law states that equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of moles, so gas volume ratios in a reaction match the mole ratios in the balanced equation.
