R3.2.1 – Defining Oxidation and Reduction

Guiding questionWhat happens when electrons are transferred?

The term oxidation was originally used to describe reactions where oxygen was gained. Magnesium burns in oxygen to form magnesium oxide, an ionic solid of Mg2+ and O2− ions:

2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s)

Each magnesium atom loses two electrons to become Mg2+. Magnesium undergoes exactly the same electron loss when it reacts with chlorine, even though no oxygen is involved:

Mg(s) + Cl2(g) → MgCl2(s)

This shows that the meaning of oxidation can be broadened beyond reactions with oxygen: oxidation is fundamentally the loss of electrons. Reduction traditionally meant the extraction of a metal from its ore. When magnesium reduces copper(II) oxide to copper metal, the copper ions gain electrons to form copper atoms, so reduction is the gain of electrons:

Mg(s) + CuO(s) → MgO(s) + Cu(s)

Because the magnesium loses electrons while the copper ions gain them, oxidation and reduction always occur together — this is why these reactions are called redox reactions (a combination of reduction and oxidation).

OILRIGOxidation Is Loss (of electrons), Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Oxidation and reduction in terms of hydrogen

Hydrogen sulfide can react with oxygen in two different ways:

H2S(aq) + 2O2(g) → H2SO4(aq)
2H2S(aq) + O2(g) → 2S(s) + H2O(l)

In the second reaction the hydrogen sulfide has clearly been oxidized (it has reacted with oxygen), yet it has lost hydrogen rather than gained oxygen. Since hydrogen removed from a compound is so often used to form water, the definition of oxidation is extended again to include the removal of hydrogen; reduction is correspondingly the addition of hydrogen. For example, chlorine is reduced when it reacts with hydrogen: H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g).

Oxidation and reduction in terms of oxidation state

For reactions that do not involve ions, such as H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl, it is hard to talk about electron loss directly, since no full electron transfer occurs. The oxidation state is the charge an atom would have if the compound were fully ionic. In H2 and Cl2 both elements have an oxidation state of 0, since the H−H and Cl−Cl bonds are non-polar. In the product HCl, the polar bond gives H an oxidation state of +1 and Cl an oxidation state of −1. Since chlorine's oxidation state has decreased, chlorine has been reduced; since hydrogen's oxidation state has increased, hydrogen has been oxidized.

Oxidation states are the most general and sensitive of these definitions, since they can be applied to any reaction, whether or not it involves ions, oxygen or hydrogen — they measure a shift in electron density from one atom to another.

R3.2.1 – Oxidation States

An oxidation state (or oxidation number) is the charge an atom would have if the compound it is part of were composed entirely of ions. In practice, oxidation states are assigned using a consistent set of conventions: an atom of an uncombined element has an oxidation state of 0; a monatomic ion has an oxidation state equal to its charge; hydrogen is usually +1 and oxygen is usually −2; and the oxidation states in a species must sum to the overall charge on that species (zero for a neutral compound).

Worked example

Assign oxidation states to the metal ion in (a) [Cu(NH3)6]2+ and (b) [Cr(NH3)4Cl2]+.

(a) NH3 is a neutral ligand, so the charge on the whole complex equals the charge on the metal ion: Cu = +2.

(b) Cl has a 1− charge, so Cr + (2 × −1) = +1, giving Cr = +3.

Worked example

Use oxidation states to identify which species is oxidized and which is reduced: 2MnO4−(aq) + 6H+(aq) + 5SO32−(aq) → 2Mn2+(aq) + 3H2O(l) + 5SO42−(aq).

Mn goes from +7 in MnO4− to +2 in Mn2+, a decrease, so MnO4− is reduced. S goes from +4 in SO32− to +6 in SO42−, an increase, so SO32− is oxidized.

DisproportionationA disproportionation reaction occurs when the oxidation state of the same element both increases and decreases in the same reaction. For example, in Cl2(g) + H2O(l) → HCl(aq) + HOCl(aq), the oxidation state of one Cl atom increases from 0 to +1 (oxidized) while the other decreases from 0 to −1 (reduced), so chlorine is simultaneously oxidized and reduced.

Average and fractional oxidation states

When a molecule contains more than one atom of the same element in different environments, the oxidation state calculated from the overall formula is an average value, and this average can be a non-integer.

n(H) × (+1) + n(C) × x = 0, solved for the average oxidation state x of carbon
Worked example

Assign an oxidation state to carbon in C3H8 (propane) and comment on the answer.

Assigning H = +1: 8 × (+1) + 3C = 0, so C = −8/3 = −2⅔. This is a fractional value because it is an average across three non-equivalent carbon atoms: the two terminal (CH3) carbons each have an oxidation state of −3 (each bonded to 3 H and 1 C), while the central (CH2) carbon has an oxidation state of −2 (bonded to 2 H and 2 C). The average is (2 × (−3) + (−2))/3 = −8/3 = −2⅔, as expected.

A limitation of oxidation statesUnlike ionic charge, oxidation states have little direct structural or physical significance — the oxidation state of carbon in CO2 is +4, even though carbon dioxide is a covalently bonded molecule, not an ionic compound. Oxidation states are a useful bookkeeping tool for tracking electron distribution during redox reactions, but they are a formal construct rather than real physical charges.

R3.2.1 – Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

In any redox reaction, the reactant that accepts electrons brings about oxidation of the other reactant, and is itself reduced — this reactant is called the oxidizing agent. The reactant that supplies electrons brings about reduction of the other reactant, and is itself oxidized — this reactant is called the reducing agent.

Oxidizing and reducing agentsThe substance that is reduced is the oxidizing agent. The substance that is oxidized is the reducing agent.
Worked example

When acidified potassium dichromate(VI), K2Cr2O7(aq), is mixed with iron(II) chloride, FeCl2(aq), Fe3+(aq) and Cr3+(aq) are produced. Identify the oxidizing and reducing agents.

Fe goes from +2 to +3 (oxidized), so FeCl2(aq) is the reducing agent. Cr goes from +6 to +3 (reduced), so K2Cr2O7(aq) is the oxidizing agent.

Common oxidizing agents include O2, O3, acidified MnO4−, acidified Cr2O72−, F2, Cl2, concentrated HNO3, and H2O2. Common reducing agents include H2, C, CO, SO2, and reactive metals.

Water can be either an oxidizing agent or a reducing agentWith reactive metals such as sodium, water is reduced to hydrogen and so acts as an oxidizing agent: 2H2O(l) + 2Na(s) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g) (O stays −2, H goes from +1 to 0, reduction of H). With fluorine, water is oxidized to oxygen and so acts as a reducing agent: 2H2O(l) + 2F2(g) → 4HF(aq) + O2(g) (O goes from −2 to 0, oxidation). This dual ability makes water a useful solvent for many redox reactions.

Oxidation numbers in systematic (IUPAC) naming

Oxidation numbers are used in IUPAC nomenclature to name compounds unambiguously: a Roman numeral corresponding to the oxidation state is inserted in brackets, with no space, after the name of the relevant element.

Name PbO2, Cr(OH)3 and KMnO4 using oxidation numbers.

R3.2.2 – Writing and Balancing Half-Equations

It is often useful to separate a redox equation into two half-equations, one for oxidation and one for reduction, which emphasize the electron transfer taking place. Electrons appear explicitly in both half-equations to balance the charge.

Worked example

Deduce the two half-equations for Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s).

Zn goes from 0 to +2 (oxidized); Cu2+ goes from +2 to 0 (reduced).

oxidation: Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−

reduction: Cu2+(aq) + 2e− → Cu(s)

The two half-equations have equal numbers of electrons, so they cancel when added together.

Steps for deducing a balanced redox equation
  1. Assign oxidation states to determine which elements are oxidized and which are reduced.
  2. Write separate half-equations for oxidation and reduction. Balance the atoms other than H and O, and add electrons to account for the change in oxidation state.
  3. Balance each half-equation for O by adding H2O as needed.
  4. Balance each half-equation for H by adding H+ as needed.
  5. Check that each half-equation is balanced for both atoms and charge.
  6. Equalize the number of electrons in the two half-equations by finding a common multiple and multiplying each equation appropriately.
  7. Add the two half-equations together, cancelling anything common to both sides, including the electrons.
Worked example

Deduce the equation for the reaction of NO3− and Cu in acidic solution to give NO and Cu2+.

Cu is oxidized (0 → +2) and N is reduced (+5 → +2).

oxidation: Cu(s) → Cu2+(aq) + 2e−

reduction (electrons and atoms balanced step by step): NO3−(aq) + 4H+(aq) + 3e− → NO(g) + 2H2O(l)

The lowest common multiple of 2 and 3 electrons is 6, so the oxidation equation is multiplied by 3 and the reduction equation by 2:

3Cu(s) → 3Cu2+(aq) + 6e− and 2NO3−(aq) + 8H+(aq) + 6e− → 2NO(g) + 4H2O(l)

Adding and cancelling the electrons gives the final, balanced equation:

3Cu(s) + 2NO3−(aq) + 8H+(aq) → 3Cu2+(aq) + 2NO(g) + 4H2O(l)

R3.2.2 – Redox Titrations

Redox titrations determine an unknown concentration by finding the volume of a standard solution needed to react exactly, via electron transfer, with a measured volume of another solution — the equivalence point. They use a burette and pipette in the same way as acid–base titrations, and the calculation is based on the balanced equation for the redox reaction, deduced from the half-equations.

The iodine–thiosulfate method

Several redox titrations use an oxidizing agent (such as KMnO4, KIO3, K2Cr2O7, or NaOCl) to convert excess iodide ions to iodine, which is then titrated against standard sodium thiosulfate solution:

2S2O32−(aq) + I2(aq) → 2I−(aq) + S4O62−(aq)

Starch indicator is added just before the endpoint: it forms a deep blue complex with free I2, and the blue colour disappears sharply as the last of the I2 is reduced to colourless I−, giving a very distinct endpoint.

Worked example

A 10.00 cm3 sample of bleach, containing OCl−(aq), was reacted with excess iodide ions: OCl−(aq) + 2I−(aq) + 2H+(aq) → I2(aq) + Cl−(aq) + H2O(l). The liberated iodine required 38.65 cm3 of 0.0200 mol dm−3 Na2S2O3(aq) to titrate. Determine [OCl−] in the bleach.

n(S2O32−) = cV = 0.0200 mol dm−3 × (38.65/1000) dm3 = 7.73 × 10−4 mol.

Ratio S2O32− : I2 = 2 : 1, so n(I2) = 7.73 × 10−4 × 0.5 = 3.865 × 10−4 mol.

This is a back-titration — the I2 was liberated by OCl− reacting with I− in a 1 : 1 ratio, so n(OCl−) = n(I2) = 3.865 × 10−4 mol.

[OCl−] = n/V = (3.865 × 10−4 mol)/(10.00/1000 dm3) = 0.0387 mol dm−3.

Analysis of iron with manganate(VII)

Acidified KMnO4 oxidizes Fe2+ to Fe3+ while MnO4− is reduced to Mn2+. This titration is self-indicating: deep purple MnO4− reacts to form nearly colourless Mn2+, so the solution stays colourless until all the Fe2+ is used up, at which point the first permanent trace of purple colour marks the endpoint — no external indicator is needed.

5Fe2+(aq) + MnO4−(aq) + 8H+(aq) → 5Fe3+(aq) + Mn2+(aq) + 4H2O(l)
Worked example

All the iron in a 2.000 g tablet was dissolved to give Fe2+(aq), which required 27.50 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 KMnO4(aq) to reach equivalence. Calculate the percentage by mass of iron in the tablet.

n(MnO4−) = 0.100 mol dm−3 × (27.50/1000) dm3 = 0.00275 mol.

Ratio MnO4− : Fe2+ = 1 : 5, so n(Fe2+) = 0.00275 × 5 = 0.01375 mol.

m(Fe) = n × M = 0.01375 mol × 55.85 g mol−1 = 0.768 g.

% Fe = (0.768 g / 2.000 g) × 100 = 38.4%.

Titration calculation strategyAlways start with the reactant for which both the reacting volume and the concentration are known, and use n = cV to find its number of moles first. Then use the mole ratio from the balanced equation to find the moles, and hence any other quantity, of the second reactant.

R3.2.3 – Trends in Ease of Oxidation and Reduction

More reactive non-metals are stronger oxidizing agents

The halogens (group 17) react by gaining electrons to form negative ions, so they act as oxidizing agents. This tendency decreases down the group, giving the trend:

Oxidizing strength of the halogensF2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2, from strongest oxidizing agent (most readily reduced) to weakest (least readily reduced).

Adding a more reactive halogen to a solution of a less reactive halide displaces the less reactive halogen, because the stronger oxidizing agent ‘wins’ the competition for electrons:

Cl2(aq) + 2I−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + I2(aq)

Adding a less reactive halogen to the ions of a more reactive halogen gives no reaction: I2(aq) + 2Cl−(aq) → no reaction. More generally: more reactive non-metals oxidize the ions of less reactive non-metals.

More reactive metals are stronger reducing agents

Metals act as reducing agents by losing electrons to form positive ions; more reactive metals lose electrons more readily and so are stronger reducing agents. Adding zinc metal to aqueous copper sulfate causes a visible reaction: the blue colour fades, pink/brown copper metal appears, and the temperature rises, as the more reactive zinc reduces Cu2+(aq) while it is itself oxidized:

Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)

There is no reaction if copper metal is added to a zinc ion solution, because copper is not a strong enough reducing agent to force electrons onto Zn2+(aq). Repeating such displacement reactions between different metals and their ions builds up the activity series, a list of relative reducing strengths that predicts whether a metal/metal-ion redox reaction will be feasible.

The activity series (a representative section)A horizontal list of metals arranged from strongest to weakest reducing agent, left to right: Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, (H), Cu, Ag. The metal furthest left (Mg) is the strongest reducing agent and is most readily oxidized; the metal furthest right (Ag) is the weakest reducing agent and is least readily oxidized. Hydrogen is inserted between lead and copper, since dilute acids release hydrogen when reacted with metals above hydrogen in the series (such as zinc or iron) but not with metals below it (such as copper or silver). This exact list does not need to be memorized — the skill being tested is interpreting activity-series data supplied in a question, not recalling the order from memory.
Worked example

Predict whether the following reactions occur, using the activity series Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > Cu > Ag (strongest to weakest reducing agent).

(a) ZnCl2(aq) + 2Ag(s) → 2AgCl(s) + Zn(s): this needs Ag to reduce Zn2+, but Ag is a weaker reducing agent than Zn, so this will not occur.

(b) 2FeCl3(aq) + 3Mg(s) → 3MgCl2(aq) + 2Fe(s): Mg is a stronger reducing agent than Fe, so this reaction will occur.

Carbon can also be fitted into this activity series by similar displacement reactions: it is able to reduce the oxides of iron and of metals below iron in the series, which is the basis for extracting these metals industrially using carbon as a cheap reducing agent.

R3.2.4 – Oxidation of Metals by Acids

Reactive metals react with dilute acids to form a salt and release hydrogen gas, since the metal reduces H+ ions to H2:

2HCl(aq) + Zn(s) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)
H2SO4(aq) + Fe(s) → FeSO4(aq) + H2(g)

Written as an ionic equation, showing only the species that change: 2H+(aq) + Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + H2(g). Zinc is oxidized from 0 to +2, acting as the reducing agent, and has the reducing strength to force H+ ions to accept electrons. Since copper does not react with dilute acids, hydrogen must be a stronger reducing agent than copper, but a weaker reducing agent than zinc — this places hydrogen between lead and copper in the activity series. Metals such as sodium and potassium, high in the activity series, react violently and dangerously with dilute acids, while metals low in the series such as silver and gold do not react at all — this resistance to corrosion by acids is part of what makes these metals valuable.

Why acids corrode reactive metalsBecause reactive metals reduce H+(aq) ions, dilute acids are corrosive towards any metal above hydrogen in the activity series — this is why, for example, car battery acid must be kept away from the reactive metal parts of a car’s bodywork.

R3.2.5 – Comparing Voltaic and Electrolytic Cells

Voltaic and electrolytic cells are collectively known as electrochemical cells. A voltaic (galvanic) cell generates electricity from a spontaneous redox reaction, converting chemical energy to electrical energy. An electrolytic cell uses an external source of electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction, converting electrical energy to chemical energy — this is the process of electrolysis, which can break stable compounds such as sodium chloride down into their reactive elements.

R3.2.6 – Primary (Voltaic) Cells

If the oxidation and reduction half-reactions of a spontaneous redox reaction are physically separated, the electrons must pass between them through an external circuit, generating an electric current. This arrangement is a voltaic (or galvanic) cell. Because the chemicals are consumed and not renewed, this is also called a primary cell; a battery normally consists of several cells connected together.

Half-cells and electrode potentials

A simple half-cell consists of a strip of metal dipped into a solution of its own ions. In a zinc half-cell, zinc atoms release electrons to form Zn2+ ions, making the metal surface negatively charged relative to the solution, until an equilibrium is reached:

Zn(s) ⇌ Zn2+(aq) + 2e−

The more reactive the metal, the further this equilibrium favours ion formation, and the more negative the charge that builds up on the electrode. Copper is less reactive than zinc, so its equilibrium favours the metal side, leaving the copper electrode less negatively charged than the zinc electrode.

A zinc–copper voltaic cellTwo separate beakers, connected by a salt bridge (a glass tube or strip of absorbent paper soaked in an aqueous ion solution, such as KNO3, that does not interfere with the electrode reactions) and by an external wire with a voltmeter in the circuit. The left beaker holds a strip of zinc metal in zinc sulfate solution; the right beaker holds a strip of copper metal in copper sulfate solution. At the zinc electrode (the anode, negative), zinc atoms are oxidized to Zn2+ ions, releasing electrons into the wire. Electrons flow through the external wire and voltmeter from the zinc electrode to the copper electrode (the cathode, positive), where Cu2+ ions in solution are reduced to copper metal, which deposits on the electrode. Inside the salt bridge, anions drift towards the zinc half-cell and cations drift towards the copper half-cell, neutralizing the charge that would otherwise build up in each beaker as ions are consumed or produced. The voltmeter registers a steady potential difference, for example 1.10 V for this particular zinc–copper cell.
Electron and ion movement in a voltaic cell
  • The anode is the negative electrode (oxidation) and the cathode is the positive electrode (reduction).
  • Electrons flow from anode to cathode through the external circuit.
  • Anions migrate through the salt bridge from the cathode side to the anode side.
  • Cations migrate through the salt bridge from the anode side to the cathode side (anions and cations move in opposite directions to each other, and anions move opposite to the direction of the electrons in the external wire).
  • Without a salt bridge the circuit is incomplete and no current, and no voltage, is generated.

Cell diagram convention

A cell diagram summarizes a voltaic cell using a compact notation: a single vertical line (|) represents a phase boundary, such as between a solid electrode and its solution, and a double vertical line (||) represents the salt bridge. By convention the anode is written on the left and the cathode on the right, so that electrons conceptually flow left to right; ions in the same phase that appear together in one half-cell are separated by a comma, and spectator ions are omitted.

Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq) || Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s)

Different half-cell combinations give different voltages

Any two half-cells can be combined; the direction of electron flow and the size of the voltage produced depend on the difference in reducing strength between the two metals, which can usually be judged from their relative positions in the activity series. A zinc–silver cell, Zn(s) + 2Ag+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + 2Ag(s), produces a larger voltage than the zinc–copper cell, because the difference in reactivity between zinc and silver is greater than between zinc and copper. In a copper–silver cell, Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) → Cu2+(aq) + 2Ag(s), electron flow is now away from copper (the anode, the more reactive of the two metals) and towards silver (the cathode) — copper is now the negative electrode because it has the lower reducing power relative to silver, even though it was previously the positive electrode when paired with zinc. Which electrode is positive or negative therefore always depends on which two half-cells are paired together.

Nature of scienceLuigi Galvani observed that a dead frog’s leg muscles twitched on contact with two different metals, and proposed a biological ‘electrical fluid’. Alessandro Volta instead reasoned that the electricity came from the contact between the two different metals themselves, and confirmed this by building the ‘voltaic pile’ from alternating metal discs, generating the first steady electric current from a battery. Both galvanic cell and voltaic cell are used today to honour their contributions — a reminder that chance observations can drive scientific discovery, but only when their significance is recognized and followed up.

R3.2.7 – Secondary (Rechargeable) Cells

Primary cells vs secondary cellsA primary cell (or battery) produces electricity from a reaction that is not reversible — once the reactants are consumed, the cell is dead. A secondary cell (or battery) uses electrode reactions that can be reversed by supplying electrical energy, so it can be recharged, giving it a longer usable life than a primary cell.

The lithium-ion battery

The lithium-ion battery benefits from lithium’s low density and high reactivity, allowing it to store a large amount of electrical energy per unit mass. Because lithium metal is very reactive, the lithium cathode is placed within a metal oxide lattice (such as MnO2) and the lithium anode is mixed with graphite, with a non-aqueous polymer-based electrolyte, to prevent an insulating oxide layer forming and reducing contact with the electrolyte.

The lithium-ion batteryA sealed cell with two electrodes separated by a polymer electrolyte. The negative electrode is lithium metal embedded in graphite; the positive electrode is LixMnO2, lithium ions held within a manganese oxide lattice. During discharge, Li+ ions move through the polymer electrolyte from the graphite/lithium electrode towards the metal-oxide electrode, while electrons travel the long way round through the external circuit, from the negative to the positive electrode, to do useful electrical work.

At the negative electrode, lithium is oxidized: Li(polymer) → Li+(polymer) + e−. At the positive electrode, Li+ and MnO2 are reduced together: Li+(polymer) + MnO2(s) + e− → LiMnO2(s). Both half-reactions simply reverse when the battery is recharged.

The nickel–cadmium battery

Worked example

In a nickel–cadmium cell with aqueous KOH electrolyte, discharging uses a nickel hydroxide positive electrode and a cadmium hydroxide negative electrode. During charging, a Cd(OH)2 electrode is reduced to the element, and a Ni(OH)2 electrode is oxidized to NiO(OH). Deduce the equation for the charging process.

Reduction (charging): Cd(OH)2(s) + 2e− → Cd(s) + 2OH−(aq).

Oxidation (charging): Ni(OH)2(s) + OH−(aq) → NiO(OH)(s) + H2O(l) + e−.

Multiplying the oxidation half-equation by 2 to balance the electrons, then adding and cancelling the 2e− and the 2OH− common to both sides, gives the overall charging equation:

2Ni(OH)2(s) + Cd(OH)2(s) → 2NiO(OH)(s) + 2H2O(l) + Cd(s)

Discharging is simply the reverse: 2NiO(OH)(s) + Cd(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2Ni(OH)2(s) + Cd(OH)2(s). This reaction can be reversed because both metal hydroxide products are insoluble and stay at the electrode surface rather than dispersing away into solution — a common feature of rechargeable secondary cells.

R3.2.7 – Fuel Cells, and Comparing Cell Types

Combustion reactions are redox reactions, so if the fuel and oxidant are kept physically separate, the reaction can generate an electric current instead of just heat. A fuel cell works this way but, unlike a primary voltaic cell, it requires a constant external supply of fuel and oxygen to keep producing electricity, since the reactants are continuously consumed.

The hydrogen–oxygen fuel cellHydrogen gas is fed continuously to the negative electrode and oxygen gas (often from the air) is fed continuously to the positive electrode, with the two gas streams kept apart by a membrane that only allows ions, not gas, to pass through. At the negative electrode (anode), hydrogen is oxidized, releasing electrons into the external circuit and H+ ions into the electrolyte. Electrons flow around the external circuit to the positive electrode (cathode), where they combine with oxygen and the H+ ions that have migrated across the membrane, forming water as the only waste product.
Worked example

Deduce the two half-reactions in an acidic hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.

At the negative electrode (anode), H2 is oxidized: 2H2(g) → 4H+(aq) + 4e−.

At the positive electrode (cathode), O2 is reduced: 4H+(aq) + O2(g) + 4e− → 4H2O(l).

R3.2.8 – Electrolytic Cells

An electrolytic cell reverses the process of a voltaic cell: an external source of electrical energy pumps electrons into the cell to drive oxidation and reduction reactions that would not otherwise happen spontaneously. This process, electrolysis, breaks down substances using electricity.

ElectrolyteAn electrolyte is a substance that does not conduct electricity in the solid state, but does conduct when molten or dissolved in water, because it then contains ions that are free to move. The reactant undergoing electrolysis must be an electrolyte so that current can pass between the electrodes; electrodes themselves are electrically conducting, while electrolytes are ionically conducting.
Components of an electrolytic cellA DC power source (battery), shown with a longer line for the positive terminal and a shorter line for the negative terminal, connected by wires to two electrodes dipped into the electrolyte. The electrodes are made of a conducting material, generally metal or graphite, and are described as inert when they do not themselves take part in the redox reactions occurring in the cell. The electrodes must not touch each other, or the circuit would short-circuit and bypass the electrolyte entirely. As current flows, cations in the electrolyte migrate to the negative electrode (the cathode) where they are reduced, and anions migrate to the positive electrode (the anode) where they are oxidized; the current through the electrolyte itself is carried by these migrating ions, not by free electrons.

Electrolysis of molten salts

When the electrolyte is a molten salt, the only ions present are those of the compound itself, so exactly one type of ion migrates to each electrode: positive ions are reduced and gain electrons at the negative cathode, and negative ions are oxidized and lose electrons at the positive anode.

Electrolysis of molten sodium chlorideA cell containing molten NaCl, with graphite electrodes connected to a DC power source. The battery pushes electrons towards the cathode (negative electrode), where Na+ ions in the melt are reduced to molten sodium metal, which collects at that electrode: Na+(l) + e− → Na(l). At the anode (positive electrode), Cl− ions are oxidized, releasing electrons that flow back towards the positive terminal of the power source, and forming chlorine gas that bubbles away: 2Cl−(l) → Cl2(g) + 2e−. The overall reaction, 2Na+(l) + 2Cl−(l) → 2Na(l) + Cl2(g), splits the stable compound into its two reactive elements.
Worked example

Describe the electrode reactions during electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide, and any observations.

PbBr2(l) → Pb2+(l) + 2Br−(l), with Pb2+ migrating to the cathode and Br− to the anode.

cathode (reduction): Pb2+(l) + 2e− → Pb(l) — a molten grey metal is produced.

anode (oxidation): 2Br−(l) → Br2(g) + 2e− — a brown vapour with a strong smell is produced.

Overall: Pb2+(l) + 2Br−(l) → Pb(l) + Br2(g). Lead(II) bromide has a relatively low melting point (373 °C); most other ionic compounds melt at much higher temperatures, so electrolysis of molten salts is usually carried out at high temperature.

The industrial extraction of aluminium

Reactive metals such as aluminium, lithium, magnesium, sodium and potassium exist naturally as positive ions in stable compounds (such as Al2O3 and NaCl); extracting the metal requires reducing these ions, which is most effectively done by electrolysis.

Electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide (the Hall–Héroult process)A steel tank lined internally with graphite, which itself acts as the cathode, containing a molten mixture of aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite. Graphite anodes are suspended in this molten mixture from above. Aluminium ions migrate to the graphite-lined cathode where they are reduced to molten aluminium metal, Al3+(l) + 3e− → Al(l), which is denser than the molten electrolyte and so sinks and collects as a layer on the floor of the cell, from where it is periodically siphoned off. Oxide ions migrate to the graphite anodes where they are oxidized to oxygen gas, 2O2−(l) → O2(g) + 4e−.
Why cryolite is addedPure aluminium oxide has a very high melting point and is a poor electrical conductor even when molten, both of which would make electrolysis very energy-intensive. Molten cryolite (Na3AlF6) is added to lower the melting point of the mixture and increase its conductivity, substantially reducing the energy requirements of the process. The graphite anodes must also be replaced regularly, since the oxygen produced at the anode reacts with the hot carbon of the electrode itself, slowly consuming it.

R3.2.9 – Oxidation of Alcohols

Beyond the complete or incomplete combustion of organic compounds, which breaks the carbon chain apart entirely, a more selective kind of oxidation occurs when an oxidizing agent attacks only the carbon atom of a specific functional group, leaving the rest of the carbon skeleton intact. Alcohols are commonly oxidized in the laboratory using acidified potassium dichromate(VI), K2Cr2O7(aq), a bright orange solution that is reduced to green Cr3+(aq) as the alcohol is oxidized on heating. The oxidizing agent is often written simply as ‘+[O]’ in these equations.

Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols oxidize differentlyThe degree of oxidation possible depends on the number of hydrogen atoms on the carbon bearing the −OH group.
  • Primary alcohols (two H atoms on that carbon) can be oxidized in two steps: first to an aldehyde, then, with further oxidation, to a carboxylic acid.
  • Secondary alcohols (one H atom on that carbon) are oxidized in a single step to a ketone.
  • Tertiary alcohols (no H atoms on that carbon) cannot be oxidized in this way without breaking the carbon skeleton, so they show no reaction and no colour change with acidified dichromate(VI).

Controlling the product: distillation versus reflux

For a primary alcohol, if the aldehyde product is removed from the reaction mixture as soon as it forms — by distillation — further oxidation to the carboxylic acid is prevented, since the aldehyde is not present in the mixture long enough to react further. This works because the aldehyde has a lower boiling point than either the alcohol or the acid, since (unlike the alcohol or the acid) it cannot hydrogen bond between its own molecules.

Distillation apparatusA round-bottomed flask containing the reaction mixture is heated, typically with an electric heating mantle. As the aldehyde forms and evaporates, its vapour rises into a condenser — a glass tube surrounded by an outer jacket through which cold water flows continuously. The vapour cools and condenses back to a liquid inside the condenser, which then drips down and is collected in a separate beaker or flask, physically separating the volatile aldehyde product from the hot reaction mixture as it forms.

To obtain the carboxylic acid instead, the reaction mixture is heated under reflux for a prolonged period, ensuring the intermediate aldehyde stays in contact with the oxidizing agent long enough to be oxidized further.

Reflux apparatusA round-bottomed flask containing the reaction mixture, heated (for example with an electric mantle) with a vertical condenser fitted directly above the flask, open to the air at its top. As the liquid boils, vapour rises into the condenser, where cooling water flowing around the outside condenses it back into a liquid, which then drips straight back down into the flask below. This continuous boiling and condensing keeps the reacting mixture at its boiling point for as long as needed, without any of the volatile reactants or intermediates escaping or being lost.

Worked example

Deduce the half-equations and overall equation for the oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid by acidified potassium dichromate(VI).

Average oxidation state of C in ethanol, C2H5OH: (−6 + 2)/2 = −2. Average oxidation state of C in ethanoic acid, CH3COOH: (−4 + 4)/2 = 0. Each carbon increases its oxidation state by 2, so 4 electrons are released overall:

oxidation: C2H5OH(aq) + H2O(l) → CH3COOH(aq) + 4H+(aq) + 4e−

Cr goes from +6 in Cr2O72− to +3 in Cr3+, a decrease of 3 per Cr atom, so 6 electrons are needed for the two Cr atoms:

reduction: Cr2O72−(aq) + 14H+(aq) + 6e− → 2Cr3+(aq) + 7H2O(l)

The lowest common multiple of 4 and 6 electrons is 12: the oxidation equation is multiplied by 3, and the reduction equation by 2. Adding and simplifying (cancelling 12e− and simplifying the H+ and H2O) gives the overall equation:

2Cr2O72−(aq) + 16H+(aq) + 3C2H5OH(l) → 4Cr3+(aq) + 11H2O(l) + 3CH3COOH(aq)

R3.2.10 – Reduction of Carbonyl Compounds

It is often more convenient in organic chemistry to analyze redox reactions in terms of gaining or losing oxygen or hydrogen atoms, rather than tracking electrons directly. Just as secondary alcohols lose two H atoms on oxidation to ketones, this can be reversed: ketones are reduced to secondary alcohols by adding two H atoms, and aldehydes are reduced to primary alcohols in the same way. Carboxylic acids can be reduced, via the aldehyde, all the way to the primary alcohol. These reactions are often written using ‘[+H]’ to represent reduction.

The hydride ion as the reducing speciesThe reducing agents used in these reactions supply hydrogen as H− and H+ ions. The carbonyl carbon (C=O) carries a partial positive charge, so it is attacked by the hydride ion, H−, which acts as a nucleophile because it carries a lone pair of electrons and a full negative charge.

Worked example

(a) Predict the product when pentan-2-one, CH3CH2CH2COCH3, is reduced. (b) Predict the product when butanoic acid, CH3CH2CH2COOH, is reduced.

(a) Two H atoms add across the carbonyl group of the ketone, giving pentan-2-ol, CH3CH2CH2CH(OH)CH3.

(b) The −OH of the carboxylic acid is effectively replaced by H, giving the aldehyde butanal, CH3CH2CH2CHO; with excess reducing agent the aldehyde is further reduced (H atoms add to the carbonyl group) to the primary alcohol, butan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2CH2OH.

Worked example

Deduce the half-equation for the reduction of methanoic acid, HCOOH, to methanol, CH3OH.

Oxidation state of C in HCOOH: −2 + 4 = +2 (bonded to O, O, H). Oxidation state of C in CH3OH: −4 + 2 = −2 (bonded to H, H, H, O). The carbon decreases its oxidation state by 4, so 4 electrons are needed:

HCOOH(aq) + 4H+(aq) + 4e− → CH3OH(aq) + H2O(l)

R3.2.11 – Reduction of Unsaturated Compounds

Alkenes and alkynes are unsaturated hydrocarbons that undergo addition reactions to form saturated products. While alkenes generally react by electrophilic addition, reaction with hydrogen gas itself (hydrogenation) is classified as a reduction reaction, since it adds hydrogen across the multiple bond:

CnH2n + H2 → CnH2n+2

For example, propene reacts with hydrogen in the presence of a nickel catalyst at about 150 °C: CH3CHCH2 + H2 → CH3CH2CH3. The nickel catalyst provides a surface on which the reactant molecules are held in the correct orientation, so the hydrogen atoms can add to both carbon atoms of the double bond simultaneously, after which the saturated product leaves the surface.

Hydrogenation is not hydrationHydrogenation (addition of H2, reducing a C=C double bond to a C−C single bond) must not be confused with hydration (addition of H2O across a double bond, an electrophilic addition reaction, not a reduction).

Alkynes, with a carbon–carbon triple bond, can be reduced in the same way: one molecule of H2 reduces the triple bond to a double bond (giving an alkene), and a second molecule of H2 reduces that double bond to a single bond (giving the fully saturated alkane). In general, the number of moles of H2 needed to fully saturate a molecule equals the number of carbon–carbon multiple bonds it contains.

Hydrogenation and margarineUnsaturated fatty acids contain one or more C=C double bonds, which cannot rotate and so create kinks in the hydrocarbon chain. These kinks prevent the molecules from packing closely together, weakening the intermolecular forces between them and lowering their melting point, so unsaturated fats tend to be liquid oils at room temperature. In the margarine industry, hydrogenation is used to partially saturate these oils, raising their melting point so that the product is solid at room temperature — though partial hydrogenation of this kind can produce so-called trans fats, which have raised widespread health concerns.

Guiding Question Revisited

What happens when electrons are transferred?
  • Oxidation is electron loss and reduction is electron gain; the two always occur together and can be separated into half-equations. Oxidation and reduction can equivalently be tracked by an increase or decrease in oxidation state.
  • The oxidizing agent is the species that is reduced; the reducing agent is the species that is oxidized. More reactive non-metals are stronger oxidizing agents, and more reactive metals are stronger reducing agents; these trends define the activity series.
  • When electron transfer in a spontaneous reaction is forced through an external circuit, it produces an electric current: this is a voltaic cell. Electrons flow from anode to cathode in the external circuit, and ions move through the electrolyte and salt bridge to complete the circuit.
  • A primary cell is used once; a secondary (rechargeable) cell has reversible electrode reactions; a fuel cell is continuously supplied with fresh reactants. In all electrochemical cells, oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction occurs at the cathode.
  • Electricity can drive non-spontaneous reactions in an electrolytic cell, breaking down stable molten compounds into their elements — the basis of extracting reactive metals such as aluminium.
  • In organic chemistry, oxidation of alcohols (addition of oxygen or removal of hydrogen) gives aldehydes, carboxylic acids or ketones depending on the class of alcohol and the conditions (distillation versus reflux); these reactions can be reversed by reduction (addition of hydrogen) using a source of hydride ions. Alkenes and alkynes are reduced by hydrogenation, adding H2 across each multiple bond.
