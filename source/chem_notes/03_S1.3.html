Guiding Question

How can we model the energy states of electrons in atoms?

An atom’s chemical behaviour is controlled by how its electrons are arranged. Since electrons cannot be observed directly inside an atom, chemists instead study the light that atoms give out (or take in) and use that evidence to build a model of where the electrons are and what energies they have. This sub-topic covers two connected ideas: first, what emission spectra are and why they prove that electrons can only have certain fixed (discrete) energies; second, the detailed rules — the Aufbau principle, Hund’s rule and the Pauli exclusion principle — used to work out the electron configuration of any atom or ion up to atomic number 36.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Visible light is only a small slice of a much larger family of waves called the electromagnetic spectrum, which ranges from long-wavelength, low-energy radio waves to short-wavelength, high-energy gamma rays. Every type of electromagnetic radiation travels through a vacuum at the same speed, the speed of light, c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1, and different types are distinguished only by their wavelength and frequency.

Wavelength and frequencyWavelength (λ) is the distance between two successive crests (or troughs) of a wave, measured in metres. Frequency (f) is the number of complete waves passing a fixed point in one second, measured in hertz (Hz, equivalent to s−1). Shorter wavelengths correspond to higher frequencies, and vice versa.
c = fλ

Here c is the speed of light (a constant, 3.00 × 108 m s−1), f is the frequency of the wave, and λ is its wavelength. Because c never changes, wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional: doubling the frequency of a wave halves its wavelength, and vice versa.

Worked example

A wave of red light has a wavelength of 700 nm (700 × 10−9 m). Calculate its frequency.
Rearranging c = fλ gives f = c ÷ λ. f = (3.00 × 108) ÷ (700 × 10−9) = 4.29 × 1014 Hz. This is the frequency of the light wave.

Moving across the electromagnetic spectrum from radio waves through microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and finally gamma rays, wavelength steadily decreases while frequency and energy steadily increase. Within visible light, red light has the longest wavelength (and lowest frequency and energy) and violet light has the shortest wavelength (and highest frequency and energy). Infrared radiation has a longer wavelength than red light, and ultraviolet radiation has a shorter wavelength than violet light.

The electromagnetic spectrumA horizontal bar running from left to right, labelled with decreasing wavelength (and increasing energy) from left to right: radio waves, microwaves, infrared (IR), then a narrow visible-light band (red through orange, yellow, green, blue, to violet), then ultraviolet (UV), X-rays, and finally gamma rays at the far right with the shortest wavelength and highest energy. An arrow beneath the bar points right and is labelled “energy increasing”; wavelength values printed along the top of the bar shrink from around 108 m on the radio-wave end down to around 10−16 m on the gamma-ray end.

White light, such as sunlight, is really a mixture of all the visible wavelengths together. Passing white light through a glass prism, or through water droplets to form a rainbow, spreads it out into a continuous spectrum, in which the colours blend smoothly into one another with no gaps.

Emission and Absorption Spectra

When atoms absorb energy — for example from an electric spark passed through a gas, or from strong heating in a flame — an electron can be promoted from its normal, lowest-energy arrangement (the ground state) up to a higher-energy arrangement (an excited state). Excited states are unstable, so the electron quickly falls back down to a lower energy level. As it does so, it releases the extra energy as a single packet of electromagnetic radiation called a photon.

Ground state and excited stateThe ground state is the lowest-energy, most stable electron arrangement of an atom. An excited state is any arrangement in which one or more electrons occupy a higher energy level than in the ground state. Excited atoms are unstable and return to the ground state by emitting photons.
Emission spectrum vs absorption spectrumAn emission spectrum is produced when excited atoms fall from a higher to a lower energy level, releasing photons; it appears as bright coloured lines on a dark background. An absorption spectrum is produced when atoms in a lower energy level absorb specific wavelengths of radiation passed through them, promoting electrons to higher levels; it appears as dark lines at specific positions on an otherwise continuous, bright background. Both types of spectrum contain lines at exactly the same set of wavelengths for a given element, because both correspond to the same set of possible energy transitions.
Origin of absorption and emission spectraTwo energy-level diagrams side by side. On the left, a sample of atoms in a low energy level absorbs incoming radiation, some atoms jumping up to a higher energy level; the transmitted radiation, when passed through a spectrometer, shows a continuous rainbow band interrupted by a few dark absorption lines at the wavelengths that were absorbed. On the right, a sample of atoms already in a high energy level falls back down to the low energy level, releasing radiation of exactly the same wavelengths as were absorbed on the left; passed through a spectrometer, this shows a plain dark background with a few bright, coloured emission lines at those same wavelengths.

Since every element has its own unique set of energy levels, each element produces its own unique pattern of spectral lines — effectively a barcode that can be used to identify it. This is how several elements (including rubidium, caesium, thallium and indium) were first discovered, and it is also how astronomers work out which elements are present in distant stars, by analysing the light the stars give out.

Line Spectra as Evidence for Quantized Energy

Continuous spectrum vs line spectrumA continuous spectrum (such as the rainbow produced by passing white light through a prism) contains every wavelength across a range, blending smoothly into one another with no gaps. A line emission spectrum contains light of only a few specific, discrete wavelengths, seen as separate coloured lines with dark gaps between them.

The fact that an atom’s emission spectrum consists of separate lines, rather than a continuous smear of colour, is powerful evidence that an electron in an atom cannot have just any energy — it can only occupy certain fixed, discrete energy levels. If energy levels could take any value at all, an atom could emit photons of every possible energy, giving a continuous spectrum instead of a line spectrum. Because each line corresponds to an electron falling between two specific, fixed energy levels, the electron can only ever gain or lose energy in fixed-size steps, never continuously.

Quantization of energyThe energy of an electron in an atom is said to be quantized: it can only take one of a set of discrete, fixed values, never anything in between. This can be pictured as a staircase — a person on a staircase can stand on one particular step or another, but never at a height between two steps.

The energy released as a photon when an electron falls from a higher energy level to a lower one is exactly equal to the difference in energy between those two levels, and this in turn determines the frequency of the light emitted, through the Planck equation (covered below). Because the emitted photon’s energy is fixed by the gap between two allowed levels, only certain frequencies (certain colours) of light can ever appear in the spectrum — producing the pattern of separate lines observed experimentally.

The Planck Equation

Light can be described either as a wave (characterized by its frequency and wavelength) or as a stream of particle-like packets of energy called photons. The energy carried by each photon is directly proportional to the frequency of the radiation.

Ephoton = hf

Here Ephoton is the energy of one photon (in joules), h is the Planck constant (a fixed value, given in the data booklet, approximately 6.63 × 10−34 J s), and f is the frequency of the radiation (in hertz). Because higher frequency corresponds to higher photon energy, ultraviolet photons carry more energy than infrared photons, and gamma-ray photons carry more energy still.

When an electron falls from a higher energy level to a lower one inside an atom, the photon that is emitted carries away exactly the energy the electron has lost:

ΔEelectron = Ephoton = hf
Worked example

An electron falls between two energy levels in an atom, emitting a photon of frequency 5.00 × 1014 Hz. Using h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s, calculate the energy of the emitted photon, and hence the size of the energy change of the electron.
Ephoton = hf = (6.63 × 10−34) × (5.00 × 1014) = 3.32 × 10−19 J. Since ΔEelectron = Ephoton, the electron’s energy has decreased by 3.32 × 10−19 J as it fell to the lower level.

Worked example: combining both equations

A spectral line in an emission spectrum has a wavelength of 486 nm (486 × 10−9 m). Calculate the energy of the corresponding photon.
First find the frequency using c = fλ: f = c ÷ λ = (3.00 × 108) ÷ (486 × 10−9) = 6.17 × 1014 Hz. Then find the photon energy using E = hf: E = (6.63 × 10−34) × (6.17 × 1014) = 4.09 × 10−19 J. This two-step method — wavelength to frequency, then frequency to energy — is the standard way of converting a measured spectral line into the energy gap it represents.

The Hydrogen Emission Spectrum

The simplest possible atom, hydrogen, has just one electron, and its line emission spectrum played a crucial role in developing atomic theory. In the (now outdated, but still useful) Bohr model, this electron is pictured as orbiting the nucleus in circular paths of fixed radius, each corresponding to a fixed energy level, labelled with an integer n (n = 1 is the lowest energy level, the ground state; n = 2, 3, 4… are progressively higher energy levels). Absorbing energy promotes the electron to a higher n; falling back down to a lower n releases a photon.

Hydrogen energy levels and the visible seriesA ladder of horizontal lines representing energy levels n = 1 (at the bottom) up through n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and higher, with the gaps between successive levels becoming steadily smaller as n increases, until the levels merge into a continuum at very high n. Several downward arrows are drawn from higher levels (n = 3, 4, 5, 6…) converging on n = 2, representing the transitions that produce the visible emission lines of hydrogen; the arrow from n = 3 to n = 2 is the longest wavelength (lowest energy, red) visible line, and arrows from higher n produce progressively shorter wavelength (higher energy, blue/violet) visible lines that crowd closer together as n increases.

Transitions ending at different energy levels fall in different regions of the electromagnetic spectrum: transitions that end at n = 1 (the ground state) release the most energy and appear in the ultraviolet region; transitions ending at n = 2 produce the visible lines; transitions ending at n = 3 or higher produce infrared lines.

Convergence of spectral linesThe lines of the hydrogen emission spectrum are spaced further apart at low energy and crowd closer together (converge) at higher energy, eventually merging into a continuum. This happens because the energy levels themselves are spaced further apart at low n and become progressively closer together as n increases, so the energy gaps between adjacent levels — and hence the spacing between the corresponding spectral lines — shrink at higher energies.
Ionization and the convergence limitAs n increases without limit (n → ∞), the energy levels converge on a single value: the point at which the electron is no longer bound to the nucleus at all. The transition from n = 1 to n = ∞ corresponds to completely removing the electron from a ground-state atom, i.e. ionization: H(g) → H+(g) + e−. The ionization energy of an element is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state. The frequency at which the lines converge (the “series limit”) can therefore be used to calculate the ionization energy of hydrogen, providing further evidence for the energy-level model.

The Bohr model successfully predicts the wavelengths of every line in the hydrogen spectrum, which was a major achievement, but it fails for any atom with more than one electron, because it does not account for the repulsion between electrons or their true wave-like behaviour. A more complete model was needed.

Beyond Bohr: Wave–Particle Duality and the Schrödinger Model

Light shows properties of both waves (it diffracts, or spreads out, when passing through a narrow slit) and particles (it can knock electrons out of a metal surface, behaving like a stream of discrete photons). Neither the wave model nor the particle model on its own fully explains light’s behaviour; both are needed together. Quantum theory extends this same dual wave/particle description to matter: a beam of electrons passed through a thin sheet of graphite produces a diffraction pattern of rings, just as a wave would, showing that electrons themselves have wave-like properties.

Electron diffractionA beam of electrons is fired from an electron gun through a thin sheet of graphite and strikes a luminescent screen beyond it. Instead of forming a single bright spot (as expected if electrons behaved only as tiny particles travelling in straight lines), the screen shows a pattern of concentric bright and dark rings, the same kind of interference pattern produced when a wave passes through a small aperture — direct evidence that electrons have wave-like character.
Heisenberg’s Uncertainty PrincipleIt is impossible to know, at the same time and with perfect precision, both the exact position and the exact momentum of a particle such as an electron. Any attempt to measure an electron’s position disturbs its motion. As a result, an electron’s path (trajectory) around the nucleus cannot be mapped out precisely, unlike the fixed circular orbits imagined in the Bohr model; only a probability description of where the electron is likely to be found is possible.

Erwin Schrödinger combined the ideas of wave behaviour and probability into a mathematical model in which the electron is described by a wave equation, whose solutions are called atomic orbitals. Unlike a Bohr orbit (a fixed circular path), an atomic orbital is a three-dimensional region of space, and it only tells us the probability of finding the electron at different points around the nucleus.

Atomic orbitalA region of space around an atomic nucleus in which there is a 90% probability of finding a given electron. Orbitals of higher energy are, on average, larger, with the electron more likely to be found further from the nucleus.
Bohr model vs Schrödinger (wave mechanical) modelThe Bohr model treats the electron as a particle following a precise, fixed circular path (an “orbit”) at a definite distance from the nucleus, and it only works for hydrogen (one electron). The Schrödinger model treats the electron as a wave, described only in terms of the probability of finding it within a three-dimensional region (an “orbital”), consistent with the Uncertainty Principle, and it can be extended (at least approximately) to atoms with many electrons.

Electron Spin and the Pauli Exclusion Principle

Refining the model further, each electron behaves as though it is spinning on its own axis, in one of two possible directions, conventionally drawn as an upward arrow (↑) or a downward arrow (↓). Two electrons occupying the same orbital can only do so if they spin in opposite directions; this reduces the repulsion between their negative charges enough for them to share the same small region of space.

Pauli exclusion principleAn atomic orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons, and if two electrons do occupy the same orbital, they must have opposite spins. No two electrons in the same atom can have an identical set of quantum descriptions (the same orbital and the same spin).

Orbital Shapes: s and p Orbitals

Different orbitals have different characteristic shapes and sizes. You are expected to recognise the shapes of s and p orbitals (not d or f orbitals).

The 1s orbitalA single, simple spherical cloud centred on the nucleus. The density of shading (or dots) is greatest close to the nucleus and fades gradually outward, representing the probability of finding the electron at each distance; the electron may be found anywhere within this roughly spherical volume, with no preferred direction.
The three 2p orbitalsThree identical dumbbell (figure-of-eight) shaped lobes, each consisting of two rounded lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus, meeting at a point at the nucleus itself. The three 2p orbitals, labelled 2px, 2py and 2pz, are oriented at right angles to one another along the x, y and z axes respectively. Because all three have exactly the same size, shape and energy, they are described as degenerate orbitals.

A 2s orbital has the same overall spherical symmetry as a 1s orbital, but it is larger: an electron in a 2s orbital is, on average, further from the nucleus and has higher energy than an electron in a 1s orbital. Orbitals in higher sublevels (d, f) exist too and hold electrons in heavier atoms, but their detailed shapes are not required — only their names, letters and electron capacities matter for this course.

Energy Levels, Sublevels and Orbitals

Electrons are organized into a hierarchy: main energy levels (shells), which are divided into sublevels, which are themselves made up of one or more individual orbitals.

Energy level, sublevel, orbitalThe main energy level (or shell) is given an integer n = 1, 2, 3…. Each main energy level is divided into sublevels, labelled s, p, d and f, of successively higher energy. Each sublevel is made up of one or more individual orbitals of equal (degenerate) energy, each of which can hold a maximum of two electrons.

Number of orbitals in level n = n2
Maximum number of electrons in level n = 2n2

Here n is the main energy level number. The nth level is divided into exactly n sublevels (so the 1st level has 1 sublevel, the 4th level has 4 sublevels); the total number of orbitals across all those sublevels is n2; and because each orbital holds a maximum of two electrons, the maximum number of electrons the whole level can hold is 2n2. Individually, an s sublevel always has 1 orbital (2 electrons), a p sublevel always has 3 orbitals (6 electrons), a d sublevel always has 5 orbitals (10 electrons), and an f sublevel always has 7 orbitals (14 electrons).

Worked example

Calculate the maximum number of electrons that the n = 3 energy level can hold, and check this against the individual sublevel capacities.
Using the formula: maximum electrons = 2n2 = 2 × 32 = 2 × 9 = 18. Checking against the sublevels present in n = 3 (3s, 3p, 3d): 2 (from 3s) + 6 (from 3p) + 10 (from 3d) = 18. Both methods agree: the third energy level holds a maximum of 18 electrons.

Relative energies of the sublevelsA vertical energy-level diagram with energy increasing up the page. Starting from the bottom: 1s, then 2s, then 2p (slightly above 2s), then 3s, then 3p, then 4s, then 3d (drawn just above 4s, since the 3d sublevel falls below 4s in energy only for atoms with atomic number greater than 20), then 4p. A note beside the 3d/4s pair states that their relative energy ordering is unusually close and changes once electrons begin to occupy the 3d sublevel.

Building Up Electron Configurations: the Aufbau Principle

Aufbau principle(German for “building up”.) Electrons fill the available orbitals of an atom starting with the lowest energy orbital first, before occupying any higher-energy orbital.
Hund’s ruleWhen two or more orbitals of the same sublevel (degenerate orbitals, such as the three 2p orbitals) are available, electrons occupy separate orbitals, with parallel spins, before any orbital is doubly occupied. This arrangement minimizes the repulsion between electrons, since electrons in separate, spatially distinct orbitals stay further apart on average than two electrons crammed into the same orbital.

These rules, together with the Pauli exclusion principle, can be applied using “arrow-in-box” orbital diagrams: each orbital is drawn as a box, and each electron as an arrow (pointing up or down for its spin) placed inside a box.

Orbital diagrams for the first five elementsFive short rows of boxes, one for each of hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium and boron. Hydrogen shows a single box (1s) with one upward arrow. Helium shows the same 1s box with two arrows, one up and one down (paired, opposite spins). Lithium adds a second box (2s) containing one upward arrow. Beryllium fills that 2s box with a second, opposite-spin arrow. Boron adds a row of three separate boxes (the three 2p orbitals), with a single upward arrow in the first 2p box and the other two 2p boxes left empty.
Orbital diagrams for carbon and nitrogen (Hund’s rule in action)Carbon’s three 2p boxes each show: one upward arrow in the first box, one upward arrow in the second box, and the third box left empty — the two 2p electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel (both upward) spins rather than pairing up in one box. Nitrogen’s three 2p boxes each contain a single upward arrow, one in every box, giving three unpaired, parallel-spin electrons, one in each of the three degenerate 2p orbitals.

Continuing across period 2: oxygen begins to pair the 2p electrons (one 2p orbital now holds two, opposite-spin electrons, while the other two 2p orbitals each still hold one unpaired electron), fluorine has two of the three 2p orbitals paired, and neon completes the 2p sublevel with all three orbitals doubly occupied (six electrons in total).

Electron configuration of sulfur (Z = 16): 1s22s22p63s23p4
Worked example

Deduce the ground-state electron configuration of a sulfur atom.
Sulfur has atomic number 16, so a neutral atom has 16 electrons. Filling the orbitals from lowest to highest energy: 2 electrons fill 1s (1s2), 2 more fill 2s (2s2), 6 more fill 2p (2p6) — that accounts for 10 electrons so far — 2 more fill 3s (3s2), leaving 4 electrons for 3p (3p4), since 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 4 = 16. The full configuration is 1s22s22p63s23p4.

The Filling Order and the 3d/4s Anomaly

Because sublevel energies get closer together at higher n, the true filling order is not simply 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s… Instead, the 4s sublevel is slightly lower in energy than 3d for potassium and calcium, so 4s fills before 3d.

Aufbau filling order (up to Z = 36):
1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p
The diagonal filling-order ruleSublevels are written out in rows by main energy level (row 1: 1s; row 2: 2s, 2p; row 3: 3s, 3p, 3d; row 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f; and so on), with each sublevel positioned in a column according to its letter (s, p, d, f). A series of diagonal arrows, each running from the top right of one row down to the bottom left of the next, passes through the sublevels in the exact order they are filled: 1s, then 2s, then (down-diagonal) 2p, 3s, then (down-diagonal) 3p, 4s, then (down-diagonal) 3d, 4p, 5s, and so on. Following these diagonals reproduces the experimentally observed filling order without needing to memorize it directly.
Filling order vs the order configurations are writtenElectrons fill the 4s sublevel before the 3d sublevel (Aufbau order: …3p, 4s, 3d, 4p…). However, once the 3d sublevel starts to fill (from scandium onward), it actually drops below 4s in energy, so by convention the electron configuration of any element after calcium is written with 3d listed before 4s (e.g. vanadium is written 1s22s22p63s23p63d34s2, not …4s23d3). This is also why, when transition metals lose electrons to form positive ions, the 4s electrons are always removed before any 3d electrons — because once the 3d sublevel is occupied, its electrons push the 4s electrons to a higher effective energy.
Vanadium (Z = 23): 1s22s22p63s23p63d34s2, condensed form [Ar]3d34s2
Worked example

State the full electron configuration of vanadium (Z = 23) and deduce the number of unpaired electrons.
Vanadium has 23 electrons. Filling in Aufbau order: 1s22s22p63s23p64s23d3 (2+2+6+2+6+2+3 = 23), conventionally re-written with 3d before 4s: 1s22s22p63s23p63d34s2. The three 3d electrons occupy three separate d orbitals with parallel spins (Hund’s rule), so there are 3 unpaired electrons.

Rather than writing out every inner sublevel each time, a condensed (noble-gas core) configuration can be used: the electron configuration of the previous noble gas is replaced by its symbol in square brackets, and only the outer (valence) electrons are written out explicitly. For example, since argon’s configuration is 1s22s22p63s23p6, vanadium’s condensed configuration is [Ar]3d34s2. A condensed configuration is not an acceptable answer if a question specifically asks for the “full” electron configuration.

The Chromium and Copper Exceptions

Two elements in period 4 do not follow the Aufbau order exactly, because half-filled and completely filled d sublevels are unusually stable (they minimize electron–electron repulsion by spreading electrons out as evenly as possible).

Chromium and copper exceptionsChromium (Z = 24) has the configuration [Ar]3d54s1 rather than the “expected” [Ar]3d44s2, because a half-filled d sublevel (five separate, unpaired d electrons) is more stable. Copper (Z = 29) has the configuration [Ar]3d104s1 rather than the “expected” [Ar]3d94s2, because a completely filled d sublevel is more stable.
Orbital diagrams for chromium and copperTwo sets of boxes for the 3d and 4s sublevels. For chromium: the 4s box contains a single upward arrow, and each of the five separate 3d boxes contains one upward arrow — six singly occupied orbitals in total, with no pairing anywhere. For copper: the 4s box contains a single upward arrow, and each of the five 3d boxes contains two arrows, one up and one down (fully paired) — a completely filled 3d sublevel plus one lone 4s electron.

Electron Configurations of Ions

Positive ions (cations) form when an atom loses one or more electrons from its outermost (highest-energy) occupied sublevel. Negative ions (anions) form when an atom gains one or more electrons, which are added into the next available orbital following the same Aufbau/Hund rules used for neutral atoms.

Forming cations and anionsCations: remove electrons starting with the highest-energy occupied sublevel. Anions: add electrons into the lowest-energy available empty orbital(s), following Hund’s rule where more than one orbital of equal energy is available.
Which sublevel loses electrons first: main group vs transition metalsFor a typical p-block or s-block atom, electrons are simply removed from the highest n, highest-energy sublevel present (e.g. aluminium: Al is 1s22s22p63s23p1; Al3+ is 1s22s22p6, having lost all three of its 3s and 3p electrons). For a transition metal, even though the configuration is written with 3d before 4s, the 4s electrons are always removed first when forming an ion, because once occupied, the 3d sublevel drops below 4s in energy (e.g. Cr is [Ar]3d54s1, and Cr3+ is [Ar]3d3, having lost the single 4s electron and two of the five 3d electrons).
Worked example

Deduce the ground-state electron configuration of the Fe3+ ion.
Iron (Z = 26) has the configuration 1s22s22p63s23p63d64s2 (26 electrons total: 2+2+6+2+6+6+2 = 26). Forming Fe3+ means removing 3 electrons: first both 4s electrons, then one 3d electron, giving 1s22s22p63s23p63d5. Note that the 4s electrons are removed before any 3d electrons, even though 3d was written before 4s in the neutral atom’s configuration.

For anions, electrons simply continue filling the next available sublevel: sulfur (1s22s22p63s23p4) gains two electrons to complete its 3p sublevel, giving the sulfide ion S2− (1s22s22p63s23p6), which has the same electron configuration as the noble gas argon. Several common ions of neighbouring elements share an electron configuration with the nearest noble gas in this way (for example Na+, F− and O2− all share neon’s configuration).

Electron Configuration and the Periodic Table

The layout of the periodic table directly reflects the pattern in which sublevels fill, because an element’s position depends on the highest-energy sublevel occupied by its valence electrons.

Blocks of the periodic tableThe s block (groups 1 and 2, plus helium) contains elements whose valence electrons occupy an s sublevel. The p block (groups 13–18, except helium) contains elements with valence electrons in p orbitals. The d block (the transition metals) contains elements with valence electrons filling d orbitals. The f block (the lanthanides and actinides, usually shown as a separate strip below the main table) contains elements filling f orbitals.
Block structure of the periodic tableThe standard periodic table grid, shaded into four connected regions: a narrow two-column block on the far left (s block, groups 1–2), a wide central block (d block, the ten columns of transition metals), a six-column block on the right (p block, groups 13–18), and a separate two-row strip beneath the main table (f block, the lanthanides and actinides). Each period (row) number corresponds to the highest main energy level (n) reached by that row’s valence electrons.

Because of this direct link, an element’s electron configuration can be read straight off its position in the periodic table, and vice versa: the period number gives the highest main energy level occupied, the block tells you which type of sublevel is being filled, and the position within the block tells you how many electrons occupy that sublevel. For example, iodine sits in group 17, period 5, in the p block, giving the condensed configuration [Kr]4d105s25p5 — seven valence electrons in total (2 in 5s, 5 in 5p), consistent with its position seven columns into the p block.

The number of electrons in an atom’s outermost occupied main energy level are called its valence electrons, and these are mainly responsible for how the atom bonds with other atoms. The number of valence electrons follows a repeating (periodic) pattern down each group of the table, which underlies many of the recurring chemical trends explored elsewhere in the course.
