Guiding Question

What happens when protons are transferred?

Reaction mechanisms describe how reactions proceed at the molecular level. There are four fundamental types of reaction based on the behaviour of subatomic particles: proton transfer reactions (acid–base reactions and neutralization), electron transfer reactions (redox), electron-sharing reactions (radicals) and electron-pair-sharing reactions (electrophiles and nucleophiles). This sub-topic deals with the first of these: proton transfer reactions. When a proton, H+, is transferred in a reaction, the reactant that loses it loses a positive charge, while the product that gains it gains a positive charge. This can only happen between a species that can release H+ and a species with a lone pair of electrons that can accept an additional H+ — this behaviour is known as Brønsted–Lowry acid–base behaviour. Acid–base chemistry underlies air and water pollution, ocean chemistry under climate change, the action of drugs in the body, food science and much of modern research.

The Development of Acid–Base Theory

Because a hydrogen atom contains just one proton and one electron, when it ionizes by losing its electron to form H+, all that remains is the proton. H+ and “a proton” can therefore be used interchangeably.

Definitions of acids and bases have evolved over time as evidence accumulated:

  • Lavoisier (1777) proposed that oxygen was the “universal acidifying principle” and that an acid was a compound of oxygen and a non-metal (he even named oxygen from the Greek for “acid-former”). This theory was dismissed once HCl was shown to contain only hydrogen and chlorine, with no oxygen at all — a definition of an acid must be valid for every acid.
  • Arrhenius (1887) defined an acid as a substance that dissociates in water to form H+ and anions, and a base as a substance that dissociates in water to form OH− and cations. He recognized that H+ and OH− combine to form water, and that the remaining ions form a salt. This theory was limited because it applied only to aqueous systems, and could not explain why some insoluble substances show basic properties.
  • Brønsted–Lowry (1923): working independently, Martin Lowry (Cambridge, England) and Johannes Brønsted (Copenhagen, Denmark) published similar conclusions that overcame the limitations of Arrhenius’ theory. This is an example of “multiple independent discovery”, alongside examples such as the independent discovery of calculus by Newton and Leibniz.
Brønsted–Lowry theory (Reactivity 3.1.1)A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H+) donor. A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton (H+) acceptor. A proton in aqueous solution can be represented as either H+(aq) or H3O+(aq).

For example, in the reaction between hydrogen chloride gas and ammonia gas, HCl transfers a proton to NH3, forming the white smoke of solid ammonium chloride:

HCl(g) + NH3(g) → NH4Cl(s), i.e. HCl + NH3 ↔ NH4+ + Cl−

Here HCl acts as an acid (donates H+) and NH3 acts as a base (accepts H+). NH4Cl is the ionic compound formed from the NH4+ and Cl− ions produced.

Worked example

Deduce the Brønsted–Lowry acid and base (forward reaction only) in: (1) NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq); (2) H2PO4−(aq) + HCO3−(aq) ↔ HPO42−(aq) + H2CO3(aq).
(1) NH3(aq) accepts H+ to become NH4+(aq), so NH3(aq) is the base. H2O(l) donates H+ to become OH−(aq), so H2O(l) is the acid.
(2) H2PO4−(aq) donates H+ to become HPO42−(aq), so H2PO4−(aq) is the acid. HCO3−(aq) accepts H+ to become H2CO3(aq), so HCO3−(aq) is the base.

Nature of ScienceBrønsted–Lowry theory has stood the test of time and experimentation, indicating it has led to testable predictions that support it and enable wide-ranging application — unlike Lavoisier’s theory, which was falsified, or Arrhenius’ theory, which proved too limited (restricted to aqueous solutions).

Conjugate Acid–Base Pairs

Donating a proton cannot happen in isolation: there must always be a base present to accept it. Consider a generic acid HA reacting with a generic base B:

HA + B ↔ A− + BH+

Reading the forward reaction, HA is the acid (donates H+) and B is the base (accepts H+). Reading the reverse reaction, BH+ is now acting as an acid (donates H+ back) and A− is acting as a base (accepts H+). Acid HA has become base A−, and base B has become acid BH+.

Conjugate acid–base pair (Reactivity 3.1.2)A pair of species differing by a single proton is called a conjugate acid–base pair. In HA + B ↔ A− + BH+, the pairs are HA/A− and B/BH+. To write the conjugate base of an acid, remove one H+ (and reduce the charge by 1). To write the conjugate acid of a base, add one H+ (and increase the charge by 1).

One conjugate pair found in every aqueous acid–base reaction is H2O/H3O+: H2O(l) + H+(aq) ↔ H3O+(aq). A proton released from an acid becomes hydrated, forming H3O+, which is variously called the hydroxonium, oxonium or hydronium ion, and is always the true form of “H+” in aqueous solution — though it is usually simplified to H+(aq) for convenience. In this pair, H3O+ is the conjugate acid and H2O is the conjugate base.

Worked example

Label the conjugate acid–base pairs in CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ CH3COO−(aq) + H3O+(aq).
CH3COOH (acid) / CH3COO− (base) is one conjugate pair. H2O (base) / H3O+ (acid) is the other conjugate pair.

Worked example

(1) Write the conjugate base of: (a) H3O+ (b) NH3 (c) H2CO3. (2) Write the conjugate acid of: (a) NO2− (b) OH− (c) CO32−.
(1) Remove one H+ from each: (a) H2O (b) NH2− (c) HCO3−.
(2) Add one H+ to each: (a) HNO2 (b) H2O (c) HCO3−.

Most polyatomic ions can act as Brønsted–Lowry bases, accepting H+ to form their conjugate acid, for example: OH− → H2O; NO3− → HNO3; HCO3− → H2CO3; CO32− → HCO3−; SO42− → HSO4−; PO43− → HPO42−. The ammonium ion, NH4+, cannot accept another H+; instead it loses H+ and acts as a Brønsted–Lowry acid, forming its conjugate base NH3.

Base vs. alkaliA Brønsted–Lowry base is any species that can accept a proton. An alkali is a smaller, specific group of soluble bases which dissolve in water to release the hydroxide ion, OH−, e.g. K2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2K+(aq) + 2OH−(aq). Every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali (e.g. CO32−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ HCO3−(aq) + OH−(aq) shows carbonate acting as a base without itself being classed as an alkali).

Amphiprotic Species

Water can be described both as a base (it accepts H+ from CH3COOH) and as an acid (it donates H+ to NH3) — it all depends on what water is reacting with, since Brønsted–Lowry theory describes acids and bases in terms of how they behave in a particular reaction:

CH3COOH + H2O ↔ CH3COO− + H3O+  (water acts as a base)
NH3 + H2O ↔ NH4+ + OH−  (water acts as an acid)

Amphiprotic (Reactivity 3.1.3)A species which can act as both a Brønsted–Lowry acid and a Brønsted–Lowry base is described as amphiprotic (from the Greek amphoteros, meaning “both”). To act as an acid it must be able to dissociate and release H+; to act as a base it must have a lone pair of electrons able to accept H+. An amphiprotic species must therefore possess both a releasable H and a lone pair.
Worked example

Write equations to show HCO3− acting (a) as an acid and (b) as a base.
(a) As an acid, it donates H+: HCO3−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ CO32−(aq) + H3O+(aq).
(b) As a base, it accepts H+: HCO3−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ H2CO3(aq) + OH−(aq).

Amphiprotic vs. amphotericAmphiprotic specifically refers to Brønsted–Lowry theory, where the emphasis is on proton transfer. Amphoteric has a broader meaning: a substance that can act as an acid and a base, including by mechanisms that do not involve proton transfer (explained by Lewis theory instead). For example, aluminium oxide is amphoteric because it reacts with both dilute acids and alkalis. All amphiprotic substances are amphoteric, but not all amphoteric substances are amphiprotic.

Across period 3, the oxides show a trend from basic through amphoteric to acidic: Na2O and MgO are basic metal oxides; Al2O3 is an amphoteric oxide; SiO2, P4O10, SO2 and Cl2O are acidic non-metal oxides.

  • Basic metal oxides react with an acid to form a salt and water: Na2O(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l).
  • Al2O3 reacts with both an acid and a base: Al2O3(s) + 3H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3H2O(l); Al2O3(s) + 3H2O(l) + 2OH−(aq) → 2Al(OH)4−(aq).
  • Non-metal oxides form weak acids in solution: SO2(g) + H2O(l) ↔ H2SO3(aq); H2SO3(aq) ↔ 2H+(aq) + SO32−(aq).
Acid rainOxides of nitrogen and sulfur (NOx and SOx), released mainly from burning coal and heavy oil, react with atmospheric moisture to form weak acids such as sulfurous and nitrous acid, e.g. 2NO2(g) + H2O(l) → HNO2(aq) + HNO3(aq). This lowers the pH of precipitation. Acid rain is defined as precipitation with a pH lowered to less than 5.6 (normal rainwater is already slightly acidic, below pH 7, because it dissolves atmospheric CO2). Acid rain damages structural materials (e.g. erosion of marble and limestone buildings), lakes, rivers and plant life, especially coniferous forests.

The pH Scale

Most acids encountered are weak, so [H+] expressed directly in mol dm−3 often has an inconveniently large negative exponent (e.g. the H+ concentration of blood is 4.6 × 10−8 mol dm−3). The Danish chemist Søren Sorensen introduced the pH scale in 1909 to give a more user-friendly, quantitative measure of acid strength.

pH = −log10[H+]      [H+] = 10−pH

pH is the negative power to which 10 must be raised to give [H+]. For example, [H+] = 0.1 mol dm−3 = 10−1 mol dm−3, so pH = 1; [H+] = 0.01 mol dm−3 = 10−2 mol dm−3, so pH = 2. These logarithms are always base 10 (log), never natural logarithms (ln).

Features of the pH scale (Reactivity 3.1.4)Although theoretically infinite and able to extend to negative numbers, most common acids and bases fall in the range pH 0–14, corresponding to [H+] from 1.0 down to 10−14 mol dm−3. pH numbers are normally positive and have no units. pH is inversely related to [H+]: a higher [H+] gives a lower pH, so stronger/more concentrated acids have a lower pH and weaker/more dilute acids have a higher pH. Because the scale is logarithmic, a change of one pH unit represents a 10-fold change in [H+].
Worked example

If the pH of a solution changes from 3 to 5, deduce how [H+] changes.
pH 3 ⇒ [H+] = 10−3 mol dm−3. pH 5 ⇒ [H+] = 10−5 mol dm−3. [H+] has therefore decreased by a factor of 102, i.e. decreased 100-fold.

Sketch graph: pH against [H+]A curve on axes labelled [H+] (horizontal) and pH (vertical), with no units and no plotted data points (a sketch graph shows only the overall shape of the relationship). As [H+] increases from left to right along the horizontal axis, the pH value on the vertical axis falls steeply at first and then levels off, forming a curve that drops quickly from a high pH at very low [H+] toward a low pH as [H+] becomes large — the classic shape of an inverse logarithmic relationship, always falling but never in a straight line.

Because a logarithmic scale compresses a huge range of concentrations (100 to 10−14 mol dm−3) into a small range of simple numbers (0 to 14), it is easy to interpret — but this can also be misleading if not read carefully. A reported change from pH 5.5 to pH 4.5 may sound small, but it represents a tenfold increase in [H+]. Human blood pH is tightly controlled at 7.4, and a change of only half a pH unit either side of this can be fatal.

Significant figures in pHThe number of decimal places in a pH value (or in any logarithm) should equal the number of significant figures in the original concentration, because the digit(s) to the left of the decimal point in the logarithm come from the exponential part of the number and are not counted as significant figures.
Worked example

A sample of lake water at 298 K has [H+] = 3.2 × 10−5 mol dm−3. Calculate its pH and comment on the value.
pH = −log10(3.2 × 10−5) = 4.49 (two significant figures in 3.2 ⇒ two decimal places in the answer). Since pH < 7 at 298 K, the lake water is acidic.

Worked example

Human blood has a pH of 7.40. Calculate [H+].
[H+] = 10−pH = 10−7.40 = 4.0 × 10−8 mol dm−3.

Measuring pH

Universal indicator (paper or solution) gives a characteristic colour that is compared against a reference colour chart. Narrower-range indicators are more accurate than broad-range ones, but this is an analogue method that depends on the user’s subjective interpretation of colour, so it is useful for estimation and comparison rather than precise measurement.

A pH meter uses a special electrode to directly measure [H+] and gives a more objective, more accurate reading (to several decimal places). It must be calibrated before use with a buffer solution of known pH and standardized for temperature, since pH is a temperature-dependent measurement.

pH probe apparatusA slim electronic probe with a glass or composite tip is dipped directly into the liquid being tested, connected by a cable to a handheld or benchtop meter with a digital numerical display showing the pH reading to two or three decimal places. Inside the tip are electrodes that measure the electrical potential generated by hydrogen ions in the solution, which the meter's electronics convert directly into the displayed pH value.
The pH scale and alkalinityBecause [H+] and [OH−] are inversely related in aqueous solution, the same 0–14 scale can describe alkalinity as well as acidity: pH 0 is increasing [H+]/increasing acidity, pH 7 is neutral (at 298 K), and pH 14 is increasing [OH−]/increasing alkalinity.

The Ion Product Constant of Water, Kw

Water itself ionizes very slightly: H2O(l) ↔ H+(aq) + OH−(aq), giving an equilibrium expression K = [H+][OH−] / [H2O]. Because so little water ionizes, [H2O] is essentially constant and can be combined with K to give a modified equilibrium constant, Kw.

Kw = [H+][OH−]    (at 298 K, Kw = 1.00 × 10−14)

Kw is called the ionic product constant of water and has a fixed value at a specified temperature (Reactivity 3.1.5). In pure water, [H+] = [OH−], so [H+] = √Kw. At 298 K this gives [H+] = 1.00 × 10−7 mol dm−3, so pH = 7.00, consistent with the familiar value for pure water at room temperature.

Classifying solutions at 298 KAcidic: [H+] > [OH−], pH < 7. Neutral: [H+] = [OH−], pH = 7. Alkaline (basic): [H+] < [OH−], pH > 7. If either [H+] or [OH−] is known, the other can always be found from Kw: [H+] = Kw/[OH−] and [OH−] = Kw/[H+].
Worked example

A sample of blood at 298 K has [H+] = 4.60 × 10−8 mol dm−3. Calculate [OH−] and state whether the blood is acidic, neutral or basic.
[OH−] = Kw/[H+] = (1.00 × 10−14) / (4.60 × 10−8) = 2.17 × 10−7 mol dm−3. Since [OH−] > [H+], the blood is basic.

Kw Is Temperature Dependent

The dissociation of water is endothermic (it involves bond breaking), so by Le Châtelier’s principle:

  • An increase in temperature shifts the equilibrium right, increasing Kw; [H+] and [OH−] both increase, so the pH of pure water decreases.
  • A decrease in temperature shifts the equilibrium left, decreasing Kw; [H+] and [OH−] both decrease, so the pH of pure water increases.

Neutral does not always mean pH 7Pure water at any temperature is always neutral because [H+] = [OH−] in it — it does not become acidic or basic as it is heated or cooled, even though its pH value changes. pH = 7.00 corresponds to neutral only at 298 K. Because of this temperature dependence, temperature should always be stated alongside any pH measurement.

Strong and Weak Acids and Bases

Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases exist in aqueous solution as equilibrium mixtures of the undissociated form and the ions. The position of this equilibrium defines the strength of the acid or base (Reactivity 3.1.6).

HA(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ A−(aq) + H3O+(aq)

Strong vs. weakA strong acid or base dissociates/ionizes almost fully in solution; its equilibrium lies essentially completely to the right, so the reaction is normally written with a single forward arrow, e.g. HCl(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + Cl−(aq). A weak acid or base dissociates/ionizes only partially; its equilibrium lies well to the left, so an equilibrium sign must be used, e.g. CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ H3O+(aq) + CH3COO−(aq). Do not confuse strong/weak (extent of dissociation, an inherent property of the acid or base) with concentrated/dilute (moles of solute per unit volume of water) — a strong acid can be dilute, and a weak acid can be concentrated.

Strong acids are good proton donors, and because their dissociation goes virtually to completion, their conjugate bases show virtually no basic properties (e.g. Cl−, the conjugate base of HCl). Weak acids are poor proton donors; because their equilibrium favours the reactants, their conjugate bases are comparatively strong bases, readily able to accept a proton (e.g. CH3COO− is a stronger base than Cl−). In general, acid dissociation reactions (and base ionization reactions) favour the production of the weaker conjugate — i.e. the equilibrium lies in the direction of the weaker conjugate acid/base.

Common strong and weak acids/basesStrong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, H2SO4. Strong bases: the group 1 hydroxides, LiOH, NaOH, KOH. Weak acids (examples): CH3COOH and other organic (carboxylic) acids, H2CO3, H3PO4. Weak bases (examples): NH3, amines such as C2H5NH2 (organic derivatives of NH3 with an alkyl group replacing one H). There are only a handful of common strong acids and bases, so this short list is worth memorizing — any acid or base not on it is very likely weak. At equilibrium, strong acids/bases have a high concentration of the ionized (product) form and so have large equilibrium constants; weak acids/bases have a low concentration of ionized product and so have much smaller equilibrium constants.
Acid strength trend down group 17The strength of a Brønsted–Lowry acid depends on how easily it releases H+, which in turn depends on the strength of the bond that must break. Going down group 17, the halogen atom gets larger, so the H–halogen bond gets longer and weaker (needs less energy to break). Acid strength therefore increases down the group: HF < HCl < HBr < HI (bond enthalpies: H–F 568.0, H–Cl 432.0, H–Br 366.3, H–I 298.3 kJ mol−1).

Distinguishing Strong and Weak Acids/Bases

At the same molar concentration and temperature, strong acids/bases contain a higher concentration of ions than weak acids/bases, giving three experimental ways to tell them apart:

  • Electrical conductivity: depends on the concentration of mobile ions, so strong acids/bases conduct more strongly than weak ones of equal concentration (measured with a conductivity meter/probe).
  • Rate of reaction: reactions of acids (e.g. with a carbonate, releasing CO2 bubbles) depend on [H+], so they proceed faster with a stronger acid at the same concentration.
  • pH: at equal molar concentration, the stronger the acid, the lower its pH (measured with universal indicator or a pH meter).

The left-hand tube, containing the strongest acid, shows vigorous fizzing with a dense stream of bubbles rising rapidly. The middle tube shows a moderate steady stream of bubbles. The right-hand tube, containing the weakest acid, shows only occasional slow bubbles forming gently at the surface of the solid.

Neutralization Reactions

The reaction between H+ ions and OH− ions to form water is a neutralization reaction (Reactivity 3.1.7):

H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)

An ionic compound called a salt also forms, as the hydrogen of the acid is replaced by a metal ion or another positive ion. The parent acid and parent base describe the acid and base from which a given salt is derived — e.g. sodium chloride is derived from parent acid HCl and parent base NaOH.

Acid + base vs. acid + reactive metalAcid + base (or metal oxide/hydroxide) is a proton-transfer (Brønsted–Lowry) reaction: H+ is transferred from acid to base. Acid + reactive metal is not a proton-transfer reaction — it is a redox reaction: H+ ions gain electrons (reduction) to form H2 gas, while the metal loses electrons (oxidation), e.g. Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g), where Mg(s) → Mg2+(aq) + 2e− and 2H+(aq) + 2e− → H2(g). This cannot be described by Brønsted–Lowry theory since no species is simply accepting a proton.

Acid + base → salt + water: metal oxides, metal hydroxides and ammonia solution all react with acids in this way, e.g. HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l); 2CH3COOH(aq) + CuO(s) → Cu(CH3COO)2(aq) + H2O(l); HNO3(aq) + NH4OH(aq) → NH4NO3(aq) + H2O(l). Useful solubility rules: the only soluble hydroxides are NH4OH, LiOH, NaOH and KOH; the only soluble carbonates/hydrogencarbonates are (NH4)2CO3, Na2CO3, NaHCO3, K2CO3, KHCO3 and Ca(HCO3)2.

Acid + carbonate/hydrogencarbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide: e.g. 2HCl(aq) + CaCO3(s) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g); ionic form: 2H+(aq) + CO32−(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(g), and H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(g). These reactions visibly produce bubbles (effervescence). This is the basis of limestone cave chemistry: rainwater dissolving CO2 forms weak carbonic acid, H2O(l) + CO2(g) ↔ H2CO3(aq), which reacts with insoluble CaCO3 to form soluble Ca(HCO3)2 underground; where the pressure later drops (inside a cave), less CO2 stays dissolved, the reaction reverses, and CaCO3 precipitates back out to form stalactites and stalagmites.

Neutralization is exothermicIn an acid–base neutralization the net (ionic) reaction is always H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l); other ions present are spectator ions and cancel out. Because bond-making (O–H bonds) is exothermic, neutralization releases energy. For any strong acid reacting with any strong base, the enthalpy of neutralization per mole of water formed is very similar, approximately ΔH = −57 kJ mol−1, reflecting that the overall reaction (formation of water from its ions) is fundamentally the same in every case.

Practical applications include antacid tablets (weak bases such as magnesium hydroxide and aluminium hydroxide neutralizing excess stomach acid) and adding lime (CaO) to acidic soil or acidified lakes to raise the pH.

Acid–Base Titrations and pH Curves

Acid–base neutralization can be studied quantitatively by titration: a controlled volume of one reactant is added gradually from a burette to a fixed, accurately measured volume of the other reactant (measured with a pipette) in a conical flask, until the equivalence point (also called the stoichiometric point) is reached, where the acid and base have exactly neutralized each other. A pH meter or indicator detects this point.

R3.1 Figure 1: Titration apparatusA retort stand holds a vertical burette filled with one reagent (the titrant), its graduated scale allowing precise volumes to be read off and a tap at the bottom to control the flow. Below the burette, on a white tile (to make colour changes easier to see), sits a conical flask containing a fixed, accurately measured volume of the other reagent, delivered earlier by pipette, with a few drops of indicator added. Titrant is released drop by drop from the burette into the swirled flask until the indicator just changes colour, marking the end point of the titration.
Equivalence point vs. end pointThe equivalence point is the point where the reacting species have combined in exactly the stoichiometric ratio of the balanced equation (determined mathematically/from the pH curve). The end point is the point during the actual experiment at which the indicator changes colour, signalling the operator to stop adding titrant. A well-chosen indicator changes colour at a pH very close to the equivalence point, so the end point closely approximates the equivalence point.

Plotting pH (measured continuously with a pH meter/data-logger) against volume of titrant added gives a pH curve. This is not a linear relationship, partly because of the logarithmic nature of the pH scale. Near equivalence there is typically a very sharp jump in pH, called the point of inflection; the equivalence point is taken as being halfway up this near-vertical jump.

The stoichiometric ratio of the neutralization reaction determines the volumes needed to reach equivalence, e.g. HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) reaches equivalence at 1 mol acid : 1 mol base, whereas 2HCl(aq) + Ba(OH)2(aq) → BaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l) reaches equivalence at 2 mol acid : 1 mol base.

Adding acid to base instead of base to acidA pH curve can equally be plotted for the reverse arrangement — acid added from the burette to base in the flask — in which case the curve has the inverse shape (starting high and falling sharply through equivalence). Always check carefully which reagent is in the flask and which is being added from the burette before interpreting or sketching a pH curve.

Titration can be used to determine the concentration of an unknown acid or base, provided the concentration of the other solution is accurately known, by using the volume added at equivalence together with the stoichiometric ratio of the balanced equation. Because most burettes have a smallest division of 0.1 cm3, readings should be recorded to half of this, i.e. ±0.05 cm3 — approximately the volume of a single drop delivered from a burette.

Summary

  • A proton is H+. A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor; a Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor.
  • A conjugate acid–base pair differs by a single proton.
  • Amphiprotic species (e.g. H2O, HCO3−) can act as both Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases.
  • pH = −log10[H+]; a change of one pH unit is a 10-fold change in [H+].
  • Kw = [H+][OH−] is temperature dependent (Kw = 1.00 × 10−14 at 298 K); it shows the inverse relationship between [H+] and [OH−].
  • Strong acids/bases are fully ionized in solution; weak acids/bases are only partially ionized. Strength (extent of dissociation) is independent of concentration.
  • Acids and bases react in neutralization reactions to form a salt: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l), which is exothermic.
  • The equivalence (stoichiometric) point of a titration is where acid and base have exactly neutralized each other; pH curves plot pH against volume of titrant added.
  • For a strong acid–strong base titration, the pH at equivalence is 7, with a very sharp jump in pH around this point.
