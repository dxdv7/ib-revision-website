R1.3 Energy from fuels — Overview

Humans depend on energy resources, and burning fuels for heat is one of the oldest human technologies. Most of our energy still comes from fossil fuels — coal, crude oil and natural gas — which are a chemical store of solar energy trapped by green plants millions of years ago. Complete combustion of fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas and a major cause of global warming; incomplete combustion produces local pollutants such as carbon monoxide and soot particulates. Alternatives include biofuels (from recent photosynthesis) and hydrogen, which can be used directly in fuel cells to generate electricity without combustion.

Guiding question: What are the challenges of using chemical energy to address our energy needs?

R1.3.1 Combustion reactions

Reactive metals, non-metals and organic compounds undergo combustion reactions when heated in oxygen. Combustion is a reaction in which an element or compound burns in oxygen.

Nature of science — phlogiston theory. Early chemists proposed that a fire-like substance called ‘phlogiston’ was released during burning and rusting. This qualitative theory could not explain later quantitative data showing that substances actually gain mass during burning. In 1783 Lavoisier’s work on oxygen showed that combustion and rusting involve combination with oxygen from the air, overturning the phlogiston theory. This shows how scientific theories must be abandoned or revised when they conflict with reproducible experimental (quantitative) evidence.

Combustion of metals and non-metals

s block metals form ionic oxides which are basic, and p block non-metals generally form covalent oxides which are acidic. Some non-metals show a range of oxidation states and form different oxides on combustion.

Mg(s) + ½O2(g) → MgO(s)
S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g)
P4(s) + 5O2(g) → P4O10(s)

Magnesium burns with an intensely brilliant white flame, leaving a residue of magnesium oxide (used in flares and flashbulbs). Sulfur burns with a blue flame, producing the toxic gas sulfur dioxide. White phosphorus (P4) reacts with air to give two oxides, P4O10 and P4O6.

Worked example Write a balanced equation for the reaction of white phosphorus (P4) with oxygen gas to form phosphorus(V) oxide.

Solution: Start with the unbalanced skeleton equation: P4(s) + O2(g) → P4O10(s). There are 10 oxygen atoms in the product, so 5O2 are needed on the left:
P4(s) + 5O2(g) → P4O10(s)

Most combustion reactions are exothermic, but nitrogen combines with oxygen only at high temperature in an endothermic reaction to give nitrogen monoxide: N2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2NO(g). These combustion reactions involve electron transfer to oxygen — oxygen is the oxidizing agent (it accepts electrons) and the metal or non-metal is the reducing agent (it loses electrons). The rusting of iron is an oxidation reaction but is not combustion, since there is no flame.

Combustion of organic compounds

Hydrocarbons and alcohols are widely used as fuels because their combustion releases energy at a useful rate. Although the products are energetically more stable, the fuels themselves are kinetically stable (they have a high activation energy), so they do not spontaneously combust and can be safely stored and transported. Complete combustion of an organic compound breaks the carbon chain, producing carbon dioxide and water and releasing a large amount of heat.

C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l)  (propane, complete combustion)
C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l)  (ethanol, complete combustion)

Alcohols with general formula CnH2n+1OH burn similarly. Since the products are fully oxidized, this is called complete combustion; it requires an excess (plentiful supply) of oxygen.

Worked example Write the equation for the complete combustion of pentane, C5H12.

Solution: Balance C and H first: C5H12(g) + O2(g) → 5CO2(g) + 6H2O(l). Counting O atoms on the right gives 10 + 6 = 16, so 8O2 are needed:
C5H12(g) + 8O2(g) → 5CO2(g) + 6H2O(l)

The colour of a flame from organic combustion is due to carbon-containing radical intermediates, which eventually all break up to form carbon dioxide. Combustion of organic compounds containing nitrogen can release nitrogen gas or nitrogen oxides. Combustion analysis (burning a sample and measuring the CO2 and H2O produced) can be used to determine a compound's empirical formula.

R1.3.2 Incomplete combustion of organic compounds

When the oxygen supply is limited, or when a fuel has a high percentage of carbon, incomplete combustion occurs, producing carbon monoxide, CO(g), or carbon (soot), C(s), instead of only carbon dioxide.

2C3H8(g) + 7O2(g) → 6CO(g) + 8H2O(g)  (incomplete, forming CO)
C3H8(g) + 2O2(g) → 3C(g) + 4H2O(g)  (incomplete, forming soot)

A Bunsen burner illustrates this: complete combustion (plentiful air) gives a hot, blue flame; incomplete combustion (limited air) gives a cooler, yellow, sooty flame. Compounds containing a benzene ring produce a particularly sooty yellow flame because of their high percentage carbon content.

Worked example How could samples of cyclohexane (C6H12), cyclohexene (C6H10) and methylbenzene (CH3C6H5) be distinguished by observing their combustion?

Solution: Calculate the percentage carbon by mass in each.
Cyclohexane, M = 84 g mol−1: %C = (6 × 12.01) / 84 × 100 = 86%
Cyclohexene, M = 82 g mol−1: %C = (6 × 12.01) / 82 × 100 = 88%
Methylbenzene, M = 92 g mol−1: %C = (7 × 12.01) / 92 × 100 = 91%
Methylbenzene has the highest %C and produces the sootiest flame; cyclohexane has the lowest %C and produces the least sooty flame.
Worked example Give the balanced equation for the formation of carbon monoxide from the incomplete combustion of octane, C8H18(l), in an internal combustion engine (producing H2O(g) and CO(g)).

Solution: Balance C and H: C8H18(l) + O2(g) → 8CO(g) + 9H2O(g). Balancing O gives 8 + 4.5 = 8.5 mol O2. Doubling to clear the fraction:
2C8H18(l) + 17O2(g) → 16CO(g) + 18H2O(g)
Carbon monoxide toxicity. CO is absorbed by the lungs and binds to haemoglobin (Hb) in red blood cells more effectively than oxygen does, forming carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb): Hb + CO → COHb. This prevents oxygen transport around the body. CO is colourless and odourless, so it can reach dangerous levels undetected: about 35 ppm over six hours causes headaches and dizziness, while 800 ppm can cause unconsciousness within hours, with higher levels causing death.

Catalytic converters oxidize CO to CO2 and also reduce nitrogen oxides:

2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g)
2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO2(g) + N2(g)

Soot (carbon) from incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons and coal is an example of a particulate — solid carbon/dust particles or liquid droplets suspended in air, typically 0.001–10 µm in diameter. They enter the body during breathing and can cause respiratory diseases (emphysema, bronchitis, cancer) and can act as catalysts that increase the harmful effects of other gaseous pollutants.

R1.3.3 Fossil fuels

An ideal fuel is cheap, plentiful, accessible, releases high-quality energy at a reasonable rate, and has minimal environmental impact. Fossil fuels (coal, crude oil, natural gas) are non-renewable and non-sustainable because they take millions of years to form and are being used far faster than they can be replaced. Wood, by contrast, is renewable since trees can be regrown.

Energy density vs specific energy. The energy density of a fuel is the energy released per unit volume (kJ cm−3). The specific energy is the energy released per unit mass (kJ g−1). Liquid fuels have much greater energy densities than gases, because gas molecules are far more spread out. Hydrogen has the highest specific energy of any common fuel (142 kJ g−1) but a very low energy density as a gas, which is why it is preferred where mass matters (rockets) but not where compactness matters (pipelines, where methane is preferred).

Formation of fossil fuels

Fossil fuels have been called ‘sunshine in solid, liquid and gaseous form’, since their stored energy originally came from sunlight trapped by green plants. They form from the slow, partial decomposition of plant and animal matter trapped in the absence of air; oxygen is lost faster than carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and sulfur, leaving reduced (hydrocarbon-rich) compounds.

Coal is a combustible sedimentary rock formed from plant remains subjected to geological heat and pressure, which progressively increases the carbon content. Coal is typically 80–90% carbon by mass; anthracite (formed under the most extreme heat/pressure) is almost pure carbon (92–98%). Coal is the most abundant fossil fuel.

Crude oil (petroleum) is a complex mixture of straight-chain and branched-chain alkanes, cycloalkanes and aromatic compounds (plus small amounts of N, O and S compounds), formed from marine organisms under high temperature/pressure in the absence of oxygen. It is both a fuel and an important chemical feedstock (for polymers, pharmaceuticals, dyes, solvents). Oil and gas are easier to extract than coal since they are fluids that can be pumped.

Natural gas is mainly methane, with nitrogen and sulfur compounds (e.g. hydrogen sulfide) as impurities. It is the cleanest fossil fuel to burn, due to its low carbon content, producing minimal CO, hydrocarbons and particulates. Where a pipeline network exists it is very efficient to distribute, though building the network requires huge capital investment.

As civilization has advanced, the carbon content of the fuels we favour has decreased: coal (highest carbon) was used first, then gasoline and natural gas, which have higher specific energies/energy densities, are easier to transport, and burn more cleanly with less CO2 per unit energy.

Should it include non-carbon greenhouse gases such as dinitrogen monoxide? Should it include toxic primary pollutants like CO, which is later oxidized to CO2 in the atmosphere? Scientific terms that affect public policy must be communicated clearly and consistently.

The alkanes in detail: carbon content, CO2 production and specific energy

The higher the percentage carbon content of an alkane, the greater its tendency to undergo incomplete combustion, the more CO2 it produces per gram burnt, and the lower its specific energy (since a higher %C means a lower %H, and C–H bonds release more energy per unit mass than C–C bonds do).

A graph of energy obtained per gram of CO2 produced against the number of carbon atoms in the alkane shows that methane (natural gas) releases the most energy per gram of CO2 added to the atmosphere; the higher alkanes found in petroleum, and the carbon in coal, release less energy for the same amount of CO2 pollution.

Worked example Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced when 1.00 g of pentane, C5H12, undergoes complete combustion.
n(CO2) = 5 × n(C5H12)
Solution: C5H12(l) + 8O2(g) → 5CO2(g) + 6H2O(l); M(C5H12) = (12.01 × 5) + (1.01 × 12) = 72.17 g mol−1.
n(C5H12) = 1.00 / 72.17 = 0.01386 mol → n(CO2) = 5 × 0.01386 = 0.06929 mol
mass(CO2) = 0.06929 × 44.01 = 3.05 g
Worked example What mass of carbon dioxide is produced when 2.9 g of butane, C4H10, undergoes complete combustion?
2C4H10(g) + 13O2(g) → 8CO2(g) + 10H2O(g)
Solution: M(C4H10) = (12.01 × 4) + (1.01 × 10) = 58.14 g mol−1.
n(C4H10) = 2.9 / 58.14 = 0.0499 mol. Each mole of butane gives 4 mol CO2: n(CO2) = 0.0499 × 4 = 0.1995 mol
mass(CO2) = 0.1995 × 44.01 = 8.8 g

Fossil fuels and the greenhouse effect

Atmospheric CO2 has been measured continuously at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1957/58, and shows a steady year-on-year rise (with small seasonal fluctuations from plant growth/decay in the northern hemisphere) as fossil fuel consumption has increased. Global average temperatures have risen over the same period, and CO2 is estimated to contribute about 50% of the overall warming effect.

Nature of science — correlation vs causation. Data alone cannot prove a causal link between CO2 concentration and temperature: both could be influenced by a third variable. A correlation is a statistical association between two variables; establishing causation requires a plausible mechanism (which exists for the greenhouse effect) and, ideally, controlled experiments — which are impossible on a planetary scale, so scientists rely on computer models.
Why CO2 is a greenhouse gas — vibrational modes. Greenhouse gases let short-wavelength radiation from the Sun pass through the atmosphere, but absorb longer-wavelength infrared (IR) radiation re-radiated from the Earth's surface, then re-emit this energy in all directions (including back down), warming the surface. A vibrational mode is IR active only if it changes the molecule's dipole moment. Linear CO2 has three relevant vibrational modes:

1. Symmetric stretch (both C=O bonds stretch and contract together, in phase): the molecule stays symmetrical at every instant, so there is no change in dipole moment. This mode is IR inactive.
2. Asymmetric stretch (one C=O bond lengthens while the other shortens): the two C–O bond lengths become unequal, so the molecule develops a temporary dipole moment. This mode is IR active.
3. Bending modes (two, at right angles to each other — one in the plane of the page, one out of it): the molecule bends away from its linear shape, creating a temporary dipole moment. Both bending modes are IR active.

Because three of CO2's vibrational modes are IR active, CO2 molecules absorb infrared radiation, increasing their vibrational energy, and then re-radiate part of that energy back towards the Earth's surface, contributing to the greenhouse effect and global warming.

It is estimated that CO2 levels could double within about 100 years, and that the Earth's temperature could rise by 2 °C within 50 years, with effects including changes in agriculture (crop yields), changes in species distribution (desertification, loss of cold-water fish habitats), and rising sea levels (thermal expansion and melting ice caps/glaciers).

R1.3.4 Biofuels

Biofuels are produced from the biological fixation of carbon over a short period of time through photosynthesis, making them renewable and sustainable (unlike fossil fuels, which take millions of years to form). Energy from the Sun drives photosynthesis: chlorophyll in green plants absorbs solar energy and uses it to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose.

6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) → C6H12O6(s) + 6O2(g)

Photosynthesis products can be used as food, burnt directly as a primary fuel (e.g. wood), or converted into other fuels such as ethanol. Photosynthesis is not very efficient, so only a small fraction of incident solar energy is actually trapped.

Wood vs coal. Wood is mainly cellulose (a polymer of glucose) and has not undergone the geological compression that converts plant matter into coal. Comparing them: the lower the oxygen content of a solid fuel, the greater its specific energy (wood ~50% C, ~43% O, specific energy 10–18 kJ g−1; anthracite ~95% C, ~2–3% O, specific energy 31 kJ g−1). As plant matter is exposed to increasing heat and pressure over geological time, oxygen and water vapour are progressively lost, raising the %C and the specific energy. Coal is more abundant and energy-dense than wood, but wood is renewable and sustainable if replanted; coal is difficult/expensive to mine and transport and produces SO2 (acid rain), particulates and CO2. Burning wood in an enclosed space can also produce harmful particulates, hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion.

Ethanol as a biofuel

Ethanol can be produced from biomass by fermenting plants rich in starch/sugar, using an enzyme from yeast, typically at about 37 °C in the absence of oxygen:

C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2
Worked example The enthalpy of combustion of ethanol is −1367 kJ mol−1. Calculate its specific energy in kJ g−1 and compare it with octane (ΔH° = −5470 kJ mol−1).
specific energy = |ΔHc°| / molar mass
Solution: M(ethanol, C2H5OH) = 46.08 g mol−1.
specific energy = 1367 / 46.08 = 29.67 kJ g−1

M(octane, C8H18) = 114.26 g mol−1.
specific energy = 5470 / 114.26 = 47.87 kJ g−1

Less heat is produced per gram of ethanol than octane because ethanol is already partially oxidized (it contains an O atom), so less energy is released as it is oxidized further to CO2 and H2O.

Gasohol is a mixture of 10% ethanol and 90% unleaded gasoline that can generally substitute for pure gasoline with only minor changes in performance. Advantages of ethanol as a fuel: it is renewable; it produces lower emissions of CO and NOx; it decreases a country's dependence on oil. Disadvantage: ethanol can absorb water (hydrogen bonding), causing it to separate from the hydrocarbon components of the fuel and causing corrosion. Methanol can also be used as a biofuel; it is produced by heating wood in the presence of steam and a limited amount of oxygen.

Biogas (methane)

Methane can be produced by the bacterial breakdown of plant material in the absence of oxygen, giving a clean-burning biogas similar to natural gas. Carbohydrates give a mixture that is about 50% methane, while fats (which have a lower oxygen content) give about 72% methane:

C6H12O6(s) → 3CO2(g) + 3CH4(g)  (from carbohydrate)
2C15H31COOH + 14H2O → 9CO2 + 23CH4  (from a fat)

R1.3.5 Fuel cells

A fuel cell converts the chemical energy of a fuel directly into electrical energy, without combustion, by physically separating the oxidation and reduction half-reactions at two electrodes and continuously supplying the reactants.

H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l)  ΔH° = −286 kJ mol−1

This overall reaction transfers electrons from hydrogen to oxygen (a redox reaction), so if the reactants are kept apart and connected by an external circuit, the electron transfer can generate a current directly — this is the basis of the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.

Alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell. Hydrogen gas is fed continuously to the negative electrode (anode), where it is oxidized. Oxygen gas is fed continuously to the positive electrode (cathode), where it is reduced. Both electrodes are immersed in an alkaline electrolyte, KOH(aq). Hydroxide ions, OH−, migrate through the electrolyte from the cathode to the anode, while electrons flow from the anode to the cathode through the external circuit, producing water at both electrodes overall.
Worked example Deduce the two half-equations for the alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.

Solution — anode (oxidation of H2): H goes from oxidation state 0 to +1, releasing 2 electrons per H2: H2(g) → 2H+ + 2e−. In alkaline conditions, add 2OH− to both sides to remove H+ and balance charge, then combine H+ and OH− into H2O:
H2(g) + 2OH−(aq) → 2H2O(l) + 2e−
Solution — cathode (reduction of O2): O goes from oxidation state 0 to −2, requiring 4 electrons per O2: O2(g) + 4e− + 2H2O(l) → 4OH−(aq).
O2(g) + 4e− + 2H2O(l) → 4OH−(aq)
Multiplying the anode equation by 2 to balance electrons and adding both half-equations gives the overall equation: 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l).

The electrodes are often porous carbon with added transition metal catalysts (e.g. nickel). A limitation of hydrogen fuel cells is that hydrogen is almost never found free in nature and must be extracted — from hydrocarbons/biomass (releasing CO2) or by electrolysis of water (which itself requires electricity). For the whole process to be genuinely clean, the hydrogen must be produced using a renewable energy source such as wind power.

The methanol fuel cell

Methanol is a stable liquid with a high energy density that is easy to transport, making it an attractive alternative fuel. In the direct methanol fuel cell (DMFC), methanol is oxidized at the anode under acidic conditions on a catalyst surface to form CO2; the H+ ions produced migrate across a proton exchange membrane to the cathode, where they react with oxygen to form water, while electrons travel through the external circuit.

Worked example Deduce the two half-equations for the acidic methanol–oxygen fuel cell.

Solution — anode (oxidation of CH3OH): C goes from oxidation state −2 to +4, releasing 6 electrons. Adding H2O to balance O, then H+ to balance charge (acidic conditions):
CH3OH(g) + H2O(l) → CO2(g) + 6H+(aq) + 6e−
Solution — cathode (reduction of O2): as in the hydrogen fuel cell but under acidic conditions, using H+ instead of OH−:
O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e− → 2H2O(l)
Balancing electrons (anode ×2, cathode ×3) and adding gives the overall equation: CH3OH(g) + 1½O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l).
Microbial fuel cells (MFC) harness energy from bacteria that oxidize a wide range of organic substances into CO2 and H2O; some of the released energy can be harvested as electricity. MFCs could offer a cheap, renewable energy source using organic waste (‘dirt cheap’ fuel), avoiding the need for a pure, highly explosive, hard-to-store gas like hydrogen, but the details are not assessed in this course.
Fuel cell vs primary (voltaic) cell. A fuel cell does not run out as long as fuel and oxidant are continuously supplied from external reservoirs — the electrodes themselves are not consumed. A primary cell has its chemicals sealed inside, so it stops producing current once the internal redox reaction is complete.

Summary — Guiding question revisited

  • Combustion is a reaction in which an element or compound burns in oxygen.
  • Fossil fuels (coal, crude oil, natural gas) originate from organisms that died millions of years ago; they are non-renewable.
  • Natural gas is mainly methane and is the cleanest-burning fossil fuel.
  • Complete combustion of hydrocarbons breaks the carbon chain, producing CO2 and H2O; incomplete combustion (limited oxygen, or high-%C fuel) produces CO and/or soot.
  • Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas because it absorbs infrared radiation; its large-scale production from fossil fuel combustion has driven global warming.
  • Carbon monoxide is poisonous because it binds to haemoglobin, limiting oxygen transport in the blood.
  • Most of our energy ultimately comes from the Sun; photosynthesis is an endothermic reaction that stores solar energy as chemical energy in glucose.
  • Biofuels are renewable if crops/trees are replanted, and include ethanol (fermentation), methanol, and biogas (methane).
  • Fuel cells generate electricity directly by reacting a fuel (hydrogen or methanol) with oxygen in an electrochemical cell, without combustion.
  • Fuels are compared using several criteria: specific energy, energy density, and their environmental/economic impact.

Determining enthalpy of combustion experimentally

The enthalpy of combustion of a liquid fuel can be measured experimentally by burning a known mass of fuel to heat a known mass of water and measuring the temperature rise. This uses the same calorimetry equation used throughout thermochemistry.

q = mcΔT
ΔHc = −q / n(fuel burnt)

where q is the heat released (J), m is the mass of water heated (g), c is the specific heat capacity of water (4.18 J g−1 K−1), ΔT is the temperature change (K), and n(fuel burnt) is the amount (mol) of fuel consumed, found from the mass of fuel burnt divided by its molar mass.

Worked example 25.0 g of water is heated from 21.0 °C to 87.0 °C by burning 0.83 g of ethanol in a spirit burner. Calculate the experimental enthalpy of combustion of ethanol, and comment on the result compared with the accepted value of −1367 kJ mol−1.

Solution: ΔT = 87.0 − 21.0 = 66.0 K
q = mcΔT = 25.0 × 4.18 × 66.0 = 6897 J = 6.90 kJ
n(ethanol) = 0.83 / 46.08 = 0.0180 mol
ΔHc = −6.90 / 0.0180 = −383 kJ mol−1

This experimental value is much less exothermic than the accepted −1367 kJ mol−1, mainly because of heat losses to the surroundings (the air and the calorimeter itself) and incomplete combustion of the fuel — only a fraction of the heat released actually reaches the water.
