Heat, Temperature, and Systems

Guiding questionWhat can be deduced from the temperature change that accompanies chemical or physical change?

Energy is a measure of the ability to do work, and can be transferred in different ways, including heat, light, sound and electricity, and stored in different forms of potential energy, such as chemical potential energy. It is important to distinguish between heat and temperature: heat is a method of energy transfer, while temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles making up an object.

Heat versus temperatureHeat is a process of energy transfer that occurs as a result of a temperature difference; it is not a property of an object itself. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in an object, and is a property of that object. When heat is transferred to a system, the average kinetic energy of its particles, and therefore its temperature, increases.

More heat energy is needed to raise the temperature of the water in a swimming pool by 1 K than for the water in a kettle, because the swimming pool contains far more water molecules; the same amount of heat is shared among a larger number of particles, giving a smaller average increase in kinetic energy, and hence a smaller temperature rise.

System and surroundings

The system is the sample or reaction vessel of interest; the surroundings are, in theory, everything else in the universe. This distinction allows chemists to describe energy transfers that occur during chemical and physical changes in test tubes, polystyrene cups, industrial plants and living cells alike.

Open, closed and isolated systemsAn open system can exchange both matter and energy with its surroundings. A closed system can exchange energy, but not matter, with its surroundings. An isolated system cannot exchange either matter or energy with its surroundings. Most chemical reactions in the laboratory occur in an open system, for example, a reaction in a test tube, where gases and heat can pass freely to the surroundings.

Although energy can be exchanged between a closed system and its surroundings, the law of conservation of energy means that the total energy of the system plus surroundings cannot change: any energy lost by the system is gained by the surroundings, and vice versa.

Enthalpy and the Sign of ΔH

EnthalpyThe chemical potential energy of a system is called its enthalpy, H, a name derived from the Greek for ‘heat inside’. A system acts like a reservoir of chemical potential energy. Changes in enthalpy are denoted ΔH.

When heat is added to a system from the surroundings, the enthalpy of the system increases, and ΔH is positive. When heat is released from the system to the surroundings, the enthalpy of the system decreases, and ΔH is negative.

The ‘after’ level is higher than the ‘before’ level, and an arrow labelled ‘heat energy’ points from a box labelled ‘surroundings’ into a box labelled ‘system’, showing heat flowing in and the potential energy of the system rising.

Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

Exothermic versus endothermic reactionsAn exothermic reaction gives out heat to the surroundings; ΔHreaction is negative. Most chemical reactions, including most combustion reactions and all neutralization reactions, are exothermic. An endothermic reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings; ΔHreaction is positive, and the products have more stored potential energy than the reactants.

Temperature changes during reactions in solution

When a reaction occurs in aqueous solution, its enthalpy change is best observed through the temperature change of the water acting as the solvent. In an exothermic reaction, heat released by the reaction is first transferred to the solvent, so the temperature of the water increases; heat is then eventually transferred from the water to the wider surroundings as the system returns to its initial temperature. The net result is a transfer of heat from the system to the surroundings, and the enthalpy of the system decreases. Conversely, in an endothermic reaction, heat needed by the reaction is taken from the water, so the temperature of the aqueous solvent decreases.

Temperature–time profile of an exothermic reaction in solutionA graph of temperature on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis. The line starts flat at room temperature, then rises sharply once the reactants are mixed, reaching a maximum temperature, and finally falls gradually back towards room temperature as heat is lost to the wider surroundings, illustrating that heat is first transferred to the water (causing the temperature rise) and then from the water to the surroundings (causing the slow decline).

Energy Profiles, Stability and Activation Energy

There is a natural direction for change: reactions tend to proceed in the direction of lower potential energy, or greater stability, in the same way that a person slipping on a ladder falls down rather than up. Methane is expected to burn because the products, carbon dioxide and water, are more stable (lower in potential energy) than the reactant mixture of methane and oxygen.

Energy profile of an exothermic reactionA graph with potential energy, H, on the vertical axis and reaction coordinate on the horizontal axis. The reactants are drawn as a horizontal level on the left at a higher potential energy, and the products as a horizontal level on the right at a lower potential energy, with a curve connecting them that rises to a peak (the transition state) before falling to the product level. The vertical drop from reactants to products is labelled ΔH, and is negative because the products are lower in energy than the reactants.
Energy profile of an endothermic reactionThe same style of graph, but now the products level on the right is drawn higher than the reactants level on the left, again joined by a curve that rises to a peak before descending slightly to the (still elevated) product level. The vertical rise from reactants to products is labelled ΔH, and is positive because the products are higher in energy than the reactants.

In both profiles, the height of the curve above the reactants level, up to the peak, is labelled as the activation energy, Ea – the minimum kinetic energy the reactants need in order to react, since some bonds in the reactants must be broken before new bonds in the products can form. A lighted match, for example, provides the activation energy needed for methane to react with oxygen.

Stability is relative, not absoluteStability is always relative to a particular comparison, not an absolute property. Hydrogen peroxide, for example, is stable with respect to its elements (hydrogen and oxygen), but unstable relative to its decomposition products, water and oxygen. Likewise, the sign of ΔH is a useful guide to the likely direction of change, but not a complete one: some endothermic reactions do occur spontaneously, and ΔH gives no information about the rate of a reaction – a reaction that is thermodynamically favourable may still occur too slowly to observe if its activation energy is high.

Standard Enthalpy Changes and Thermochemical Equations

Standard conditionsThe standard enthalpy change, ΔH°, is measured under standard conditions: a pressure of 100 kPa, a concentration of 1 mol dm−3 for all solutions, and all substances in their standard states. A temperature of 298 K is usually specified, although temperature is not technically part of the definition of standard state.

A thermochemical equation is a balanced chemical equation, including state symbols, together with the enthalpy change for the reaction as written. State symbols must always be included, because the enthalpy change of a reaction depends on the physical states of the reactants and products.

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l) ΔH°reaction = −890 kJ mol−1

This equation is shorthand for: one mole of gaseous methane reacts with two moles of gaseous oxygen to give one mole of gaseous carbon dioxide and two moles of liquid water, releasing 890 kJ of heat energy under standard conditions.

6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) → C6H12O6(aq) + 6O2(g) ΔH°reaction = +2802.5 kJ mol−1

This equation, representing photosynthesis, is endothermic: 2802.5 kJ of energy is absorbed when one mole of glucose is formed under standard conditions from carbon dioxide and water.

Calculating Enthalpy Changes from Temperature Changes

The increase in temperature, ΔT, when an object is heated depends on the mass of the object, the heat added, and the nature of the substance. The specific heat capacity, c, is the heat needed to raise the temperature of a unit mass of a substance by 1 K.

The q = mcΔT equationheat added (q) = mass (m) × specific heat capacity (c) × temperature change (ΔT)
q (J) = m (g) × c (J g−1 K−1) × ΔT (K)

Here, q is the heat energy transferred, in joules; m is the mass of the substance being heated or cooled, usually the mass of water in a calorimetry experiment, in grams; c is the specific heat capacity of that substance, in J g−1 K−1; and ΔT is the change in temperature, in kelvin (a temperature change of 1 K is identical to a change of 1°C, so there is no need to convert units of temperature change when using this equation). For water, c = 4.18 J g−1 K−1, a value given in the IB data booklet.

Worked example

How much heat is released when 10.0 g of copper, with specific heat capacity 0.385 J g−1 °C−1, is cooled from 85.0°C to 25.0°C?

q = m × c × ΔT = 10.0 g × 0.385 J g−1 °C−1 × (−60.0°C) = −231 J (the value is negative because the copper has lost heat).

Measuring Enthalpy Changes of Combustion

The enthalpy change of combustion, ΔHc, of a liquid fuel such as ethanol can be determined by burning it beneath a metal calorimeter containing a known mass of water. Copper is often used for the calorimeter because it conducts heat well. The heat given out by the flame heats the water, and the heat absorbed by the water is calculated from its temperature change and mass; assuming that all the heat released is transferred to the water, the heat absorbed by the water is taken to equal the heat released by combustion.

Apparatus for measuring the enthalpy of combustion of a liquid fuelA copper calorimeter containing a known mass of water sits on a clamp stand, with a thermometer inserted into the water to measure its temperature. Below the calorimeter is a burner containing the liquid fuel, with a wick that produces a flame directly under the calorimeter. A draught shield and insulating card surround the apparatus to reduce heat loss to the surroundings, and to shield the flame from air currents.
ΔHc = −m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) / n(fuel)

Here, m(H2O) is the mass of water heated, c(H2O) is the specific heat capacity of water (4.18 J g−1 K−1), ΔT(H2O) is the temperature rise of the water, and n(fuel) is the amount, in moles, of fuel burnt (found from its mass and molar mass). The negative sign is included because the enthalpy change of the reaction is opposite in sign to the temperature change of the water: ΔT is positive for an exothermic reaction, but ΔH itself is negative.

Worked example

Calculate the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol from the following data, assuming all the heat from the reaction is absorbed by the water: mass of water in copper calorimeter = 200.00 g; temperature increase of water = 13.00°C; mass of ethanol burnt = 0.45 g.

M(C2H5OH) = (12.01 × 2) + (6 × 1.01) + 16.00 = 46.08 g mol−1

ΔHc = −m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) / n(C2H5OH) = −(200 g × 4.18 J g−1 °C−1 × 13.00°C) / (0.45 g / 46.08 g mol−1)

= −1 112 883 J mol−1 = −1100 kJ mol−1 (to 2 significant figures, limited by the precision of the mass of ethanol)

The IB data booklet value is −1367 kJ mol−1. The experimental value is less exothermic because: not all the heat produced is transferred to the water; some heat is absorbed by the copper calorimeter itself and some passes to the surroundings; combustion may be incomplete owing to limited available oxygen (rather than complete, as assumed by the literature value); and the experiment was not performed under standard conditions.

Combustion reactions are exothermicCombustion reactions are generally exothermic, so ΔHc values are generally negative. Evidence of incomplete combustion, such as black soot on the underside of the calorimeter or a yellow, sooty flame, should always be recorded as qualitative data alongside the quantitative temperature measurements, since it helps explain why an experimental ΔHc value is less exothermic than the literature value.

Measuring Enthalpy Changes of Reactions in Solution

The enthalpy change of a reaction occurring in aqueous solution can be found by carrying out the reaction inside an insulated container, such as a polystyrene cup, and measuring the resulting temperature change of the water acting as the solvent. Polystyrene is used because it is a very good thermal insulator with a low heat capacity, which maximizes the fraction of heat transferred between the reaction and the water rather than lost to the surroundings.

Correcting for heat loss by extrapolation

One of the largest sources of error in a polystyrene-cup experiment is heat loss to the surroundings as soon as the temperature rises above room temperature. Consider the exothermic reaction Cu2+(aq) + Zn(s) → Cu(s) + Zn2+(aq): once zinc powder is added to copper(II) sulfate solution, the temperature rises to a maximum and then falls approximately linearly as heat is lost to the surroundings.

Temperature–time graph with extrapolation for heat lossA graph of temperature on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis. The line is flat at room temperature until zinc is added; after this point, it rises steeply to a recorded maximum temperature and then declines slowly and linearly as heat is lost to the surroundings. A dashed straight line is drawn back through this linear cooling section, extrapolated backward to the exact time at which the zinc was added; the temperature where this dashed line crosses that time is higher than the recorded maximum, and represents the maximum temperature that would have been reached if the reaction had occurred instantaneously with no heat loss.

Extrapolating the linear cooling section of the graph back to the moment of mixing gives a corrected (higher) maximum temperature, allowing for the heat lost during the time the reaction actually took to occur.

ΔHreaction = −m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) / n(limiting reactant)

Assuming no heat loss from the system, all the heat produced passes from the reaction to the water, the solution is dilute enough that its volume equals the volume of water present, and water has a density of 1.00 g cm−3, the molar enthalpy change of the reaction can be calculated by dividing the heat released by the amount, in moles, of the limiting reactant.

Worked example

Calculate the molar enthalpy change for the reaction between zinc and copper(II) sulfate, given 100 cm3 of 1.00 mol dm−3 CuSO4(aq), a corrected (extrapolated) maximum temperature of 70.0°C, and an initial temperature of 20.0°C, with excess zinc added so that CuSO4 is the limiting reactant.

ΔHreaction = −c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) / [CuSO4] = −(4.18 × (70.0 − 20.0)) / 1.00 = −209 kJ mol−1 (for 1 mole of a 1 mol dm−3 solution)

Enthalpy of neutralization

The enthalpy change of a neutralization reaction between an acid and a base in solution can be measured the same way, again using q = mcΔT with the total volume of solution (assumed to have the density and specific heat capacity of water) as the mass of water heated.

Worked example

Sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid solutions, both 1.00 mol dm−3, were mixed with a total volume of 120.0 cm3. The largest temperature increase occurred using V(NaOH) = 80.0 cm3 and V(H2SO4) = 40.0 cm3, with an initial temperature of 25.0°C rising to a maximum of 33.5°C. The literature value for the enthalpy of neutralization is −57.5 kJ mol−1.

(b) Heat produced, assuming 120.0 cm3 of solution contains 120.0 g of water and all the heat produced passes into the water: ΔHreaction = −m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) = −120.0 g × 4.18 J g−1 K−1 × (33.5 − 25.0) K = −4264 J

(c) Amount of NaOH reacted: n(NaOH) = 1.00 mol dm−3 × (80.0 / 1000) dm3 = 0.0800 mol. Heat produced per mole of NaOH: ΔHreaction = −4264 J / 0.0800 mol = −53 300 J mol−1 = −53.3 kJ mol−1

(d) Percentage error = |(−57.5) − (−53.3)| / |−57.5| × 100% = 4.2 / 57.5 × 100% = 7% (to 1 significant figure)

The calculated value assumes no heat loss from the system, that all the heat is transferred to the water, and that the solutions contain exactly 120 g of water; there are also uncertainties in the measured temperature, volume and concentration values. The literature value, by contrast, assumes standard conditions.

A common error to avoidWhen reporting an enthalpy change calculated from calorimetry data, always include the correct units (usually kJ mol−1) and the correct sign: forgetting the negative sign for an exothermic reaction such as neutralization is one of the most common errors made in this type of calculation.

Enthalpy Cycles: A First Look at Hess's Law

Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2(l), can be formed directly from hydrogen and oxygen, or it can decompose into water and oxygen. Because H2O2(l) can be converted into H2O(l) either directly or by way of its elements, these enthalpy changes are linked.

Enthalpy cycle linking hydrogen, oxygen, hydrogen peroxide and waterA potential energy diagram with three horizontal levels. The highest level, on the left, is labelled H2(g) + O2(g). A downward arrow labelled ΔH1 leads from this level to a middle level labelled H2O2(l). A further downward arrow labelled ΔH2 leads from the middle level down to the lowest level, labelled H2O(l) + ½O2(g). A third arrow, drawn directly from the highest level down to the lowest level, is labelled ΔH3, representing the same overall change reached in a single step.

Since the direct route (ΔH3) and the two-step indirect route (ΔH1 followed by ΔH2) both start from the same initial state and end at the same final state, the overall enthalpy change must be the same by either route:

ΔH3 = ΔH1 + ΔH2

This relationship is an example of Hess's law: the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the pathway taken between the same initial and final states. Hess's law follows from the law of conservation of energy introduced earlier in this chapter, and is explored in full, together with its use in calculating enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly, in the next chapter, Reactivity 1.2.

Guiding Question Revisited

What can be deduced from the temperature change that accompanies chemical or physical change?
  • A system is the reaction container and its contents; the surroundings are everything outside the system. Exothermic reactions release heat energy to the surroundings (ΔH negative); endothermic reactions absorb heat energy from the surroundings (ΔH positive).
  • Heat is a process of energy transfer, while temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles; the heat transferred is related to a temperature change through the specific heat capacity, c, using q = mcΔT for a pure substance.
  • Heat changes in the laboratory are usually determined from the temperature change of a known mass of water: heated in a copper calorimeter for enthalpy of combustion experiments, or acting as the solvent in an insulated polystyrene-cup calorimeter for reactions in solution.
  • A thermochemical equation gives the balanced equation for a reaction, with state symbols, together with its corresponding enthalpy change, measured under standard conditions (100 kPa, 1 mol dm−3 solutions, substances in their standard states, usually at 298 K).
  • An energy profile diagram plots potential energy against reaction coordinate, showing reactants above products for an exothermic reaction and below products for an endothermic reaction, with the activation energy, Ea, as the height of the energy barrier above the reactants.
  • Hess's law – that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the pathway taken, provided the initial and final states are the same – can be introduced through simple enthalpy cycles, and is developed fully in the next chapter.
