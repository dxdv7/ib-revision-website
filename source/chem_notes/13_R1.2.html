Energy Changes, Bonds, and the Law of Conservation of Energy

Guiding questionHow does application of the law of conservation of energy help us to predict energy changes during reactions?

Every chemical reaction involves breaking existing bonds in the reactants and forming new bonds in the products. Separating atoms that are held together by a bond requires energy, while bringing atoms together to form a new bond releases energy. The overall enthalpy change of a reaction is simply the balance between these two opposing energy contributions: a reaction is exothermic overall when the energy released by forming the new bonds is greater than the energy needed to break the old ones, and endothermic overall when the reverse is true.

The law of conservation of energy states that the total energy of the universe is constant: energy can be transformed from one form into another, but it can never be created or destroyed. This law places a powerful restriction on chemical reactions that can occur by more than one route.

Figure R1.2.1: A general energy cycleThree labelled boxes, A, B and C, arranged in a triangle. An arrow labelled ΔH1 points from A to B, an arrow labelled ΔH2 points from B to C, and a third arrow labelled ΔH3 points directly from A to C. The cycle shows that substance A can be converted into substance C either directly (via ΔH3) or indirectly by first forming B (via ΔH1 and then ΔH2).

If we imagine travelling all the way around this cycle – from A to B, from B to C, and then back from C to A – we end up exactly where we started, so the net enthalpy change for the complete loop must be zero. Travelling backward from C to A is the reverse of ΔH3, so it has enthalpy change −ΔH3. This gives:

ΔH1 + ΔH2 − ΔH3 = 0

ΔH1, ΔH2 and ΔH3 are the enthalpy changes of the three individual steps shown in the cycle above. Rearranging this equation gives ΔH3 = ΔH1 + ΔH2, which means the enthalpy change of the direct route can be calculated from the enthalpy changes of the two-step indirect route, even if the direct reaction is difficult, dangerous, or impossible to carry out in the laboratory. If this were not true – if the enthalpy change around the cycle were not zero – it would be possible to generate energy continuously simply by going around the cycle repeatedly, which would violate the law of conservation of energy. Energy cycles like this one are one of the most powerful tools in chemistry for calculating enthalpy changes indirectly.

Bond Enthalpy

A covalent bond exists because of the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the positively charged nuclei of the bonded atoms. Separating two bonded atoms therefore always requires an input of energy.

Bond enthalpyThe bond enthalpy is the energy needed to break one mole of a given covalent bond in gaseous molecules, under standard conditions.

For a simple diatomic molecule such as chlorine, the bond enthalpy can be measured directly:

Cl2(g) → 2Cl(g) ΔH° = +242 kJ mol−1

The enthalpy change is positive because breaking a bond is always an endothermic process; energy must be supplied to pull the two attracted atoms apart.

Why bond enthalpies are average values

In molecules containing more than two atoms, breaking apparently identical bonds one at a time can require different amounts of energy, because the local chemical environment of a bond changes as neighboring bonds are broken. For example, breaking the two O−H bonds in a single water molecule, one after the other, requires different amounts of energy for each step:

H2O(g) → H(g) + OH(g) ΔH° = +502 kJ mol−1
OH(g) → H(g) + O(g) ΔH° = +427 kJ mol−1

To obtain a single, useful value for the strength of an O−H bond, chemists take the mean of these two values, since there are two equivalent O−H bonds overall in the original molecule: E(O−H) = (502 + 427)/2 = 464.5 kJ mol−1. This kind of averaging is repeated over many different molecules that contain the same type of bond, producing the tabulated average bond enthalpy values found in the data booklet. Because they are averaged over a range of chemical environments, these values will generally differ somewhat from the bond enthalpy measured experimentally in any one specific molecule – the data-booklet value is a useful and broadly reliable estimate, not an exact figure for every compound containing that bond.

Bond enthalpies always refer to the gaseous stateAll tabulated bond enthalpies apply strictly to bonds within gaseous molecules. This means that any additional enthalpy changes caused by forming or breaking intermolecular forces (for example, when a substance condenses from gas to liquid) are never included in a bond enthalpy value or in a calculation based on bond enthalpies.

Bond enthalpy, bond length and multiple bonds

Multiple bonds, which involve more shared electron pairs, are generally shorter and have substantially higher bond enthalpies than single bonds between the same two elements. For example, the C=C double bond (bond enthalpy +614 kJ mol−1, bond length 134 pm) is both shorter and much stronger than the C−C single bond (+346 kJ mol−1, 154 pm), and the C=O double bond (+804 kJ mol−1, 122 pm) is shorter and far stronger than the C−O single bond (+358 kJ mol−1, 143 pm). More generally, a shorter bond length between two given elements corresponds to a stronger, higher-enthalpy bond, because the shared electrons are held closer to both nuclei.

Bond enthalpy and polarity

Comparing the bond enthalpy of a polar bond, X−Y, with the average of the bond enthalpies of the corresponding non-polar bonds, X−X and Y−Y, reveals a consistent pattern.

In every case the polar H−X bond is stronger than the simple average of the two non-polar bonds, H−H and X−X, and this extra strength – shown in the final column – is greatest for the most polar bond, H−Cl, and smallest for the least polar bond, H−I. This pattern is general: a polar bond between two different elements, X and Y, is stronger than the average of the non-polar X−X and Y−Y bonds, and the size of the extra strength increases with the polarity of the X−Y bond. This relationship is, in fact, the basis for one method of calculating Pauling electronegativity values from bond enthalpy data.

Bond-Making Is Exothermic

Exactly as much energy is released when a bond is formed as is absorbed when the same bond is broken. Bond-breaking is endothermic because it separates particles that are held together by an attractive force; bond-making is exothermic because it brings together particles that are mutually attracted.

H(g) + H(g) → H2(g) ΔH° = −436 kJ mol−1

The negative sign confirms that forming a bond from two separate atoms releases energy to the surroundings.

An upward arrow from XY(g) to the separated atoms represents bond-breaking, labelled as requiring an input of energy since it moves the system to higher potential energy; a downward arrow from the separated atoms to XY(g) represents bond-making, labelled as releasing energy since it moves the system to lower potential energy. Both arrows have the same length, showing that the energy absorbed on breaking a bond exactly equals the energy released on forming it.
Endothermic and exothermic processesEndothermic processes involve separating particles that are held together by a force of attraction, such as breaking a covalent bond or removing an electron from an atom to form a cation. Exothermic processes involve bringing together particles that are mutually attracted, such as forming a covalent bond or combining oppositely charged ions into an ionic lattice.

Calculating Enthalpy Changes from Bond Enthalpies

In a chemical reaction, the bonds in the reactants must first be broken (an endothermic process) before the bonds in the products can form (an exothermic process). The overall enthalpy change of the reaction is exothermic if the bonds formed are collectively stronger than the bonds broken, and endothermic if the bonds broken are collectively stronger than the bonds formed.

ΔH° = ΣE(bonds broken) − ΣE(bonds formed)

ΔH° is the standard enthalpy change of the reaction, ΣE(bonds broken) is the total bond enthalpy of every bond broken in the reactants, and ΣE(bonds formed) is the total bond enthalpy of every bond formed in the products. To apply this equation, first draw out the full structural (or Lewis) formula of every reactant and product so that every individual bond can be identified and counted.

Worked example

Use bond enthalpies to calculate the enthalpy of combustion of methane, CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g).

Bonds broken: 4 C−H bonds (4 × 414 = 1656 kJ mol−1) and 2 O=O bonds (2 × 498 = 996 kJ mol−1), giving a total of 1656 + 996 = 2652 kJ mol−1.

Bonds formed: 2 C=O bonds (2 × 804 = 1608 kJ mol−1) and 4 O−H bonds (4 × 463 = 1852 kJ mol−1), giving a total of 1608 + 1852 = 3460 kJ mol−1.

ΔH° = 2652 − 3460 = −808 kJ mol−1. This should be compared with the experimental value of −891 kJ mol−1 (measured with liquid water as the product). The difference arises because the bond enthalpy method assumes water forms as a gas, while the standard enthalpy of combustion is measured with liquid water as the product; using average, rather than exact, bond enthalpies also introduces a small additional error.

Worked example

Use bond enthalpies to calculate the enthalpy change for the reaction between ethene and chlorine, C2H4(g) + Cl2(g) → CH2ClCH2Cl(g).

Bonds broken: 1 C=C bond (614 kJ mol−1) and 1 Cl−Cl bond (242 kJ mol−1), giving a total of 614 + 242 = 856 kJ mol−1.

Bonds formed: 1 C−C bond (346 kJ mol−1) and 2 C−Cl bonds (2 × 324 = 648 kJ mol−1), giving a total of 346 + 648 = 994 kJ mol−1.

ΔH° = 856 − 994 = −138 kJ mol−1. Notice that only the bonds that actually change need to be counted – the four C−H bonds present in ethene are unaffected by the reaction and can be left out of the calculation entirely, since they appear unchanged on both sides.

A limitation of the bond enthalpy methodBecause bond enthalpies apply only to the gaseous state, this method cannot give an accurate enthalpy change for any reaction in which a reactant or product is a liquid or solid, since it completely ignores the intermolecular forces (or ionic/metallic bonding) that would need to be broken or formed. It also relies on average bond enthalpy data, so answers calculated this way are always only close estimates of the true, experimentally measured value.

Hess's Law

Hess's lawThe enthalpy change for a chemical reaction is independent of the pathway taken between the initial and final states, provided the initial reactants and final products (and their physical states) are the same.

Hess's law is a direct consequence of the law of conservation of energy, applied to a cycle of chemical reactions in exactly the same way as the general A/B/C cycle introduced earlier in this chapter. Consider the formation of water from hydrogen and oxygen, which can be imagined as happening either directly, or in two steps via hydrogen peroxide:

H2(g) + O2(g) → H2O2(l) ΔH1 = −188 kJ mol−1
H2O2(l) → H2O(l) + ½O2(g) ΔH2 = −98 kJ mol−1
H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l) ΔH3 = −286 kJ mol−1

Adding the first two equations together and cancelling the H2O2(l) and the extra ½O2(g) that appear on both sides reproduces the third equation exactly, and ΔH1 + ΔH2 = −188 + (−98) = −286 kJ mol−1, which is indeed equal to ΔH3. The overall enthalpy change of forming water from hydrogen and oxygen is exactly the same, whether the reaction happens directly in one step, or indirectly in two steps by way of hydrogen peroxide.

Figure R1.2.3: Energy level diagram illustrating Hess's lawA potential energy diagram with three horizontal levels connected by vertical or diagonal arrows. The highest level, on the left, is labelled H2(g) + O2(g); a downward arrow labelled ΔH1 = −188 kJ mol−1 leads to a middle level labelled H2O2(l); a further downward arrow labelled ΔH2 = −98 kJ mol−1 leads to the lowest level, labelled H2O(l) + ½O2(g). A third, longer arrow drawn directly from the highest level straight down to the lowest level is labelled ΔH3 = −286 kJ mol−1, showing visually that the two-step indirect drop (ΔH1 then ΔH2) covers exactly the same total change in potential energy as the single direct drop (ΔH3).
Reversing a reaction reverses the sign of ΔHIf a thermochemical equation must be flipped (written in the opposite direction) to fit into a cycle, the sign of its enthalpy change must also be reversed. For example, if X → Y has ΔH = +50 kJ mol−1, then Y → X has ΔH = −50 kJ mol−1. If an equation must also be multiplied by some factor to balance the cycle, its enthalpy change must be multiplied by the same factor.

Using Hess's law to find enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly

The real power of Hess's law is that it allows the enthalpy change of a reaction to be calculated even when that reaction cannot be carried out directly in the laboratory – for example, because it is too slow, too dangerous, or because the elements involved simply do not react together directly to give a clean product. The only requirement is that a cycle of other, measurable reactions can be constructed that connects the same starting reactants to the same final products; the individual steps of the cycle may even be entirely hypothetical, as long as each individual equation balances correctly.

Worked example

Calculate the standard enthalpy change for the formation of sulfur dioxide, S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g), using the following data:

(1) S(s) + 1½O2(g) → SO3(g) ΔH° = −395 kJ mol−1

(2) SO2(g) + ½O2(g) → SO3(g) ΔH° = −98 kJ mol−1

Equation (1) already starts from the correct reactant, S(s). Equation (2) needs to be reversed so that SO2(g) appears as a product rather than a reactant, which also reverses the sign of its enthalpy change to +98 kJ mol−1: SO3(g) → SO2(g) + ½O2(g). Adding this reversed equation to equation (1) and cancelling SO3(g), which appears on both sides, along with ½O2(g) appearing on both sides too, leaves S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g), with ΔH° = −395 + 98 = −297 kJ mol−1.

Worked example

Calculate ΔH° for C(graphite) + ½O2(g) → CO(g), given: C(graphite) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −394 kJ mol−1, and CO(g) + ½O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −283 kJ mol−1.

Both given equations produce CO2(g), which does not appear in the target equation, so the second equation must be reversed to remove CO2(g) as a product: CO2(g) → CO(g) + ½O2(g), ΔH° = +283 kJ mol−1. Adding this to the first equation and cancelling CO2(g) from both sides gives C(graphite) + O2(g) + CO2(g) → CO2(g) + CO(g) + ½O2(g), which simplifies to C(graphite) + ½O2(g) → CO(g). The enthalpy change is ΔH° = −394 + 283 = −111 kJ mol−1. This is a good example of Hess's law in action, since the direct combustion of carbon to carbon monoxide (rather than carbon dioxide) is very difficult to control and measure experimentally.

Figure R1.2.4: A Hess's law cycle for an indirect formation reactionA triangular cycle with the target reactants at the top left, the target product at the top right, and a third species – the common product of two combustion or oxidation reactions – positioned at the bottom of the triangle. A horizontal arrow along the top, labelled with a question mark, represents the target (often unmeasurable) reaction directly connecting the reactants to the product. Two diagonal arrows lead down from the top left and top right corners respectively to the bottom species, each labelled with a known, measurable enthalpy change. Because both diagonal routes lead to the same species at the bottom, the enthalpy change of the top route can always be found by combining the two diagonal, measurable enthalpy changes.

Guiding Question Revisited

How does application of the law of conservation of energy help us to predict energy changes during reactions?
  • Every reaction involves breaking bonds in the reactants (endothermic) and forming new bonds in the products (exothermic); the overall enthalpy change is the balance between these two contributions, ΔH° = ΣE(bonds broken) − ΣE(bonds formed).
  • Average bond enthalpies are averaged over many similar molecules and always refer to bonds in the gaseous state, so calculations based on them are estimates rather than exact experimental values, and cannot account for intermolecular forces in liquids or solids.
  • Hess's law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken between the same initial and final states; it is a direct consequence of the law of conservation of energy, since any closed energy cycle must have a total enthalpy change of zero.
  • Energy cycles built from Hess's law let chemists calculate enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly in the laboratory, using only reactions that can be measured, as long as every equation in the cycle balances.
  • When combining equations in a cycle, reversing a reaction reverses the sign of its enthalpy change, and multiplying a reaction by a factor multiplies its enthalpy change by the same factor.
