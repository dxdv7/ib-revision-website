R3.4 Electron-pair sharing reactions — Overview

When one reactant shares an electron pair with another, a coordination bond forms. This is a common theme across acid–base chemistry, transition metal chemistry and organic chemistry. This topic focuses on two examples involving organic compounds: nucleophilic substitution of halogenoalkanes, and electrophilic addition of alkenes.

Guiding question: What happens when reactants share their electron pairs with others?

R3.4.1 Nucleophiles

A nucleophile is a reactant that forms a bond to its reaction partner (the electrophile) by donating both electrons of the new bond. Nucleophiles tend to be electron rich: they have one or more lone pairs and may carry a negative charge.

Examples of nucleophiles. Neutral: H2O, NH3, ROH (alcohols), RNH2 (amines). Anions: OH−, F−, Cl−, Br−, I−, CN−, R− (carbanions).

The name nucleophile means ‘nucleus-loving’: being electron rich, nucleophiles are attracted to positive charges such as an atomic nucleus (or a region of partial positive charge).

Coordination bond formation. When a nucleophile (labelled Nuc, shown with a lone pair) reacts with an electrophile (labelled A, which is electron deficient), a curved arrow is drawn starting at the lone pair on the nucleophile and pointing towards the electrophile. This arrow shows both bonding electrons moving from the nucleophile to form a brand-new covalent bond between Nuc and A in the product. Because both electrons of this new bond originated from the nucleophile alone, the new bond is specifically called a coordination bond (or dative covalent bond).

R3.4.2 Nucleophilic substitution reactions

In a nucleophilic substitution reaction, a nucleophile donates an electron pair to form a new bond to a substrate, while another bond in the substrate breaks, releasing a leaving group.

Halogenoalkanes as substrates

Halogenoalkanes are polar: the halogen is more electronegative than carbon, so it pulls the shared C–X electron density towards itself. This leaves the halogen with a partial negative charge (δ−) and the carbon atom bonded to it with a partial positive charge (δ+), making that carbon electron deficient and attractive to nucleophiles.

CH3Cl + OH− → CH3OH + Cl−  (chloromethane → methanol)
C3H7Br + OH− → C3H7OH + Br−  (1-bromopropane → propan-1-ol)

Because the halogen can be substituted by many different nucleophiles, halogenoalkanes are very useful intermediates for converting one class of organic compound into another in synthesis pathways. Halogens make good leaving groups because they form relatively weak bonds to carbon, and their high electronegativity makes the attached carbon electron deficient and susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

General mechanism, R–X + Nu:− → R–Nu + X−. The substrate R–X is drawn with the carbon atom bonded to the leaving group X (a halogen). A curved arrow starts at a lone pair on the nucleophile, Nu, and points towards that carbon atom, showing the nucleophile donating an electron pair to form the new C–Nu bond. At the same time, a second curved arrow starts on the C–X bond (the bonding pair) and points towards X, showing that bond breaking heterolytically so that both electrons leave with the departing group, forming X−. The bond that forms (C–Nu) and the bond that breaks (C–X) both involve the same carbon atom — the one originally bonded to the leaving group.

For example, in the reaction of hydroxide with chloromethane: a curved arrow runs from a lone pair on the O of OH− to the carbon of CH3Cl (the δ+ carbon), while a second curved arrow runs from the C–Cl bond to the Cl atom, releasing it as Cl− and forming CH3OH.

Neutral nucleophiles

If the nucleophile is a neutral species such as water, the immediate product carries a positive charge, because the O atom has donated a lone pair without losing its own H atoms. This intermediate then loses a proton (H+) to give a neutral alcohol product. If the leaving group was a halide ion, it can combine with this released H+ to form a hydrogen halide.

Overall: H2O + RX → ROH + HX
Mechanism with a neutral nucleophile (water). A curved arrow runs from a lone pair on the O of H2O to the electron-deficient carbon of R–X, while a second curved arrow runs from the C–X bond to X, releasing X−. This first gives a positively charged intermediate, R–OH2+, because the oxygen (still bonded to its two original H atoms) has formed a third bond and so carries a formal positive charge. A third curved arrow then runs from one O–H bond in this intermediate to the O atom, releasing H+ and leaving the neutral alcohol product, R–OH.

R3.4.3 Heterolytic fission

Heterolytic fission is the breaking of a covalent bond such that both bonding electrons end up on one of the two fragments, forming a pair of oppositely charged ions. (‘Hetero-’ means ‘different’, reflecting the unequal split of the bonding electrons between the two products.) When the bond is between two different atoms, the more electronegative atom keeps both electrons and becomes the negative ion.

Cl–Cl → Cl+ + Cl−
H–Cl → H+ + Cl−
Homolytic vs heterolytic fission. Homolytic fission splits the bonding electron pair equally, one electron to each fragment, producing two neutral radicals (each with an unpaired electron); this is shown using a single-barbed curly arrow (a ‘fish hook’) for each unpaired electron that moves. Heterolytic fission sends both bonding electrons to one fragment, producing two oppositely charged ions; this is shown using one full double-headed curly arrow, since a whole electron pair moves together.
Heterolytic fission of H–Cl. A single curved (double-barbed) arrow starts on the H–Cl bonding pair and points towards the Cl atom, showing both electrons moving onto chlorine. This leaves Cl as Cl− (now holding both electrons, plus its original lone pairs) and H as H+ (having lost its only electron). The tail of the arrow always shows where the electron pair starts (the bond), and the head shows where it ends up (the more electronegative atom).

The reverse of heterolytic fission is the formation of a coordination bond: a nucleophile donates an electron pair to an electrophile, and this is shown with the same style of curved arrow, running from the nucleophile's lone pair to the electrophile.

R3.4.4 Electrophiles

An electrophile is a reactant that forms a bond to its reaction partner (the nucleophile) by accepting both bonding electrons from that partner. Electrophiles tend to be electron deficient, or contain an electron-deficient region, so they often carry a positive charge or partial positive charge.

Examples of electrophiles. Neutral: HX (hydrogen halides), X2 (halogens), H2O, halogenoalkanes (RX). Cations: H+, NO2+, NO+, CH3+, R+ (carbocations).
Nature of science. Definitions in science must be broad enough to be inclusive. Describing electrophiles simply as ‘electron deficient’ would wrongly exclude molecules such as bromine, Br2, which is not itself electron deficient but still acts as an electrophile in addition reactions (its bond becomes polarized as it approaches an electron-rich double bond).

R3.4.5 Electrophilic addition of alkenes

Alkenes, general formula CnH2n, are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing a carbon–carbon double bond. The C=C double bond has a high electron density, making alkenes attractive to electrophiles. One of the two bonds making up the double bond is relatively easily broken, creating two new bonding positions on the carbon atoms; this allows alkenes to undergo addition reactions, in which an electrophile X–Y adds across the double bond so that X and Y each bond to one of the two former double-bond carbons, giving a saturated product.

Addition of water (hydration)

Water is a poor electrophile on its own, so hydration of an alkene requires a strong acid catalyst (e.g. concentrated sulfuric acid in the laboratory, or phosphoric acid adsorbed on silica industrially). The reaction converts an alkene into an alcohol.

CH2=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH2OH  (conc. H2SO4 catalyst; ethene → ethanol)

This reaction is industrially important because ethanol is manufactured on a large scale as a solvent (and is used, for example, in hand sanitizers at a minimum recommended concentration of 60% to be effective against bacteria and viruses).

Addition of halogens

Halogens (X2) react rapidly with alkenes at room temperature to give dihalogeno compounds, with one halogen atom adding to each of the two former double-bond carbons. The reaction is accompanied by loss of colour of the halogen.

CH3CH=CH2 + Br2 → CH3CHBrCH2Br  (propene → 1,2-dibromopropane)
Bromine water test — alkanes vs alkenes. Halogen solutions have distinct colours (Cl2 green, Br2 orange/brown, I2 purple), especially vivid in non-polar solvents. Bromine water is used to distinguish alkanes from alkenes: alkenes decolorize bromine water rapidly at room temperature in the dark, via electrophilic addition, because the electron-rich C=C double bond attracts the Br2 electrophile. Alkanes only react with bromine water via a radical substitution mechanism, which requires UV light to initiate it — so alkanes do not decolorize bromine water in the dark.
Nature of science. The bromine water test is simple but relies on qualitative, subjective visual observation (loss of colour, judged by eye). Wherever possible, experiments should be designed to produce quantitative, objective data (for example using a colorimeter/spectrophotometer) that can be analyzed more rigorously.

Addition of hydrogen halides

Hydrogen halides (HCl, HBr, HI) react rapidly with alkenes in solution at room temperature to give halogenoalkanes.

CH2=CH2 + HCl → CH3CH2Cl  (ethene → chloroethane)
Reactivity order: HI > HBr > HCl. This order reflects the decreasing strength of the hydrogen–halogen bond down group 17: the H–I bond is the weakest (largest, most diffuse iodine orbital gives poorest orbital overlap), so it is broken most easily and HI reacts fastest, while H–Cl is the strongest of the three and reacts most slowly.

Because alkenes can react with a range of electrophiles to give alcohols, halogenoalkanes and dihalogenoalkanes, and these products can be converted into many further classes of compound, alkenes are described as very useful ‘starting molecules’ in organic synthesis pathways (for example, in the industrial production of many plastics).

Summary of alkene addition pathways. Starting from an alkene: reaction with H2O (conc. H2SO4 catalyst) gives an alcohol; reaction with Br2 gives a dihalogenoalkane, visibly losing the brown/orange colour of the bromine as it reacts; reaction with HCl gives a halogenoalkane. In every case the electrophile adds across the C=C double bond, converting the alkene into a saturated product.
Nature of science. Alkanes (as fuels) and alkenes (as chemical feedstock for plastics) have had an enormous impact on society, but at a significant environmental cost (climate change, air pollution, plastic disposal) whose long-term consequences are still being determined. Scientists who develop such technologies also bear responsibility for helping address the resulting problems.

Summary tables

Saturated vs unsaturated. Saturated compounds contain only single bonds (e.g. alkanes). Unsaturated compounds contain double or triple bonds (e.g. alkenes, arenes).
Electrophile vs nucleophile. An electrophile (‘electron-seeking’) forms a covalent bond by accepting both bonding electrons from another reactant; electrophiles are typically positive ions or have a partial positive charge, and are attracted to electron-rich parts of molecules (examples: NO2+, H+, Br+). A nucleophile (‘nucleus-seeking’) forms a covalent bond by donating both bonding electrons to another reactant; nucleophiles have a lone pair and may carry a negative charge, and are attracted to electron-deficient parts of molecules (examples: Cl−, OH−, NH3).
Addition vs substitution reactions. Addition reactions occur when two reactants combine to form a single product; they are characteristic of unsaturated compounds, e.g. C2H4 + Br2 → C2H4Br2. Substitution reactions occur when one atom or group in a compound is replaced by a different atom or group; they are characteristic of saturated and aromatic compounds, e.g. CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl.
Homolytic vs heterolytic fission (bond-breaking). Homolytic fission splits the bonding pair equally between the two products, giving two radicals, each with an unpaired electron. Heterolytic fission sends both bonding electrons to one product, giving two oppositely charged ions.
Curly arrow convention. A curly arrow represents the movement of electrons and is drawn starting at the site of electron availability (for example a lone pair, or a bonding pair in a bond that is breaking) and ending at the site of electron deficiency (for example an atom bearing a partial or full positive charge). A ‘normal’ double-barbed curly arrow (a full arrowhead with two barbs) represents the movement of a whole pair of electrons. Where a mechanism involves several steps, the electrons are ultimately transferred to an atom or group that then detaches as the leaving group. A single-barbed curly arrow, called a ‘fish hook’ (an arrowhead with only one barb), instead represents the movement of a single electron, and is used specifically for reactions involving radicals (homolytic fission and radical mechanisms).

Summary — Guiding question revisited

  • Donation of an electron pair from an electron-rich species to an electron-deficient species forms a coordination bond; curly arrows show the movement of electron pairs in these reactions.
  • A nucleophile forms a bond by donating a pair of electrons to its reaction partner.
  • In nucleophilic substitution, a leaving group forms as another bond breaks while the nucleophile bonds to the substrate.
  • Heterolytic fission of a covalent bond produces two oppositely charged ions.
  • An electrophile forms a bond by accepting a pair of electrons from its reaction partner.
  • Alkenes undergo electrophilic addition reactions in which the carbon–carbon double bond breaks, converting the alkene into a saturated product.
  • Halogenoalkanes are the key example of nucleophilic substitution; alkenes are the key example of electrophilic addition.
