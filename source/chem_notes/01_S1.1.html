Guiding Question

How can we model the particulate nature of matter?

Everything you can touch, drink or breathe is built from atoms — particles so small that no human eye, and no ordinary microscope, can see them directly. Because chemists cannot observe atoms directly, they build models: simplified pictures that explain and predict how matter behaves. This sub-topic introduces the most basic of these models, the particulate model of matter, and uses it to explain how we classify substances and why matter exists in different physical states.

Nature of scienceA model is an artificial representation of a natural phenomenon, built to explain observations and to make predictions. Every model has limitations, and models are revised or replaced as new evidence emerges. The particulate model (that all matter is made of discrete, indivisible-in-practice particles) has survived since it was proposed by Democritus in ancient Greece, but it has been continually refined — for example once it became clear that atoms themselves contain smaller particles.

Classifying Matter: Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

The first step in understanding chemistry is to sort matter into categories. All matter can be classified as either a pure substance or a mixture. Pure substances are further divided into elements and compounds, while mixtures are divided into homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures.

ElementA pure chemical substance made of atoms that all have the same number of protons. Elements are the primary constituents of matter and cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical means. Around 100 elements combine, in different ratios and arrangements, to form every substance in the universe. Each element has its own internationally agreed chemical symbol (for example C for carbon, K for potassium, Hg for mercury), a system introduced by the Swedish chemist Berzelius in 1814.
CompoundA substance formed when atoms of two or more different elements are chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. The physical and chemical properties of a compound are completely different from the properties of the elements it is made from. Sodium chloride (NaCl), for instance, is a harmless white crystalline solid, even though it is made from sodium (a metal that reacts violently with water) and chlorine (a toxic gas).

The chemical formula of a compound combines the symbols of its elements, with subscripts showing how many atoms of each element are present in one unit of the compound. Some common examples: water, H2O; methane, CH4; ammonia, NH3; magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)2; copper sulfate, CuSO4; sulfuric acid, H2SO4; glucose, C6H12O6.

MixtureMore than one element or compound physically combined, in no fixed ratio, without any chemical bonding between the components. Because a mixture has no fixed composition, it cannot be represented by a single chemical formula, and because there is no chemical bond to break, mixtures can be separated by physical methods.
Compounds vs. mixturesA compound has a fixed ratio of elements, is chemically bonded, has properties completely different from its constituent elements, and can only be separated back into elements by a chemical reaction. A mixture has a variable composition, involves no chemical bonding, and each component keeps its own original properties (nitrogen and oxygen in air still behave exactly as they do when pure), so a mixture can be separated by purely physical means.

Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Mixtures

Mixtures themselves are divided into two types depending on how uniform they are.

Homogeneous mixtureA mixture with uniform composition and properties throughout, so that the separate components cannot usually be distinguished by eye. Air (a mixture of gases, mainly N2 and O2), salt water, and metal alloys such as bronze (copper and tin) or steel are all homogeneous mixtures.
Heterogeneous mixtureA mixture with non-uniform composition, so that its properties differ from one region of the sample to another. Water and oil, or sand and salt, are heterogeneous mixtures; the separate components can usually be seen or distinguished.

Whether a mixture forms a uniform (homogeneous) blend depends on whether the forces of attraction between the different particles in the mixture are similar in strength and nature to the forces between particles of the same substance. This idea is explored fully once intermolecular forces are studied, but the basic rule is that “like dissolves in like”: substances with similar inter-particle attractions mix uniformly, while dissimilar ones tend to separate into visible layers or regions.

Filtration apparatusA conical filter funnel lined with filter paper sits inside the neck of a beaker or conical flask. The mixture (for example, sand suspended in salt water) is poured into the funnel. The insoluble solid (sand) is trapped on the filter paper as the residue, while the liquid (salt water) passes through the paper and drips down into the collecting vessel below as the filtrate. Afterward, gently heating the filtrate causes the water to evaporate, leaving solid salt crystals behind (crystallization/recrystallisation).
Distillation apparatusA round-bottomed flask containing the mixture (for example, salt water) sits over a heat source. As the flask is heated, the substance with the lower boiling point (the solvent, water) vaporizes first and rises into a sloped side-arm connected to a long glass condenser tube. The condenser is a tube-within-a-tube, with cold water constantly flowing through the outer jacket in the opposite direction to the vapour; this cools the vapour so that it condenses back into a liquid inside the inner tube. The condensed liquid (pure water) drips out of the far end of the condenser into a collecting beaker, while the higher-boiling solute (salt) is left behind in the original flask.
Paper chromatography apparatusSmall spots of the sample solutions (for example, different food dyes) are placed in a line near the bottom edge of a strip of chromatography paper, above the level any solvent will reach. The paper is lowered into a shallow layer of solvent inside a closed container (jar or tank), with the spots kept above the solvent’s surface. The container is sealed so the atmosphere inside becomes saturated with solvent vapour. As the solvent is drawn up the paper, different dissolved components travel at different speeds depending on how strongly each is adsorbed onto the paper versus how soluble it is in the moving solvent, so a single spot separates into several spots spread out vertically up the paper, each corresponding to one component of the original mixture.
Solvation, filtration, evaporation, distillation, recrystallisation, chromatographySolvation: a solute dissolving into a solvent to form a solution. Filtration: separating an insoluble solid from a liquid or gas using a membrane (filter paper). Evaporation: driving off a solvent as a vapour, leaving a dissolved solid behind. Distillation: separating two liquids (or a solvent from a dissolved solid) using a difference in boiling point, with the vapour re-condensed and collected. Recrystallisation: dissolving an impure solid and allowing pure crystals to re-form as the solution cools or the solvent evaporates, leaving impurities in solution. Paper chromatography: separating dissolved substances based on differing affinities for a stationary phase (the paper) versus a moving solvent.

The Kinetic Molecular Theory

Matter exists in different physical states — solid, liquid and gas — and can change between them depending on temperature and pressure. The kinetic molecular theory is the model used to explain the properties of each state and the transitions between them, in terms of the motion and arrangement of particles.

Kinetic molecular theoryA model stating that matter is made of constantly moving particles, and that the state of a substance (solid, liquid, or gas) at a given temperature and pressure is determined by the strength of the attractive forces between its particles relative to the particles’ average kinetic energy. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample.
Particle arrangement in the three statesThree boxes are shown side by side, representing the same substance as a solid, a liquid, and a gas, with an arrow beneath labelled “increasing temperature, increasing average kinetic energy of particles,” pointing from solid through liquid to gas. In the solid box, particles are drawn as circles packed tightly together in a regular, ordered grid, each vibrating only slightly about a fixed position. In the liquid box, the circles are still close together and touching but arranged irregularly, able to slide past one another. In the gas box, the circles are few and far apart, scattered randomly across the whole box, each moving independently in a straight line until it collides with another particle or the container wall.

Liquids and gases are both called fluids because they can flow. Diffusion, the process by which particles of a substance spread out and mix evenly due to their own random motion, occurs mainly in these two fluid states, since particles in a solid are not free to move from place to place. A classic demonstration is releasing bromine liquid, Br2(l), in a sealed gas jar connected to an empty one: the orange-brown bromine vapour is observed spreading (diffusing) until it fills both jars evenly, showing that gas particles move and spread out on their own.

A fourth state: plasmaPlasma exists only under conditions of extremely high temperature and pressure, such as inside stars including our Sun. It is not part of the everyday solid/liquid/gas classification but is often mentioned as a fourth state of matter.

In a solution, the more abundant component is the solvent and the less abundant component is the solute. Solutions in water are especially important in chemistry and are given the symbol (aq), short for “aqueous.” For example, the reaction of sodium with water is written: 2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g). It is good practice to always include state symbols in a chemical equation, even if a question does not explicitly ask for them.

Changes of State

As particles gain kinetic energy (usually by heating), they can overcome the inter-particle forces holding them in a more ordered state and change to a less ordered one. Each of the six changes of state has its own name.

The changes of stateA triangle diagram with “solid” at the top-left corner, “liquid” at the top-right corner, and “gas” at the bottom, connected by six labelled arrows. An arrow from solid to liquid is labelled “melting”; the reverse arrow from liquid to solid is labelled “freezing.” An arrow from liquid to gas is labelled “vaporization (boiling/evaporation)”; the reverse arrow from gas to liquid is labelled “condensation.” A long arrow running directly from solid to gas (skipping the liquid state entirely) is labelled “sublimation”; the reverse arrow from gas directly to solid is labelled “deposition.”
Sublimation and depositionSublimation is the direct change from solid to gas without passing through the liquid state (for example, solid carbon dioxide, iodine, and ammonium chloride sublime under normal conditions). Deposition is the reverse process, a gas changing directly into a solid, as occurs when frost or hair-ice forms directly from water vapour in cold air.
Melting/freezing point and boiling/condensation pointThe temperature at which a solid melts is exactly the same temperature at which the liquid form of that substance freezes: melting point = freezing point. Likewise, the temperature at which a liquid boils is exactly the same temperature at which its gas condenses: boiling point = condensation point. These can be written as reversible equations, for example X(s) ⇌ X(l) and Y(l) ⇌ Y(g).

A liquid can turn to gas by two different processes. Boiling occurs at one fixed temperature (for a given external pressure) throughout the whole liquid, producing visible bubbles of vapour. Evaporation happens only at the liquid’s surface, occurs over a whole range of temperatures below the boiling point, and speeds up with a larger surface area, a higher temperature, or air movement across the surface.

Vapour Pressure and Boiling Point

As a liquid is heated, more of its particles have enough energy to escape into the vapour phase, so the vapour pressure above the liquid rises. More precisely, the boiling point is defined as the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid becomes equal to the external (atmospheric) pressure acting on its surface.

Vapour pressure vs. temperature at different external pressuresA graph with temperature (°C) on the horizontal axis and vapour pressure (Pa) on the vertical axis. A single upward-curving line shows how a liquid’s vapour pressure rises steeply as temperature increases. Three horizontal dashed lines cross this curve at different heights, representing three different external pressures: a low external pressure (as found on the summit of Mount Everest), standard atmospheric pressure (1.00 × 105 Pa), and a high pressure (as found inside a sealed pressure cooker). Each horizontal line meets the vapour-pressure curve at a different point, and dropping straight down from that intersection to the temperature axis gives the boiling point at that particular external pressure. The point for low pressure (Everest) gives the lowest boiling point of the three; the point for high pressure (pressure cooker) gives the highest boiling point of the three.
Pressure cooker vs. high altitudeA pressure cooker is a sealed vessel that builds up pressure above atmospheric, which raises the boiling point of water inside it and so cooks food faster. At high altitude (such as on a mountain), atmospheric pressure is lower than at sea level, so water boils at a lower temperature, meaning food takes longer to cook.

Kinetic Energy, Mass and Diffusion Rate

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample; particles of different substances at the same temperature all have the same average kinetic energy, but not the same speed, because kinetic energy also depends on mass.

Ek = ½mv2

Here Ek is the kinetic energy of a particle, m is its mass, and v is its speed. Because kinetic energy depends on both mass and the square of speed, a lighter particle must move faster than a heavier particle to have the same kinetic energy.

Worked example

A gas particle of mass 5.0 × 10−26 kg is moving at 400 m s−1. Calculate its kinetic energy.
Ek = ½mv2 = ½ × (5.0 × 10−26) × (400)2 = ½ × (5.0 × 10−26) × 160 000 = 4.0 × 10−21 J. This tiny value is typical of the kinetic energy of a single molecule — a normal sample contains an enormous number of such particles.

Since particles of different substances at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy, setting ½m1v12 = ½m2v22 and rearranging gives a relationship between the relative masses and relative speeds of two types of particle at the same temperature.

m1 ÷ m2 = v22 ÷ v12

This means that, at a given temperature, lighter particles move faster on average than heavier particles, so lighter particles diffuse more quickly through a space than heavier ones.

Worked example

Compare the average speeds of H2 molecules (relative mass 2) and O2 molecules (relative mass 32) at the same temperature.
Using m1 ÷ m2 = v22 ÷ v12 with H2 as particle 1 and O2 as particle 2: 2 ÷ 32 = v22 ÷ v12, so v12 ÷ v22 = 32 ÷ 2 = 16, giving v1 ÷ v2 = √16 = 4. On average, H2 molecules move four times as fast as O2 molecules at the same temperature, so hydrogen gas diffuses noticeably faster than oxygen gas.

Temperature: the Celsius and Kelvin Scales

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. The Celsius scale is defined relative to the freezing point (0 °C) and boiling point (100 °C) of water, but it is not an absolute scale, since negative temperatures are possible. The kelvin (K) is the SI unit of temperature and is an absolute scale: absolute zero, 0 K (equal to −273.15 °C), is the lowest temperature theoretically possible, the point at which particles have the minimum possible kinetic energy.

Kelvin vs. CelsiusA change of 1 °C is exactly the same size as a change of 1 K, which makes converting between the scales straightforward — only the zero point differs, not the size of each degree/kelvin.
T (K) = T (°C) + 273.15

T(K) is the temperature in kelvin and T(°C) is the same temperature in degrees Celsius. Because the two scales have the same-sized unit, this is a simple addition (or, rearranged, subtraction), never a multiplication.

Worked example

Convert 25.00 °C to kelvin, and convert 373.15 K to degrees Celsius.
T(K) = 25.00 + 273.15 = 298.15 K.
Rearranging, T(°C) = T(K) − 273.15 = 373.15 − 273.15 = 100.00 °C (the boiling point of water at standard atmospheric pressure).

Heating Curves and Energy Changes

If a solid is heated at a steady rate and its temperature is tracked over time, the resulting graph reveals exactly when energy is raising the particles’ kinetic energy and when it is instead being used to break inter-particle forces.

Heating curve of a solidA graph of temperature (vertical axis) against energy input or time (horizontal axis), divided into five labelled sections a-b-c-d-e-f. From a to b, the line rises steadily as the solid is heated (temperature increasing, still solid). From b to c, the line is completely flat at the melting point — this plateau is the melting process, with solid and liquid coexisting. From c to d, the line rises again as the liquid is heated (temperature increasing, still liquid). From d to e, the line is flat again, this time at the (higher) boiling point — this plateau is the boiling process, with liquid and gas coexisting, and it is noticeably longer/flatter for longer than the melting plateau because more energy is required to separate particles completely into a gas than to loosen a solid into a liquid. From e to f, the line rises once more as the gas continues to heat up.
Why temperature stays constant during a change of stateDuring melting or boiling, the energy supplied is used entirely to overcome (break) the inter-particle attractive forces, not to increase the particles’ kinetic energy. Since temperature depends only on average kinetic energy, and that is not increasing during the plateau, the temperature itself does not change until the change of state is complete.
Endothermic vs. exothermic changes of stateMelting and boiling are endothermic: energy must be absorbed from the surroundings to separate particles against their attractive forces. Freezing and condensation are exothermic: energy is released to the surroundings as particles move closer together and inter-particle attractions re-form.
