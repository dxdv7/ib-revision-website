Classifying Organic Compounds

Guiding questionHow does the classification of organic molecules help us to predict their properties?

The total number of organic compounds on Earth is so large that it is impossible to give a definite number; it is currently estimated that there are over 20 million different organic molecules, each unique in its chemical structure and specific properties. With such a large number of compounds, it is necessary to have a system for classifying and naming them.

Functional groupA functional group is usually a small group of atoms attached to a carbon atom in a molecule, and it gives the characteristic properties to the compound. Compounds that contain the same functional group belong to the same class, and their properties can be predicted and explained by the functional groups present.

Given the incredible diversity of organic compounds, it is possible for compounds with the same molecular formula to have different structures, along with different physical and chemical properties. Applying the rules for IUPAC nomenclature (the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry system of naming) allows a unique name to be obtained for each individual compound, which also assists in classification.

Structural Representations of Organic Compounds

Chemical compounds can be represented by empirical and molecular formulas, which give the identities of the elements present. However, for organic compounds, understanding how the atoms are linked is often required to predict and explain their properties and reactivity, so structural and skeletal formulas are commonly used.

Empirical and molecular formula

The empirical formula of a compound is the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms it contains. The molecular formula is the actual number of atoms of each element present, and so it is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. This multiple, n, can be found from the molar mass, M:

M = (molar mass of empirical formula) × n

M is the actual molar mass of the compound (found experimentally), the molar mass of the empirical formula is calculated from the empirical formula alone, and n is a whole number.

Worked example

The empirical formula of ethane is CH3, and its molar mass is M = 30 g mol−1. Determine the molecular formula of ethane.

The molar mass of the empirical formula CH3 is 12 + (3 × 1) = 15 g mol−1.

n = M / (molar mass of empirical formula) = 30 / 15 = 2

The molecular formula is therefore (CH3)2, or C2H6.

Structural formula

The structural formula shows how the atoms in a molecule are bonded to each other, with variations in the level of detail shown.

  • A full structural formula (graphic or displayed formula) shows every bond and every atom explicitly. Angles of 60°, 90° and 180° are usually used to draw the bonds because this is the clearest representation on a two-dimensional page, even though it is not the true geometry of the molecule.
  • A condensed structural formula often omits bonds where they can be assumed, and groups atoms together. It contains the minimum information needed to describe the molecule unambiguously — there is only one possible structure that the formula could describe.

Ethane has empirical formula CH3, molecular formula C2H6, and condensed structural formula CH3CH3 (two carbons joined by a single bond, each carbon carrying three hydrogens). Ethanoic acid has empirical formula CH2O, molecular formula C2H4O2, and condensed structural formula CH3COOH (a methyl group joined to a carboxyl carbon, which is double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to a hydroxyl oxygen). Glucose has empirical formula CH2O, molecular formula C6H12O6, and condensed structural formula CHO(HCOH)4CH2OH, a six-carbon chain with an aldehyde at one end, four carbons each bearing a hydroxyl group, and a terminal CH2OH group.

Stereochemical formula

A stereochemical formula attempts to show the relative positions of atoms and groups around a central carbon atom in three dimensions. By convention, a bond sticking forwards, out of the page, is shown as a solid wedge; a bond sticking backwards, behind the page, is shown as a dashed wedge; and a bond lying in the plane of the paper is shown as an ordinary solid line.

When carbon forms four single bonds, the arrangement around it is tetrahedral, with bond angles of 109.5° (as in methanol, CH3OH). When carbon forms a double bond, the local arrangement is trigonal planar, with bond angles of 120° (as around each carbon in ethene, C2H4).

Skeletal formula

A skeletal formula is a shorthand representation of a structural formula. It shows all the bonds present in the molecule except C−H bonds, and omits the element symbols for carbon and hydrogen atoms entirely. The symbols of all atoms other than C and H are included, along with the condensed formula of any functional group present. C−C bonds in a carbon chain are drawn as a zig-zag of offset lines, and the positions and numbers of carbon atoms are identified only by the points where bond lines meet or end.

Each carbon atom has four bonds in total, so the number of hydrogen atoms attached to a given carbon can be found by subtracting the number of bonds drawn to that carbon (to other non-hydrogen atoms) from four. A point at the very end of a skeletal chain, with only one bond line drawn to it, therefore represents a CH3 group; a point in the middle of a chain, with two bond lines drawn to it, represents a CH2 group.

Worked example

A skeletal formula shows a chain of four carbons. Carbon 1 has only one bond, connecting it to carbon 2, so it must carry three hydrogens (a CH3 group). There is a double bond between carbons 2 and 3. Carbon 3 also has a bond to a chlorine atom. Carbon 4 has a bond to an OH group. Deduce the condensed structural formula.

Carbon 1 is CH2= (two bonds used by the double bond partner and remaining bonds to H, so with the double bond to C2 it becomes CH2=). Carbon 2 is =CH− (double bond to C1, single bond to C3, one H). Carbon 3 has bonds to C2 (double), C4 (single) and Cl, so has no H. Carbon 4 has a bond to C3, a bond to OH, and two remaining bonds to H.

CH2=CHCCl=CHCH2OH (skeletal reasoning applied to a chlorinated, unsaturated alcohol chain)

The general method is always the same: count the non-hydrogen bonds drawn to each carbon vertex, and fill in the remaining bonds on that carbon with hydrogen atoms.

Worked example

A skeletal formula shows a four-carbon chain (points 1, 2, 3, 4). Point 1 has only one C−C bond (to point 2). Point 2 has two C−C bonds (to points 1 and 3) plus a bond to Cl. Point 3 has two C−C bonds (to points 2 and 4) plus a bond to Br. Point 4 has only one C−C bond (to point 3). Deduce the condensed structural formula.

Point 1 has one C−C bond, so it needs three H atoms: CH3. Point 2 has two C−C bonds and one C−Cl bond, so it needs one H atom: CHCl. Point 3 has two C−C bonds and one C−Br bond, so it needs one H atom: CHBr. Point 4, like point 1, needs three H atoms: CH3.

Structural formula versus skeletal formulaIn a full structural formula, the element symbols of every carbon and hydrogen atom must be included, along with the bonds between them. In a skeletal formula, carbon and hydrogen symbols are omitted entirely (except for hydrogens attached to atoms other than carbon), and the structure is read from the pattern of vertices and line-ends. Identifying whether a carbon atom is primary, secondary or tertiary can help in working out how many hydrogens are attached to it in a skeletal formula.

Abbreviations: R groups and the phenyl ring

When the exact details of the hydrocarbon, or alkyl, part of a molecule are not important, this part can be abbreviated to R (or R′ for a second, different alkyl group). For molecules containing a benzene ring, C6H6, known as aromatic compounds, a hexagon with a circle inside it is used as shorthand for the ring of alternating double bonds. Carbon compounds can also form ring structures that are saturated (containing only C−C single bonds, such as cyclohexane, C6H12) or unsaturated (containing C=C bonds, such as benzene, C6H6).

Functional groups influence chemical reactivity: reaction pathways

Synthetic organic chemists use their knowledge of functional group reactions to convert readily available starting materials into target compounds. This often requires organizing several reactions in sequence, so that the product of one reaction becomes the reactant of the next; this series of steps is called a reaction pathway, and each step typically involves a functional group interconversion. For example, ethene can be converted into ethanoic acid via a two-step pathway, in which ethanol is formed as an intermediate product:

CH2=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH2OH (addition of water, catalyzed by H2SO4)
CH3CH2OH → CH3COOH (oxidation)

Ethanoic acid is an important industrial chemical; one major use is its conversion into vinyl acetate for the production of polymers such as polyvinyl acetate (PVA), used in many glues.

Amino acids and condensation reactions

Amino acids each contain two functional groups: an amine group (−NH2) and a carboxylic acid group (−COOH). These two groups can react together in a condensation reaction, in which a molecule of water is eliminated and a new bond forms between the acid group of one amino acid and the amino group of another. This new bond is a substituted amide link known as a peptide bond, and two amino acids linked this way form an amide product called a dipeptide.

Formation of a peptide bondTwo amino acid units are drawn side by side: the first has a free amino group (−NH2, the N-terminal end) on its left and a carboxylic acid group (−COOH) on its right; the second, drawn to the right of the first, has its own amino group and its own free carboxylic acid group (the C-terminal end). An arrow shows the carboxylic acid group of the first amino acid reacting with the amino group of the second: a molecule of H2O is released, and a new C(=O)−N(H) linkage (the peptide bond) joins the two amino acid units into a single dipeptide chain, which still has a free amino group at its far left end and a free carboxylic acid group at its far right end.

Because the resulting dipeptide still has a free functional group at each end (−NH2 at one end, −COOH at the other), it can react again in another condensation reaction, forming a tripeptide, and eventually a chain of many linked amino acids known as a polypeptide.

Homologous Series

Homologous seriesA homologous series is a family of compounds in which successive members differ by a common structural unit, typically CH2. Each homologous series can be described by a general formula.

Consider the alkanes methane (CH4), ethane (C2H6), propane (C3H8) and butane (C4H10), all bonded only by single covalent bonds. Neighbouring members differ from each other by −CH2−, and this same increment applies to successive members of every homologous series, meaning the molecular mass increases by a fixed amount as you move up a series. All members of a homologous series can be described by the same general formula: the alkanes shown above are all described by CnH2n+2. Other series are characterized by the presence of a particular functional group, which appears in the general formula — for example, the alcohols (methanol, CH3OH; ethanol, C2H5OH; propanol, C3H7OH; butanol, C4H9OH) are all described by CnH2n+1OH.

The data show that boiling point increases with increasing carbon number in every series, because a longer chain length increases both the instantaneous and induced dipoles occurring between molecules, resulting in stronger London (dispersion) forces and therefore stronger overall intermolecular forces. Melting point, density and viscosity all show similar predictable trends with increasing carbon number. For the alkanes, the increase in boiling point is not linear but is steeper near the beginning of the series, since the effect of an added CH2 unit is proportionally greater for small molecules; for the chloroalkanes and alcohols, the increase is more consistent, because their strongest intermolecular forces are associated with their functional group rather than simply chain length.

Functional groups and volatility

Order of volatilityOwing to their different polarities and the resulting differences in intermolecular forces, functional groups have a significant effect on volatility (the tendency of a substance to vaporize). From most volatile to least volatile: alkane > halogenoalkane > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > carboxylic acid. This corresponds to increasing strength of intermolecular attraction and increasing boiling point, from London (dispersion) forces only, through dipole–dipole interactions, to hydrogen bonding.

Reading across a row of the table above, compounds with the same number of carbons increase in boiling point in the order alkane < chloroalkane < alcohol, consistent with the strength of the intermolecular forces present: London dispersion forces only (alkane) < dipole–dipole interactions (chloroalkane) < hydrogen bonding (alcohol). The hydrogen bonding present in alcohols is so strong that even the smallest alcohol, methanol, is a liquid at room temperature, whereas methane and chloromethane are both gases at room temperature. Both chain length and the functional group present affect the strength of intermolecular forces: longer chains increase London dispersion forces, and polar functional groups add dipole–dipole interactions or hydrogen bonding. Branching of the carbon chain also affects intermolecular forces, since it reduces surface contact between neighbouring molecules and so results in weaker London dispersion forces and a lower boiling point.

IUPAC Nomenclature

IUPAC nomenclatureIUPAC nomenclature refers to a set of rules used by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry to apply systematic names to organic and inorganic compounds. Names generated this way are logically based on the chemistry of the compound and give information about the functional groups present and the size of the molecule.

Rule 1: Identify the longest straight chain of carbon atoms

The longest continuous (unbranched) chain of carbon atoms gives the stem of the name.

Carbons in longest chainStemExample
1meth-CH4, methane
2eth-C2H6, ethane
3prop-C3H8, propane
4but-C4H10, butane
5pent-C5H12, pentane
6hex-C6H14, hexane

“Straight chain” refers to a continuous, unbranched chain of carbon atoms; it does not mean the chain must be drawn at 180° angles. Because of free rotation around carbon–carbon single bonds, the same straight-chain molecule can look quite different when drawn in different ways on paper, so care is needed to identify the true longest chain correctly.

Rule 2: Identify the functional group

The functional group usually determines the suffix, which replaces the “-ane” ending of the parent alkane name. The stem is derived from the longest chain, which may include the carbon of the functional group itself (as it does for carboxylic acids, esters and amides). The position of a functional group is shown by a number, inserted before the suffix with dashes, that refers to the carbon atom to which the functional group is attached; the chain is always numbered starting from the end that gives the smallest possible number to the functional group. For example, but-1-ene is numbered from the end nearer the double bond so that it receives position 1, rather than position 3. Sometimes a functional group can only occupy one possible position in the chain, in which case no locant number is needed: a carboxylic acid group must always be at the end of a chain, and a three-carbon ketone (propanone) has only one possible position for its carbonyl group.

Worked example

Name the compound CH3CH(OH)CH3 using IUPAC rules.

The longest chain has three carbons (prop-). The functional group is −OH (suffix -anol). Numbering from either end places the OH group on carbon 2, so no ambiguity arises about which end to number from.

propan-2-ol

Rule 3: Identify side chains or substituent groups

Side chains, or functional groups additional to the one used as the suffix, are called substituents and are given as a prefix at the start of the name. Common substituent prefixes include methyl, ethyl and propyl (for alkyl branches) and fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo (for halogens). The position of a substituent is given by a locant number placed before its name, again numbering the chain to give the smallest possible number(s) overall. If there is more than one substituent of the same type, the prefixes di-, tri- or tetra- are used before the substituent name, with the locant numbers separated by commas; substituents are listed in the name in alphabetical order.

Worked example

Name the compound with a three-carbon chain bearing a chlorine atom on carbon 1 and a methyl group on carbon 2.

1-chloro-2-methylpropane

The substituents chloro and methyl are listed alphabetically (chloro before methyl), each with its own locant.

Note that −NH2 can appear as either a suffix (amino, -anamine) or a prefix (amino-), depending on context: when it is the only functional group present it is usually named as the suffix, but if two or more functional groups are present in the same molecule, the amino group becomes a prefix, as in amino acids — for example, CH2(NH2)COOH is 2-aminoethanoic acid, since the carboxylic acid group takes priority as the suffix.

Naming esters

Esters form when the alkyl group of an alcohol replaces the acidic hydrogen of a carboxylic acid in a condensation reaction:

R−COOH + R′OH → R−COO−R′ + H2O

Esters are named in a similar way to salts (which form when a metal replaces the acidic hydrogen of a carboxylic acid, and take the stem of the name from the parent acid, with the metal as the prefix — for example, CH3COONa is sodium ethanoate). In an ester, the stem still comes from the parent acid, but the alkyl group derived from the alcohol becomes the prefix. The ester formed from ethanol and ethanoic acid is therefore called ethyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH2CH3.

Naming ethers

Ethers consist of two alkyl chains linked by an oxygen atom (R−O−R′). When naming an ether, the longer chain is treated as the stem and keeps its ordinary alkane name, while the shorter chain is treated as a substituent and given the prefix alkoxy. For example, an ether with a one-carbon chain and a three-carbon chain joined by oxygen is named methoxypropane, CH3OCH2CH2CH3. When both chains are the same length, one is arbitrarily chosen as the stem and the other becomes the alkoxy substituent, as in ethoxyethane, CH3CH2OCH2CH3 (diethyl ether), historically used as one of the first anaesthetics.

Summary of IUPAC nomenclatureAn IUPAC name has up to three parts, usually written together as a single word: a prefix giving the position, number and name of any substituents; a stem giving the number of carbon atoms in the longest chain; and a suffix giving the class of compound, determined by the principal functional group.

Structural Isomers

Structural isomersStructural isomers are molecules that have the same molecular formula but different connectivities (different arrangements of the atoms). Because of their different structures, they also have different physical and chemical properties.

Chain isomers: straight-chain and branched alkanes

Butane, C4H10, has two structural isomers: butane itself (a straight chain, boiling point −0.5°C) and 2-methylpropane (one branch, boiling point −11.7°C). Pentane, C5H12, has three structural isomers: pentane (straight chain, boiling point 36.1°C), 2-methylbutane (one branch, boiling point 27.8°C), and 2,2-dimethylpropane (two branches, boiling point 9.5°C). Hexane, C6H14, has five structural isomers in total: one straight-chain isomer, two isomers with a five-carbon longest chain and one methyl branch, and two isomers with a four-carbon longest chain and two methyl branches.

These examples show a clear trend: the more branching present in an isomer, the lower its boiling point. Branching reduces the surface contact between neighbouring molecules (in the same way that logs with many branches cannot stack as closely as smooth, unbranched logs), which weakens the London dispersion forces between molecules and lowers the boiling point. This trend is significant in the petroleum industry, since branched-chain hydrocarbon isomers generally burn more smoothly in internal combustion engines than straight-chain isomers, giving oil fractions with a higher proportion of branched isomers a higher “octane number” and a higher price. The number of possible isomers increases exponentially with molecular size: there are 75 possible isomers of C10H22, and 366 319 isomers of C20H42.

Positional isomers

Alkenes can show positional isomerism, where the same functional group (the C=C double bond) occurs in different positions along the same length of carbon chain. For example, C4H8 has two straight-chain positional isomers, but-1-ene and but-2-ene; C5H10 has pent-1-ene and pent-2-ene; and C6H12 has hex-1-ene, hex-2-ene and hex-3-ene. The isomers of alkynes, which contain at least one carbon–carbon triple bond, can similarly be deduced by considering different possible positions for the triple bond.

Primary, secondary and tertiary carbon atoms

Positional isomers of alcohols, halogenoalkanes and amines are of particular importance, since they can have significantly different chemical reactivity.

Primary, secondary and tertiary carbonsA primary carbon atom is attached to the functional group and to at least two hydrogen atoms (for example, ethanol, C2H5OH, is a primary alcohol, and chloroethane, C2H5Cl, is a primary halogenoalkane). A secondary carbon atom is attached to the functional group, one hydrogen atom, and two alkyl groups (for example, propan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH3, is a secondary alcohol, and butan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is also a secondary alcohol). A tertiary carbon atom is attached to the functional group and to three alkyl groups, with no hydrogen atoms attached (for example, 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH, is a tertiary alcohol, and 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CCl, is a tertiary halogenoalkane).

A similar classification applies to amines, based on the number of alkyl groups and hydrogen atoms bonded to the central nitrogen atom.

Functional group isomers

Structural isomers can also have entirely different functional groups, so that they belong to different classes of compound with very different reactivities. For example, the molecular formula C2H6O describes both an alcohol (ethanol, CH3CH2OH, a liquid widely used in alcoholic drinks) and an ether (methoxymethane, CH3OCH3, a gas used in aerosol propellants). Similarly, the formula C3H6O describes both an aldehyde (propanal) and a ketone (propanone), and C4H8O2 describes both a carboxylic acid (butanoic acid) and several possible esters (such as methyl propanoate, ethyl ethanoate, or propyl methanoate).

Three types of structural isomerChain isomers occur when C−C bonds are connected differently to form structures that are straight-chain or branched. Positional isomers contain the same functional group, but it is located at a different position on the carbon chain. Functional group isomers contain different functional groups entirely, and so belong to different classes of compound.

Guiding Question Revisited

How does the classification of organic molecules help us to predict their properties?
  • Organic compounds can be represented by empirical, molecular, structural (full and condensed), stereochemical and skeletal formulas, each giving a different level of structural detail.
  • Organic compounds can be grouped into classes based on the functional groups present. Different classes of compound have different physical and chemical properties.
  • Homologous series exist for each class of compound; successive members of a series differ by a CH2 unit and are described by a common general formula, with a gradual trend in physical properties such as boiling point.
  • Functional groups affect physical properties through the strength of the intermolecular forces they allow (London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole interactions, or hydrogen bonding), giving the volatility order: alkane > halogenoalkane > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > carboxylic acid.
  • Applying IUPAC nomenclature (stem, suffix and prefix) generates a unique, systematic name for each individual compound.
  • Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different connectivities, and so have unique IUPAC names as well as different physical and chemical properties. These include chain isomers, positional isomers, and functional group isomers.
