The Ideal Gas Model

Guiding questionHow does the model of ideal gas behavior help us to predict the behavior of real gases?

Gases are the simplest state of matter to describe because their particles are widely spaced and move in continuous, chaotic random motion. Early scientists such as Robert Boyle, Jacques Charles and Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac investigated how the pressure, volume and temperature of gases are related, and found that all gases obey the same simple relationships regardless of their chemical identity. These empirical relationships can be explained by a simple theoretical model called the ideal gas model.

Assumptions of the ideal gas model
  • The particles of a gas have negligible volume compared with the volume occupied by the gas as a whole.
  • There are no intermolecular forces between the particles, except during the instant of a collision.
  • The particles move randomly with a range of speeds, and the average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the absolute temperature.
  • Collisions between particles, and between particles and the walls of the container, are perfectly elastic – kinetic energy is conserved.

No real gas satisfies these assumptions exactly, but applying Newton's second law (force = mass × acceleration) to this simple model gives remarkably accurate predictions for the physical properties of most gases under everyday conditions of temperature and pressure. A gas that behaves exactly as this model predicts is called an ideal gas. Crucially, the model predicts that the chemical identity of the gas makes no difference at all to this behavior – only the number of particles, their volume, their temperature and their pressure matter.

Why Gases Exert Pressure

The pressure of a gas arises because its particles are constantly colliding with the walls of their container. Each collision is elastic, so a particle rebounds with the same speed it arrived with, and the resulting change in momentum exerts a tiny force on the wall. Summing the effect of an enormous number of such collisions every second produces the steady pressure that we measure with a gauge. Because the particles move randomly with no preferred direction, the pressure is the same on every wall of the container.

Figure S1.5.1: Pressure from particle collisionsA container wall shown with a gas particle approaching along an arrow, striking the wall, and rebounding along an arrow of the same length pointing in the opposite direction. This represents an elastic collision in which the particle's speed (and hence its kinetic energy) is unchanged but its direction is reversed. The pressure of the gas is the combined effect of countless such impacts occurring every second across the entire surface of the container.

This simple picture explains what happens when the conditions of a fixed mass of gas change:

  • Increasing the volume at constant temperature spreads the same number of particles over a larger space, so each particle must travel further, on average, before it next strikes a wall. Collisions become less frequent, so the pressure decreases.
  • Increasing the temperature at constant volume increases the average kinetic energy, and therefore the average speed, of the particles. Faster particles strike the walls more often and with a greater force per collision, so the pressure increases.
Figure S1.5.2: Volume and collision frequencyTwo identical containers holding the same fixed number of gas particles, drawn as dots with short zig-zagging trails to show their random motion. In the container drawn with a smaller volume, the particles are closer together and short arrows show them striking the walls frequently. In the container drawn with a larger volume, the same particles are spread further apart and strike the walls far less often, illustrating why expanding a gas at constant temperature lowers its pressure, and compressing it raises the pressure.

Real Gases: Where the Ideal Model Breaks Down

There is no such thing as a truly ideal gas. Every real gas deviates from ideal behavior to some extent because the first two assumptions of the model – negligible particle volume and no intermolecular forces – are never perfectly true. Real gases behave most like an ideal gas at low pressure and high temperature, and deviate most strongly at high pressure and low temperature, which are exactly the conditions under which a gas is closest to condensing into a liquid.

1. The volume of the particles is not negligible

If the particles themselves take up an appreciable fraction of the container's volume, they have less empty space to travel through between collisions than the ideal model assumes. This makes collisions with the walls more frequent than predicted, so the real pressure ends up higher than the ideal gas model predicts. This effect matters most at high pressure and low temperature, when the particles are squeezed into a small volume and are, on average, close together.

Figure S1.5.3: Particle volume shortens the distance between collisionsTwo versions of the same container of width L, viewed side-on. In the upper version an idealized, point-like particle travels the full distance L to the far wall and back – a round trip of 2L – between successive collisions with a given wall. In the lower version a real particle is drawn with a finite diameter d; because part of the container's width is occupied by the particle's own size, it only needs to travel a shorter round trip of 2(L − d) between collisions with that wall, so it collides more frequently than the idealized point particle would. The difference becomes most significant when L is small, which happens at high pressure.

2. There are attractive forces between the particles

Real particles attract one another weakly through intermolecular forces. As a particle approaches the wall to collide, neighboring particles pull it backward, slowing it down slightly just before impact. The resulting collision is less energetic than the ideal model predicts, so the real pressure ends up lower than the ideal gas model predicts. This effect matters most at low temperature, when the particles are already moving relatively slowly, so the same attractive pull has a proportionally larger braking effect on their speed.

Figure S1.5.4: Intermolecular attraction slows an approaching particleA gas particle approaching a container wall, with short arrows drawn from several neighboring particles pointing back toward it, representing the net attractive pull of intermolecular forces on the approaching particle. Its velocity arrow just before collision is drawn noticeably shorter than the velocity arrow it would have had with no neighbors pulling on it, showing that the attractive forces reduce its speed, and therefore the force of its impact, at the moment it strikes the wall.
Which real gases deviate most from ideal behavior?Gases made of small, non-polar molecules with only weak intermolecular forces (such as helium or hydrogen) behave almost ideally over a wide range of conditions. Gases whose molecules attract each other more strongly deviate more under the same conditions – for example, ammonia, NH3, which can form hydrogen bonds between its molecules, shows a greater deviation from ideal behavior than methane, CH4, a molecule of similar size that only experiences weak dispersion forces.

Units for Gas Calculations

Before using any of the gas laws, it is essential to work in a consistent set of units, because a calculation that mixes units carelessly will give a meaningless answer.

Temperature must always be in kelvinGas calculations only work correctly with the absolute (kelvin) temperature scale, because it is absolute temperature, not the Celsius value, that is directly proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles. To convert from Celsius to kelvin, add 273 (or 273.15 for extra precision): T(K) = T(°C) + 273. A temperature of 0°C is therefore 273 K, and 25°C is 298 K. Using a Celsius value directly in any gas law equation gives an incorrect answer.

The SI unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), equivalent to 1 N m−2. Laboratory volumes are usually measured in cm3 or dm3, but the SI unit of volume is the m3, so these values often need converting during a calculation.

The Empirical Gas Laws

By changing one variable of a fixed mass of gas while holding another constant, three simple relationships between pressure, volume and temperature can be found experimentally.

Boyle's law

Boyle's lawAt constant temperature, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its volume: P ∝ 1/V, so the product of pressure and volume is constant.
P1V1 = P2V2

P1 and V1 are the pressure and volume of the gas in its initial state, and P2 and V2 are the pressure and volume in its final state, at the same constant temperature and for the same fixed mass of gas. This follows directly from the collision model: compressing a gas into a smaller volume packs its particles closer together, so they strike the walls more frequently, raising the pressure.

Worked example

A gas syringe holds 500 cm3 of gas at a pressure of 100 kPa. At constant temperature the gas is compressed until its pressure is 250 kPa. What is its new volume? Using P1V1 = P2V2: 100 × 500 = 250 × V2, so V2 = 50 000 / 250 = 200 cm3. Increasing the pressure two and a half times has reduced the volume to two-fifths of its original value, confirming the inverse relationship.

Figure S1.5.5: Pressure–volume graph for Boyle's lawA graph of pressure (vertical axis) against volume (horizontal axis) at constant temperature is a smoothly falling curve that drops steeply at small volumes and flattens out at large volumes, never touching either axis; this curve is called an isotherm. A second graph, of pressure multiplied by volume (vertical axis) against pressure (horizontal axis), is instead a horizontal straight line, showing directly that the product PV stays constant as the pressure is varied.

Charles' law

Charles' lawAt constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature: V ∝ T.
V1/T1 = V2/T2

V1 and T1 are the volume and absolute temperature of the gas in its initial state, and V2 and T2 are the volume and absolute temperature in its final state, at the same constant pressure. As the gas is heated, its particles move faster and would tend to collide with the walls more forcefully; if the container is free to expand, its volume increases just enough to keep the pressure unchanged.

Worked example

The molar volume of a gas at standard temperature and pressure (STP), 273 K, is 22.7 dm3 mol−1. What would the molar volume be at 25.0°C and the same pressure? First convert 25.0°C to kelvin: T2 = 25.0 + 273 = 298 K. Using V1/T1 = V2/T2: 22.7/273 = V2/298, so V2 = (298 × 22.7)/273 = 24.8 dm3 mol−1. Warming the gas by 25 K at constant pressure has increased its molar volume, exactly as Charles' law predicts.

Figure S1.5.6: Volume–temperature graph for Charles' lawA graph of volume (vertical axis) against temperature in degrees Celsius (horizontal axis) is a straight line that slopes upward and, when extended backward with a dashed line, crosses the temperature axis (volume = 0) at exactly −273°C. This intercept is the same for every gas and defines absolute zero, 0 K, the coldest temperature that is theoretically possible. When the same data are plotted against temperature in kelvin instead, the straight line passes directly through the origin, showing that volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature.

The pressure law

Pressure lawAt constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature: P ∝ T.
P1/T1 = P2/T2

P1 and T1 are the pressure and absolute temperature of the gas in its initial state, and P2 and T2 are the pressure and absolute temperature in its final state, at the same constant volume. Heating a gas that cannot expand increases the average kinetic energy of its particles, so they strike the fixed walls more often and with greater force, increasing the pressure.

Worked example

A 200 cm3 rigid container holds gas at 15°C and 1.0 × 105 Pa. It is then heated to 160°C. Since the container is rigid, the volume does not change. Converting temperatures to kelvin: T1 = 15 + 273 = 288 K, T2 = 160 + 273 = 433 K. Using P1/T1 = P2/T2: (1.0 × 105)/288 = P2/433, so P2 = (433 × 1.0 × 105)/288 = 1.5 × 105 Pa. The pressure rises by half again as the container is heated through 145 K, because pressure and absolute temperature are directly proportional here.

Pressurized cans, such as aerosols or drink cans, carry warnings against storage in hot places precisely because of this relationship: at constant volume, a rise in temperature causes a proportional rise in internal pressure, which can be enough to cause the container to burst.

Molar Volume and Avogadro's Law

The first assumption of the ideal gas model – that particle volume is negligible – implies that the identity of the gas particles should make no difference to the volume the gas occupies. This is consistent with Avogadro's law: equal volumes of any gas, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. It follows that one mole of any ideal gas occupies the same volume as one mole of any other ideal gas, under the same conditions of temperature and pressure – this volume is called the molar volume.

Standard temperature and pressure (STP)Under standard temperature and pressure – defined here as 273 K (0°C) and 100 kPa – the molar volume of an ideal gas is 2.27 × 10−2 m3 mol−1, equivalent to 22.7 dm3 mol−1. This value, given in the data booklet, is the same for every gas.
n = V / Vm

n is the amount of gas in moles, V is the volume the gas occupies, and Vm is the molar volume under the same conditions of temperature and pressure. This lets the amount of a gas be found directly from its volume, without needing to know its molar mass, provided the temperature and pressure are known.

Worked example

Calculate the volume occupied by 0.020 g of helium (molar mass 4.00 g mol−1) at STP. First find the amount in moles: n = m/M = 0.020/4.00 = 0.0050 mol. Then use the molar volume: V = n × Vm = 0.0050 mol × 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 0.114 dm3. This shows how the molar volume converts an amount in moles directly into a gas volume, in exactly the way molar mass converts an amount in moles into a mass.

The Combined Gas Law

Boyle's law, Charles' law and the pressure law each describe how two variables of a gas relate when a third is held constant. They can be combined into one equation that applies whenever a fixed mass of gas changes from one set of conditions to another, whether or not any variable happens to stay constant.

P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2

P1, V1 and T1 are the pressure, volume and absolute temperature of a fixed mass of gas in its initial state, and P2, V2 and T2 are its pressure, volume and absolute temperature in its final state. If any one of the three quantities is unchanged between the two states, it cancels from both sides, and the equation reduces to whichever of the three simpler gas laws applies.

Worked example

A 2.50 dm3 sample of helium at 85 kPa and 25°C is heated to 75°C, and its volume expands to 2.75 dm3. What is its final pressure? Converting temperatures to kelvin: T1 = 25 + 273 = 298 K, T2 = 75 + 273 = 348 K. Using P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2: (85 × 2.50)/298 = (P2 × 2.75)/348. Rearranging: P2 = (85 × 2.50 × 348)/(298 × 2.75) = 73 950/819.5 = 90.2 kPa. The pressure has fallen slightly overall, because the volume increase outweighs the effect of the temperature increase.

The Ideal Gas Equation, PV = nRT

Combining the combined gas law with Avogadro's law shows that PV/T is proportional to the amount of gas, n: PV/T ∝ n. Introducing a constant of proportionality, R, known as the universal gas constant because it is the same for every gas, turns this into an equation:

PV = nRT

P is the pressure of the gas, V is its volume, n is the amount of gas in moles, T is its absolute temperature, and R is the gas constant, 8.31 J K−1 mol−1. This single equation applies to a gas at any single instant, not just to a change between two states, and can be rearranged to find any one of P, V, n or T given the other three.

Units and the gas constantBecause 1 kPa × 1 dm3 is numerically equal to 1 J, the gas constant can equally be written R = 8.31 kPa dm3 K−1 mol−1. This means pressure in kPa and volume in dm3 can be substituted directly into PV = nRT without first converting to pascals and cubic metres, as long as both units are used consistently and the temperature is in kelvin. If pressure or volume are given in other units (Pa and m3, or Pa and dm3), always convert to a matching, consistent pair of units first.
Worked example

Determine the value of the gas constant, R, from the molar volume of an ideal gas at STP: P = 1.00 × 105 Pa, V = 2.27 × 10−2 m3, n = 1 mol, T = 273 K. Rearranging PV = nRT: R = PV/(nT) = (1.00 × 105 × 2.27 × 10−2)/(1 × 273) = 2270/273 = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1. This is exactly the value of R given in the data booklet, showing that the ideal gas equation is consistent with the experimentally measured molar volume.

Worked example

A helium party balloon has a volume of 18.0 dm3. At 25°C its internal pressure is 108 kPa. Calculate the mass of helium inside it. Using kPa and dm3 consistently with R = 8.31 kPa dm3 K−1 mol−1, and T = 25 + 273 = 298 K: PV = nRT, so 108 × 18.0 = n × 8.31 × 298, giving n = 1944/2476.4 = 0.785 mol. The mass is then m = nM = 0.785 mol × 4.00 g mol−1 = 3.14 g.

Applying PV = nRT: Molar Mass and Density

Because n = m/M, the ideal gas equation can be rearranged to calculate the molar mass of an unknown gas from a measurement of its mass, volume, pressure and temperature – a genuinely useful experimental technique.

M = mRT / (PV)

M is the molar mass of the gas, m is the mass of the sample, R is the gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, P is the pressure, and V is the volume. Since density is mass divided by volume (ρ = m/V), this can also be written M = ρRT/P, allowing the molar mass to be found directly from a measured gas density.

Worked example

A sample of gas has a volume of 445 cm3 and a mass of 1.50 g at a pressure of 95.0 kPa and a temperature of 28.0°C. Calculate its molar mass. Converting to consistent SI units: P = 95.0 × 103 Pa, V = 445 × 10−6 m3, T = 28.0 + 273 = 301 K. Using M = mRT/(PV): M = (1.50 × 8.31 × 301)/(95.0 × 103 × 445 × 10−6) = 3750/42.3 = 88.7 g mol−1 (to 3 significant figures).

Worked example

A gas has a density of 1.65 g dm−3 at 27°C and 92.0 kPa. Determine its molar mass. A density of 1.65 g dm−3 means 1.65 g occupies exactly 1.00 dm3. Using kPa and dm3 units with R = 8.31 kPa dm3 K−1 mol−1, and T = 27 + 273 = 300 K: M = mRT/(PV) = (1.65 × 8.31 × 300)/(92.0 × 1.00) = 4113/92.0 = 44.7 g mol−1, which is close to the molar mass of carbon dioxide.

Guidelines for ideal gas equation calculations
  • Pressure, P: convert to Pa (multiply kPa by 103), unless working consistently in kPa and dm3 together.
  • Volume, V: convert to m3 (divide dm3 by 103, or cm3 by 106), unless working consistently in kPa and dm3 together.
  • Amount of gas, n: often found first from n = m/M if only a mass is given.
  • Temperature, T: always convert to kelvin by adding 273 to a Celsius value – never use a Celsius value directly.

Quantifying the Deviation of Real Gases from Ideal Behavior

An ideal gas is, by definition, one that obeys PV = nRT under every condition. For one mole of gas this means the ratio PV/RT should always equal exactly 1. Since real gases are not truly ideal, this ratio drifts away from 1 as conditions change, and the size of that drift is a direct, quantitative measure of how far a real gas departs from ideal behavior.

Figure S1.5.7: PV/RT against pressure for one mole of an ideal gasA graph with pressure on the horizontal axis and the ratio PV/RT on the vertical axis. For a perfectly ideal gas this ratio would be a perfectly horizontal straight line at a height of exactly 1, for every possible pressure, since PV/RT = n = 1 mol at all times for an ideal gas.
Figure S1.5.8: PV/RT against pressure for a real gas at different temperaturesThe same axes as above, but now showing three curves for a real gas (such as nitrogen) at three different fixed temperatures. At a low temperature, the curve dips noticeably below 1 at moderate pressures – where intermolecular attractive forces dominate – before rising back above 1 at very high pressure, where the particles' own volume dominates instead. At a high temperature, the curve stays much closer to the ideal value of 1 across the whole range, only rising gently above 1 at the highest pressures. This shows that a real gas approaches ideal behavior most closely at high temperature and low pressure, and deviates most strongly at low temperature and high pressure.
  • When the volume of the particles is not negligible, collisions with the walls are more frequent than the ideal model predicts, and PV/nRT > 1.
  • When attractive forces between particles are significant, collisions with the walls are less energetic than the ideal model predicts, and PV/nRT < 1.

Chemists have developed more sophisticated equations, such as the van der Waals equation, which adjust the ideal gas equation with extra correction terms to account for both the finite volume of real particles and the attractive forces between them. These refined equations describe real gas behavior more accurately across a wider range of conditions, but the simplicity of PV = nRT, together with its accuracy for a wide range of gases under everyday laboratory conditions, is what makes it such a valuable and widely used approximation.

Guiding Question Revisited

How does the model of ideal gas behavior help us to predict the behavior of real gases?
  • The ideal gas model treats gas particles as having negligible volume and no intermolecular forces, moving randomly and colliding elastically; this simple picture explains why gases exert pressure and why that pressure changes with volume and temperature.
  • Absolute temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the gas particles.
  • The empirical gas laws – Boyle's law, Charles' law and the pressure law – describe how pressure, volume and temperature relate for a fixed mass of gas, and are combined in the combined gas law, P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2.
  • Incorporating Avogadro's law gives the ideal gas equation, PV = nRT, which can be applied at a single instant to relate pressure, volume, amount and temperature, and rearranged to find the molar mass of a gas from its measured density.
  • Real gases deviate from ideal behavior, especially at high pressure and low temperature, because real particles have a non-negligible volume (which tends to increase the pressure above the ideal prediction) and experience intermolecular attractive forces (which tend to decrease the pressure below the ideal prediction). The ideal gas model remains a highly useful approximation because these effects are small under most everyday laboratory conditions.
