Guiding Question

Guiding QuestionWhat determines the ionic nature and properties of a compound?

All atoms are electrically neutral, since the number of protons equals the number of electrons. If an electron is transferred from one atom, M, to another atom, X, the two atoms become charged particles called ions: M becomes a positively charged cation and X becomes a negatively charged anion. Because the resulting ions carry opposite charges, they are pulled together by electrostatic attraction. This attraction between oppositely charged ions is the ionic bond.

Electron transfer forms oppositely charged ionsTwo neutral atoms, M and X, are shown side by side. An arrow shows a single electron moving from M to X. After the transfer, M is drawn as a smaller sphere labelled M+ and X as a larger sphere labelled X−, with curved attraction lines linking the two oppositely charged spheres to show the electrostatic pull between them. In general M is a metal (found on the left of the periodic table) and X is a non-metal (found on the right).

A single pair of ions is rarely found in isolation. Instead, huge numbers of cations and anions arrange themselves into a repeating, three-dimensional lattice, with every positive ion surrounded by negative neighbours and vice versa. It is this large-scale, orderly packing of ions — not just the attraction between one pair — that gives ionic compounds their characteristic crystalline structure and physical properties. The ionic character of a bond is closely related to how far apart the two elements sit in the periodic table: the further apart, the more ionic the compound tends to be.

Why Metals Form Cations and Non-Metals Form Anions

Cation and anionA cation is a positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses one or more electrons. An anion is a negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains one or more electrons. A useful memory aid: CATion is PAWsitive, aNion is Negative.

It is always the outer (valence) electrons that are lost or gained in ion formation, because they are furthest from the nucleus and least strongly held. The atomic number, and therefore the identity of the element, never changes during ionic bonding — only electrons move.

Effective Nuclear Charge

Although the actual nuclear charge of an atom increases by one unit for every proton added going across a period, the outer electrons do not experience this full charge. Inner-shell electrons shield the outer electrons from the nucleus and repel them, so the outer electrons only feel a reduced pull called the effective nuclear charge. For period 3 elements, the ten inner electrons of the neon core shield the nucleus almost completely, so the effective nuclear charge felt by the valence electrons is roughly equal to the group number (for example, sodium, with 11 protons and 10 shielding electrons, has an effective nuclear charge of about +1; chlorine, with 17 protons and the same shielding core, has an effective nuclear charge of about +7).

Effective nuclear charge: across a period vs down a groupAcross a period, effective nuclear charge increases, because one proton is added to the nucleus each time but the number of inner shielding electrons stays the same. Down a group, effective nuclear charge stays roughly constant (about +1 for group 1, for example), because the increase in nuclear charge is offset by an equal increase in the number of inner shielding electrons.

This pattern explains ion formation directly. Metals on the lower left of the periodic table have low effective nuclear charge and low ionization energy, so they lose electrons easily to form cations. Non-metals on the upper right have high effective nuclear charge and small atomic radii, so they attract additional electrons strongly and form anions readily. The most vigorous ionic reactions occur between elements from opposite extremes of the table, such as caesium (bottom of group 1) and fluorine (top of group 17).

The noble gases are the great exception. Their outer shells are already full and their ionization energies are very high, so they have no tendency to lose electrons, and any extra electron would have to enter a completely new, unshielded energy level. This lack of reactivity means the noble gas electron configuration behaves like an energetic “finishing line”: metal atoms lose electrons to reach the electron configuration of the noble gas that precedes them, and non-metal atoms gain electrons to reach the configuration of the noble gas that follows them. For example, when sodium reacts with chlorine, the sodium atom loses its single 3s electron to reach the electron configuration of neon, while the chlorine atom gains that electron to reach the electron configuration of argon.

Predicting Ion Charge from the Periodic Table

The number of electrons an atom needs to lose or gain to reach a noble-gas configuration can be read directly from its group number.

Group 14 elements such as carbon and silicon have four valence electrons; losing or gaining four electrons is too costly in energy, so these elements do not readily form simple ions and instead form covalent bonds. As negative charge on an ion increases, extra electrons experience greater electron–electron repulsion and a lower effective nuclear charge, which is why an ion such as Si4− is not feasible even though the group number would suggest it. Hydrogen is unusual: it usually loses its single 1s electron to form H+ (like a group 1 metal), but can occasionally gain an electron to form the hydride ion, H− (like a halogen).

Worked exampleDeduce the charge on the ion formed by (a) lithium (b) oxygen (c) carbon.
(a) Lithium is in group 1, so it forms Li+.
(b) Oxygen is in group 16, so it forms O2−.
(c) Carbon is in group 14; it does not readily form a simple ion.

Transition Metals and Variable Oxidation States

Unlike the metals of groups 1, 2 and 13, transition metals often form more than one stable ion, because the energies of their outer electrons are close together. Iron commonly forms Fe2+ and Fe3+; copper commonly forms Cu+ and Cu2+. Other elements outside the transition block also show more than one common ion despite their periodic table position: lead and tin (group 14) form stable 2+ and 4+ ions, and silver reliably forms Ag+.

Because these elements can form ions of different charge, systematic (IUPAC) names use a Roman numeral in brackets after the element name to state the oxidation number unambiguously. FeO is iron(II) oxide and Fe2O3 is iron(III) oxide; Cu2O is copper(I) oxide and CuO is copper(II) oxide. This Roman-numeral naming is only necessary when an element commonly shows more than one oxidation state — sodium, for instance, is simply called sodium oxide (not "sodium(I) oxide") because +1 is its only common state.

Oxidation state vs oxidation numberAn oxidation state is written with a sign and an Arabic numeral (e.g. +2). An oxidation number is shown as a Roman numeral in brackets after the element's name or symbol (e.g. iron(II)). The two terms are often used interchangeably.

Formulas and Names of Ionic Compounds

An ionic compound is always electrically neutral overall: the total positive charge from the cations exactly balances the total negative charge from the anions. Finding the formula of an ionic compound is therefore an exercise in balancing charge, using the smallest whole-number ratio of ions possible. Sodium chloride, Na+Cl−, needs equal numbers of each ion and is written NaCl. Magnesium fluoride needs two F− ions to balance each Mg2+ ion, giving MgF2.

Worked exampleDeduce the formula of the compound formed between aluminium and oxygen.
1. Identify the ions: aluminium (group 13) forms Al3+; oxygen (group 16) forms O2−.
2. Find the lowest common multiple of the charges: 6. This needs 2 × Al3+ (giving 6+) and 3 × O2− (giving 6−).
3. Write the formula using these numbers as subscripts, dropping the charges: Al2O3.

By convention, the cation is always named/written first, followed by the anion, and the anion's ending is usually changed to "-ide" (chlorine becomes chloride, oxygen becomes oxide, nitrogen becomes nitride). Naming an ionic compound is simply the reverse process: state the cation name, then the anion name.

Polyatomic Ions

Some common ions are made of more than one atom bonded covalently together, with the group as a whole carrying an overall charge; these are called polyatomic ions. It is worth learning the names, formulas and charges of the following polyatomic ions, since they appear constantly in chemical formulas and equations.

Note that the bonding between the atoms within a polyatomic ion (e.g. between S and O in sulfate) is covalent, while the bonding between the polyatomic ion and its counter-ion (e.g. between K+ and SO42− in potassium sulfate) is ionic. When a formula needs more than one of a polyatomic ion, the ion's formula is placed in parentheses with the multiplying subscript outside.

Worked exampleDeduce the formula of ammonium phosphate.
1. The two ions involved are NH4+ and PO43−.
2. The lowest common multiple of the charges is 3: this needs 3 × NH4+ (3+) and 1 × PO43− (3−).
3. Formula: (NH4)3PO4.
Common mistakeIonic compound formulas always show the simplest whole-number ratio of ions, never a multiple of it. Magnesium oxide is MgO, never Mg2O2. Also, never call an ionic compound a "molecule" — the correct term is formula unit, since a lattice has no fixed, discrete number of ions.

The Ionic Lattice and Its Physical Properties

The sodium chloride latticeA three-dimensional grid of alternating spheres represents the NaCl lattice. Smaller spheres (Na+) and larger spheres (Cl−) alternate in all three directions, so that every Na+ ion is surrounded by six nearest-neighbour Cl− ions, and every Cl− ion is surrounded by six nearest-neighbour Na+ ions. The pattern repeats indefinitely in a regular, repeating unit (the unit cell), which is why ionic solids form crystals with flat faces and regular geometric shapes.

Because a lattice can grow indefinitely and has no fixed total number of ions, the formula of an ionic compound is an empirical formula — it states only the simplest ratio of ions present, referred to as the formula unit.

Lattice Enthalpy

Lattice enthalpyThe lattice enthalpy (ΔH⊖lat) is the enthalpy change when one mole of a solid ionic lattice is separated into its gaseous ions. It is always positive, since energy must be supplied to overcome the electrostatic attraction holding the lattice together, and it is used as a direct measure of the strength of the ionic bond in a compound.
Na+(g) + Cl−(g) → NaCl(s) ΔH = −790 kJ mol−1
NaCl(s) → Na+(g) + Cl−(g) ΔH⊖lat = +790 kJ mol−1

The first equation shows that 790 kJ of energy is released when one mole of gaseous sodium ions and one mole of gaseous chloride ions come together to form the solid lattice; this favourable energy release is what drives ionic compounds to form. The lattice enthalpy is defined as the reverse of this process, so it carries the opposite (positive) sign.

Worked exampleExplain, using lattice enthalpy, why magnesium oxide (ΔH⊖lat = +3795 kJ mol−1) has a far higher melting point than sodium fluoride (ΔH⊖lat = +923 kJ mol−1). Mg2+ and O2− ions both carry twice the charge of Na+ and F−, and are also similar in size, so the electrostatic attraction between them in the MgO lattice is far stronger. A much larger amount of energy is needed to separate the ions in MgO, which is reflected directly in its much larger lattice enthalpy and its much higher melting point (3125 K for MgO compared with 1266 K for NaF).
Trends in lattice enthalpyLattice enthalpy increases as ionic charge increases (greater charge means stronger electrostatic attraction). Lattice enthalpy decreases as ionic radius increases (the ions cannot approach as closely, weakening the attraction). This explains why the melting points of the sodium halides fall steadily from NaF (1266 K) to NaCl (1074 K) to NaBr (1020 K) to NaI (934 K), since the halide ion gets larger down the group while its charge stays the same.

Melting Point, Boiling Point and Volatility

Because a large amount of energy is needed to pull the ions in a lattice apart, ionic compounds are crystalline solids at room temperature with high melting and boiling points, and low volatility (a low tendency to vaporize).

Solubility

Dissolving of an ionic lattice in waterA crystal surface of alternating Na+ and Cl− ions is shown surrounded by polar water molecules. Each water molecule is drawn bent, with a small δ+ symbol on each hydrogen atom and a small δ− symbol on the oxygen atom. Water molecules orient their oxygen (δ−) ends toward Na+ ions and their hydrogen (δ+) ends toward Cl− ions. As ions at the crystal surface become surrounded by these oriented water molecules, they are pulled away from the lattice into solution, a process called hydration.

Water is a polar molecule: the oxygen atom carries a small negative charge and the hydrogen atoms carry small positive charges. These partial charges attract the ions at the surface of an ionic crystal away from the lattice, surrounding (hydrating) them and allowing the solid to dissolve: NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq). This same principle extends to other polar solvents, giving the general rule "like dissolves like": ionic compounds tend to be soluble in polar solvents such as water, but insoluble in non-polar solvents such as hexane or oil, since non-polar molecules have no partial charges to attract the ions away from the lattice. As with most generalizations there are exceptions — calcium carbonate, for instance, is an ionic compound that does not dissolve appreciably in water.

Electrical Conductivity

Whether an ionic compound conducts electricity depends entirely on whether its ions are free to move as mobile charge carriers. In the solid state, the ions are fixed in place within the lattice, so solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity. When melted or dissolved in water, however, the ions become mobile and are free to carry charge, so ionic compounds do conduct electricity in the molten or aqueous state.

Brittleness

Why ionic solids are brittleTwo diagrams show a layer of an ionic lattice before and after a shear force is applied. Before: rows of alternating + and − ions sit directly above rows of opposite charge, so all neighbouring ions attract each other. After a sideways force displaces the top layer by one ion-spacing: ions of the same charge now sit directly above one another. The like-charged ions strongly repel each other and the layers fly apart, shattering the crystal.

A shearing force can shift one layer of ions relative to the layer beneath it, so that ions of the same charge end up next to each other instead of ions of opposite charge. The resulting repulsion between like-charged ions splits the crystal apart, which is why ionic solids are typically hard but brittle.

The Bonding Continuum and Electronegativity

Not every compound formed from a metal and a non-metal behaves as a perfect ionic model would predict. Ionic bonding can be viewed as an extreme case of a polar covalent bond, in which the bonding electrons are not merely shared unequally but are considered to belong almost entirely to the more electronegative atom. Electronegativity measures an atom's ability to attract a shared pair of bonding electrons, and the difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms (ΔχP, on the Pauling scale) gives a way of estimating how ionic (or how covalent) a bond really is.

% ionic character ≈ (ΔχP ÷ 3.2) × 100%

where ΔχP is the electronegativity difference between the two bonded elements, and 3.2 is the electronegativity difference of caesium fluoride, CsF, the compound generally taken as being 100% ionic (from the most electropositive metal and the most electronegative non-metal). A difference of about 1.6 corresponds to roughly 50% ionic character, and a difference greater than about 1.8 is usually described as predominantly ionic.

Worked exampleWhich pair of elements is most likely to form an ionic bond: (A) B and F, (B) Si and O, (C) N and Cl, or (D) K and Cl?
Electronegativity differences: (A) 4.0 − 2.0 = 2.0; (B) 3.4 − 1.9 = 1.5; (C) 3.2 − 3.0 = 0.2; (D) 3.2 − 0.8 = 2.4.
Option D has the largest electronegativity difference, so KCl is the most ionic pairing.
The bonding continuumA horizontal number line runs from an electronegativity difference of 0 on the left to a large value on the right. At the far left (difference much less than 1.8, two non-metals of similar electronegativity) sits the label "covalent compounds." In the middle range (difference between 0 and 1.8) sits "polar covalent compounds." At the right, beyond a difference of about 1.8 (a reactive metal paired with a reactive non-metal), sits "ionic compounds." The diagram emphasizes that there is no sharp boundary between the three regions — bonding character changes gradually along the line.

Sodium chloride's melting point and conductivity data match a highly ionic compound, but as electronegativity difference falls across a period, so does ionic character. This is well illustrated by the period 3 chlorides: NaCl and MgCl2 behave as classic ionic solids with high melting points and high electrical conductivity when molten, but AlCl3 has a much lower melting point and only poor conductivity in the liquid state, showing it has significant covalent character, while SiCl4, PCl5, SCl2 and Cl2 are essentially covalent, with low melting points and no conductivity at all. The ionic model is a very useful simplification, but real bonding across the periodic table forms a continuous spectrum rather than three separate boxes.

Guiding Question Revisited

  • An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom (or group of atoms) loses or gains one or more electrons.
  • Metals lose electrons to form cations; non-metals gain electrons to form anions. Transition metals and some other elements (e.g. Pb, Sn) can form ions of more than one charge.
  • The charge on a main-group ion can usually be predicted from its group number in the periodic table.
  • Ionic compounds are electrically neutral formula units, with the simplest whole-number ratio of ions balancing total positive and negative charge.
  • Ionic compounds form three-dimensional lattices held together by strong electrostatic attraction (the ionic bond), which is measured by lattice enthalpy.
  • Lattice enthalpy increases with greater ionic charge and decreases with greater ionic radius.
  • The lattice structure explains the characteristic properties of ionic compounds: high melting and boiling points, low volatility, brittleness, solubility in polar solvents but not non-polar solvents, and electrical conductivity only when molten or in aqueous solution.
  • The distinction between ionic and covalent bonding is a continuum, not a strict divide; the electronegativity difference between bonded atoms indicates the degree of ionic character.
