What determines the direction of chemical change?

Bubbles rise and escape from a carbonated drink, and warm cups of coffee become
cold. These examples illustrate a general principle known as the second law of
thermodynamics: matter and energy tend to disperse and become more dispersed.
Carbon dioxide tends to diffuse from the drink into the surroundings, and the energy
tends to be transferred from a cup with a high temperature to the surroundings at

a lower temperature. These everyday experiences can be expressed more precisely
when the degree of dispersal of matter and energy of a system is quantified by its
entropy (S). The direction of natural or spontaneous change is from low dispersal or
entropy to high entropy. This principle also provides us with a signpost for chemical
change. Chemical reactions will occur if the total entropy of the universe increases. We

see in Reactivity 1.1 that exothermic reactions are more common than endothermic
reactions. We will see that these reactions increase the total entropy of the universe as
heat is dispersed into the surroundings. We will learn that as heat can be transferred in
the reverse direction in endothermic reactions, there is high-level dispersal generated
within the system by the heat transfer.

(0) Nature of Science

Scientists have called the second law of thermodynamics the most fundamental
law in all of science and some, including Albert Einstein, have argued that it

is one of the few laws that will never be overthrown. It is important, however,
to understand that it is a statement of experience which cannot be proved. It

is not impossible for a disordered arrangement to spontaneously change into

a more ordered arrangement, but it is statistically unlikely. The term ‘law’ is

sometimes used for statements that allow predictions to be made about natural
phenomena without explaining them. Does the second law fit this description?
Can the second law ever be falsified?

Entropy is a measure of the dispersal or distribution
of matter

If a bottle of a carbonated drink is left open, we expect it to find it ‘flat’ after a couple
of days. The carbon dioxide escapes from solution and diffuses (spreads out) into the
wider surroundings. We do not expect all the carbon dioxide to return to the bottle
at a later date. In a similar way, a hot cup of coffee will cool down and transfer heat to
the surroundings. The heat will not return to the cup. Both these examples illustrate
a general principle: energy and matter tend to disperse and the universe becomes
more disordered. These are both examples of spontaneous change; they occur
naturally without the need to do work. We can reverse the natural tendency of change
but only at the expense of doing work. Similarly, sodium and chlorine have a natural
tendency to react together to form sodium chloride. We can reverse this process and
split sodium chloride into its constituents, but only at the expense of using valuable
electrical energy, as discussed in Reactivity 3.2.

Entropy, S, is a measure of the dispersal or distribution of matter and/or energy in a
system. Ordered states with a small energy distribution are said to have low entropy;
disordered states with a high energy distribution have high entropy. As time moves
forward, matter and energy become more dispersed, and the total entropy of the
universe increases.

This is an expression of the second law of thermodynamics, which is one of the most
important laws in science (Figure 1).

Predicting entropy changes

As the solid state is the most ordered state with the least dispersal of matter and

the gaseous state is the most dispersed, we can predict that the entropy of a system
increases as solid changes to liquid and as liquid changes to gas. Similarly, doubling
the number of particles present in a sample also increases the opportunity for matter
and energy to be more dispersed and for its entropy to increase. More precisely, it
can be shown that doubling the amount of a substance doubles the entropy. Similar
considerations allow us to predict the entropy changes of the system (AS) during any
physical or chemical change. Some examples are tabulated below.

Guiding Question revisited

What determines the direction of chemical change?

In this chapter we introduced the concept of entropy, S, which is a measure of
the dispersal or distribution of matter and/or energy. We have seen that entropy
determines the direction of reactions. Reactions can occur spontaneously if the
entropy of the universe increases. These ideas can be summarized as follows:

* Changes are spontaneous if they correspond to an increase in total entropy of
the systems and the surroundings.

* The changes in entropy of the surroundings are related to the enthalpy
change of the system and the temperature. When heat is transferred to the
surroundings during exothermic reactions, the entropy of the surroundings
increases.

* Endothermic reactions are less common but can be spontaneous if the increase
in the entropy of the system is sufficient to offset the decrease in entropy of the
surroundings that occurs when heat is transferred from the surroundings to the
system.

* The total entropy change can be expressed in terms of the Gibbs energy of a
system. The direction of spontaneous change is the direction of decreasing
Gibbs energy. A reaction is spontaneous if the standard Gibbs energy change is
negative.

¢ -AG is the useful energy that can be obtained from a chemical reaction.

* The direction of some reactions can be changed by altering the temperature:
AG(system) = AH(system) - TAS(system)

* The standard Gibbs energy change at 298 K can be calculated from the standard

free energies of formation. The Gibbs energy change at other temperatures can
be calculated from enthalpy and entropy changes.

* The standard Gibbs energy depends on the composition of the reaction system.

* A system is at equilibrium when AG = 0. As a reaction approaches equilibrium,
the reaction quotient approaches the equilibrium constant.
* Equilibrium constants and standard Gibbs energy changes are related by the
equation:
AGS = -RTInK
