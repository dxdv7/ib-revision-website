What happens when reactants share their electron pairs with others?

When one reactant shares an electron pair with another reactant, this results in

the formation of a coordination bond. This is acommon theme in many areas of
chemistry, including the reactions of acids and bases, transition metal ions and many
organic compounds. Specific examples of these different reactions, highlighting how
each involves the movement of electron pairs, will be presented in this chapter. In
particular we will examine how:

* organic compounds react with electrophiles and nucleophiles, donating or
accepting electron pairs to form new covalent bonds

. Lewis bases can react by donating electron pairs to Lewis acids

. ligands donate electron pairs to transition metal ions and form complex
ions.

We will also look at how the use of curly arrows, that represent the movement of
electron pairs, provides an effective way of explaining the mechanisms of electron-
pair sharing reactions. This is of particular relevance to the reactions of organic
compounds, such as the substitution reactions of halogenoalkanes and the addition
reactions of alkenes, as we will see in this chapter.

Bronsted—Lowry theory. Realizing that the base must have a lone pair of electrons, he
reasoned that the entire reaction could be viewed in terms of the electron pair rather
than in terms of proton transfer. For example, the reaction in which ammonia acts as a
base can be represented as follows:

Different theories to explain a phenomenon can sometimes supplement
rather than contradict each other. Bronsted—Lowry and Lewis theories each
contribute to our understanding of acid-base behaviour.

CB Nature of Science

Bronsted, Lowry and Lewis independently developed the acid-base theories
that are named for them. They worked in an era before mass communication
and would only have become aware of each other’s results through written
correspondence and/or publication in printed journals. Scientists today have
the ability to communicate and share data almost instantaneously through
digital communications and are therefore able to more readily engage in
collaborative research.

In the case of acids, however, the Lewis definition is broader than the Brensted—Lowry
theory which is restricted to H*. An acid, by Lewis definition, is any species capable of
accepting a lone pair of electrons. Of course, this includes H* with its vacant orbital (so
all Bronsted—Lowry acids are Lewis acids) — but it will also include molecules that have
an incomplete valence shell.

Lewis acid—base reactions result in the formation of a covalent bond, which will always
be a coordination bond because both the electrons come from the base.

Lewis acids are electrophiles and Lewis bases are nucleophiles

In previous sections, we introduced substitution reactions (page 833) and addition
reactions (page 837) which both involve the formation of covalent bonds due to
the actions of electrophiles or nucleophiles. The definitions presented earlier for
electrophiles and nucleophiles were also defined in terms of bond formation.

A nucleophile (‘likes nucleus’) is an electron-rich species that donates a lone pair to
another reactant to form a new covalent bond.

An electrophile (‘likes electrons’) is an electron-deficient species that accepts a lone pair
from another reactant to form a new covalent bond.

Clearly these definitions are consistent with those derived by Lewis. In other words, a
nucleophile is a Lewis base and an electrophile is a Lewis acid.

The carbon atoms of the double bond are sp? hybridized, forming a planar triangular

for incoming groups to attack.

The pi (x) bond represents an area of electron density above and below the plane

electrophiles, species that either are electron deficient or that become electron deficient
in the presence of the 7 bond.

Because electrons in the x bond are less closely associated with the nuclei, it is a weaker
bond than the sigma (o) bond and so breaks more easily during the addition reactions.

Convention for depicting organic reaction mechanisms

Describing organic reaction mechanisms often involves showing the movement of
electrons within bonds and between reactants. The convention adopted for this is
a curly arrow, drawn from the site of electron availability, such as a pair of non-
bonding electrons, to the site of electron deficiency, such as an atom with a partial
positive charge.

ultimately to an atom or group of atoms that then detaches itself and is known as the

of electrons.

Note that a single-barbed arrow (7 ™), known as a fish hook, represents the
