The scalar (dot) product

For \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix}u_1\\u_2\\u_3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}v_1\\v_2\\v_3\end{pmatrix}\), the scalar (dot) product is defined algebraically as:
\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3\]and is equal to the following geometric expression, where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) when they are placed tail to tail:
\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\]The result of a dot product is always a scalar, not a vector.
Worked exampleFind \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) for \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix}\).
\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (2)(1)+(-1)(4)+(3)(-2) = 2-4-6\]\[\boxed{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -8}\]

Algebraic properties of the dot product

The dot product is commutative and distributes over vector addition, and scalars can be factored out of either vector:
\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}\]\[\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}\]\[k(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = (k\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{v}\]
Worked exampleGiven \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\), and \(\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\4\\1\end{pmatrix}\), verify that \(\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}\).
\[\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = (1)(3)+(2)(3)+(-1)(3) = 3+6-3 = 6\]\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 3-2-2 = -1, \qquad \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w} = 0+8-1 = 7, \qquad \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w} = -1+7 = 6\]\[\boxed{\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = 6 = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}}\]

Finding the angle between two vectors

Rearranging \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\) gives:
\[\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}\right)\]Since \(\cos^{-1}\) always returns a value in \([0,\pi]\), the angle \(\theta\) found this way is always between \(0\) and \(\pi\) (that is, between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\)), measured between the two vectors when placed tail to tail.
Worked exampleFind the angle between \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{i} - 2\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = -3\mathbf{i} + 6\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}\).
\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (1)(-3)+(-2)(6)+(2)(2) = -3-12+4 = -11\]\[|\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{1+4+4} = 3, \qquad |\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{9+36+4} = \sqrt{49} = 7\]\[\cos\theta = \frac{-11}{(3)(7)} = -\frac{11}{21}\]\[\boxed{\theta = \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{11}{21}\right) \approx 2.12 \text{ radians} \approx 121.6^\circ}\]

Perpendicular vectors

Two vectors are perpendicular (orthogonal) exactly when their dot product is zero, since \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\):
\[\mathbf{u} \perp \mathbf{v} \iff \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0\]
Worked exampleDetermine whether \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{pmatrix}7\\3\\2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}-3\\5\\3\end{pmatrix}\) are orthogonal.
\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (7)(-3)+(3)(5)+(2)(3) = -21+15+6\]\[\boxed{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0, \text{ so } \mathbf{u} \text{ and } \mathbf{v} \text{ are orthogonal}}\]

The dot product of parallel vectors

When two non-zero vectors are parallel, \(\theta\) is either \(0\) or \(\pi\):
\[\theta = 0 \text{ (same direction)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\]\[\theta = \pi \text{ (opposite directions)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\]These are the largest and smallest possible values of \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) for vectors of given magnitudes: in general \(|\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}| \le |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\).
Worked exampleGiven \(|\mathbf{u}| = 3\) and \(|\mathbf{v}| = 5\), with \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) pointing in opposite directions, find \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\).
Since \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are parallel with \(\theta = \pi\):
\[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}| = -(3)(5)\]\[\boxed{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = -15}\]

Using the dot product to identify right angles

For a triangle with vertices \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\), the triangle is right-angled at \(A\) exactly when the two sides meeting at \(A\) are perpendicular:
\[\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC} = 0\]
Worked exampleShow that the triangle with vertices \(A(1,2,3)\), \(B(-3,2,4)\), and \(C(1,-4,3)\) is right-angled, and state at which vertex.
\[\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix}-3-1\\2-2\\4-3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-4\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \overrightarrow{AC} = \begin{pmatrix}1-1\\-4-2\\3-3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\-6\\0\end{pmatrix}\]\[\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC} = (-4)(0)+(0)(-6)+(1)(0) = 0\]\[\boxed{\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{AC} = 0, \text{ so the triangle is right-angled at } A}\]
