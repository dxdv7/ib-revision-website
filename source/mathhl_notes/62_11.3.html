Conditional probability

The conditional probability of \(A\) given \(B\) is the probability that \(A\) occurs, updated on the knowledge that \(B\) has occurred. Knowing that \(B\) happened restricts the sample space to \(B\) itself, so:
\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) \neq 0\]
Worked exampleIn a class of 30 students, 18 study French and 6 of these 18 also study Spanish. Overall, \(P(F) = \frac{18}{30}\) and \(P(F \cap S) = \frac{6}{30}\). Find \(P(S \mid F)\), the probability a student studies Spanish given that they study French.
\[P(S \mid F) = \frac{P(F \cap S)}{P(F)} = \frac{6/30}{18/30} = \frac{6}{18}\]\[\boxed{P(S \mid F) = \frac{1}{3}}\]

The general multiplication rule

Rearranging the definition of conditional probability gives a rule for the probability that two events both occur:
\[P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)\]This is especially useful when events occur in sequence, such as successive draws from a bag without replacement, where the second probability depends on the outcome of the first.
Worked exampleA bag contains 5 red and 3 blue marbles (8 in total). Two marbles are drawn at random, one after the other, without replacement. Find the probability that both marbles are red.
The first marble is red with probability \(P(R_1) = \frac{5}{8}\). Given that the first was red, only 4 red marbles remain out of 7, so \(P(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{4}{7}\).
\[P(R_1 \cap R_2) = P(R_1)\,P(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{5}{8}\times\frac{4}{7} = \frac{20}{56}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{both red}) = \frac{5}{14}}\]

Testing for independence using conditional probability

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent precisely when knowing that one occurred does not change the probability of the other:
\[A \text{ and } B \text{ are independent} \iff P(A \mid B) = P(A) \iff P(B \mid A) = P(B) \iff P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\]If any one of these equalities fails, the events are not independent.
Worked exampleAmong 100 customers at a cafe, 60 buy a coffee, 30 buy a muffin, and 18 buy both. Let \(C\) be "buys a coffee" and \(M\) be "buys a muffin". Determine whether \(C\) and \(M\) are independent.
\[P(C) = 0.6, \qquad P(M) = 0.3, \qquad P(C \cap M) = 0.18\]\[P(C \mid M) = \frac{P(C \cap M)}{P(M)} = \frac{0.18}{0.3} = 0.6\]Since \(P(C \mid M) = 0.6 = P(C)\):
\[\boxed{C \text{ and } M \text{ are independent}}\]

Conditional probability from a two-way table

When data is organised in a two-way frequency table, a conditional probability is found by restricting attention to the row or column representing the condition, then dividing the joint count by that row or column total:
\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)}\]
Worked exampleOf 100 students, 50 studied for a test and 50 did not. Among those who studied, 42 passed and 8 failed. Among those who did not study, 18 passed and 32 failed (so 60 passed overall and 40 failed overall). Find (a) \(P(\text{Pass} \mid \text{Studied})\) and (b) \(P(\text{Studied} \mid \text{Pass})\), and compare them.
(a) Restricting to the 50 students who studied: \[P(\text{Pass} \mid \text{Studied}) = \frac{42}{50} = 0.84\](b) Restricting instead to the 60 students who passed: \[P(\text{Studied} \mid \text{Pass}) = \frac{42}{60} = 0.7\]\[\boxed{P(\text{Pass}\mid\text{Studied}) = 0.84, \quad P(\text{Studied}\mid\text{Pass}) = 0.7 \text{ — these are different quantities}}\]

Conditional probability, complements and subset events

Since \(A\) and \(A'\) partition the restricted sample space \(B\):
\[P(A' \mid B) = 1 - P(A \mid B)\]Two extreme cases follow directly from the definition: if \(B \subseteq A\) (so \(B\) guarantees \(A\)), then \(P(A \mid B) = 1\); if \(A \cap B = \varnothing\) (so \(B\) rules out \(A\)), then \(P(A \mid B) = 0\).
Worked exampleUsing the French/Spanish class from above, where \(P(S \mid F) = \frac{1}{3}\), find \(P(S' \mid F)\), the probability a French student does not study Spanish, and verify it directly from the counts.
\[P(S' \mid F) = 1 - P(S \mid F) = 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}\]Directly: of the 18 French students, \(18 - 6 = 12\) do not study Spanish, giving \(\frac{12}{18} = \frac{2}{3}\).
\[\boxed{P(S' \mid F) = \frac{2}{3}}\]

Conditional probability from a Venn diagram

When a Venn diagram shows the number of outcomes in each region, a conditional probability is read off by dividing the count in the overlap by the count in the whole conditioning circle:
\[P(B \mid A) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(A)}\]
Worked exampleA survey of 50 people produces a Venn diagram for events \(A\) and \(B\) with: \(A\) only \(=14\), \(B\) only \(=9\), both \(A\) and \(B = 12\), and neither \(=15\) (check: \(14+9+12+15=50\)). Find \(P(B \mid A)\).
\[n(A) = 14 + 12 = 26, \qquad n(A \cap B) = 12\]\[P(B \mid A) = \frac{12}{26}\]\[\boxed{P(B \mid A) = \frac{6}{13}}\]
