The area of a triangle using sine

For a triangle with two known sides \(a\) and \(b\) and the included angle \(C\) between them, the area is given by:
\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C\]and similarly \(\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}bc\sin A\) or \(\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}ac\sin B\), depending on which angle and pair of sides are known. This formula follows from the standard area formula \(\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{base})(\text{height})\) by writing the height in terms of \(\sin C\), and it applies to any triangle, not only right-angled triangles.
Worked exampleFind the area of a triangle with sides \(a = 10\text{ cm}\) and \(b = 14\text{ cm}\) and included angle \(C = 42^\circ\), correct to 3 significant figures.
\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin C\]\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(10)(14)\sin 42^\circ\]\[\text{Area} = 70\sin 42^\circ\]\[\boxed{\text{Area} \approx 46.8 \text{ cm}^2}\]

Finding an angle when the area is known

Rearranging the area formula makes \(\sin C\) the subject:
\[\sin C = \frac{2 \times \text{Area}}{ab}\]Since \(\sin C = \sin(180^\circ - C)\), there are generally two possible values of \(C\) between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\) that give the same area: an acute angle and its supplementary obtuse angle. A calculator's \(\sin^{-1}\) function returns only the acute value.
Worked exampleA triangle has sides \(12\text{ cm}\) and \(16\text{ cm}\) and an area of \(80\text{ cm}^2\). Find the two possible sizes of the included angle, correct to 3 significant figures.
\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(12)(16)\sin\theta\]\[80 = 96\sin\theta\]\[\sin\theta = \frac{80}{96} \approx 0.8333\]\[\theta_1 = \sin^{-1}(0.8333)\]\[\boxed{\theta_1 \approx 56.4^\circ}\]\[\theta_2 = 180^\circ - \theta_1\]\[\boxed{\theta_2 \approx 124^\circ}\]

Three-figure bearings

A bearing gives a direction as an angle measured clockwise from north. Bearings are always written using three digits, with leading zeros where needed, so they range from \(000^\circ\) to \(360^\circ\). North is \(000^\circ\), east is \(090^\circ\), south is \(180^\circ\) and west is \(270^\circ\).
Worked exampleWrite each of the following as a three-figure bearing: (a) due west, (b) north-west, (c) a direction \(15^\circ\) east of south.
(a) Due west points directly along the west direction:
\[\boxed{270^\circ}\](b) North-west lies exactly halfway between north \((360^\circ)\) and west \((270^\circ)\):
\[\boxed{315^\circ}\](c) Measured clockwise from north, \(15^\circ\) east of south is:
\[180^\circ - 15^\circ\]\[\boxed{165^\circ}\]

Back bearings

If the bearing of point \(B\) from point \(A\) is known, the back bearing (the bearing of \(A\) from \(B\)) is found by adding or subtracting \(180^\circ\), keeping the result between \(000^\circ\) and \(360^\circ\):
\[\text{back bearing} = \text{bearing} + 180^\circ \quad (\text{if bearing} < 180^\circ)\]\[\text{back bearing} = \text{bearing} - 180^\circ \quad (\text{if bearing} \geq 180^\circ)\]
Worked exampleThe bearing of town \(Y\) from town \(X\) is \(250^\circ\). Find the bearing of \(X\) from \(Y\).
Since \(250^\circ \geq 180^\circ\):
\[250^\circ - 180^\circ\]\[\boxed{070^\circ}\]

Using the sine rule in bearing problems

In many navigation problems, the angle between two legs of a journey is not given directly but must be found from the bearings of each leg, since angles measured clockwise from north differ by the angle between the two directions. Once the included or opposite angles are known, the sine rule can be applied as usual to find unknown sides.
Worked exampleFrom point \(A\), the bearing of a tower \(T\) is \(048^\circ\). Point \(B\) is \(420\text{ m}\) due east of \(A\). From \(B\), the bearing of the tower is \(310^\circ\). Find the distance \(BT\), correct to 3 significant figures.
The bearing of \(B\) from \(A\) is \(090^\circ\), so:
\[\angle TAB = 090^\circ - 048^\circ = 42^\circ\]The back bearing of \(A\) from \(B\) is \(090^\circ + 180^\circ = 270^\circ\), so:
\[\angle TBA = 310^\circ - 270^\circ = 40^\circ\]\[\angle ATB = 180^\circ - 42^\circ - 40^\circ = 98^\circ\]\[\frac{BT}{\sin A} = \frac{AB}{\sin(\angle ATB)}\]\[BT = \frac{420\sin 42^\circ}{\sin 98^\circ}\]\[\boxed{BT \approx 284 \text{ m}}\]

Using the cosine rule in bearing problems

When two distances from a common point and the angle between the corresponding bearings are known (SAS), the cosine rule can be used directly to find the distance between the two end points.
Worked exampleA boat sails from harbour \(H\) on a bearing of \(130^\circ\) for \(35\text{ km}\) to a buoy \(B\). It then sails from \(B\) on a bearing of \(255^\circ\) for \(50\text{ km}\) to a lighthouse \(L\). Find the distance \(HL\), correct to 3 significant figures.
The back bearing of \(H\) from \(B\) is \(130^\circ + 180^\circ = 310^\circ\), so:
\[\angle HBL = 310^\circ - 255^\circ = 55^\circ\]\[HL^2 = BH^2 + BL^2 - 2(BH)(BL)\cos(\angle HBL)\]\[HL^2 = 35^2 + 50^2 - 2(35)(50)\cos 55^\circ\]\[HL^2 \approx 1717\]\[HL \approx \sqrt{1717}\]\[\boxed{HL \approx 41.4 \text{ km}}\]
