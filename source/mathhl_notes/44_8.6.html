De Moivre's theorem

If \(z = r\,\text{cis}\,\theta = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\) and \(n\) is a positive integer, then:
\[z^n = (r\,\text{cis}\,\theta)^n = r^n\,\text{cis}(n\theta) = r^n(\cos n\theta + i \sin n\theta)\]In words: to raise a complex number in polar form to the power \(n\), raise the modulus to the power \(n\) and multiply the argument by \(n\). The result also extends to negative integers \(n\):
\[z^{-n} = r^{-n}\,\text{cis}(-n\theta) = r^{-n}(\cos n\theta - i\sin n\theta)\]
Worked exampleUse De Moivre's theorem to find \((1+i)^6\).
First convert \(1+i\) to polar form: \(r = \sqrt{1^2+1^2} = \sqrt{2}\), \(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}\), so \(1+i = \sqrt{2}\,\text{cis}\dfrac{\pi}{4}\).
\[(1+i)^6 = \left(\sqrt{2}\right)^6\text{cis}\left(6 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 8\,\text{cis}\frac{3\pi}{2}\]\[= 8\left(\cos\frac{3\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}\right) = 8(0 - i)\]\[\boxed{(1+i)^6 = -8i}\]

The nth roots of a complex number

An \(n\)th root of a complex number \(z\) is any number \(w\) satisfying \(w^n = z\). If \(z = r\,\text{cis}\,\theta\) and \(n\) is a positive integer, then \(z\) has exactly \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots, given by:
\[w_k = r^{1/n}\,\text{cis}\left(\frac{\theta + 2\pi k}{n}\right), \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1\]
Worked exampleFind the three cube roots of \(z = 8\).
In polar form, \(z = 8\,\text{cis}\,0\), so \(r = 8\) and \(\theta = 0\).
\[w_k = 8^{1/3}\,\text{cis}\left(\frac{0 + 2\pi k}{3}\right) = 2\,\text{cis}\left(\frac{2\pi k}{3}\right), \qquad k = 0,1,2\]\[w_0 = 2\,\text{cis}\,0 = 2\]\[w_1 = 2\,\text{cis}\frac{2\pi}{3} = 2\left(-\frac{1}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = -1+i\sqrt3\]\[\boxed{w_0 = 2, \quad w_1 = -1+i\sqrt3, \quad w_2 = -1-i\sqrt3}\]

Finding roots of a complex number not on the real axis

To find the \(n\)th roots of a general complex number \(z = a+bi\), first write \(z\) in polar form \(z = r\,\text{cis}\,\theta\) with \(r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) and \(\theta\) the argument of \(z\), then apply the \(n\)th roots formula.
Worked exampleFind the three cube roots of \(z = -4\sqrt2 + 4\sqrt2\,i\).
\[r = \sqrt{(-4\sqrt2)^2 + (4\sqrt2)^2} = \sqrt{32+32} = 8, \qquad \theta = \frac{3\pi}{4}\]\[w_k = 8^{1/3}\,\text{cis}\left(\frac{\frac{3\pi}{4} + 2\pi k}{3}\right) = 2\,\text{cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{2\pi k}{3}\right), \qquad k=0,1,2\]\[w_0 = 2\,\text{cis}\frac{\pi}{4} = \sqrt2 + i\sqrt2\]\[w_1 = 2\,\text{cis}\frac{11\pi}{12}, \qquad w_2 = 2\,\text{cis}\frac{19\pi}{12}\]\[\boxed{w_0 = \sqrt2+i\sqrt2, \quad w_1 = 2\,\text{cis}\frac{11\pi}{12}, \quad w_2 = 2\,\text{cis}\frac{19\pi}{12}}\]

Geometric placement of the nth roots

The \(n\) distinct \(n\)th roots of \(z = r\,\text{cis}\,\theta\) all have the same modulus \(r^{1/n}\), so they lie on a circle of radius \(r^{1/n}\) centred at the origin. Consecutive roots have arguments differing by \(\dfrac{2\pi}{n}\), so the \(n\) roots are equally spaced around this circle.
Worked exampleConfirm that the cube roots of \(z=8\) found above, \(w_0 = 2\), \(w_1 = -1+i\sqrt3\), \(w_2=-1-i\sqrt3\), lie on a circle of radius 2 and are equally spaced.
\[|w_0| = 2, \qquad |w_1| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + (\sqrt3)^2} = \sqrt{1+3} = 2, \qquad |w_2| = \sqrt{(-1)^2+(-\sqrt3)^2}=2\]The arguments are \(0\), \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\), and \(\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\), each consecutive pair differing by \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\).
\[\boxed{\text{All three roots lie on the circle } |w|=2 \text{, spaced } \frac{2\pi}{3} \text{ apart}}\]
