The definite integral

The definite integral of \(f\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) is written:
\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\]Unlike the indefinite integral, which represents a family of functions, the definite integral is a single number. The values \(a\) and \(b\) are called the limits of integration, with \(a\) the lower limit and \(b\) the upper limit.
Worked exampleWrite down the definite integral representing "the integral of \(f(x)=x^2\) from \(x=1\) to \(x=3\)", without evaluating it.
The lower limit is \(1\) and the upper limit is \(3\), and the integrand is \(x^2\).
\[\boxed{\int_1^3 x^2\,dx}\]

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

If \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\) (so \(F'(x) = f(x)\)), then:
\[\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)\]This is often written using the notation \(\Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b\), meaning "evaluate \(F(x)\) at \(x=b\), then subtract its value at \(x=a\)." The theorem links differentiation and integration: it shows that a definite integral can be evaluated by finding any antiderivative of the integrand, rather than by a direct computation of area. Because \(F(b)-F(a)\) does not depend on which antiderivative is chosen, the constant of integration \(c\) is not needed (and may be taken as \(c=0\)) when evaluating a definite integral.
Worked exampleEvaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\).
An antiderivative of \(x^2\) is \(F(x) = \dfrac{x^3}{3}\). By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:
\[\int_1^3 x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^3 = \frac{3^3}{3} - \frac{1^3}{3} = \frac{27}{3}-\frac{1}{3} = 9 - \frac{1}{3}\]\[\boxed{\int_1^3 x^2\,dx = \frac{26}{3}}\]

Evaluating a definite integral of a polynomial

To evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) for a polynomial \(f\): find an antiderivative \(F(x)\) by integrating term by term (power rule), then compute \(F(b) - F(a)\).
Worked exampleEvaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 (3x^2 - 2x + 1)\,dx\).
An antiderivative is \(F(x) = x^3 - x^2 + x\).
\[\int_0^2 (3x^2-2x+1)\,dx = \Big[x^3-x^2+x\Big]_0^2 = (2^3-2^2+2) - (0^3-0^2+0)\]\[= (8-4+2) - 0 = 6\]\[\boxed{\int_0^2 (3x^2-2x+1)\,dx = 6}\]

The definite integral as area under a curve

If \(f(x) \geq 0\) for all \(x\) on \([a,b]\), then the definite integral \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) gives exactly the area of the region bounded by the curve \(y=f(x)\), the \(x\)-axis, and the vertical lines \(x=a\) and \(x=b\). This is because the integral can be thought of as the limit of a sum of areas of thin rectangles of width \(\Delta x\) and height \(f(x)\), as \(\Delta x \to 0\).
Worked exampleFind the area of the region bounded by \(y = x^2\), the \(x\)-axis, and the lines \(x=0\) and \(x=2\).
Since \(x^2 \geq 0\) on \([0,2]\), the area equals the definite integral:
\[A = \int_0^2 x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \frac{8}{3} - 0\]\[\boxed{A = \frac{8}{3} \text{ square units}}\]

Areas when the curve lies below the x-axis

If \(f(x) \leq 0\) on \([a,b]\), then \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) is negative: it gives the signed area, not the actual (geometric) area. To find the actual area of a region lying below the \(x\)-axis, take the absolute value of the integral:
\[\text{Area} = \left|\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\right|\]For a curve that crosses the \(x\)-axis within \([a,b]\), the interval must be split at each crossing point, the integral evaluated (and its absolute value taken) on each piece separately, and the results added — simply integrating over the whole interval in one piece would let positive and negative parts cancel.
Worked exampleFind the area of the region bounded by \(y = x^2-4\), the \(x\)-axis, and the lines \(x=0\) and \(x=2\).
On \([0,2]\), \(x^2-4 \leq 0\) (it equals \(0\) only at \(x=2\)), so the curve lies on or below the \(x\)-axis throughout.
\[\int_0^2 (x^2-4)\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}-4x\right]_0^2 = \left(\frac{8}{3}-8\right) - 0 = \frac{8}{3}-\frac{24}{3} = -\frac{16}{3}\]The integral is negative, so the actual area is its absolute value:
\[\boxed{\text{Area} = \left|-\frac{16}{3}\right| = \frac{16}{3} \text{ square units}}\]
