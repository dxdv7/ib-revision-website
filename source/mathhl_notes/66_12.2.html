The derivative of a constant

The graph of \(y=c\), where \(c\) is a constant, is a horizontal line, which has gradient \(0\) everywhere. Hence:
\[\frac{d}{dx}(c) = 0\]This can also be confirmed from first principles: if \(f(x)=c\), then \(f(x+h)=c\) too, so the difference quotient is always zero.
\[f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{c-c}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} 0 = 0\]
Worked exampleFind the derivative of \(f(x)=7\).
Since \(7\) is a constant, its graph is the horizontal line \(y=7\), which has zero gradient everywhere.
\[\boxed{f'(x) = 0}\]

The power rule

For any real number \(n\):
\[\frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}\]This rule extends the first-principles results \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^2)=2x\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^3)=3x^2\) found earlier, and also applies to negative and fractional powers.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(f(x)=x^5\) and \(g(x)=x^{-2}\).
Using the power rule with \(n=5\):
\[f'(x) = 5x^{5-1} = 5x^4\]Using the power rule with \(n=-2\):
\[g'(x) = -2x^{-2-1} = -2x^{-3}\]\[\boxed{f'(x)=5x^4, \ \ g'(x)=-2x^{-3}}\]
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(h(x)=\sqrt{x}\).
Rewrite as a power: \(h(x)=x^{\frac{1}{2}}\). Using the power rule with \(n=\frac{1}{2}\):
\[h'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{\frac{1}{2}-1} = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-\frac{1}{2}}\]\[\boxed{h'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}}\]

Differentiating a sum or difference of terms

A function made up of a sum or difference of terms can be differentiated term by term:
\[\frac{d}{dx}\big(f(x) \pm g(x)\big) = f'(x) \pm g'(x)\]Combined with the power rule and the constant rule, this allows any polynomial to be differentiated directly, term by term, including multiplying through by any constant coefficient: \(\frac{d}{dx}\big(k \cdot x^n\big) = k \cdot nx^{n-1}\).
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(f(x) = 4x^3 - 5x^2 + 7x - 9\).
Differentiate term by term:
\[\frac{d}{dx}(4x^3) = 12x^2, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(-5x^2) = -10x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(7x) = 7, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(-9) = 0\]\[\boxed{f'(x) = 12x^2 - 10x + 7}\]

Derivatives of sin x and cos x

For \(x\) measured in radians, the derivatives of the sine and cosine functions are standard results:
\[\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x \qquad \qquad \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x\]These results only hold when \(x\) is in radians; they are a direct consequence of the limit \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\), applied to the first-principles definition of the derivative.
Worked exampleFind the gradient of the tangent to \(y=\sin x\) at \(x=0\) and at \(x=\frac{\pi}{2}\).
Since \(\frac{dy}{dx}=\cos x\):
\[\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=0} = \cos 0 = 1, \qquad \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=\frac{\pi}{2}} = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 0\]\[\boxed{\text{gradient } =1 \text{ at } x=0; \text{ gradient } = 0 \text{ at } x=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\]

Combining the rules on polynomial and trigonometric expressions

The power rule, constant rule, sum/difference rule, and the derivatives of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) can all be applied together, term by term, to differentiate expressions that mix polynomial and trigonometric terms.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y = 3x^2 - 2\cos x + 5\sin x - 1\).
Differentiate term by term:
\[\frac{d}{dx}(3x^2) = 6x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(-2\cos x) = -2(-\sin x) = 2\sin x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(5\sin x) = 5\cos x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(-1)=0\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = 6x + 2\sin x + 5\cos x}\]
