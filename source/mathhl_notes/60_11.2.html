Union and intersection of events

For two events \(A\) and \(B\) in the same sample space:
• The union \(A \cup B\) ("\(A\) or \(B\)") is the event that \(A\) occurs, or \(B\) occurs, or both.
• The intersection \(A \cap B\) ("\(A\) and \(B\)") is the event that both \(A\) and \(B\) occur.
On a Venn diagram, \(A \cup B\) is the whole region covered by either circle, and \(A \cap B\) is the overlapping region where the circles cross.
Worked exampleA fair six-sided die is rolled once. Let \(A\) = "the result is even" and \(B\) = "the result is greater than 3". List \(A \cup B\) and \(A \cap B\).
\[A = \{2,4,6\}, \qquad B = \{4,5,6\}\]\[A \cup B = \{2,4,5,6\}\]\[\boxed{A \cap B = \{4,6\}}\]

The addition rule for combined events

For any two events \(A\) and \(B\):
\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]The term \(P(A \cap B)\) is subtracted because outcomes in the overlap would otherwise be counted twice, once in \(P(A)\) and once in \(P(B)\).
Worked exampleUsing the die from above, with \(A\) = "even" and \(B\) = "greater than 3", find \(P(A \cup B)\) using the addition rule, and check it against a direct count.
\[P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5, \qquad P(B) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5, \qquad P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}\]\[P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.5 - \frac{1}{3} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}\]Direct count: \(A \cup B = \{2,4,5,6\}\), so \(n(A\cup B) = 4\) and \(\frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}\).
\[\boxed{P(A \cup B) = \frac{2}{3}, \text{ which agrees with the direct count}}\]

Mutually exclusive events

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive (or disjoint) if they cannot both happen at the same time, that is,
\[P(A \cap B) = 0\]In this case the addition rule simplifies to
\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\]On a Venn diagram, mutually exclusive events are drawn as two circles that do not overlap.
Worked exampleA fair six-sided die is rolled once. Let \(C\) = "the result is 1 or 2" and \(D\) = "the result is 5 or 6". Show that \(C\) and \(D\) are mutually exclusive, and find \(P(C \cup D)\).
\[C = \{1,2\}, \qquad D = \{5,6\}, \qquad C \cap D = \varnothing\]So \(C\) and \(D\) are mutually exclusive. \(P(C) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}\) and \(P(D) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}\).
\[P(C \cup D) = P(C) + P(D) = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3}\]\[\boxed{P(C \cup D) = \frac{2}{3}}\]

Independent events and the multiplication rule

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if the occurrence of one has no effect on the probability of the other occurring. For independent events, the probability that both occur is the product of their individual probabilities:
\[P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)\]This is the multiplication rule for independent events, and it applies whenever the two events come from separate, unrelated trials (such as tossing a coin and rolling a die).
Worked exampleA fair coin is tossed and a fair six-sided die is rolled, independently of each other. Let \(E\) = "the coin shows heads" and \(F\) = "the die shows a 6". Find \(P(E \cap F)\).
\[P(E) = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P(F) = \frac{1}{6}\]Since the coin toss and the die roll do not affect each other, \(E\) and \(F\) are independent:
\[P(E \cap F) = P(E) \times P(F) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{6}\]\[\boxed{P(E \cap F) = \frac{1}{12}}\]

Mutually exclusive versus independent events

These two ideas are easily confused, but they are different:
• Mutually exclusive means \(A\) and \(B\) cannot both happen: \(P(A \cap B) = 0\).
• Independent means one event does not influence the other: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)\).
If two events have \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\) and are mutually exclusive, they cannot be independent, since \(P(A \cap B) = 0\) but \(P(A) \times P(B) > 0\). Events can, however, be independent without being mutually exclusive.
Worked exampleA card is drawn at random from a standard deck of 52 cards. Let \(H\) = "the card is a heart" and \(K\) = "the card is a king". Show that \(H\) and \(K\) are independent, and explain why they are not mutually exclusive.
\[P(H) = \frac{13}{52} = \frac{1}{4}, \qquad P(K) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}\]\[P(H) \times P(K) = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{13} = \frac{1}{52}\]There is exactly one card that is both a heart and a king (the king of hearts), so \(P(H \cap K) = \frac{1}{52}\), which matches \(P(H)\times P(K)\): the events are independent. Since \(P(H \cap K) \ne 0\), they are not mutually exclusive.
\[\boxed{H \text{ and } K \text{ are independent but not mutually exclusive}}\]

The complement of a combined event

Since \(A \cup B\) and its complement \((A \cup B)'\) together account for the whole sample space:
\[P((A \cup B)') = 1 - P(A \cup B)\]This lets us find the probability that neither \(A\) nor \(B\) occurs.
Worked exampleUsing \(A\) = "even" and \(B\) = "greater than 3" from the die example above, where \(P(A \cup B) = \frac{2}{3}\), find the probability that neither \(A\) nor \(B\) occurs.
\[P((A \cup B)') = 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}\]Direct check: \((A \cup B)' = \{1,3\}\), so \(n((A\cup B)') = 2\) and \(\frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}\).
\[\boxed{P((A \cup B)') = \frac{1}{3}}\]
