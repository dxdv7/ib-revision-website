Polar (modulus-argument) form of a complex number

Every complex number \(z = x+yi\) can be written in modulus-argument (polar) form:
\[z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\operatorname{cis}\theta\]where \(r = |z| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is the modulus and \(\theta = \arg(z)\) is the argument, the angle the vector \(z\) makes with the positive real axis. The Cartesian coordinates relate to \(r\) and \(\theta\) by:
\[x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \ (x \ne 0)\]
Worked exampleWrite \(z = 1+i\) in polar form.
\[r = \sqrt{1^2+1^2} = \sqrt{2}\]\[\tan\theta = \frac{1}{1} = 1\]Since the real and imaginary parts are both positive, \(z\) lies in the first quadrant, so \(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}\).
\[\boxed{z = \sqrt{2}\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sqrt{2}\operatorname{cis}\frac{\pi}{4}}\]

Finding the argument: using the correct quadrant

The equation \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{y}{x}\) alone does not determine \(\theta\) uniquely, since \(\tan\theta\) has period \(\pi\). The signs of \(x = \operatorname{Re}(z)\) and \(y = \operatorname{Im}(z)\) must be used to place \(\theta\) in the correct quadrant. The principal argument is usually chosen so that \(-\pi < \theta \le \pi\); all other values of \(\arg(z)\) differ from it by a multiple of \(2\pi\).
Worked exampleWrite \(z = \sqrt{3} - i\) in polar form, with \(-\pi < \theta \le \pi\).
\[r = \sqrt{(\sqrt{3})^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{3+1} = 2\]\[\tan\theta = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{3}}\]The real part is positive and the imaginary part is negative, so \(z\) lies in the fourth quadrant, giving \(\theta = -\dfrac{\pi}{6}\).
\[\boxed{z = 2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right) + i\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)\right) = 2\operatorname{cis}\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)}\]

Converting from polar form back to Cartesian form

Given \(z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\), the Cartesian form is recovered using:
\[x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta \implies z = r\cos\theta + (r\sin\theta)i\]
Worked exampleConvert \(z = 6(\cos 50^\circ + i\sin 50^\circ)\) to Cartesian form, correct to 3 decimal places.
\[x = 6\cos 50^\circ \approx 6(0.643) \approx 3.857\]\[y = 6\sin 50^\circ \approx 6(0.766) \approx 4.596\]\[\boxed{z \approx 3.857 + 4.596i}\]

Euler's formula and the exponential form of a complex number

Euler's formula states that for any real \(\theta\):
\[e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\]Combining this with the polar form \(z = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) gives Euler's (exponential) form of a complex number:
\[z = re^{i\theta}\]where \(r = |z|\) and \(\theta = \arg(z)\), exactly as in modulus-argument form.
Worked exampleExpress \(z = 5+5i\) in Euler form.
\[r = \sqrt{5^2+5^2} = 5\sqrt{2}\]\[\tan\theta = \frac{5}{5} = 1 \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{4} \quad (\text{first quadrant})\]\[\boxed{z = 5\sqrt{2}\,e^{i\pi/4}}\]

Evaluating special values in Euler form

Since \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\), substituting particular angles gives exact values, including Euler's identity:
\[e^{i\pi} = \cos\pi + i\sin\pi = -1 \implies e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0\]
Worked exampleEvaluate \(e^{i\pi/2}\).
\[e^{i\pi/2} = \cos\frac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 0 + i(1)\]\[\boxed{e^{i\pi/2} = i}\]
