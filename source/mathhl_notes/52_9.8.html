Finding the intersection of two lines

Two lines in space, \(L_1: \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a_1} + s\mathbf{d_1}\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a_2} + t\mathbf{d_2}\), intersect if there exist values of \(s\) and \(t\) that satisfy all three component equations simultaneously. Equating the \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-components gives three equations in the two unknowns \(s\) and \(t\). Solve any two of these equations for \(s\) and \(t\), then substitute into the third: if it is satisfied, the lines intersect at the point found by substituting back into either line's equation; if it is not satisfied, the system is inconsistent and the lines do not intersect.
Worked exampleShow that the lines \(L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\3\end{pmatrix} + s\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\4\\-1\end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix}3\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\) intersect, and find the point of intersection.
Equating components:
\[x: 3+s = 2+3t \quad\Rightarrow\quad s-3t=-1\]\[y: 1+s = 4-t \quad\Rightarrow\quad s+t=3\]\[z: 3-s = -1+2t \quad\Rightarrow\quad s+2t=4\]From the second equation, \(s = 3-t\). Substituting into the first:
\[(3-t)-3t=-1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 3-4t=-1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t=1, \quad s=2\]Checking the third equation: \(s+2t = 2+2(1) = 4\), which agrees, so the system is consistent. Substituting \(s=2\) into \(L_1\):
\[\boxed{\text{the lines intersect at the point } (5,3,1)}\]

Identifying skew lines

If the direction vectors of two lines are not parallel, but the system of equations obtained by equating their components is inconsistent (no values of \(s\) and \(t\) satisfy all three equations), the lines do not intersect and are not parallel either. Such lines are called skew lines: they lie in different planes and never meet, however far they are extended.
Worked exampleShow that the lines \(L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\3\end{pmatrix} + s\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(L_3: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\4\\0\end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix}3\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\) are skew.
The direction vectors \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}3\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\) are not scalar multiples of each other, so the lines are not parallel. Equating components:
\[x: 3+s=2+3t \quad\Rightarrow\quad s-3t=-1\]\[y: 1+s=4-t \quad\Rightarrow\quad s+t=3\]\[z: 3-s=0+2t \quad\Rightarrow\quad s+2t=3\]From the second equation, \(s=3-t\). Substituting into the first gives \(t=1\) and \(s=2\), exactly as before. Checking the third equation requires \(s+2t=3\), but:
\[\boxed{s+2t = 2+2(1) = 4 \ne 3, \text{ so the system is inconsistent and the lines are skew}}\]

Finding the intersection of a line and a plane

A line with parametric equations \(x=x_0+sl\), \(y=y_0+sm\), \(z=z_0+sn\) meets a plane with Cartesian equation \(Ax+By+Cz=D\) at the parameter value \(s\) satisfying:
\[A(x_0+sl)+B(y_0+sm)+C(z_0+sn)=D\]This is a single linear equation in \(s\). Solving it gives the parameter value at the point of intersection, which is then substituted back into the line's equations to find the coordinates of that point.
Worked exampleFind the point of intersection of the line \(\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix} + s\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}\) and the plane \(x+2y+3z=23\).
Substituting \(x=1+s\), \(y=-1+s\), \(z=2+s\):
\[(1+s)+2(-1+s)+3(2+s)=23\]\[(1-2+6)+(s+2s+3s)=23\]\[5+6s=23\]\[6s=18\]\[\boxed{s=3}\]Substituting \(s=3\) back into the line's equations gives \(x=4\), \(y=2\), \(z=5\):
\[\boxed{\text{the point of intersection is } (4,2,5)}\]

Lines that do not meet a plane

If the direction vector \(\mathbf{d}\) of a line is perpendicular to the normal vector \(\mathbf{N}\) of a plane, that is \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{N}=0\), then the line is parallel to the plane. Substituting the line's equations into the plane's equation then produces an equation with no \(s\) term left in it: if the resulting constant equation is false, the line never meets the plane; if it happens to be true, every point of the line satisfies the plane's equation, and the line lies entirely within the plane.
Worked exampleDetermine the relationship between the line \(\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}+s\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\) and the plane \(2x+y-z=9\).
The direction vector is \(\mathbf{d}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}\) and the normal vector is \(\mathbf{N}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\).
\[\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{N} = 2(2)+(-1)(1)+3(-1) = 4-1-3=0\]so the line is parallel to the plane. Substituting the line's equations into the plane's equation:
\[2(1+2s)+(2-s)-(-1+3s)=9\]\[(2+2+1)+(4s-s-3s)=9\]\[5+0s=9\]\[\boxed{5=9 \text{ is false, so the line does not meet the plane at all}}\]

Finding the line of intersection of two planes

Two non-parallel planes with normal vectors \(\mathbf{N_1}\) and \(\mathbf{N_2}\) intersect in a straight line. Since this line lies in both planes, its direction vector \(\mathbf{d}\) is perpendicular to both normals, so:
\[\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{N_1}\times\mathbf{N_2}\]A specific point on the line is found by setting one coordinate (commonly \(z=0\)) and solving the two resulting equations in the remaining two coordinates simultaneously.
Worked exampleFind the line of intersection of the planes \(P: x+y-z=2\) and \(Q: 2x-y+z=1\).
The normal vectors are \(\mathbf{N_1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{N_2}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix}\), so:
\[\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{N_1}\times\mathbf{N_2} = \begin{pmatrix}(1)(1)-(-1)(-1)\\(-1)(2)-(1)(1)\\(1)(-1)-(1)(2)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\-3\\-3\end{pmatrix}\]which simplifies to the direction \(\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}\). Setting \(z=0\) in both plane equations:
\[x+y=2, \qquad 2x-y=1\]Adding these equations gives \(3x=3\), so \(x=1\) and \(y=1\).
\[\boxed{\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}}\]

Finding the intersection of three planes

Three planes, represented by a 3x3 system of linear equations, can intersect in exactly one point (a unique solution, when the system is consistent with a unique solution), along a common line (infinitely many solutions lying on one line, when the three planes share that line without being identical), or not at all (an inconsistent system, for example when two of the planes are parallel, or the three planes form a triangular prism). This matches the classification of 3x3 linear systems met earlier through Gaussian elimination.
Worked exampleFind the point common to the planes \(x+y+z=6\), \(x-y+2z=5\), and \(2x+y-z=1\).
Adding the first two equations eliminates \(y\):
\[(x+y+z)+(x-y+2z)=6+5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2x+3z=11\]Subtracting the third equation from the first also eliminates \(y\):
\[(x+y+z)-(2x+y-z)=6-1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad -x+2z=5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=2z-5\]Substituting this into \(2x+3z=11\):
\[2(2z-5)+3z=11\]\[7z-10=11\]\[7z=21\]\[\boxed{z=3}\]Then \(x=2(3)-5=1\), and from the first equation, \(1+y+3=6\), so \(y=2\). Since this system has exactly one solution, the three planes meet at a single point:
\[\boxed{\text{the planes meet at } (1,2,3)}\]
