Scatter diagrams and correlation

A scatter diagram plots paired data \((x_i, y_i)\) as points, with the explanatory variable \(x\) on the horizontal axis and the response variable \(y\) on the vertical axis. The pattern of points reveals the association between the two variables:
• Positive correlation — as \(x\) increases, \(y\) tends to increase (points trend upward).
• Negative correlation — as \(x\) increases, \(y\) tends to decrease (points trend downward).
• No correlation — the points show no clear upward or downward trend.
Correlation only describes the strength of a linear trend; a strong curved (non-linear) pattern can still give a weak linear correlation.
Worked exampleA scatter diagram of "hours of sunshine" against "ice-cream sales" shows points rising steadily from bottom-left to top-right, clustered tightly around an upward-sloping line. Describe the correlation.
The points trend upward as \(x\) increases, and they lie close to a straight line, so the association is linear.
\[\boxed{\text{Strong, positive, linear correlation}}\]

Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient

For paired data \((x_i, y_i)\), the correlation coefficient \(r\) measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between \(x\) and \(y\):
\[r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx} \, S_{yy}}}\]where
\[S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}), \qquad S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2, \qquad S_{yy} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\]and \(\bar{x}\), \(\bar{y}\) are the means of the \(x\)- and \(y\)-data. In examinations, \(r\) is read directly from your GDC.
Worked exampleA student records hours studied \(x\) and test score \(y\) for five tests:
\(x\): 2, 4, 6, 8, 10   \(y\): 50, 60, 65, 70, 80.
Find the correlation coefficient \(r\).
\[\bar{x} = \frac{2+4+6+8+10}{5} = 6, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{50+60+65+70+80}{5} = 65\]Deviations from the mean, \((x_i-\bar{x})\): \(-4,-2,0,2,4\); \((y_i-\bar{y})\): \(-15,-5,0,5,15\).
\[S_{xy} = (-4)(-15)+(-2)(-5)+0+2(5)+4(15) = 60+10+0+10+60 = 140\]\[S_{xx} = 16+4+0+4+16 = 40, \qquad S_{yy} = 225+25+0+25+225 = 500\]\[r = \frac{140}{\sqrt{40 \times 500}} = \frac{140}{\sqrt{20000}} = \frac{140}{141.42\ldots}\]\[\boxed{r \approx 0.990}\]

Interpreting the correlation coefficient

The correlation coefficient always satisfies \(-1 \le r \le 1\):
• \(r\) close to \(+1\): strong positive linear correlation.
• \(r\) close to \(-1\): strong negative linear correlation.
• \(r\) close to \(0\): little or no linear correlation.
As a rough guide, \(|r| > 0.8\) is often described as strong, \(0.5 < |r| \le 0.8\) as moderate, and \(|r| \le 0.5\) as weak. \(r\) has no units and is unaffected by the scale of the data. A high \(|r|\) can be distorted by outliers, and correlation does not imply causation — two variables can be strongly correlated without one causing the other.
Worked exampleTwo data sets give correlation coefficients \(r_1 = 0.99\) and \(r_2 = -0.42\). Compare the strength and direction of the two linear relationships.
\(|r_1| = 0.99\) is very close to 1, indicating a strong positive linear relationship. \(|r_2| = 0.42\) is well below 0.5, indicating a weak linear relationship, and the negative sign shows the variables tend to move in opposite directions.
\[\boxed{\text{Data set 1: strong positive correlation; Data set 2: weak negative correlation}}\]

The least-squares regression line

When a scatter diagram suggests a linear trend, the line of best fit is the least-squares regression line, which minimises the sum of the squares of the vertical distances from the points to the line. It is written as
\[y = a + bx\]with slope and intercept given by
\[b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}\]The regression line always passes through the point \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\). In examinations, \(a\) and \(b\) are usually read from your GDC.
Worked exampleUsing the study-hours data above (\(\bar{x}=6\), \(\bar{y}=65\), \(S_{xy}=140\), \(S_{xx}=40\)), find the equation of the regression line of \(y\) on \(x\).
\[b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{140}{40} = 3.5\]\[a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x} = 65 - 3.5 \times 6 = 65 - 21 = 44\]\[\boxed{y = 44 + 3.5x}\]

Using the regression line for prediction

Once the regression equation \(y = a+bx\) is found, it can be used to predict a value of \(y\) for a given value of \(x\) by substitution. Such predictions are only reliable when \(x\) lies within, or close to, the range of the \(x\)-values used to build the model.
Worked exampleUsing the regression line \(y = 44 + 3.5x\) from above, predict the test score for a student who studies for 7 hours.
\[y = 44 + 3.5(7) = 44 + 24.5\]\[\boxed{y = 68.5, \text{ a predicted score of about } 68.5}\]

Interpolation versus extrapolation

Predicting a value of \(y\) for an \(x\) that lies within the range of the original data is called interpolation, and is generally reliable. Predicting for an \(x\) outside that range is called extrapolation; since there is no evidence the linear trend continues beyond the observed data, extrapolated predictions can be inaccurate or meaningless.
Worked exampleThe study-hours data above ranged from \(x=2\) to \(x=10\) hours. Using \(y = 44+3.5x\), predict the score for a student who studies 20 hours, and comment on the reliability of this prediction.
\[y = 44 + 3.5(20) = 44 + 70 = 114\]A predicted score of 114 is impossible, since test scores cannot exceed 100. This is because \(x=20\) lies far outside the data range \([2,10]\), so the prediction is an unreliable extrapolation.
\[\boxed{y = 114 \text{ (unreliable: this is extrapolation, far outside the data range)}}\]
