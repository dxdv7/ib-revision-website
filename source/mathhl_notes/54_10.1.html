Populations, samples, and censuses

A population is the entire collection of individuals, items, or measurements that a study is concerned with. A sample is a subset of the population that is actually examined, chosen so that it represents the population well, and is used to draw conclusions about the whole population without examining every member of it. A census collects data from every member of the population, while a sample survey examines only part of it; a sample is generally preferred over a census because it is cheaper, faster, and often the only practical option when the population is very large.
Worked exampleA city has \(85000\) registered voters. A polling company wants to estimate support for a new transport policy, and surveys \(500\) randomly chosen voters rather than asking every registered voter. Identify the population and the sample, and state what a census would involve here.
The population is all \(85000\) registered voters in the city.
The sample is the \(500\) voters who were surveyed.
A census would involve asking every one of the \(85000\) registered voters, rather than just a sample of \(500\).

Discrete and continuous data

Numerical data is discrete if it arises from counting, so it can only take specific, separate values, usually whole numbers, such as a number of items or people. Numerical data is continuous if it arises from measuring, so it can take any value within a range, such as a length, mass, or time. This distinction matters because the way data is later organised into a frequency distribution depends on whether the underlying variable is discrete or continuous.
Worked exampleClassify each variable as discrete or continuous.
(a) The number of emails received by an office in a day.
This is discrete, since it arises from counting a whole number of emails.
(b) The mass, in kg, of parcels processed by a courier.
This is continuous, since mass can take any value within a range, and arises from measuring.

Reliability and validity

A measurement process is reliable if repeating it under the same conditions produces consistent (the same or very similar) results. A measurement process is valid if it actually measures what it is intended to measure. Reliability and validity are independent properties: a process can be reliable without being valid, valid without being perfectly reliable, both, or neither.
Worked exampleClassify each situation as reliable, valid, both, or neither.
(a) A kitchen scale is miscalibrated so that it always reads \(20\text{ g}\) heavier than the true mass, but gives the same reading every time the same object is weighed.
This scale is reliable (consistent) but not valid (it does not give the true mass).
(b) A short mood questionnaire is used to estimate a student's long-term mathematical ability.
This questionnaire may be reliable if it gives similar results when repeated, but it is not valid, since mood is not the same thing as mathematical ability.

Simple random sampling and systematic sampling

In simple random sampling, every member of the population is numbered and a sample of size \(n\) is chosen using a random number generator, so that every member of a population of size \(N\) has the same probability \(\frac{n}{N}\) of being selected. Systematic sampling is a variant of this: a starting point is chosen at random, and then every \(k\)th member after that is selected, where the sampling interval is:
\[k = \frac{N}{n}\]rounded to the nearest whole number if it is not already an integer.
Worked exampleA charity has \(760\) volunteers.
(a) Find the probability that a given volunteer is chosen in a simple random sample of \(40\) volunteers.
\[P(\text{selected}) = \frac{n}{N} = \frac{40}{760}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{selected}) = \frac{1}{19}}\]
(b) Find the sampling interval for a systematic sample of \(40\) volunteers from the same population of \(760\), and describe how the sample is chosen if the random starting point is \(12\).
\[k = \frac{N}{n} = \frac{760}{40}\]\[\boxed{k=19}\]Starting at volunteer \(12\), every \(19\)th volunteer after that is selected: volunteers \(12, 31, 50, 69, \ldots\) Notice that this matches the probability \(\frac{1}{19}\) found in part (a), since both methods give each volunteer the same overall chance \(\frac{n}{N}\) of being included.

Stratified sampling

In stratified sampling, the population is divided into non-overlapping subgroups called strata, based on a shared characteristic, and a simple random sample is taken from each stratum. The sample size from each stratum is usually proportional to its size within the population:
\[n_i = \frac{N_i}{N}\times n\]where \(N_i\) is the size of stratum \(i\), \(N\) is the population size, and \(n\) is the total sample size.
Worked exampleA company has \(180\) managers and \(420\) other staff, giving a total of \(N=600\) employees. A stratified sample of \(60\) employees, proportional to each stratum, is required. Find the number of managers and the number of other staff to be sampled.
\[n_{\text{managers}} = \frac{180}{600}\times 60\]\[\boxed{n_{\text{managers}}=18}\]\[n_{\text{staff}} = \frac{420}{600}\times 60\]\[\boxed{n_{\text{staff}}=42}\]As a check, \(18+42=60\), which is the required total sample size.

Convenience sampling, quota sampling, and bias

Convenience sampling selects whichever members of the population are easiest to reach, such as people passing a particular location at a particular time. Quota sampling sets a fixed number (a quota) to be selected from each subgroup, filled using a non-random method, so that the sample matches the population's known proportions for those subgroups. Neither technique gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection, so both can introduce bias: a systematic tendency for the sample to misrepresent the population, which reduces how effectively the sample's results can be generalised.
Worked exampleA researcher stands outside one entrance of a train station between \(7\) a.m. and \(9\) a.m. and interviews the first \(80\) commuters who pass by, in order to estimate the opinions of all adults in the city on a new transport policy.
This is convenience sampling, since the commuters are selected simply because they are readily available at that place and time. The sample is likely to be biased, since it excludes people who do not use that entrance or do not travel during those hours, and overrepresents commuters relative to the wider adult population, so it may not give an effective estimate of the opinions of all adults in the city.
