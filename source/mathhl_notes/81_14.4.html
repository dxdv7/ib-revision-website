Reversing differentiation: from acceleration to velocity

For a particle moving along a line, differentiation gives \(v(t)=s'(t)\) and \(a(t)=v'(t)\). Integration reverses each step:
\[v(t) = \int a(t)\,dt \qquad\text{and}\qquad s(t) = \int v(t)\,dt\]Each indefinite integral introduces a constant of integration, which must be found from a given initial condition (such as a known velocity or position at a particular time).
Worked exampleA particle moving along the \(x\)-axis has acceleration \(a(t)=6t\). Given that \(v(0)=2\), find \(v(t)\).
\[v(t) = \int 6t\,dt = 3t^2+C\]Using \(v(0)=2\): \(3(0)^2+C=2 \Rightarrow C=2\).
\[\boxed{v(t) = 3t^2+2}\]

Using an initial condition to find the position function

Once \(v(t)\) is known, the position function is found by integrating again: \(s(t)=\int v(t)\,dt\). The resulting constant of integration is found from a given position at a particular time, such as \(s(0)=0\) or another stated initial position.
Worked exampleContinuing the previous example with \(v(t)=3t^2+2\) and given that \(s(0)=4\), find \(s(t)\).
\[s(t) = \int (3t^2+2)\,dt = t^3+2t+C\]Using \(s(0)=4\): \((0)^3+2(0)+C=4 \Rightarrow C=4\).
\[\boxed{s(t) = t^3+2t+4}\]

Displacement as a definite integral of velocity

The displacement of a particle between \(t=t_1\) and \(t=t_2\) is given by the definite integral of velocity over that interval, which by the fundamental theorem of calculus equals \(s(t_2)-s(t_1)\):
\[\text{displacement} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt = s(t_2)-s(t_1)\]Because this is a signed quantity, intervals during which the particle moves in the negative direction contribute negatively.
Worked exampleA particle has velocity \(v(t)=-2t+6\) and \(s(0)=0\). Find its displacement over the interval \(0\leq t\leq 4\).
\[\text{displacement} = \int_0^4 (-2t+6)\,dt = \left[-t^2+6t\right]_0^4 = (-16+24)-0\]\[\boxed{\text{displacement} = 8 \text{ units}}\]

Total distance travelled as the integral of speed

Speed is \(|v(t)|\), the magnitude of velocity, and it is always non-negative. The total distance travelled over \([t_1,t_2]\) is:
\[\text{total distance} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\,dt\]If \(v(t)\) changes sign at some point(s) \(c\) inside \([t_1,t_2]\) (the particle reverses direction), the integral must be split at each such \(c\) and each piece taken as a positive contribution, since displacement and distance disagree whenever the particle doubles back on itself.
Worked exampleUsing the same velocity \(v(t)=-2t+6\), find the total distance travelled over \(0\leq t\leq 4\).
\(v(t)=0\) when \(-2t+6=0 \Rightarrow t=3\), which lies inside \([0,4]\), so the particle changes direction at \(t=3\).
\[\int_0^3 (-2t+6)\,dt = \left[-t^2+6t\right]_0^3 = 9, \qquad \int_3^4 (-2t+6)\,dt = \left[-t^2+6t\right]_3^4 = 8-9=-1\]\[\text{total distance} = |9|+|-1| = 9+1\]\[\boxed{\text{total distance} = 10 \text{ units}}\]Note this differs from the displacement found above (8 units), since the particle travelled forward then partly back.

Interpreting displacement and distance as areas on a velocity-time graph

On a velocity-time graph, displacement over \([t_1,t_2]\) is the signed (net) area between the velocity curve and the \(t\)-axis: area above the axis counts positively, area below counts negatively. Total distance is the sum of the absolute (unsigned) areas of each piece, which is always at least as large as the magnitude of the displacement.
Worked exampleFor \(v(t)=-2t+6\) on \([0,4]\) from the previous examples, describe the velocity-time graph and relate its areas to the displacement and distance found.
The line \(v(t)=-2t+6\) crosses the \(t\)-axis at \(t=3\): it is positive (above the axis) on \([0,3]\), giving a triangular area of \(9\), and negative (below the axis) on \([3,4]\), giving an area of \(1\) counted as \(-1\) for signed purposes.
Signed total: \(9+(-1)=8\), matching the displacement. Unsigned total: \(9+1=10\), matching the total distance.
\[\boxed{\text{displacement} = 9-1 = 8, \qquad \text{total distance} = 9+1 = 10}\]

A full problem: from acceleration to total distance

A complete kinematics problem typically proceeds in stages: integrate \(a(t)\) to find \(v(t)\) using an initial velocity, locate any times at which \(v(t)=0\) inside the interval of interest, then integrate \(|v(t)|\) in pieces between those times to find the total distance (or integrate \(v(t)\) directly, without splitting, to find displacement).
Worked exampleA particle has acceleration \(a(t)=2t-6\) and initial velocity \(v(0)=5\). Find the total distance it travels during \(0\leq t\leq 4\).
First find \(v(t)\): \(v(t) = \int (2t-6)\,dt = t^2-6t+C\). Using \(v(0)=5\): \(C=5\), so \(v(t)=t^2-6t+5=(t-1)(t-5)\).
On \([0,4]\), \(v(t)=0\) only at \(t=1\) (the other root, \(t=5\), lies outside the interval), so the particle changes direction once, at \(t=1\).
\[\int_0^1 (t^2-6t+5)\,dt = \left[\frac{t^3}{3}-3t^2+5t\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{3}-3+5 = \frac{7}{3}\]\[\int_1^4 (t^2-6t+5)\,dt = \left[\frac{t^3}{3}-3t^2+5t\right]_1^4 = \left(\frac{64}{3}-48+20\right)-\frac{7}{3} = -\frac{20}{3}-\frac{7}{3} = -9\]\[\text{total distance} = \left|\frac{7}{3}\right| + |-9| = \frac{7}{3}+9 = \frac{7+27}{3}\]\[\boxed{\text{total distance} = \frac{34}{3} \text{ units}}\]
