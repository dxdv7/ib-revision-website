Statements and negation

A statement (or proposition) is a sentence that is either true or false, but not both. If \(P\) is a statement, the negation of \(P\), denoted \(\neg P\) (read "not \(P\)"), is the statement that \(P\) is false. The truth value of \(\neg P\) is always the opposite of the truth value of \(P\):
\[\begin{array}{c|c} P & \neg P \\ \hline T & F \\ F & T \end{array}\]
Worked exampleWrite the negation of each statement in simple English, and give its truth value.
(a) \(P\): 12 is a prime number.
(b) \(Q\): \(n\) is an even integer.
For (a), 12 is not prime (since \(12 = 4 \times 3\)), so \(P\) is false and \(\neg P\) is true.
For (b), negating "is even" gives "is not even", that is, "\(n\) is odd".
\[\boxed{\neg P: \text{12 is not a prime number, which is true}}\]\[\boxed{\neg Q: n \text{ is not an even integer}}\]

Conjunction and disjunction

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be statements. The conjunction of \(P\) and \(Q\), written \(P \land Q\) ("\(P\) and \(Q\)"), is true only when both \(P\) and \(Q\) are true. The disjunction of \(P\) and \(Q\), written \(P \lor Q\) ("\(P\) or \(Q\)"), is true when at least one of \(P\) or \(Q\) is true:
\[\begin{array}{c c|c c} P & Q & P \land Q & P \lor Q \\ \hline T & T & T & T \\ T & F & F & T \\ F & T & F & T \\ F & F & F & F \end{array}\]
Worked exampleLet \(C\): "41 is composite" and \(M\): "45 is a multiple of 5". Find the truth value of \(C \land M\) and of \(C \lor M\).
41 is prime, so \(C\) is false. 45 is a multiple of 5, so \(M\) is true.
Since \(C\) is false, \(C \land M\) is false no matter what \(M\) is. Since \(M\) is true, \(C \lor M\) is true no matter what \(C\) is.
\[\boxed{C \land M \text{ is false}}\]\[\boxed{C \lor M \text{ is true}}\]

Conditional statements (implication)

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be statements. The conditional statement \(P \implies Q\) ("if \(P\) then \(Q\)", or "\(P\) implies \(Q\)") asserts that \(Q\) is true whenever \(P\) is true. Here \(P\) is the hypothesis (or antecedent) and \(Q\) is the conclusion (or consequent). The statement \(P \implies Q\) is false only when \(P\) is true and \(Q\) is false; it is true in every other case:
\[\begin{array}{c c|c} P & Q & P \implies Q \\ \hline T & T & T \\ T & F & F \\ F & T & T \\ F & F & T \end{array}\]
Worked exampleDetermine the truth value of each conditional statement.
(a) If \(\cos \pi = 0\), then 12 is a prime number.
(b) If \(2 > 5\), then \(3 + 4 = 7\).
For (a), the hypothesis "\(\cos \pi = 0\)" is false, since \(\cos \pi = -1\). A conditional with a false hypothesis is automatically true, regardless of the conclusion.
For (b), the hypothesis "\(2 > 5\)" is false, so this conditional is also true, even though the conclusion \(3+4=7\) happens to be a true statement on its own.
\[\boxed{\text{(a) true, since the hypothesis is false}}\]\[\boxed{\text{(b) true, since the hypothesis is false}}\]

Converse, inverse, and contrapositive

Let \(P \implies Q\) be a conditional statement. Its converse is \(Q \implies P\). Its inverse is \(\neg P \implies \neg Q\). Its contrapositive is \(\neg Q \implies \neg P\). A conditional statement and its contrapositive always share the same truth value, so \(P \implies Q\) is logically equivalent to \(\neg Q \implies \neg P\). The converse and the inverse are equivalent to each other, but neither is in general equivalent to the original statement.
Worked exampleConsider the statement: "If a number is divisible by 6, then it is divisible by 3." Write the converse, inverse, and contrapositive, and state whether each is true or false.
Converse: If a number is divisible by 3, then it is divisible by 6. False: 9 is divisible by 3 but not by 6.
Inverse: If a number is not divisible by 6, then it is not divisible by 3. False, by the same example: 9 is not divisible by 6 yet is divisible by 3.
Contrapositive: If a number is not divisible by 3, then it is not divisible by 6. True, since every multiple of 6 is a multiple of 3.
\[\boxed{\text{original true; converse false; inverse false; contrapositive true}}\]

Biconditional statements

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be statements. The biconditional \(P \iff Q\) ("\(P\) if and only if \(Q\)", or "\(P\) iff \(Q\)") is true exactly when \(P\) and \(Q\) have the same truth value, that is, when \(P \implies Q\) and \(Q \implies P\) are both true:
\[\begin{array}{c c|c} P & Q & P \iff Q \\ \hline T & T & T \\ T & F & F \\ F & T & F \\ F & F & T \end{array}\]
Worked exampleDetermine the truth value of the biconditional "\(n\) is even \(\iff n^2\) is even" for \(n = 6\) and for \(n = 7\).
For \(n=6\): "\(n\) is even" is true, and "\(n^2 = 36\) is even" is true, so the two sides agree and the biconditional holds.
For \(n=7\): "\(n\) is even" is false, and "\(n^2 = 49\) is even" is also false, so the two sides again agree and the biconditional holds.
\[\boxed{\text{the biconditional is true for both } n=6 \text{ and } n=7}\]

De Morgan's laws and negating implications

De Morgan's laws describe how negation distributes over conjunction and disjunction:
\[\neg(P \land Q) \equiv \neg P \lor \neg Q\]\[\neg(P \lor Q) \equiv \neg P \land \neg Q\]The negation of a conditional statement is not itself a conditional statement; it is a conjunction:
\[\neg(P \implies Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q\]
Worked exampleWrite the negation of the statement: "If \(x\) is odd, then \(x^2\) is odd."
Here \(P\): \(x\) is odd, and \(Q\): \(x^2\) is odd. Using \(\neg(P \implies Q) \equiv P \land \neg Q\):
\[\boxed{\neg(P \implies Q): x \text{ is odd and } x^2 \text{ is even}}\]
