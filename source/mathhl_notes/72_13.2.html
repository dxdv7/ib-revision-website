The product rule

If \(u\) and \(v\) are both functions of \(x\), and \(y=uv\), then:
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=u\frac{dv}{dx}+v\frac{du}{dx}\]In function notation, for \(y=f(x)g(x)\):
\[\frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)g(x)\big]=f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)\]In words: the derivative of a product is the first factor times the derivative of the second, plus the second factor times the derivative of the first. This is not the product of the derivatives.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y=x^2\sin x\).
Let \(u=x^2\) and \(v=\sin x\), so \(u'=2x\) and \(v'=\cos x\).
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=u'v+uv'=2x\sin x+x^2\cos x\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=2x\sin x+x^2\cos x}\]

Checking the product rule against expansion

When a product can also be expanded into a polynomial, the product rule result must agree with differentiating the expanded form term by term. This is a useful way to confirm the product rule has been applied correctly.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y=(x^2+3x)(2x-1)\) both by expanding and by the product rule.
Expanding: \(y=2x^3-x^2+6x^2-3x=2x^3+5x^2-3x\), so \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=6x^2+10x-3\).
By the product rule: let \(u=x^2+3x\), \(v=2x-1\), so \(u'=2x+3\), \(v'=2\).
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=u'v+uv'=(2x+3)(2x-1)+(x^2+3x)(2)\]\[=(4x^2+4x-3)+(2x^2+6x)=6x^2+10x-3\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=6x^2+10x-3 \text{ by both methods}}\]

The quotient rule

If \(u\) and \(v\) are both functions of \(x\), and \(y=\dfrac{u}{v}\) with \(v\neq 0\), then:
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}\]In function notation, for \(y=\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\):
\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right]=\frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}\]In words: the derivative of a quotient is (derivative of the top times the bottom, minus the top times the derivative of the bottom), all over the bottom squared. The order of subtraction in the numerator matters — it is not symmetric like the product rule.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y=\dfrac{x}{\cos x}\).
Let \(u=x\) and \(v=\cos x\), so \(u'=1\) and \(v'=-\sin x\).
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}=\frac{(1)(\cos x)-(x)(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x}\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{\cos x+x\sin x}{\cos^2 x}}\]

Combining the quotient rule with the chain rule

When the numerator or denominator of a quotient is itself a composite function, the chain rule must be used to differentiate that part before applying the quotient rule formula. The quotient rule structure stays the same; only the computation of \(u'\) or \(v'\) changes.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y=\dfrac{\sin(2x)}{x^2}\) for \(x\neq 0\).
Let \(u=\sin(2x)\) and \(v=x^2\). By the chain rule, \(u'=2\cos(2x)\), and \(v'=2x\).
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}=\frac{2\cos(2x)\cdot x^2-\sin(2x)\cdot 2x}{x^4}\]\[=\frac{2x\big[x\cos(2x)-\sin(2x)\big]}{x^4}\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2\big[x\cos(2x)-\sin(2x)\big]}{x^3}}\]

Choosing between the product, quotient, and chain rules

Before differentiating, identify the overall structure of the function: a single function with something substituted inside it (e.g. \(\sin(x^2)\)) needs only the chain rule; two factors multiplied together (e.g. \(x^2\sin x\)) need the product rule; one expression divided by another (e.g. \(\dfrac{x}{\cos x}\)) needs the quotient rule. Many problems require more than one rule nested together, for example a product in which one factor is itself a composite function.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y=\cos(2x)\sin x\), identifying which rules are needed.
This is a product of \(\cos(2x)\) and \(\sin x\), so the product rule applies overall; but \(\cos(2x)\) is itself a composite, requiring the chain rule for its derivative.
Let \(u=\cos(2x)\), \(v=\sin x\). By the chain rule, \(u'=-2\sin(2x)\), and \(v'=\cos x\).
\[\frac{dy}{dx}=u'v+uv'=-2\sin(2x)\sin x+\cos(2x)\cos x\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=\cos(2x)\cos x-2\sin(2x)\sin x}\]
