The angle between two lines

Let two lines have direction vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\). Since a direction vector can point either way along its line, the angle between the lines is defined to be the acute angle \(\theta\) (so \(0^\circ \le \theta \le 90^\circ\)) satisfying:
\[\cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2|}{|\mathbf{v}_1||\mathbf{v}_2|}\]The absolute value in the numerator guarantees that \(\cos\theta \ge 0\), so \(\theta\) always comes out acute (or a right angle).
Worked exampleLine \(L_1\) has direction vector \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (2,-1,2)\) and line \(L_2\) has direction vector \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (1,2,2)\). Find the angle between \(L_1\) and \(L_2\).
\[\mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2 = 2(1) + (-1)(2) + 2(2) = 2 - 2 + 4 = 4\]\[|\mathbf{v}_1| = \sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2+2^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3, \qquad |\mathbf{v}_2| = \sqrt{1^2+2^2+2^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3\]\[\cos\theta = \frac{|4|}{3 \times 3} = \frac{4}{9}\]\[\boxed{\theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{9}\right) \approx 63.6^\circ}\]

Parallel lines

Two lines with direction vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\) are parallel if and only if one direction vector is a scalar multiple of the other:
\[\mathbf{v}_2 = k\mathbf{v}_1 \text{ for some nonzero scalar } k\]Being parallel does not mean the lines are the same line - a further test (below) is needed to decide that.
Worked exampleShow that the lines with direction vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (2,-4,6)\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1,2,-3)\) are parallel.
\[\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1,2,-3) = -\frac{1}{2}(2,-4,6) = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}_1\]\[\boxed{\text{Since } \mathbf{v}_2 \text{ is a scalar multiple of } \mathbf{v}_1, \text{ the lines are parallel}}\]

Coincident lines

If the direction vectors of two lines are parallel, the lines are either coincident (they are actually the same line) or parallel and distinct. To decide which, take a known point on one line and substitute it into the equation of the other line:
\[\text{if a consistent value of the parameter exists, the lines are coincident; otherwise they are parallel and distinct}\]
Worked exampleLine \(L_1: \mathbf{r} = (1,0,-1) + t(2,-4,6)\) and line \(L_2: \mathbf{r} = (3,-4,5) + s(-4,8,-12)\). Show that \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) are coincident.
\[\mathbf{v}_2 = (-4,8,-12) = -2(2,-4,6) = -2\mathbf{v}_1, \text{ so the direction vectors are parallel}\]Substitute the point \((1,0,-1)\) from \(L_1\) into the equations of \(L_2\):
\[3 - 4s = 1 \Rightarrow s = \frac{1}{2}\]\[y: -4 + 8\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 0 \quad\checkmark \qquad z: 5 - 12\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -1 \quad\checkmark\]\[\boxed{\text{Since } s = \frac{1}{2} \text{ satisfies all three equations, } L_1 \text{ and } L_2 \text{ are coincident}}\]

Testing non-parallel lines for intersection

If the direction vectors of two lines are not parallel, equate the two vector equations component-wise. This produces three equations in the two unknown parameters \(t\) and \(s\). Solve two of the equations for \(t\) and \(s\), then substitute into the third:
\[\text{if the third equation is also satisfied, the lines intersect at the corresponding point}\]
Worked exampleShow that \(L_1: \mathbf{r} = (1,0,3) + t(1,1,1)\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r} = (1,4,1) + s(1,-1,2)\) intersect, and find the point of intersection.
The direction vectors \((1,1,1)\) and \((1,-1,2)\) are not scalar multiples, so the lines are not parallel. Equate components:
\[1+t = 1+s \Rightarrow t = s\]\[t = 4-s \Rightarrow t+s = 4\]\[3+t = 1+2s \Rightarrow t-2s = -2\]From the first two equations, \(t=s=2\). Checking the third equation:
\[t - 2s = 2 - 2(2) = -2 \quad\checkmark\]\[\boxed{\text{The lines intersect at } (1+2,\ 0+2,\ 3+2) = (3,2,5)}\]

Skew lines

Two lines are skew if their direction vectors are not parallel, and the system of equations obtained by equating the two vector equations has no solution. Skew lines never meet, yet they are not parallel either.
Worked exampleShow that \(L_1: \mathbf{r} = (0,1,2) + t(1,1,1)\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r} = (2,3,0) + s(1,-1,2)\) are skew.
The direction vectors \((1,1,1)\) and \((1,-1,2)\) are not scalar multiples, so the lines are not parallel. Equate components:
\[t = 2+s \Rightarrow t-s = 2\]\[1+t = 3-s \Rightarrow t+s = 2\]Solving these two equations gives \(t=2, s=0\). Checking the third (z) equation:
\[2+t = 2s \Rightarrow 2+2 = 2(0) \Rightarrow 4 = 0\]\[\boxed{\text{This is false, so the system is inconsistent and the lines are skew}}\]

Classifying a pair of lines

To classify two lines in space, follow this procedure:
\[\text{Step 1: compare direction vectors - scalar multiples} \Rightarrow \text{parallel; otherwise go to Step 3}\]\[\text{Step 2 (if parallel): substitute a known point of one line into the other - works} \Rightarrow \text{coincident; fails} \Rightarrow \text{parallel and distinct}\]\[\text{Step 3 (if not parallel): solve the system from equating components - consistent} \Rightarrow \text{intersecting; inconsistent} \Rightarrow \text{skew}\]
Worked exampleClassify the lines \(L_1: \mathbf{r} = (1,-1,0) + t(2,1,-1)\) and \(L_2: \mathbf{r} = (3,1,-2) + s(-4,-2,2)\).
\[\mathbf{v}_2 = (-4,-2,2) = -2(2,1,-1) = -2\mathbf{v}_1, \text{ so the direction vectors are parallel}\]Substitute the point \((1,-1,0)\) from \(L_1\) into the equations of \(L_2\):
\[3-4s = 1 \Rightarrow s = \frac{1}{2}\]\[y: 1-2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 0, \text{ but the required value is } -1\]\[\boxed{\text{Since } (1,-1,0) \text{ does not lie on } L_2, \text{ the lines are parallel but not coincident}}\]
