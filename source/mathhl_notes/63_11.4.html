Venn diagrams for two events

A Venn diagram for events \(A\) and \(B\) splits the sample space into four regions: \(A\) only, \(B\) only, \(A \cap B\), and neither. Given \(P(A)\), \(P(B)\) and \(P(A \cap B)\), the remaining regions follow:
\[P(A \text{ only}) = P(A) - P(A \cap B), \qquad P(B \text{ only}) = P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]\[P(\text{neither}) = 1 - P(A \cup B) = 1 - \big[P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\big]\]
Worked exampleGiven \(P(A) = 0.5\), \(P(B) = 0.4\) and \(P(A \cap B) = 0.2\), find all four Venn diagram regions and hence \(P(A \mid B)\).
\[P(A \text{ only}) = 0.5 - 0.2 = 0.3, \qquad P(B \text{ only}) = 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.2\]\[P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.7, \qquad P(\text{neither}) = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3\]\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{0.2}{0.4}\]\[\boxed{A \text{ only} = 0.3,\ B \text{ only} = 0.2,\ \text{neither} = 0.3,\ P(A\mid B) = 0.5}\]

Venn diagrams for three events

A Venn diagram for three events \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) has eight regions. The union is found by the inclusion–exclusion formula:
\[P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A)+P(B)+P(C)-P(A \cap B)-P(A \cap C)-P(B \cap C)+P(A \cap B \cap C)\]The region belonging to exactly two of the three sets is found by removing the triple-overlap from each pairwise overlap.
Worked exampleOf 60 students: 25 study French (F), 20 study Spanish (S), 18 study German (G); \(n(F \cap S) = 8\), \(n(F \cap G) = 7\), \(n(S \cap G) = 6\), and \(n(F \cap S \cap G) = 3\). Find the probability a randomly chosen student studies exactly two of the three languages.
Students in exactly two languages: \((8-3) + (7-3) + (6-3) = 5 + 4 + 3 = 12\).
\[P(\text{exactly two}) = \frac{12}{60}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{exactly two languages}) = \frac{1}{5}}\]

Tree diagrams with replacement (independent trials)

A tree diagram represents successive stages of an experiment as branches. The probability of any complete path is the product of the probabilities along its branches; the probability of an outcome that can be reached by more than one path is the sum of those path probabilities. When trials are independent (e.g. drawing with replacement), the branch probabilities are the same at every stage.
Worked exampleA bag contains 4 red and 6 blue marbles (10 in total). A marble is drawn, replaced, and a second marble is drawn. Find (a) \(P(\text{red then blue})\) and (b) \(P(\text{exactly one red})\).
At each draw, \(P(R) = 0.4\) and \(P(B) = 0.6\), unchanged by replacement.
(a) \[P(R \text{ then } B) = 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.24\](b) Exactly one red occurs via \(RB\) or \(BR\):
\[P(\text{exactly one red}) = 0.24 + (0.6\times0.4) = 0.24+0.24\]\[\boxed{P(\text{red then blue}) = 0.24, \quad P(\text{exactly one red}) = 0.48}\]

Tree diagrams without replacement (dependent trials)

When trials are dependent (e.g. drawing without replacement), the branch probabilities at the second stage are conditional on what happened at the first stage. The same multiply-along-branches, add-across-branches rules still apply, but each branch after the first uses a conditional probability.
Worked exampleThe same bag (4 red, 6 blue, 10 total) has two marbles drawn without replacement. Find \(P(\text{exactly one red})\).
Path \(R_1B_2\): \[P(R_1)\,P(B_2\mid R_1) = \frac{4}{10}\times\frac{6}{9} = \frac{4}{15}\]Path \(B_1R_2\): \[P(B_1)\,P(R_2\mid B_1) = \frac{6}{10}\times\frac{4}{9} = \frac{4}{15}\]Adding the two paths that give exactly one red:
\[P(\text{exactly one red}) = \frac{4}{15}+\frac{4}{15}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{exactly one red}) = \frac{8}{15}}\]

Finding a conditional probability from a tree diagram

A conditional probability can be read directly from a tree diagram by restricting attention to only the paths consistent with the given condition, then taking the proportion of those paths' probabilities that also satisfy the event of interest:
\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{\text{sum of path probabilities ending in } A \cap B}{\text{sum of path probabilities ending in } B}\]
Worked exampleUsing the without-replacement tree above (4 red, 6 blue, 10 total), given that the second marble drawn is blue, find the probability the first marble drawn was red.
From before, \(P(R_1 \cap B_2) = \frac{4}{15}\). Also \(P(B_1 \cap B_2) = \frac{6}{10}\times\frac{5}{9} = \frac{30}{90} = \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{15}\).
\[P(B_2) = \frac{4}{15}+\frac{5}{15} = \frac{9}{15} = \frac{3}{5}\]\[P(R_1 \mid B_2) = \frac{4/15}{9/15} = \frac{4}{9}\]\[\boxed{P(R_1 \mid B_2) = \frac{4}{9}}\]

Tree diagrams with more than two outcomes per stage

A tree diagram is not limited to two branches at each stage: any number of outcomes may emerge from a node, provided the branch probabilities at that node sum to 1. The multiply-along, add-across rules apply exactly as before.
Worked exampleA machine produces items that are A-grade with probability 0.70, B-grade with probability 0.25, or defective with probability 0.05. Two items are checked independently. Find (a) \(P(\text{both A-grade})\) and (b) \(P(\text{exactly one defective})\).
(a) \[P(AA) = 0.70 \times 0.70 = 0.49\](b) Exactly one defective occurs as (defective, not defective) or (not defective, defective), where \(P(\text{not defective}) = 0.95\):
\[P(\text{exactly one defective}) = (0.05\times0.95)+(0.95\times0.05) = 0.0475+0.0475\]\[\boxed{P(AA) = 0.49, \quad P(\text{exactly one defective}) = 0.095}\]
