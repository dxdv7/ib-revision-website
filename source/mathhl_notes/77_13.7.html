Indeterminate forms

If \(f\) and \(g\) are functions with \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = 0\) and \(\lim_{x\to a}g(x) = 0\), then the limit \(\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) is said to be in the indeterminate form \(\dfrac{0}{0}\). Similarly, if \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = \pm\infty\) and \(\lim_{x\to a}g(x) = \pm\infty\), the limit is in the indeterminate form \(\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). In either case, direct substitution gives a meaningless expression, so the actual value of the limit (if it exists) cannot be read off directly — it must be found by another method.
Worked exampleShow that \(\lim_{x\to 2}\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}\) is in indeterminate form, and identify which form.
As \(x\to 2\): the numerator \(x^2-4 \to 4-4=0\), and the denominator \(x-2\to 0\).
\[\boxed{\text{The limit is in the indeterminate form } \tfrac{0}{0}}\]

Statement of L'Hopital's rule

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable functions near \(x=a\) (with \(g'(x)\neq 0\) near \(a\), except possibly at \(a\) itself). If \(\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\) is in the indeterminate form \(\dfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}\), then:
\[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\]provided the limit on the right-hand side exists (or is infinite). The rule works by differentiating the numerator and the denominator separately — this is not the quotient rule, and the denominator of the original fraction is never differentiated as part of a quotient.
Worked exampleUse L'Hopital's rule to evaluate \(\lim_{x\to 2}\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}\), and check the result by factorising.
The limit is in the form \(\frac{0}{0}\), so L'Hopital's rule applies. Differentiating numerator and denominator:
\[\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \lim_{x\to 2}\frac{2x}{1} = 2(2) = 4\]Check by factorising: \(\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x+2 \to 2+2 = 4\) as \(x\to 2\).
\[\boxed{\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = 4}\]

Applying the rule to a standard limit

To apply L'Hopital's rule: (1) confirm the limit is genuinely in the form \(\frac{0}{0}\) or \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\) by substitution; (2) differentiate the numerator and denominator separately; (3) evaluate the limit of the new quotient. If this new limit is still indeterminate, the rule may be applied again.
Worked exampleEvaluate \(\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\) using L'Hopital's rule.
As \(x\to 0\): \(\sin x \to 0\) and \(x\to 0\), so the limit is in the form \(\frac{0}{0}\).
Differentiating numerator and denominator: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x) = 1\).
\[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{1} = \cos 0 = 1\]\[\boxed{\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1}\]

Applying the rule repeatedly

If, after one application of L'Hopital's rule, the resulting limit \(\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\) is still in indeterminate form \(\frac{0}{0}\) or \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\), the rule can be applied again to \(f'\) and \(g'\), giving \(\lim_{x\to a}\dfrac{f''(x)}{g''(x)}\). This can be repeated as many times as necessary, as long as each new limit is checked for indeterminate form before differentiating again.
Worked exampleEvaluate \(\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{x-\sin x}{x^3}\).
As \(x\to 0\): numerator \(\to 0-0=0\), denominator \(\to 0\), so the limit is \(\frac{0}{0}\). Differentiate once:
\[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1-\cos x}{3x^2}\]As \(x\to 0\): \(1-\cos x \to 0\) and \(3x^2\to 0\), still \(\frac{0}{0}\). Differentiate again:
\[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{6x}\]Still \(\frac{0}{0}\) as \(x\to 0\). Differentiate a third time:
\[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{6} = \frac{\cos 0}{6} = \frac{1}{6}\]\[\boxed{\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x-\sin x}{x^3} = \frac{1}{6}}\]

Recognising when L'Hopital's rule does not apply

L'Hopital's rule must only be applied when the limit is already confirmed, by direct substitution, to be in the indeterminate form \(\frac{0}{0}\) or \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\). If direct substitution gives a finite, non-indeterminate value (such as \(\frac{3}{5}\)), or gives a different type of indeterminate form (such as \(0\times\infty\) or \(1^{\infty}\)) without first being rewritten as a quotient in one of the two accepted forms, then differentiating the numerator and denominator separately is not valid and will give an incorrect answer.
Worked exampleExplain why L'Hopital's rule cannot be applied directly to \(\lim_{x\to 1}\dfrac{x^2+3}{x+1}\), and evaluate the limit correctly.
Substituting \(x=1\): numerator \(\to 1^2+3 = 4\), denominator \(\to 1+1=2\). Since \(\frac{4}{2}\) is not an indeterminate form, L'Hopital's rule does not apply here — the limit can simply be evaluated by direct substitution.
\[\boxed{\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x^2+3}{x+1} = \frac{4}{2} = 2}\]
