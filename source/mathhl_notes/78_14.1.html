Antidifferentiation as the reverse of differentiation

A function \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) if \(F'(x) = f(x)\) for all \(x\) in the domain considered. Finding an antiderivative is the reverse process of differentiation: given the derivative \(f(x)\), we look for a function \(F(x)\) that would produce it. Because the derivative of a constant is zero, if \(F(x)\) is one antiderivative of \(f(x)\), then \(F(x)+c\) is also an antiderivative for any constant \(c\) — so an antiderivative is never unique.
Worked exampleShow that \(F(x) = x^3\) is an antiderivative of \(f(x) = 3x^2\), and give another antiderivative of \(f\).
Differentiating \(F(x)=x^3\) gives \(F'(x) = 3x^2 = f(x)\), so \(F\) is indeed an antiderivative of \(f\).
Since adding any constant does not change the derivative, \(x^3+5\) is also an antiderivative, because \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3+5) = 3x^2\).
\[\boxed{F(x)=x^3 \text{ and } x^3+5 \text{ are both antiderivatives of } 3x^2}\]

Notation for the indefinite integral

The process of antidifferentiation is also called integration, and the general antiderivative of \(f(x)\) is written as the indefinite integral:
\[\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + c\]where \(F'(x) = f(x)\) and \(c\) is an arbitrary constant. Here \(f(x)\) is called the integrand, and \(dx\) indicates that the integration is being carried out with respect to \(x\).
Worked exampleWrite the statement "the derivative of \(\sin x\) is \(\cos x\)" as an indefinite integral.
Since \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x\), reversing this gives an antiderivative of \(\cos x\), namely \(\sin x\), plus an arbitrary constant.
\[\boxed{\int \cos x\,dx = \sin x + c}\]

The power rule for integration

For \(n \neq -1\):
\[\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + c\]That is, raise the power by 1 and divide by the new power. This rule is the reverse of the power rule for differentiation, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{n+1}) = (n+1)x^n\).
Worked exampleFind \(\displaystyle\int x^5\,dx\).
Using the power rule with \(n=5\):
\[\int x^5\,dx = \frac{x^{5+1}}{5+1}+c = \frac{x^6}{6}+c\]\[\boxed{\int x^5\,dx = \frac{x^6}{6}+c}\]

The constant of integration

The constant \(c\) in \(\int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+c\) is called the constant of integration. It is necessary because infinitely many functions share the same derivative \(f(x)\) — any two antiderivatives of the same function differ only by a constant. Without \(c\), the indefinite integral would represent only one of infinitely many possible antiderivatives, rather than the complete family of them. The value of \(c\) can only be determined if extra information is given, such as a known point on the graph of \(F\).
Worked exampleGiven that \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2x\) and the curve \(y=F(x)\) passes through \((1,4)\), find \(F(x)\).
Integrating: \(F(x) = \displaystyle\int 2x\,dx = x^2+c\). Substituting \(x=1,\ y=4\):
\[4 = 1^2+c \implies c = 3\]\[\boxed{F(x) = x^2+3}\]

Integrating term by term

Integration is linear: the integral of a sum or difference of terms is the sum or difference of their integrals, and constant multiples can be taken outside the integral sign:
\[\int \big(f(x) \pm g(x)\big)\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx \pm \int g(x)\,dx\]\[\int k\,f(x)\,dx = k\int f(x)\,dx \quad (k \text{ a constant})\]This allows polynomials to be integrated term by term, applying the power rule to each term and combining all the resulting constants into a single constant \(c\) at the end.
Worked exampleFind \(\displaystyle\int (4x^3 - 6x^2 + 2)\,dx\).
Integrate each term separately using the power rule:
\[\int 4x^3\,dx = 4\cdot\frac{x^4}{4} = x^4, \qquad \int 6x^2\,dx = 6\cdot\frac{x^3}{3}=2x^3, \qquad \int 2\,dx = 2x\]Combining, with a single constant of integration:
\[\boxed{\int (4x^3-6x^2+2)\,dx = x^4 - 2x^3 + 2x + c}\]

Integrating simple polynomials

To integrate a polynomial \(a_nx^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x + a_0\), apply the power rule to each term:
\[\int \big(a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0\big)\,dx = \frac{a_n}{n+1}x^{n+1}+\cdots+\frac{a_1}{2}x^2+a_0x+c\]A constant term \(a_0\) integrates to \(a_0x\), since \(a_0 = a_0x^0\) and \(\int a_0x^0\,dx = a_0 x^1/1 = a_0x\).
Worked exampleFind \(\displaystyle\int (3x^2 - 5x + 7)\,dx\).
Applying the power rule term by term:
\[\int 3x^2\,dx = 3\cdot\frac{x^3}{3}=x^3, \qquad \int 5x\,dx = 5\cdot\frac{x^2}{2}=\frac{5x^2}{2}, \qquad \int 7\,dx = 7x\]\[\boxed{\int (3x^2-5x+7)\,dx = x^3 - \frac{5x^2}{2} + 7x + c}\]
