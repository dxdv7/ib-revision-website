The fundamental principle of counting

If an event can occur in \(m\) ways and a second, independent event can then occur in \(n\) ways, the two events together can occur in
\[m \times n \text{ ways}\]More generally, if there are \(k\) independent events that can happen in \(n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k\) ways respectively, the whole sequence of events can happen in
\[n_1 \times n_2 \times \cdots \times n_k \text{ ways}\]
Worked exampleA large school issues ID codes consisting of two letters followed by three digits, where letters and digits may repeat. How many different codes are possible?
Each of the \(2\) letter positions can be filled in \(26\) ways, and each of the \(3\) digit positions can be filled in \(10\) ways:
\[\text{number of codes} = 26 \times 26 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10\]\[\boxed{\text{number of codes} = 676000}\]

Permutations of n objects

A permutation is an ordered arrangement of objects. Applying the fundamental counting principle to arranging all \(n\) different objects in a row, there are \(n\) choices for the first position, \(n-1\) choices for the second, and so on, giving
\[n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1 = n!\]different arrangements (permutations) of \(n\) different objects.
Worked exampleIn how many different ways can 5 different books be arranged in a row on a shelf?
\[\text{number of arrangements} = 5!\]\[\text{number of arrangements} = 5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1\]\[\boxed{\text{number of arrangements} = 120}\]

Permutations of n objects taken r at a time

The number of ordered arrangements of \(r\) objects chosen from \(n\) different objects, written \(^nP_r\) or \(P(n,r)\), is
\[^nP_r = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-r+1)\]which in factorial notation is
\[^nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\]A permutation is used whenever the order in which the \(r\) objects are arranged matters.
Worked exampleFifteen drivers are taking part in a race. In how many different ways can the top 6 finishing positions be filled?
Since the drivers are all different and the order of finishing matters, this is a permutation of 15 objects taken 6 at a time:
\[^{15}P_6 = \frac{15!}{(15-6)!} = \frac{15!}{9!}\]\[^{15}P_6 = 15 \times 14 \times 13 \times 12 \times 11 \times 10\]\[\boxed{^{15}P_6 = 3603600}\]

Combinations of n objects taken r at a time

A combination is an unordered selection of objects. Since every selection of \(r\) objects can be arranged in \(r!\) different orders, the number of permutations is \(r!\) times the number of combinations, so the number of combinations of \(r\) objects chosen from \(n\) different objects, written \(^nC_r\) or \(\binom{n}{r}\), is
\[^nC_r = \frac{^nP_r}{r!} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\]A combination is used whenever the order of selection does not matter.
Worked exampleA lottery has 45 numbers, and a ticket requires choosing 6 of them. In how many ways can 6 numbers be selected from 45?
Since the order in which the 6 numbers are chosen does not matter, this is a combination of 45 objects taken 6 at a time:
\[^{45}C_6 = \frac{45!}{6!(45-6)!} = \frac{45!}{6!39!}\]\[\boxed{^{45}C_6 = 8145060}\]

Distinguishing permutations from combinations

Before applying either formula, decide whether the order of the selected objects matters. If rearranging the chosen objects produces a different outcome, use a permutation, \(^nP_r\); if rearranging them produces the same outcome, use a combination, \(^nC_r\). Since every combination corresponds to \(r!\) permutations, \(^nP_r = r! \cdot {^nC_r}\), so \(^nP_r \geq {^nC_r}\) for all valid \(n\) and \(r\).
Worked example(a) A code is made up of 6 different digits chosen from the digits \(0\)-\(9\). How many codes are possible?
(b) A hand of 5 cards is dealt from a standard deck of 52 cards. How many different hands are possible?
In (a), swapping two digits gives a different code, so order matters and this is a permutation. In (b), a player can reorder the cards after receiving them, so order does not matter and this is a combination.
\[^{10}P_6 = \frac{10!}{4!}\]\[\boxed{^{10}P_6 = 151200 \text{ codes}}\]\[^{52}C_5 = \frac{52!}{5!47!}\]\[\boxed{^{52}C_5 = 2598960 \text{ hands}}\]

Combined counting problems

Many problems require combining the fundamental counting principle with permutations or combinations: when a task splits into independent stages, the number of ways for each stage is found separately (using \(^nP_r\) or \(^nC_r\) as appropriate) and the results are multiplied together.
Worked exampleFrom a standard deck of 52 cards, how many 5-card hands contain exactly 3 diamonds and 2 hearts?
There are 13 diamonds and 13 hearts, and the order within each suit does not matter, so each stage is a combination; the two stages are independent, so the counts are multiplied:
\[\binom{13}{3} \times \binom{13}{2} = 286 \times 78\]\[\boxed{\binom{13}{3} \times \binom{13}{2} = 22308 \text{ hands}}\]
