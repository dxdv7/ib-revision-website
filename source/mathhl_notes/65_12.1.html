The intuitive idea of a limit

The limit of a function \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches a value \(a\) is the value that \(f(x)\) gets arbitrarily close to as \(x\) gets arbitrarily close to \(a\) (from either side), regardless of whether \(f(a)\) itself exists. This is written:
\[\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\]Many useful limits arise from expressions that are undefined at \(x=a\) (often giving the indeterminate form \(\frac{0}{0}\)) but which approach a well-defined value as \(x\) gets closer and closer to \(a\).
Worked exampleInvestigate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x-2}\).
Direct substitution gives \(\frac{0}{0}\), which is undefined, so the function has a "hole" at \(x=2\). For \(x \neq 2\):
\[\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x+2\]As \(x\) gets closer and closer to \(2\) (from either side), \(x+2\) gets closer and closer to \(4\), even though the original function is undefined at \(x=2\) itself.
\[\boxed{\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = 4}\]

The gradient of a chord and the idea of a tangent

For a curve \(y=f(x)\) and a fixed point \(P(x,f(x))\), consider a second point \(Q(x+h,f(x+h))\) on the curve, where \(h\) is a small nonzero number. The gradient of the chord \(PQ\) is:
\[\text{gradient of } PQ = \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{(x+h)-x} = \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]As \(h \to 0\), the point \(Q\) slides along the curve towards \(P\), and the chord \(PQ\) approaches the tangent line to the curve at \(P\). The gradient of this tangent is the limiting value of the gradient of the chord.
Worked exampleFor \(f(x)=x^2\), find the gradient of the chord joining the points where \(x=1\) and \(x=1+h\), and state what this gradient approaches as \(h \to 0\).
\[\text{gradient} = \frac{f(1+h)-f(1)}{h} = \frac{(1+h)^2-1^2}{h} = \frac{1+2h+h^2-1}{h} = \frac{2h+h^2}{h} = 2+h\]As \(h \to 0\), \(2+h \to 2\).
\[\boxed{\text{The gradient of the tangent to } y=x^2 \text{ at } x=1 \text{ is } 2}\]

The derivative as the limit of the gradient of a chord (first principles)

The derivative of \(f\) at \(x\), written \(f'(x)\), is defined as the limit of the gradient of the chord as \(h \to 0\), provided the limit exists:
\[f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\]Geometrically, \(f'(x)\) is the gradient of the tangent to \(y=f(x)\) at the point with \(x\)-coordinate \(x\). Finding a derivative directly from this limit definition is called differentiating from first principles.
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(f(x)=x^2\) from first principles.
\[f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}\]\[= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^2+2xh+h^2-x^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2xh+h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(2x+h)\]As \(h \to 0\), the term \(h\) vanishes, leaving:
\[\boxed{f'(x) = 2x}\]
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(f(x)=x^3\) from first principles.
\[f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^3-x^3}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3-x^3}{h}\]\[= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0}(3x^2+3xh+h^2)\]As \(h \to 0\), the terms containing \(h\) vanish, leaving:
\[\boxed{f'(x) = 3x^2}\]

The derivative as a gradient function and as a rate of change

The derivative \(f'(x)\) is itself a function of \(x\): it gives the gradient of the tangent to \(y=f(x)\) at every point on the curve, so it is called the gradient function. Equivalently, if \(y=f(x)\), then \(f'(x)\) gives the instantaneous rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\) at a given value of \(x\) — the rate at which \(y\) is changing at that exact instant, as opposed to the average rate of change over an interval.
Worked exampleGiven that \(f(x)=x^2\) has derivative \(f'(x)=2x\), find the gradient of the tangent to \(y=x^2\) at the points where \(x=1\) and \(x=-1\), and comment on the result.
\[f'(1) = 2(1) = 2, \qquad f'(-1) = 2(-1) = -2\]The tangents at \(x=1\) and \(x=-1\) have opposite-signed gradients of equal magnitude, consistent with the symmetric, U-shaped graph of \(y=x^2\): decreasing for \(x<0\) and increasing for \(x>0\).
\[\boxed{f'(1)=2, \ f'(-1)=-2}\]

Notation for the derivative

If \(y=f(x)\), the derivative can be written in several equivalent ways:
\[f'(x), \qquad y', \qquad \frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big(f(x)\big)\]The notation \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) is read "the derivative of \(y\) with respect to \(x\)" and comes directly from the limit \(\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\) as the change in \(x\), \(\Delta x\) (here called \(h\)), approaches zero. The process of finding a derivative is called differentiation.
Worked exampleGiven \(y=x^3\), with derivative found above to be \(\frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2\), find \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) at \(x=2\), and interpret the result.
\[\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=2} = 3(2)^2 = 3(4) = 12\]This means that at the instant \(x=2\), \(y\) is increasing at an instantaneous rate of \(12\) units of \(y\) per unit of \(x\).
\[\boxed{\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=2} = 12}\]
