Guiding Question

How does the classification of organic molecules help us to predict their properties, and how can analytical techniques confirm the structure of an unknown organic compound?

Organic chemistry is built on classification. Because functional groups give predictable physical and chemical properties, recognizing a functional group lets you predict how a compound will behave before you have ever seen it react. At Higher Level, this sub-topic builds the complete toolkit used throughout the rest of organic chemistry: structural representations, functional groups and classes, homologous series, IUPAC nomenclature, and structural isomerism, exactly as at SL — but then goes substantially further into stereoisomerism (geometric cis–trans isomerism and, going well beyond SL, optical isomerism arising from chiral carbons) and into the analytical techniques used to determine structure: mass spectrometry, infrared spectroscopy, and — almost entirely new material at HL — proton nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, including chemical shift, the number of distinct signals, integration, and spin–spin splitting patterns. The final skill is combining all three techniques to deduce the full structure of an unknown compound.

Roadmap of this sub-topicStructural representations → functional groups & classes → homologous series → IUPAC nomenclature → structural isomers → HL: stereoisomerism (cis–trans & optical) → HL: mass spectrometry → HL: infrared spectroscopy → HL: NMR spectroscopy (chemical shift, integration, splitting) → HL: combining analytical techniques. The final stage depends on everything before it: you cannot interpret a spectrum of an unknown compound without first knowing how functional groups, homologous series and isomerism determine the possible structures consistent with a given formula.

Structural Representations of Organic Compounds

Chemical compounds can be represented by empirical and molecular formulas, which give the identities of the elements present. For organic compounds specifically, understanding how the atoms are linked is usually required to predict and explain properties and reactivity, so structural and skeletal formulas are used throughout organic chemistry.

Empirical and molecular formula

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound, and can be obtained from percentage composition data (for example, from combustion analysis). The molecular formula is the actual number of atoms of each element present in one molecule, and is therefore a whole-number multiple, n, of the empirical formula.

M = (molar mass of empirical formula) × n
Worked example

The empirical formula of ethane is CH3, and its molar mass M = 30 g mol−1. Find the molecular formula.

Molar mass of CH3 = 12 + (3 × 1) = 15 g mol−1. n = 30 / 15 = 2.

Molecular formula = (CH3)2 = C2H6

Structural formulas

The molecular formula alone is of limited use, since properties depend on how atoms are arranged, not just which atoms are present. The structural formula shows how atoms are bonded to each other, at varying levels of detail.

  • A full structural formula (graphic or displayed formula) shows every bond and atom explicitly. Bond angles of 60°, 90° and 180° are conventionally used for clarity on a flat page, even though these are not the true geometry.
  • A condensed structural formula omits bonds that can be assumed and groups atoms together, while still containing the minimum information needed to describe the molecule unambiguously — only one structure is consistent with the formula.
Table: formulas of ethane, ethanoic acid and glucoseEthane: empirical CH3, molecular C2H6, condensed CH3CH3 (two carbons single-bonded, each carrying three hydrogens). Ethanoic acid: empirical CH2O, molecular C2H4O2, condensed CH3COOH (a methyl carbon bonded to a carboxyl carbon, which is double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to a hydroxyl oxygen bearing an H). Glucose: empirical CH2O, molecular C6H12O6, condensed CHO(HCOH)4CH2OH — a six-carbon chain with an aldehyde at one end, four central carbons each bearing a hydroxyl group, and a terminal CH2OH.

Stereochemical formula

A stereochemical formula shows the relative positions of atoms around a central carbon in three dimensions: a solid wedge for a bond coming forwards out of the page, a dashed wedge for a bond going backwards behind the page, and an ordinary line for a bond in the plane of the paper. Four single bonds around a carbon give a tetrahedral arrangement (bond angle 109.5°, as in methanol, CH3OH); a double bond gives a trigonal planar local arrangement (bond angle 120°, as around each carbon of ethene, C2H4).

Skeletal formula

A skeletal formula is shorthand for a structural formula: it shows every bond except C−H bonds, and omits the symbols for carbon and hydrogen entirely (symbols for every other element are kept, along with the condensed formula of any functional group). Carbon–carbon chains are drawn as a zig-zag of offset lines, and the number and position of carbon atoms are read from the points where lines meet or end. Since every carbon has four bonds in total, the number of hydrogens on a given carbon equals four minus the number of bonds drawn to non-hydrogen atoms at that point: an end point with one line drawn to it is CH3; a point with two lines is CH2; a point with three lines is CH; and a point with four lines has no hydrogens.

Worked example

A skeletal formula shows a four-carbon chain. Carbon 1 has one bond only (to carbon 2). There is a double bond between carbons 2 and 3. Carbon 3 also bonds to a chlorine atom. Carbon 4 bonds to an OH group. Deduce the condensed structural formula.

Carbon 1: one bond drawn, a single bond to C2 (one non-H bond) → three H → CH3−. Carbon 2: single bond to C1 and double bond to C3 (three non-H bonds in total, since the double bond counts twice) → one H → −CH=. Carbon 3: double bond to C2, single bond to C4, single bond to Cl (four non-H bonds in total) → no H → =CCl−. Carbon 4: single bond to C3, single bond to OH (two non-H bonds) → two H → −CH2OH.

CH3CH=CClCH2OH
Structural formula versus skeletal formulaA full structural formula includes the element symbols of every carbon and hydrogen atom, with all bonds drawn. A skeletal formula omits carbon and hydrogen symbols entirely (keeping hydrogens attached to other elements), reading the structure from vertices and line-ends. Recognizing whether a carbon is primary, secondary or tertiary helps determine the number of hydrogens attached to it in a skeletal formula.

Abbreviations and catenation

When the exact details of an alkyl part of a molecule are not important, it can be abbreviated to R (or R′ for a second, different group). A hexagon with a circle inside represents the delocalized ring of a benzene-containing (aromatic) compound. Carbon rings may be saturated (only C−C single bonds, as in cyclohexane, C6H12) or unsaturated (containing C=C, as in benzene, C6H6).

CatenationCatenation is the ability of an element's atoms to bond to one another, forming chains and rings. Carbon's unusually strong, stable C−C bonds (bond enthalpy about 346 kJ mol−1) allow extensive catenation into straight chains, branched chains and rings, which — combined with the possibility of single, double and triple bonds and the huge range of functional groups — is the fundamental reason organic chemistry contains vastly more compounds than all other elements combined.

Saturated and unsaturated compoundsSaturated compounds contain only single bonds. Unsaturated compounds contain one or more double or triple bonds. The two undergo different types of reactions — addition reactions require an unsaturated site, while substitution reactions can occur on saturated carbon skeletons.

Halogens in a halogenoalkane are treated as substituents, since they have taken the position of a hydrogen atom, and so are always named with prefixes (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo) rather than a suffix. Methylbenzene, −CH3 attached to a phenyl group −C6H5, is historically known as toluene and is used as a solvent in markers, paint thinners and glues; naming phenyl-containing compounds is not required at this level, but recognizing the phenyl group in a structure is.

Reaction pathways

Synthetic organic chemists convert readily available starting materials into target compounds by organizing several reactions in sequence, so the product of one step becomes the reactant of the next: a reaction pathway, each step typically a functional group interconversion. For example, ethene can be converted into ethanoic acid in two steps, with ethanol formed as an intermediate:

CH2=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH2OH (addition of water, H2SO4 catalyst)
CH3CH2OH → CH3COOH (oxidation)

Amino acids and condensation

Amino acids each contain an amine group (−NH2) and a carboxylic acid group (−COOH). These react together in a condensation reaction, eliminating a molecule of water and forming a new bond between the acid group of one amino acid and the amino group of another: a substituted amide link called a peptide bond. Two amino acids linked this way form a dipeptide.

Formation of a peptide bondTwo amino acid units side by side: the first has a free −NH2 (N-terminal) on its left and a −COOH on its right; the second has its own amino group and free −COOH (C-terminal). An arrow shows the first acid group reacting with the second's amino group: H2O is released and a new C(=O)−N(H) linkage (the peptide bond) joins the two units, leaving a free −NH2 at the far left and a free −COOH at the far right of the dipeptide.

Because the dipeptide still has a free functional group at each end, repeated condensation reactions extend it into a tripeptide and eventually a polypeptide, a chain of many linked amino acids.

Homologous Series

Homologous seriesA homologous series is a family of compounds in which successive members differ by a common structural unit, typically CH2, and which can be described by a single general formula.

Alkanes (CH4, C2H6, C3H8, C4H10, …) differ by −CH2− between neighbours and are all described by CnH2n+2. Other series are characterized by a functional group, which appears in the general formula — alcohols (CH3OH, C2H5OH, …) are described by CnH2n+1OH.

Trends in physical properties

Boiling point increases with increasing carbon number in every homologous series, because a longer chain increases both the instantaneous and induced dipoles between molecules, strengthening London (dispersion) forces. Melting point, density and viscosity show similar trends. For alkanes, the increase in boiling point is steeper near the start of the series, since an added CH2 unit has proportionally greater effect on a small molecule; for chloroalkanes and alcohols, the rise is more even, since their strongest intermolecular force is tied to the functional group rather than chain length alone.

Order of volatilityFrom most volatile to least volatile: alkane > halogenoalkane > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > carboxylic acid. This matches increasing strength of intermolecular force — London forces only, through dipole–dipole, to hydrogen bonding — and so increasing boiling point.

Comparing compounds with the same number of carbons but different functional groups: alkane < chloroalkane < alcohol in boiling point, consistent with London forces only (alkane) < dipole–dipole (chloroalkane) < hydrogen bonding (alcohol). Hydrogen bonding in alcohols is strong enough that even methanol is a liquid at room temperature, while methane and chloromethane are gases. Branching of a carbon chain lowers boiling point relative to a straight-chain isomer, since branching reduces surface contact between neighbouring molecules, weakening London dispersion forces (in the same way that logs with many branches cannot stack as closely as smooth, unbranched logs).

“Straight chain” means continuous and unbranched, not drawn at 180°. Because of free rotation about C−C single bonds, the same molecule can look different on paper, so care is needed to identify the true longest chain.

Rule 2: the functional group gives the suffix and numbering

The functional group usually determines the suffix, replacing the “-ane” ending. The stem's chain may include the functional-group carbon itself (as for acids, esters, amides). A locant number, placed before the suffix, shows the position of the functional group; the chain is numbered from whichever end gives the smallest possible locant. Some groups need no locant, since they can only occupy one position (a carboxylic acid is always terminal; a three-carbon ketone has only one possible carbonyl position).

Worked example

Name CH3CH(OH)CH3.

Longest chain = 3 carbons (prop-). Functional group −OH (suffix -anol). Numbering from either end places OH on carbon 2.

propan-2-ol

Rule 3: substituents are prefixes

Side chains or additional functional groups (substituents) are prefixes, numbered to give the lowest overall locants and listed alphabetically; di-, tri-, tetra- are used for repeated substituents, with locants separated by commas.

Worked example

Name the three-carbon chain with Cl on carbon 1 and a methyl group on carbon 2.

1-chloro-2-methylpropane

Chloro is listed before methyl, alphabetically, each with its own locant.

−NH2 can be a suffix (amino, -anamine) or a prefix (amino-): when it is the only functional group, it is the suffix; when another, higher-priority group is also present, amino becomes a prefix, as in amino acids — CH2(NH2)COOH is 2-aminoethanoic acid, since the acid group takes the suffix.

Naming esters and ethers

Esters form when the alkyl group of an alcohol replaces the acidic H of a carboxylic acid:

R−COOH + R′OH → R−COO−R′ + H2O

The stem comes from the parent acid; the alcohol's alkyl group is the prefix. Ethanol + ethanoic acid gives ethyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH2CH3. For ethers, R−O−R′, the longer chain is the stem (keeping its alkane name) and the shorter chain becomes an alkoxy substituent, as in methoxypropane, CH3OCH2CH2CH3; when both chains are equal length, one is arbitrarily the stem, as in ethoxyethane (diethyl ether), CH3CH2OCH2CH3.

Summary of IUPAC nomenclatureAn IUPAC name has up to three parts written as one word: a prefix (position, number, name of substituents), a stem (longest chain length), and a suffix (class, from the principal functional group).

Structural Isomers

Structural isomersMolecules with the same molecular formula but different connectivities, and therefore different physical and chemical properties.

Chain and positional isomers

Butane, C4H10, has two structural isomers (butane, −0.5°C; 2-methylpropane, −11.7°C); pentane, C5H12, has three (pentane, 36.1°C; 2-methylbutane, 27.8°C; 2,2-dimethylpropane, 9.5°C); hexane, C6H14, has five. More branching lowers boiling point, since branching reduces intermolecular surface contact and weakens London dispersion forces. The number of isomers increases exponentially with size: 75 isomers of C10H22, 366 319 of C20H42. Alkenes show positional isomerism, where the C=C can occupy different positions along the same chain length, as in but-1-ene and but-2-ene (both C4H8); alkynes show the analogous isomerism for the C≡C.

Primary, secondary and tertiary carbonsA primary carbon is attached to the functional group and at least two hydrogens (ethanol, C2H5OH). A secondary carbon is attached to the functional group, one hydrogen, and two alkyl groups (propan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH3). A tertiary carbon is attached to the functional group and three alkyl groups, with no hydrogens (2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH). The same classification applies to amines, based on H and alkyl groups on nitrogen: primary (2 H, 1 C, e.g. CH3NH2), secondary (1 H, 2 C, e.g. CH3NHCH3), tertiary (0 H, 3 C, e.g. (CH3)3N).

Functional group isomers

Structural isomers can belong to entirely different classes: C2H6O describes both ethanol (an alcohol) and methoxymethane (an ether); C3H6O describes both propanal (an aldehyde) and propanone (a ketone); C4H8O2 describes a carboxylic acid (butanoic acid) and several esters (methyl propanoate, ethyl ethanoate, propyl methanoate).

Disubstituted benzene isomersNo structural isomers exist for monosubstituted benzene (all six ring positions are equivalent). Three structural isomers exist for a disubstituted benzene such as dibromobenzene: 1,2- (adjacent), 1,3- (one position apart), and 1,4- (directly opposite) — a hexagon with Br at two of the six vertices, in each of the three distinct relative arrangements. That only three isomers are observed, rather than the larger number predicted by a structure with fixed alternating single and double bonds, is itself evidence for benzene's delocalized, symmetric ring structure.

HL: Stereoisomerism — An Overview

Structural isomers differ in connectivity — which atoms are bonded to which. Stereoisomers have identical connectivity (the same atoms bonded in the same order) but differ in their spatial, three-dimensional arrangement. Because structural isomers can usually be told apart in two dimensions, but stereoisomers cannot, stereochemistry requires genuinely three-dimensional thinking — stereoisomers can never be distinguished using a condensed formula; a full structural, stereochemical or skeletal formula is required.

The classification of isomerismIsomerism splits into structural isomerism (different connectivity: chain, positional, functional group isomers) and stereoisomerism (same connectivity, different spatial arrangement). Stereoisomerism splits further into conformational isomerism (interconverted by free rotation about single/sigma bonds, not usually isolable as separate compounds) and configurational isomerism (a fixed, permanent difference in geometry, requiring bonds to be broken to interconvert). Configurational isomerism splits again into cis–trans (geometric) isomerism, arising from restricted rotation, and optical isomerism, arising from a chiral (asymmetric) carbon atom.

Conformational isomers usually interconvert spontaneously by bond rotation and so cannot normally be isolated as separate compounds, though some conformations are more stable and so favoured. Cyclohexane, C6H12, has two conformers, called the boat and chair forms, which interconvert by ring-flipping through bond rotation; the chair form is the more stable and predominant conformation. Conformational isomerism is not otherwise assessed in this course — the focus from here on is configurational isomerism, where the two forms are genuinely separate, isolable compounds.

HL: Cis–Trans (Geometric) Isomerism

When a constraint in a molecule prevents free rotation, substituted groups become fixed in position relative to each other, and their arrangement must be described relative to a reference plane. Two situations create this restriction.

Double bonds

A C=C double bond consists of one sigma bond and one pi bond, the pi bond formed by sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals above and below the bond axis. Free rotation about the double bond is not possible, since rotating one carbon relative to the other would twist the p orbitals out of alignment and break the pi bond. If each double-bonded carbon carries two different substituents, two distinct, non-interconvertible arrangements exist.

Criterion for cis–trans isomerism at a C=CCis–trans isomerism requires each carbon of the C=C to bear two different groups. If either carbon bears two identical groups, only one arrangement is possible and no cis–trans isomerism occurs — for example, CH2=CHCl has no cis–trans isomers, since one carbon carries two hydrogens.
cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-eneBoth isomers are CH3CH=CHCH3. In cis-but-2-ene, the two methyl groups lie on the same side of the C=C (and the two H atoms lie together on the other side). In trans-but-2-ene, the two methyl groups lie on opposite sides of the C=C, with a hydrogen on the same side as each methyl group. cis (Latin, “on this side”) describes matching groups on the same side of the reference plane through the double bond; trans (“across”) describes matching groups on opposite sides.

Cyclic molecules

A ring of carbon atoms similarly restricts rotation (the reference plane is the plane of the ring), and the strained bond angles in small rings — a triangle of 60° in cyclopropane, an approximate square of 90° in cyclobutane, both well below the tetrahedral 109.5° — do not affect this restriction. Substituents on a ring can lie on the same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans) of the ring plane; the substituted carbons need not be adjacent, only their relative position above or below the ring plane matters.

Worked example

Draw and name the cis–trans isomers of butenedioic acid, HOOC−CH=CH−COOH.

Since each −COOH group must sit at a chain terminus (not on the double bond itself), the skeleton is fixed, but each carbon of the C=C carries one H and one COOH group — two different groups on each carbon, so cis–trans isomerism is possible. In the cis isomer, the two COOH groups lie on the same side of the double bond (with the two H atoms together on the other side); in the trans isomer, the two COOH groups lie on opposite sides.

cis-butenedioic acid (maleic acid) and trans-butenedioic acid (fumaric acid)

Cis and trans isomers of the same compound can have substantially different physical properties (boiling point, melting point, solubility) and different chemical reactivity, since the spatial arrangement affects polarity and symmetry. Biologically, fumaric acid (trans) is an intermediate in the Krebs cycle of aerobic respiration, while maleic acid (cis) inhibits enzymes that interconvert amino acids — their different shapes change how they bind to enzymes.

HL: Optical Isomerism and Chirality

Chiral carbonA carbon atom bonded to four different atoms or groups is chiral (also called asymmetric, or a stereocentre). The four different groups, arranged tetrahedrally (109.5°) around the chiral carbon, can be arranged in two distinct three-dimensional configurations that are mirror images of each other — these are enantiomers, or optical isomers.

When identifying a chiral carbon, the whole group attached must be compared, not just the directly bonded atom: −CH3 is a different group from −C2H5, even though both begin with carbon. A chiral molecule has no internal plane of symmetry, exactly as a human hand is a non-superimposable mirror image of the other hand: placing both palms up or both palms down, the fingers and thumbs do not line up.

Enantiomers of butan-2-olThe chiral carbon of butan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is bonded to four different groups: −OH, −H, −CH3, and −CH2CH3. Drawn with a stereochemical formula and a mirror plane placed between two drawings, one structure is the exact mirror image of the other; rotating either structure in space can never make it coincide with the other — they are non-superimposable.
Worked example

Draw the enantiomers of 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH3CH(OH)COOH.

The chiral carbon is bonded to −CH3, −OH, −COOH and −H — four different groups. Draw a stereochemical formula with these four groups arranged tetrahedrally around the central carbon (two in the plane, one wedge forward, one dash back), add a mirror plane beside it, and reflect every group through that plane to generate the non-superimposable mirror-image structure.

Multiple chiral centres

A molecule with n chiral centres has a maximum of 2n possible stereoisomers (some may be too strained to exist in practice). When a molecule has two or more chiral centres, two distinct relationships are possible between any pair of its stereoisomers.

Enantiomers versus diastereomersEnantiomers have the opposite configuration at every chiral centre, and so are complete, non-superimposable mirror images of each other. Diastereomers have the opposite configuration at only some, but not all, chiral centres, and so are not mirror images of each other — they typically differ in physical and chemical properties, unlike true enantiomers. Common sugars such as glucose and galactose are diastereomers of each other.

HL: Properties of Enantiomers — Optical Activity

Diastereomers usually differ in physical and chemical properties, like any other pair of isomers. Enantiomers, however, have identical physical and chemical properties, with two important exceptions.

Optical activity

Ordinary light oscillates in every plane perpendicular to its direction of travel. Passing it through a polarizer blocks every plane except one, producing plane-polarized light. When plane-polarized light passes through a solution of a single enantiomer, the plane of polarization is rotated by a measurable angle; the amount and direction of rotation is measured with a polarimeter.

Optical activity (definition)A substance is optically active if it rotates the plane of plane-polarized light passing through it. The two enantiomers of a chiral compound, at equal concentration, rotate plane-polarized light by an equal angle but in opposite directions — one clockwise (+), the other anticlockwise (−).
Schematic of a polarimeterOrdinary light enters a polarizer, emerging as plane-polarized light in a single plane. This passes through a sample tube containing a solution of the chiral compound, which rotates the plane of polarization by an angle, θ. The light then reaches a second polarizer, the analyser, which is rotated until maximum light passes through it, allowing θ to be read directly. Concentration, wavelength of light, and path length must all be fixed to allow comparison between different samples.
Racemic mixtureA racemic mixture contains equal concentrations of both enantiomers of a chiral compound. The equal and opposite rotation caused by each enantiomer exactly cancels, so a racemic mixture is optically inactive overall, even though each pure enantiomer, taken alone, is optically active.

Naturally occurring chiral molecules are usually optically active, existing as essentially only one enantiomer (for example, naturally occurring sugars and amino acids are almost all a single enantiomer), whereas ordinary laboratory synthesis from non-chiral starting materials typically produces a racemic (optically inactive) mixture of both.

Notations for enantiomers(+) and (−) describe the direction of rotation of plane-polarized light directly: (+), or dextrorotatory (d-), rotates it clockwise; (−), or laevorotatory (l-), rotates it anticlockwise. This is unrelated to the separate D-/L- notation (based on spatial configuration relative to the reference molecule glyceraldehyde, widely used for sugars and amino acids) and to the R-/S- notation (based on priority rules using atomic number, giving the absolute spatial configuration). You are not expected to assign a specific enantiomer using any of these systems — only to recognize that a chiral carbon gives rise to two enantiomers with opposite optical rotation.

Reactivity in chiral environments

Although the two enantiomers of a compound have identical physical and chemical properties towards non-chiral (achiral) reagents, they can react completely differently with another chiral substance, since a chiral environment can accommodate one enantiomer's shape better than the other's — in the same way a right-hand glove fits a right hand but not a left hand. This matters enormously in biology, since enzymes, receptors and most biological molecules are themselves chiral.

Thalidomide: a cautionary exampleThalidomide has one chiral carbon and so exists as two enantiomers. One enantiomer (R) is an effective, safe sedative; the other (S) is teratogenic, causing severe birth defects when taken during pregnancy. Prescribed as a racemic (50:50) mixture in the late 1950s, it caused thousands of birth defects worldwide before being withdrawn; the two enantiomers are also known to interconvert inside the body, meaning that administering only the pure safe enantiomer would not have prevented the tragedy. This episode led directly to far stricter drug-testing requirements, including separately testing the biological effects of each enantiomer of any new chiral drug.

A racemic mixture can sometimes be separated into its two pure enantiomers (a process called resolution) by reacting it with a single enantiomer of a different chiral compound: the two components of the racemic mixture react at different rates or form different products with this chiral reagent, allowing them to be physically separated afterwards.

HL: Determining Organic Structures — An Overview

Chemical analysts use instrumental techniques to identify unknown substances and confirm proposed structures. Their work falls into three categories: qualitative analysis (detecting the presence, but not the amount, of a substance); quantitative analysis (measuring how much of a substance is present); and structural analysis (determining how atoms are arranged within a molecule). Three instrumental techniques dominate structural analysis in organic chemistry, each contributing a different type of evidence.

The three analytical techniquesMass spectrometry (MS) gives the relative molecular mass and, from characteristic fragmentation, clues to specific groups and branching present. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy identifies the types of covalent bond (and therefore functional groups) present. Proton NMR spectroscopy reveals the number of distinct hydrogen environments, their relative numbers, their immediate chemical surroundings, and the number of neighbouring hydrogens — collectively, powerful evidence for the full carbon skeleton. No single technique is normally conclusive alone; combining evidence from two or more techniques provides much stronger, often definitive, support for one particular structure.

HL: Mass Spectrometry of Organic Compounds

In the mass spectrometer, a high-energy electron beam knocks an electron out of each molecule, forming a positively charged molecular ion (or parent ion):

X(g) + e− → X+(g) + 2e−
Molecular ionThe molecular ion (parent ion) forms when a molecule loses one electron but otherwise remains unchanged; its mass/charge ratio, usually the peak at the highest m/z value in the spectrum, gives the relative molecular mass of the compound directly.

The ionizing collision is often energetic enough to break the molecular ion apart into smaller fragments. Only positively charged fragments are detected (electric and magnetic fields inside the instrument have no effect on neutral fragments), and for each bond that breaks, the positive charge ends up on whichever fragment forms the more stable cation — so not every conceivable fragment is observed with equal intensity.

Worked example — ethanol, C2H5OH, M = 46

The molecular ion appears at m/z = 46. A peak at m/z = 45 corresponds to loss of one H atom (46 − 1). Breaking the C−C bond can give either CH3+ (m/z = 15) with neutral •CH2OH, or CH2OH+ (m/z = 31, loss of −CH3, 46 − 15 = 31) with neutral •CH3 — both charged fragments are observed. Breaking the C−O bond preferentially gives the more stable C2H5+ ion (m/z = 29, loss of −OH, 46 − 17 = 29) rather than OH+ (m/z = 17), so a peak is seen at 29 but essentially none at 17.

Common fragment losses (from the data booklet)Mass lost → fragment lost: 15 → −CH3; 17 → −OH; 18 → H2O; 28 → CH2=CH2 or C≡O; 29 → −CH2CH3 or −CHO; 31 → −OCH3; 45 → −COOH. Subtracting an observed fragment's m/z from the molecular ion's m/z identifies the mass (and so the identity) of the piece that broke off.
Worked example — ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, M = 60

An unknown compound has empirical formula CH2O (empirical mass 30) and a molecular ion at m/z = 60. Since 60 / 30 = 2, the molecular formula is C2H4O2. Peaks appear at m/z = 15 (CH3+), m/z = 45 (loss of CH3, 60 − 15 = 45, giving the COOH+ ion, mass 12 + 16 + 16 + 1 = 45), and m/z = 43 (loss of OH, 60 − 17 = 43, giving the acylium ion CH3CO+, mass 12 + 3 + 12 + 16 = 43). This fragmentation pattern, together with the molecular formula C2H4O2, is consistent with ethanoic acid, CH3COOH.

Worked example — a six-carbon ring

An unknown compound has molecular formula C6H12 (molecular ion at m/z = 84). A saturated, non-cyclic (straight or branched chain) compound with six carbons would be C6H14 (hexane), so this C6H12 compound must contain either one C=C double bond or one ring. No peak is observed at m/z = 69 (84 − 15), so the molecule has no CH3 group to lose — ruling out methylcyclopentane and any branched or terminal-alkene structure. This evidence, combined with the molecule's very high symmetry (confirmed independently by NMR, covered later in this sub-topic), identifies the compound as cyclohexane.

HL: Infrared Spectroscopy

A covalent bond behaves somewhat like a spring: it vibrates (stretching and bending) at a natural frequency that depends on bond strength and the masses of the bonded atoms. Lighter atoms and stronger (multiple) bonds vibrate at higher frequency. In IR spectroscopy, frequency is conventionally expressed as a wavenumber (number of waves per cm, units cm−1) rather than as an ordinary frequency in s−1.

IR activityA bond or vibration only absorbs IR radiation if it produces a change in the molecule's dipole moment as it vibrates. A bond between two identical atoms (such as the N≡N in N2, or O=O in O2) is IR inactive, since stretching it causes no change in dipole; most bonds between different atoms are IR active.
Vibrational modes of CO2Linear CO2 has several vibrational modes. The symmetric stretch (both C=O bonds lengthening and shortening together, in opposite directions) causes no net change in dipole moment, since the two equal and opposite C=O dipoles remain in balance throughout — this mode is IR inactive. The asymmetric stretch (one C=O bond lengthening while the other shortens) and the bending modes all create a temporary net dipole and so are IR active, making CO2 an effective greenhouse gas despite its inactive symmetric stretch.

Hydrogen bonding broadens and lowers the frequency of an O−H absorption, which is why the hydroxyl of a carboxylic acid (very strong hydrogen bonding, often dimerized) gives an extremely broad band at a lower wavenumber (2500–3000 cm−1) than the hydroxyl of a simple alcohol (3200–3600 cm−1). A sharp peak near 1700–1750 cm−1 is a reliable signature of a C=O bond, present in aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, esters and amides alike — distinguishing between these classes requires combining the C=O signal with the presence or absence of a broad O−H band and other evidence.

Worked example

Compounds A and B both have molecular formula C3H6O2. Compound A shows IR absorptions at 3000 cm−1 (broad), 1730 cm−1, and 1230 cm−1. Compound B shows absorptions only at 1720 cm−1 and 1230 cm−1, with no broad band near 3000 cm−1.

Both show a strong C=O band (1700–1750 cm−1) and a C−O band (1230 cm−1, within the 1050–1410 cm−1 range). Compound A additionally shows the broad O−H band characteristic of a carboxylic acid, so A is propanoic acid, CH3CH2COOH. Compound B has no O−H band at all, so it must be an ester with the same molecular formula — either methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3, or ethyl methanoate, HCOOCH2CH3.

Greenhouse gases such as CO2, CH4, and water vapour absorb long-wavelength IR radiation re-emitted by the warmed Earth's surface, re-radiating some of this energy back towards the surface (the greenhouse effect). A gas's global warming potential compares the IR energy absorbed by one tonne of that gas, over a fixed time period, with the energy absorbed by one tonne of CO2 over the same period, and depends both on how effectively the gas absorbs IR radiation and on how long it persists in the atmosphere.

HL: NMR — Principles and Chemical Environments

Nuclei with an odd number of nucleons (including 1H, 13C, 19F and 31P) possess nuclear spin and behave like tiny bar magnets. In an external magnetic field, such a nucleus can occupy one of two energy states, depending on whether its magnetic field is aligned with or opposed to the external field; the small energy gap between these states can be bridged by absorbing a photon of low-energy radio-wave radiation, flipping the nucleus from the lower to the higher energy state — this absorption is what NMR spectroscopy detects. Electrons around the nucleus partially shield it from the external field, so nuclei in different chemical (electronic) environments absorb at slightly different frequencies, making NMR a powerful structural probe. Proton (1H) NMR, used throughout this course, exploits the hydrogen nuclei present in virtually every organic molecule.

Equivalent protonsHydrogen nuclei in identical chemical environments (related by the molecule's symmetry) are equivalent and give rise to a single, shared signal in the 1H NMR spectrum. The number of signals observed therefore equals the number of distinct proton environments in the molecule, not the total number of hydrogen atoms.
Worked example — counting signals

Methane, CH4: all four H atoms are equivalent (each bonded to a carbon attached to three other identical H atoms) — 1 signal. Ethane, CH3CH3: although the six H atoms sit on two different carbons, every H is attached to a carbon bonded to two other H atoms and one CH3 group, making all six equivalent — 1 signal. Propane, CH3CH2CH3: symmetrical, so the two terminal CH3 groups are equivalent to each other, but the central CH2 is a different environment — 2 signals. Propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH: not symmetrical, so each of the three carbons' hydrogens is a distinct environment, plus the OH proton is a fourth environment — 4 signals. Butane, CH3CH2CH2CH3: symmetrical, two CH3 environments (equivalent to each other) and two CH2 environments (equivalent to each other) — 2 signals. Butan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2CH2OH: not symmetrical — 5 signals (four distinct carbons' worth of H plus the OH).

Chemical shift

The position of a signal along the horizontal axis is its chemical shift, δ, measured in parts per million (ppm). A hydrogen nucleus close to an electronegative atom (or an electron-withdrawing group such as a carbonyl) has electron density pulled away from it, becoming deshielded; a deshielded nucleus is more exposed to the external field and resonates at a higher chemical shift. The closer a proton is to an electronegative atom, the greater this deshielding effect and the higher its chemical shift.

Signals from −OH (and −NH) protons are unusually variable in position, since hydrogen bonding (which depends strongly on concentration, solvent and temperature) shifts them considerably — this variability is itself a useful clue that a broad, position-shifting signal belongs to an −OH or −NH group rather than a C−H.

Worked example — ethyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH2CH3

Three proton environments: the CH3 attached to the carbonyl carbon (δ 2.0–2.5, next to C=O), the OCH2 attached to the ester oxygen (δ 3.7–4.8, deshielded by the adjacent electronegative O), and the terminal CH3 of the ethyl group (δ 0.9–1.0, simple alkyl environment, furthest from any electronegative atom).

Integration

The area under each signal (shown as a step on the integrated trace, superimposed on the spectrum) is directly proportional to the number of hydrogen nuclei producing that signal. Comparing the relative areas of all the signals in a spectrum gives the ratio of hydrogen atoms in each environment.

Worked example — ethanal, CH3CHO

Two signals: the CHO proton at δ ≈ 9.7 ppm, and the CH3 protons at δ ≈ 2.1–2.2 ppm. The integrated areas are in the ratio 1 : 3 (one CHO proton to three CH3 protons), directly reflecting the number of hydrogens in each environment.

HL: NMR — Spin–Spin Coupling and Splitting Patterns

At high resolution, many 1H NMR signals are not single peaks but a cluster of closely spaced lines, called a splitting pattern. This arises because the small magnetic field of a proton on a neighbouring carbon slightly modifies the effective field experienced by the proton(s) being observed — an effect called spin–spin coupling.

The n + 1 ruleIf a signal's protons have n equivalent protons on an immediately adjacent carbon, the signal is split into (n + 1) lines (a multiplet). The relative intensities of these lines follow Pascal's triangle.

Three further rules refine the basic n + 1 count: (1) protons bonded to the same atom are equivalent to each other and do not split one another's signal, since they behave as a single group; (2) protons on carbons that are not directly adjacent are generally too far apart to couple, and so do not split each other's signal; (3) −OH (and −NH) protons typically do not engage in spin–spin coupling with neighbouring C−H protons at all — an −OH signal is almost always a singlet, and is not counted as a “neighbour” when applying the n + 1 rule to other protons.

Worked example — ethanal, CH3CHO

The CHO proton has three neighbouring H (the CH3 group), so by the n + 1 rule its signal is split into 3 + 1 = 4 lines: a quartet, intensities 1:3:3:1. The CH3 protons have one neighbouring H (the CHO proton), so their signal is split into 1 + 1 = 2 lines: a doublet, intensities 1:1.

Worked example — propanoic acid, CH3CH2COOH

Three chemical environments, giving three signals. The CH3 protons (δ 0.9–1.0) neighbour the CH2 group's 2 protons, so n + 1 = 3: a triplet. The CH2 protons (δ 2.0–2.5) neighbour the CH3 group's 3 protons (the COOH proton, two bonds further away and non-coupling regardless, does not count), so n + 1 = 4: a quartet. The COOH proton (δ 9.0–13.0) has no adjacent C−H protons to couple with (and would not couple with them even if present), so it appears as a singlet.

A common shorthand records both the integration and the splitting together, for example "3H, t" for a three-proton triplet, or "1H, s" for a one-proton singlet.

Low-resolution versus high-resolution 1H NMRA low-resolution spectrum shows only the number of signals, their chemical shifts, and their relative integrated areas — still enough to identify the number of proton environments and their relative proportions. A high-resolution spectrum additionally resolves the fine splitting pattern of each signal, revealing the number of protons on each immediately adjacent carbon and so giving much more detailed structural information about how the carbon skeleton is connected.

HL: Combining Analytical Techniques

Mass spectrometry, IR spectroscopy and 1H NMR each give partial structural evidence; combining all three (and, where available, elemental composition data to find the empirical formula) typically allows a unique structure to be deduced with confidence.

Worked example: distinguishing three isomers of C3H6O2 (M = 74)

Methyl ethanoate (CH3COOCH3), ethyl methanoate (HCOOCH2CH3), and propanoic acid (CH3CH2COOH) are all functional group isomers of formula C3H6O2. Unknown X shows: IR — a strong, sharp C=O band at 1740 cm−1, a C−O band at 1200 cm−1, and no broad absorption in the 2500–3250 cm−1 region. Mass spectrum — molecular ion at m/z = 74, with major fragments at m/z = 59 (loss of 15, −CH3) and m/z = 15 (CH3+). 1H NMR — exactly two signals, both singlets, in a 1:1 integration ratio.

The absence of a broad O−H band rules out propanoic acid (a carboxylic acid would show one in the 2500–3000 cm−1 range). Propanoic acid would also show 3 signals, not 2, in its NMR spectrum (CH3, triplet; CH2, quartet; COOH, singlet), and ethyl methanoate would likewise show 3 signals (the formate CHO proton near δ 8, a singlet; OCH2, a quartet; CH3, a triplet) — ruling both out. Only methyl ethanoate gives exactly two signals: the CH3CO− protons (δ ≈ 2.1, no adjacent C−H, so a singlet) and the −OCH3 protons (δ ≈ 3.7, likewise no adjacent C−H, so a singlet), in a 1:1 ratio since each environment contains exactly three protons. The mass spectrum is consistent: m/z = 59 corresponds to loss of −CH3 from the OCH3 end (74 − 15 = 59, giving the CH3OCO+ cation), and m/z = 15 corresponds to CH3+. All three techniques agree: X is methyl ethanoate.

Worked example: an unknown hydrocarbon derivative

An unknown compound is 85.6% C and 14.4% H by mass. Moles: C = 85.6 / 12.01 = 7.13; H = 14.4 / 1.01 = 14.26. Dividing by the smaller value, 7.13, gives a ratio C : H = 1.00 : 2.00, so the empirical formula is CH2 (empirical mass 14.03). The mass spectrum shows a molecular ion at m/z = 84; since 84 / 14.03 ≈ 6, the molecular formula is C6H12. A saturated, acyclic C6 compound would be C6H14, so C6H12 must contain either one C=C or one ring. The IR spectrum shows a strong absorption near 2900 cm−1 (C−H) but none near 1620–1680 cm−1, ruling out a C=C double bond and confirming a ring structure. The 1H NMR spectrum shows only a single signal, meaning every hydrogen atom is in an identical environment — only possible for a highly symmetric ring. The compound is cyclohexane, C6H12.

A general strategy for combined analysis1. Use elemental composition (if given) to find the empirical formula. 2. Use the mass spectrum's molecular ion to find the molecular formula (and therefore the degree of saturation, from comparison with the saturated acyclic formula CnH2n+2). 3. Use the IR spectrum to identify which functional group(s) are present or absent (especially C=O, O−H, N−H, C=C). 4. Use the number of 1H NMR signals, their chemical shifts, integration ratios, and splitting patterns to deduce the carbon skeleton and the position of each functional group. 5. Cross-check that every piece of evidence is consistent with the single proposed structure, and rule out alternative isomers using any evidence they are inconsistent with.

Guiding Question Revisited

How does the classification of organic molecules help us to predict their properties, and how can analytical techniques confirm structure?
  • Organic compounds are represented by empirical, molecular, structural (full and condensed), stereochemical and skeletal formulas, each giving a different level of structural detail.
  • Classes of compound are defined by their functional groups, which determine physical and chemical properties and the IUPAC suffix/prefix used to name them; homologous series show a gradual, predictable trend in properties such as boiling point as chain length increases.
  • Structural isomers (chain, positional, functional group) share a molecular formula but differ in connectivity, and so have distinct properties and unique IUPAC names.
  • HL: Stereoisomers share both molecular formula and connectivity but differ in spatial arrangement. Cis–trans (geometric) isomerism arises from restricted rotation about a C=C or around a ring; optical isomerism arises from a chiral carbon bonded to four different groups, producing enantiomers that are non-superimposable mirror images with identical physical/chemical properties except in their rotation of plane-polarized light and their reactivity in chiral (e.g. biological) environments.
  • HL: Mass spectrometry identifies the molecular ion (giving M) and characteristic fragment ions (giving structural clues); infrared spectroscopy identifies the bonds and functional groups present from characteristic wavenumber absorptions; 1H NMR spectroscopy reveals the number of proton environments (number of signals), their type (chemical shift), their relative number (integration), and the number of neighbouring protons (splitting pattern, via the n + 1 rule).
  • HL: No single analytical technique is normally conclusive on its own; combining mass spectrometry, infrared spectroscopy and 1H NMR spectroscopy allows the full structure of an unknown organic compound to be deduced with confidence.
