Guiding Question

What are the challenges of using chemical energy to address our energy needs?

Humans are almost entirely dependent on chemical energy resources. Burning fuels for heat is probably the oldest human technology, and combustion still provides most of the world's primary energy today. Most of this energy comes from fossil fuels – coal, crude oil and natural gas – a chemical store of solar energy captured by green plants millions of years ago. Complete combustion of fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas and a major driver of climate change; incomplete combustion produces additional local pollutants such as carbon monoxide and soot. This chapter compares fuels quantitatively (by specific energy and energy density), and explores the main alternatives to simply burning fossil fuels: biofuels, captured from recent photosynthesis rather than ancient geology, and hydrogen, which can be used in a fuel cell to generate electricity directly, without combustion at all.

Combustion Reactions

Combustion is a reaction in which an element or compound burns in oxygen. Reactive metals, non-metals and organic compounds can all undergo combustion when heated in air or oxygen.

Nature of science – the phlogiston theoryEarly chemists explained burning and rusting using the ‘phlogiston’ theory: a fire-like substance, phlogiston, was supposedly released whenever a material burned. The theory fitted the qualitative observations of its time, but could not explain later quantitative data showing that substances actually gain mass during combustion. In 1783 Antoine Lavoisier's careful, quantitative work on oxygen showed that combustion and rusting are both combination with oxygen from the air, overturning phlogiston theory completely. This is a clear example of how a scientific theory, however well established, must be abandoned or revised once it conflicts with reproducible experimental evidence.

Combustion of metals and non-metals

s-block metals form ionic oxides, which are basic; p-block non-metals generally form covalent oxides, which are acidic. Some non-metals can show a range of oxidation states and form more than one oxide on combustion.

Mg(s) + ½O2(g) → MgO(s)
S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g)
P4(s) + 5O2(g) → P4O10(s)

Magnesium burns with an intensely bright white flame (used in flares and old-style flashbulbs); sulfur burns with a pale blue flame, releasing the toxic gas sulfur dioxide; white phosphorus, P4, reacts with air to give phosphorus(V) oxide, P4O10, among other products.

Worked example

Write a balanced equation for the combustion of white phosphorus, P4(s), in oxygen to form phosphorus(V) oxide.

Start from the skeleton equation P4(s) + O2(g) → P4O10(s). There are 10 oxygen atoms on the right, so 5O2 are needed on the left:

P4(s) + 5O2(g) → P4O10(s)

Most combustion reactions are strongly exothermic, but nitrogen combines with oxygen only at very high temperature in an unusual, endothermic combination reaction: N2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2NO(g). Every combustion reaction of a metal or non-metal involves electron transfer to oxygen: oxygen is the oxidizing agent (it gains electrons) and the element burning is the reducing agent (it loses electrons). The slow rusting of iron is also an oxidation reaction, but it is not combustion, since there is no flame.

Combustion of organic compounds

Hydrocarbons and alcohols are widely used as fuels because their combustion releases energy at a conveniently rapid, controllable rate. Although fuels are energetically unstable relative to their combustion products, they are kinetically stable – the reaction has a high activation energy – so they do not spontaneously ignite and can be safely stored and transported. Complete combustion of an organic compound breaks every carbon–carbon and carbon–hydrogen bond in the chain, fully oxidizing it to carbon dioxide and water and releasing a large amount of heat; it requires a plentiful (excess) supply of oxygen.

C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l)  (propane)
C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l)  (ethanol)
Worked example

Write the equation for the complete combustion of pentane, C5H12.

Balance C and H first: C5H12(g) + O2(g) → 5CO2(g) + 6H2O(l). There are 10 + 6 = 16 oxygen atoms on the right, so 8O2 are needed:

C5H12(g) + 8O2(g) → 5CO2(g) + 6H2O(l)

Combustion of an organic compound containing nitrogen can additionally release nitrogen gas or a mixture of nitrogen oxides. Combustion analysis (burning a sample and measuring the mass of CO2 and H2O produced) can be used to determine a compound's empirical formula.

Incomplete Combustion of Organic Compounds

When the oxygen supply is limited, or when a fuel has a high percentage of carbon by mass, incomplete combustion occurs: carbon monoxide, CO(g), or elemental carbon in the form of soot, C(s), is formed instead of (or alongside) carbon dioxide.

2C3H8(g) + 7O2(g) → 6CO(g) + 8H2O(g)  (incomplete, forming CO)
C3H8(g) + 2O2(g) → 3C(s) + 4H2O(g)  (incomplete, forming soot)
A Bunsen burner as a model of combustion controlWith the air hole fully open, plenty of air mixes with the gas and the burner gives a hot, almost invisible blue flame – complete combustion. With the air hole closed, much less oxygen reaches the flame, which becomes a cooler, luminous yellow flame – incomplete combustion, with visible soot particles glowing in the heat.

Compounds containing a benzene ring burn with a particularly sooty yellow flame, because of their high percentage carbon content.

Worked example

How could samples of cyclohexane (C6H12), cyclohexene (C6H10) and methylbenzene (CH3C6H5) be distinguished by observing their combustion?

Calculate the percentage carbon by mass in each: cyclohexane (M = 84 g mol−1), %C = (6 × 12.01) ÷ 84 × 100 = 86%; cyclohexene (M = 82 g mol−1), %C = (6 × 12.01) ÷ 82 × 100 = 88%; methylbenzene (M = 92 g mol−1), %C = (7 × 12.01) ÷ 92 × 100 = 91%. Methylbenzene has the highest %C and produces the sootiest flame; cyclohexane has the lowest %C and the least sooty flame.

HL worked example: comparing the heat released by complete and incomplete combustion

Use ΔHf° data [ΔHf°(C6H14,l) = −199, ΔHf°(CO2,g) = −394, ΔHf°(CO,g) = −111, ΔHf°(H2O,l) = −286, all kJ mol−1] to compare the heat released by the complete and incomplete (to CO) combustion of hexane, C6H14.

Complete: C6H14(l) + 9½O2(g) → 6CO2(g) + 7H2O(l). ΔH° = [6(−394) + 7(−286)] − (−199) = (−2364 − 2002) + 199 = −4167 kJ mol−1.

Incomplete (to CO): C6H14(l) + 6½O2(g) → 6CO(g) + 7H2O(l). ΔH° = [6(−111) + 7(−286)] − (−199) = (−666 − 2002) + 199 = −2469 kJ mol−1.

Incomplete combustion releases substantially less heat (−2469 kJ mol−1) than complete combustion (−4167 kJ mol−1) from the same amount of fuel, because the enthalpy of formation of CO2(g) is considerably more exothermic (more negative) than that of CO(g) – not all of the chemical energy available in the fuel's carbon atoms has been released.

Worked example

Deduce the balanced equation for the incomplete combustion of octane, C8H18(l), to carbon monoxide and steam, as occurs inside an internal combustion engine.

Balance C and H: C8H18(l) + O2(g) → 8CO(g) + 9H2O(g). Balancing O gives 8 + 4.5 = 8.5 mol O2; doubling throughout to clear the fraction:

2C8H18(l) + 17O2(g) → 16CO(g) + 18H2O(g)

Carbon monoxide toxicityCO is absorbed by the lungs and binds to haemoglobin (Hb) in red blood cells far more effectively than oxygen does, forming carboxyhaemoglobin: Hb + CO → COHb. This prevents oxygen from being transported around the body. Because CO is colourless and odourless, it can build up to dangerous levels completely undetected: around 35 ppm sustained over six hours causes headaches and dizziness, while 800 ppm can cause unconsciousness within hours, and higher concentrations are fatal.

Catalytic converters oxidize unburnt CO fully to CO2, and simultaneously reduce nitrogen oxides to harmless nitrogen gas:

2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g)
2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO2(g) + N2(g)

Soot from the incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons and coal is an example of a particulate – a solid carbon/dust particle or liquid droplet suspended in air, typically 0.001–10 µm in diameter. Particulates enter the body during breathing and can cause respiratory disease (emphysema, bronchitis, cancer); they are especially dangerous because they can act as catalytic surfaces that increase the harmful effects of other gaseous pollutants.

Fossil Fuels, Specific Energy and Energy Density

An ideal fuel is cheap, plentiful, accessible, releases high-quality energy at a reasonable rate, and has minimal environmental impact. Fossil fuels – coal, crude oil and natural gas – are non-renewable and non-sustainable, because they take millions of years to form yet are being consumed far faster than nature can replace them. Wood, by contrast, is renewable, since trees can be regrown within a human lifetime.

Energy density vs specific energyThe energy density of a fuel is the energy released per unit volume burned (kJ cm−3). The specific energy is the energy released per unit mass burned (kJ g−1). Liquid fuels have far greater energy densities than gaseous fuels, because gas molecules are spread much further apart. Hydrogen has by far the highest specific energy of any common fuel but a very low energy density as a gas, so it is favoured where mass is critical (rockets, aircraft) but not where compactness is critical (gas pipelines, where methane is preferred).

How fossil fuels form

Fossil fuels have been described as ‘sunshine in solid, liquid and gaseous form’, since their stored chemical energy originally came from sunlight trapped by photosynthesis millions of years ago. They form by the slow, partial decomposition of plant and animal matter trapped in the absence of air; oxygen is lost from the biological molecules faster than carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and sulfur, leaving reduced, hydrocarbon-rich residues.

Coal, crude oil and natural gasCoal is a combustible sedimentary rock formed from plant remains subjected to intense geological heat and pressure, which progressively raises the carbon content; coal is typically 80–90% carbon by mass, and anthracite (formed under the most extreme conditions) is almost pure carbon. Coal is the most abundant fossil fuel. Crude oil (petroleum) is a complex mixture of straight- and branched-chain alkanes, cycloalkanes and aromatic compounds (plus small amounts of N, O and S compounds), formed mainly from marine organisms under high temperature and pressure in the absence of oxygen; it is both a fuel and a vital chemical feedstock (polymers, pharmaceuticals, dyes, solvents). Natural gas is mainly methane, with small amounts of nitrogen and sulfur compounds (e.g. hydrogen sulfide) as impurities; it is the cleanest-burning fossil fuel because of its low percentage carbon content, and where a pipeline network exists it is very efficient to distribute, though building that network requires enormous capital investment.

As civilization has advanced, the carbon content of the fuels we favour has generally decreased: coal (highest %C) was used first; gasoline and natural gas, with higher specific energies and energy densities, easier transport, and cleaner combustion, followed.

Carbon content, CO2 output and specific energy across the alkanes

The higher the percentage carbon content of an alkane, the greater its tendency towards incomplete combustion, the more CO2 it produces per gram burnt, and the lower its specific energy – because a higher %C means a lower %H, and C–H bonds release more energy per unit mass on combustion than C–C bonds do.

Worked example

Calculate the mass of CO2 produced when 1.00 g of pentane, C5H12 (M = 72.17 g mol−1), undergoes complete combustion.

n(C5H12) = 1.00 ÷ 72.17 = 0.01386 mol. Each mole of pentane gives 5 mol CO2: n(CO2) = 0.0693 mol. mass(CO2) = 0.0693 × 44.01 = 3.05 g.

Fossil fuels and the greenhouse effect

Atmospheric CO2 has been measured continuously at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1957/58, and shows a steady year-on-year rise (with small seasonal fluctuations from northern-hemisphere plant growth and decay) as fossil fuel consumption has increased; global average temperature has risen over the same period, and CO2 is estimated to contribute about 50% of the overall warming effect.

Nature of science – correlation versus causationData alone cannot prove a causal link between rising CO2 and rising temperature: both could, in principle, be influenced by some third variable. A correlation is a statistical association; establishing causation also requires a plausible mechanism (which exists for the greenhouse effect) and, ideally, controlled experiments – impossible at a planetary scale, so scientists rely on computer models instead.
Why CO2 is a greenhouse gas: vibrational modesGreenhouse gases let short-wavelength solar radiation pass through the atmosphere, but absorb longer-wavelength infrared (IR) re-radiated from the Earth's surface, then re-emit it in all directions, including back down. A vibrational mode is IR active only if it changes the molecule's dipole moment. Linear CO2 has four vibrational modes: the symmetric stretch (both C=O bonds stretch and contract together, in phase) keeps the molecule symmetrical at every instant, so there is no change in dipole moment – this mode is IR inactive. The asymmetric stretch (one C=O bond lengthens while the other shortens) makes the two C–O bonds unequal, creating a temporary dipole – IR active. Two bending modes (perpendicular to each other, one in the plane of the page and one out of it) bend the molecule away from linearity, each creating a temporary dipole – both IR active. Because three of CO2's four vibrational modes are IR active, CO2 absorbs infrared radiation and re-radiates part of it back towards the surface, contributing to the greenhouse effect.

It is estimated that CO2 levels could double within about 100 years, and that the Earth's temperature could rise by roughly 2 °C within 50 years, with effects including changed agricultural yields, shifting species distributions (desertification, loss of cold-water fish habitats), and rising sea levels (thermal expansion plus melting ice caps and glaciers).

Four distinct pollution problems from fossil fuel combustionThese should not be confused with one another: CO2 (from complete combustion) is a greenhouse gas driving long-term global climate change; CO and soot (from incomplete combustion) are local, short-term toxic/respiratory hazards; SO2 (from sulfur impurities, mainly in coal and oil) dissolves in atmospheric water to form acid rain (H2SO3/H2SO4), acidifying soil and lakes and corroding buildings; and NOx (formed at the high temperatures inside engines, from N2 and O2 in the air itself, not from the fuel) reacts with unburnt hydrocarbons in sunlight to generate photochemical smog, and also contributes to acid rain.

Biofuels

Biofuels are produced from the biological fixation of carbon over a short period of time through photosynthesis, making them renewable and sustainable, unlike fossil fuels, which take millions of years to form. Chlorophyll in green plants absorbs solar energy and uses it to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose:

6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) → C6H12O6(s) + 6O2(g)

Photosynthesis products can be used as food, burnt directly as a primary fuel (e.g. wood), or converted into other fuels such as ethanol or biodiesel. Photosynthesis is not very efficient, so only a small fraction of incident solar energy is actually captured this way.

Bioethanol

Ethanol can be produced from biomass by fermenting plant material rich in starch or sugar, using an enzyme provided by yeast, typically at about 37 °C in the absence of oxygen:

C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2
Worked example

The enthalpy of combustion of ethanol is −1367 kJ mol−1. Calculate its specific energy and compare it with octane (ΔHc° = −5470 kJ mol−1).

M(C2H5OH) = 46.08 g mol−1; specific energy = 1367 ÷ 46.08 = 29.7 kJ g−1. M(C8H18) = 114.26 g mol−1; specific energy = 5470 ÷ 114.26 = 47.9 kJ g−1. Less heat is released per gram from ethanol than octane because ethanol already contains an oxygen atom – it is partially oxidized already, so less energy is released as it is oxidized the rest of the way to CO2 and H2O.

Gasohol (10% ethanol, 90% unleaded gasoline) can generally substitute for pure gasoline with only minor changes in performance. Advantages of ethanol as a fuel: it is renewable; it produces lower CO and NOx emissions than gasoline; it reduces a country's dependence on imported oil. Disadvantage: ethanol readily absorbs atmospheric water by hydrogen bonding, which can cause it to separate from the hydrocarbon components of a fuel blend and promote corrosion.

Biodiesel: transesterification of vegetable oils

Vegetable oils (and used cooking oils, or animal fats) are triglycerides: esters formed from glycerol and three long-chain fatty acids. Reacting a triglyceride with an excess of a small alcohol – almost always methanol – in the presence of a base catalyst (typically NaOH or KOH) breaks each of the three ester links and re-forms them as three separate, smaller methyl esters, releasing glycerol as a by-product. This reaction is called transesterification.

Transesterification, in outlineA triglyceride molecule (one glycerol backbone with three long fatty-acid chains attached by ester linkages) reacts with three molecules of methanol, catalysed by a base. The three ester linkages in the triglyceride are broken and reformed as three separate, smaller methyl ester molecules (these methyl esters are biodiesel), while the glycerol backbone is released as a separate, small by-product molecule.
triglyceride + 3CH3OH →catalyst glycerol + 3 (fatty acid methyl ester)

The resulting methyl esters (biodiesel) have physical properties – viscosity, energy density, cetane number – close enough to those of ordinary (petroleum) diesel that biodiesel can be used in existing diesel engines with little or no modification, either as a blend with petroleum diesel or, less commonly, on its own. Glycerol, the by-product, is a valuable chemical in its own right (used in cosmetics, pharmaceuticals and food).

Is biodiesel genuinely ‘carbon-neutral’?The basic argument is that biodiesel only releases, on combustion, the CO2 that the source crop itself absorbed from the atmosphere during its growth by photosynthesis – so, in principle, there is no net addition of CO2 to the atmosphere over the fuel's life cycle, unlike a fossil fuel, which adds CO2 that had been locked away for millions of years. In practice this claim has important caveats: growing dedicated energy crops can require clearing existing land (including, in some cases, carbon-rich forest or peatland), reducing the amount of carbon already stored in natural vegetation and soil; and the farming, harvesting, transporting and processing of the crop into biodiesel itself consumes energy, which is very often still supplied by fossil fuels. A full life-cycle analysis, not just the combustion step itself, is needed to judge how close to carbon-neutral a given biofuel supply chain actually is.

Biogas (methane)

Methane can also be produced by the bacterial breakdown of plant material in the absence of oxygen, giving a clean-burning biogas similar to natural gas. Carbohydrates give a biogas mixture that is about 50% methane, while fats (which have a lower oxygen content) give about 72% methane:

C6H12O6(s) → 3CO2(g) + 3CH4(g)  (from carbohydrate)

Hydrogen as a Fuel: Production, ‘Grey’ and ‘Green’

Hydrogen is attractive as a fuel because it has the highest specific energy of any common fuel (142 kJ g−1) and produces only water on combustion – no CO2 at all. The practical challenge is that hydrogen gas is almost never found free in nature; it must be manufactured, and the environmental benefit of using hydrogen depends entirely on how it was made.

‘Grey’ hydrogen: steam reforming of methaneAround 95% of hydrogen produced today is made from natural gas (methane) by steam reforming, reacting methane with steam over a nickel catalyst at high temperature:
CH4(g) + H2O(g) → CO(g) + 3H2(g)  (steam reforming, endothermic)
CO(g) + H2O(g) → CO2(g) + H2(g)  (water-gas shift)
Overall: CH4(g) + 2H2O(g) → CO2(g) + 4H2(g)
This hydrogen is called ‘grey’ hydrogen: it releases a substantial amount of CO2 during its production, so simply switching a vehicle or a fuel cell from petrol to grey hydrogen does not, by itself, remove fossil carbon from the overall energy cycle – the CO2 is just released at the reforming plant instead of at the point of use. (If the CO2 released during reforming is instead captured and stored underground rather than vented, the product is sometimes called ‘blue’ hydrogen.)
‘Green’ hydrogen: electrolysisThe alternative is to split water directly using electricity:
2H2O(l) → 2H2(g) + O2(g)  (electrolysis)
This route produces no CO2 directly. Whether the overall process counts as genuinely clean depends on where the electricity used for electrolysis came from: hydrogen made using electricity generated from fossil fuels is still, indirectly, a fossil fuel product, whereas hydrogen made using electricity from a renewable source (wind, solar, hydro) is called ‘green’ hydrogen, and is close to carbon-neutral over its full production cycle. Electrolysis is also one way of storing surplus renewable electricity (for example, from wind turbines generating more power than the grid needs at a given moment) as chemical energy in hydrogen gas, to be released again later, either by combustion or in a fuel cell.

A further practical challenge is hydrogen storage: as a gas, hydrogen has a very low energy density (0.0129 kJ cm−3, see table above), so it must be compressed to very high pressure or liquefied at extremely low temperature to be transported or stored in a reasonably sized tank, both of which require further energy input and specialized, expensive equipment.

Fuel Cells

A fuel cell converts the chemical energy of a fuel directly into electrical energy, without combustion, by physically separating the oxidation and reduction half-reactions at two electrodes and continuously supplying both reactants from outside the cell.

H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l)  ΔH° = −286 kJ mol−1

This overall reaction transfers electrons from hydrogen to oxygen, so if the two half-reactions are kept physically apart and connected only by an external circuit, that same electron transfer can be made to flow as a useful electric current, rather than simply as heat. This is the basis of the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.

Alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cellHydrogen gas is fed continuously to the negative electrode (anode), where it is oxidized; oxygen gas is fed continuously to the positive electrode (cathode), where it is reduced. Both electrodes are immersed in an alkaline electrolyte, KOH(aq). Hydroxide ions, OH−, migrate through the electrolyte from the cathode to the anode, while electrons flow from the anode to the cathode through the external circuit (doing useful electrical work on the way), with water produced at both electrodes overall.
Worked example

Deduce the two half-equations for the alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.

Anode (oxidation of H2): H goes from oxidation state 0 to +1, releasing 2 electrons per H2 molecule: H2(g) → 2H+ + 2e−. Under alkaline conditions, add 2OH− to both sides to remove the H+ and balance charge, then combine H+ and OH− into H2O:

H2(g) + 2OH−(aq) → 2H2O(l) + 2e−

Cathode (reduction of O2): O goes from oxidation state 0 to −2, requiring 4 electrons per O2 molecule:

O2(g) + 4e− + 2H2O(l) → 4OH−(aq)

Multiplying the anode equation by 2 to balance electrons, then adding both half-equations and cancelling, gives the overall equation: 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l).

The electrodes are often porous carbon with added transition-metal catalysts (e.g. nickel). As discussed above, hydrogen must first be manufactured, by steam reforming or by electrolysis; for the whole fuel-cell process to be genuinely clean overall, the hydrogen supplied to it should ideally be green hydrogen, produced by electrolysis using a renewable energy source.

The methanol fuel cell

Methanol is a stable liquid at ordinary conditions, with a much higher energy density than hydrogen gas, making it easier to store and transport. In the direct methanol fuel cell (DMFC), methanol is oxidized at the anode under acidic conditions, on a catalyst surface, all the way to CO2; the H+ ions produced migrate across a proton exchange membrane (PEM) to the cathode, where they combine with reduced oxygen to form water, while electrons travel separately through the external circuit.

Worked example

Deduce the two half-equations for the acidic methanol–oxygen fuel cell.

Anode (oxidation of CH3OH): C goes from oxidation state −2 to +4, releasing 6 electrons. Adding H2O to balance O, then H+ to balance charge (acidic conditions):

CH3OH(g) + H2O(l) → CO2(g) + 6H+(aq) + 6e−

Cathode (reduction of O2): as in the hydrogen fuel cell, but using H+ rather than OH−:

O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e− → 2H2O(l)

Balancing electrons (anode ×2, cathode ×3) and adding gives the overall equation: CH3OH(g) + 1½O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l).

Microbial fuel cells (MFC)MFCs harness energy from bacteria that oxidize a wide range of organic substances (including organic waste) to CO2 and H2O; some of the released energy can be harvested directly as electricity. They could offer a cheap, renewable energy source using material that is otherwise simply waste, avoiding the need for a pure, highly explosive, hard-to-store gas like hydrogen – though the operating details of MFCs are not assessed further in this course.
Fuel cell vs combustion, and fuel cell vs primary (voltaic) cellA fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy via a controlled electrochemical reaction at two separated electrodes, with no flame and (in principle) much higher efficiency than burning the same fuel to drive a heat engine, since combustion first converts chemical energy to heat, and a heat engine can never convert all of that heat into useful work. A fuel cell also differs from an ordinary primary (voltaic) cell such as a battery: a fuel cell does not run down as long as fuel and oxidant are continuously resupplied from external reservoirs, and its electrodes are not themselves consumed, whereas a primary cell has a fixed, sealed supply of reacting chemicals and simply stops producing current once that internal redox reaction is complete.

Guiding Question Revisited

What are the challenges of using chemical energy to address our energy needs?
  • Combustion is a reaction in which an element or compound burns in oxygen; complete combustion of hydrocarbons gives CO2 and H2O, while incomplete combustion (limited oxygen, or a high-%C fuel) gives CO and/or soot, releasing substantially less heat per mole of fuel.
  • Fossil fuels (coal, crude oil, natural gas) are chemical stores of ancient solar energy, non-renewable on a human timescale; fuels are compared quantitatively by specific energy (kJ g−1) and energy density (kJ cm−3).
  • Burning fossil fuels causes at least four distinct environmental problems: CO2 drives long-term climate change (it is a greenhouse gas because several of its vibrational modes are IR active); CO and soot are acute local toxins/pollutants from incomplete combustion; SO2 causes acid rain; and NOx causes photochemical smog and also contributes to acid rain.
  • Biofuels (bioethanol from fermentation, biodiesel from the transesterification of vegetable oils with methanol) are renewable because their carbon was fixed from the atmosphere only recently, but genuine carbon-neutrality depends on land use and processing energy, not on the combustion step alone.
  • Hydrogen has an exceptionally high specific energy and produces no CO2 on combustion, but it must be manufactured: ‘grey’ hydrogen (from steam reforming of methane) still releases CO2 during production, while ‘green’ hydrogen (from electrolysis powered by renewable electricity) does not.
  • Fuel cells generate electricity directly from a fuel (hydrogen or methanol) reacting with oxygen at separated electrodes, without combustion, and do not run down while fuel and oxidant keep being supplied – unlike a primary (voltaic) cell, which stops once its sealed internal reaction is complete.

Determining Enthalpy of Combustion Experimentally

The enthalpy of combustion of a liquid fuel can be measured in the laboratory by burning a known mass of fuel to heat a known mass of water and measuring the temperature rise, using the same calorimetry relationship used throughout thermochemistry.

q = mcΔT
ΔHc = −q ÷ n(fuel burnt)
Worked example

25.0 g of water is heated from 21.0 °C to 87.0 °C by burning 0.83 g of ethanol in a spirit burner (c(water) = 4.18 J g−1 K−1). Calculate the experimental enthalpy of combustion of ethanol and compare it with the accepted value of −1367 kJ mol−1.

ΔT = 66.0 K; q = 25.0 × 4.18 × 66.0 = 6897 J = 6.90 kJ. n(ethanol) = 0.83 ÷ 46.08 = 0.0180 mol. ΔHc = −6.90 ÷ 0.0180 = −383 kJ mol−1, far less exothermic than −1367 kJ mol−1, mainly because of heat lost to the surroundings (the air and the calorimeter) and incomplete combustion of the fuel – only a fraction of the heat released actually reaches the water being heated.
