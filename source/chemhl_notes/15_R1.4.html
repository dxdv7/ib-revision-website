Guiding Question

What determines the direction of chemical change?

Bubbles escape from an open bottle of carbonated drink and never spontaneously return; a hot cup of coffee cools down and never spontaneously reheats itself from the surrounding air. Both are everyday examples of the second law of thermodynamics: matter and energy tend to spread out, or disperse, and the universe as a whole becomes progressively more disordered. This chapter introduces entropy, S, as the precise, quantitative measure of this dispersal, and Gibbs free energy, G, as the single quantity that combines entropy with enthalpy to predict, decisively, whether a given reaction will occur spontaneously under given conditions – something that enthalpy change, ΔH, alone could never fully explain, since many familiar reactions are endothermic and yet still happen on their own.

Entropy as a Measure of Disorder

Entropy, S, is a measure of the number of ways in which the particles and the energy of a system can be arranged – its number of accessible microstates, or, more loosely, its degree of disorder or dispersal. A system with many equivalent ways of arranging its particles and energy has high entropy; a system with very few such arrangements has low entropy. As time passes, an isolated system naturally moves towards the arrangement with the greatest number of accessible microstates, so entropy naturally increases – this is the second law of thermodynamics.

Units of entropy: J K−1 mol−1, NOT kJEntropy values are always quoted in joules, not kilojoules, per kelvin per mole: J K−1 mol−1. This is a genuine, very common source of error, because enthalpy values, ΔH, are almost always quoted in kJ mol−1. Whenever ΔS and ΔH are combined in the same calculation (as they are below, in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS), one of the two values must first be converted so that both are in the same energy unit – otherwise the final answer will be wrong by a factor of 1000. A further distinctive feature of entropy, unlike enthalpy, is that absolute entropy values are always positive: a perfectly ordered crystal at absolute zero (0 K) has exactly zero entropy (there is only one possible microstate), and every other state of every substance, at any temperature above 0 K, has some positive entropy value, S° > 0.

Factors that increase entropy

Several general, qualitative rules let us predict the sign of an entropy change without needing any numerical data at all:

  • Physical state: for a given substance, S°(gas) is very much greater than S°(liquid), which in turn is greater than S°(solid) – particles in a gas are free to occupy a huge number of positions and have a continuous range of translational kinetic energies, while particles in a solid are fixed in position, vibrating about one lattice site.
  • Number of particles: increasing the number of individual particles in a system (for example, producing more moles of gas in a reaction) increases the number of ways that matter and energy can be distributed, so entropy increases. When predicting the entropy change of a reaction, a change in the number of moles of gas is usually the dominant factor, far outweighing any change in the number of moles of solid or liquid.
  • Dissolving: dissolving a solid or a liquid into solution generally increases entropy, since the solute particles become dispersed throughout the solvent rather than confined to a fixed lattice or a separate layer.
  • Temperature: entropy increases as temperature rises, because the available thermal (and, for a gas, translational, rotational and vibrational) energy becomes spread over an increasing number of accessible energy levels.
  • Molecular complexity: a larger, more complex molecule generally has a higher molar entropy than a smaller, simpler one in the same physical state, because it has more internal degrees of freedom (more ways its atoms can vibrate and rotate relative to one another) over which energy can be dispersed.
Worked example

Predict the sign of ΔS for: (a) Br2(l) → Br2(g); (b) 2Cu(s) + O2(g) → 2CuO(s); (c) H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g); (d) Ag+(aq) + Br−(aq) → AgBr(s).

(a) One mole of liquid becomes one mole of gas: dispersal increases, so ΔS is positive. (b) One mole of gas is consumed and none is produced: dispersal decreases, so ΔS is negative. (c) Two moles of gas become two moles of gas: no significant change in the number of gas particles, so ΔS ≈ zero. (d) Two moles of dissolved ions become one mole of solid: dispersal decreases sharply, so ΔS is negative.

Calculating ΔS°(reaction) from Standard Entropy Values

Every substance has a tabulated standard molar entropy, S°, measured in J K−1 mol−1 under standard conditions. Because entropy is a state function, the standard entropy change of a reaction can be calculated exactly like a standard enthalpy change from formation data, but using absolute entropies directly (there is no need to subtract entropies of elements, since – unlike ΔHf° – the entropy of an element in its standard state is not zero):

ΔS°(reaction) = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants)

Worked example: entropy change of the Haber process

Calculate ΔS° for the Haber process, N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g), using the table above. Predict the sign first: 4 mol of gaseous reactants form only 2 mol of gaseous product, so dispersal decreases and ΔS° should be negative.

ΣS°(products) = 2 × 193 = 386 J K−1 mol−1

ΣS°(reactants) = 191 + (3 × 131) = 191 + 393 = 584 J K−1 mol−1

ΔS° = 386 − 584 = −198 J K−1 mol−1, a substantial decrease in entropy, exactly as predicted, since the total number of moles of gas falls from 4 to 2.

Worked example: entropy change for the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate

Calculate ΔS° for CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g), using the table above. One mole of solid becomes one mole of solid plus one mole of gas, so ΔS° should be positive.

ΔS° = [S°(CaO) + S°(CO2)] − S°(CaCO3) = (39.7 + 214) − 92.9 = 253.7 − 92.9 = +160.8 J K−1 mol−1, positive as predicted, since one mole of gas is produced from entirely solid reactants.

Entropy of the Surroundings and the Total Entropy Change

Entropy alone, applied only to the chemicals actually present in a reaction (the ‘system’), cannot fully explain why reactions happen: many familiar, spontaneous reactions actually have a negative ΔS(system) (ionic precipitations, the Haber process above), and some spontaneous reactions are endothermic, which seems to disperse energy the ‘wrong’ way. The resolution is that the second law of thermodynamics applies to the total entropy of system plus surroundings, not to the system in isolation:

ΔS(total) = ΔS(system) + ΔS(surroundings) > 0  for a spontaneous change

Whenever a reaction releases heat to the surroundings (an exothermic reaction, ΔH(system) < 0), the entropy of the surroundings increases; whenever a reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings (endothermic, ΔH(system) > 0), the entropy of the surroundings decreases. The size of this effect also depends on the temperature at which the heat is transferred: the same quantity of heat causes a much bigger change in the dispersal of a cold environment than of an already-hot one. Both effects together give:

ΔS(surroundings) = −ΔH(system) ÷ T  (T in kelvin)
Worked example: total entropy change for the formation of liquid water

For H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l), ΔH° = −286 kJ mol−1. Using S°(H2) = 131, S°(O2) = 205 and S°(H2O,l) = 70.0 J K−1 mol−1, calculate ΔS(system), ΔS(surroundings) and ΔS(total) at 298 K.

ΔS°(system) = 70.0 − [131 + (½ × 205)] = 70.0 − 233.5 = −163.5 J K−1 mol−1 (negative, as expected: 1.5 mol of gas becomes zero mol of gas).

ΔS(surroundings) = −(−286 000 J mol−1) ÷ 298 K = +960 J K−1 mol−1 (note ΔH had to be converted from kJ to J mol−1 first, to match the J-based units of S).

ΔS(total) = −163.5 + 960 = +796 J K−1 mol−1. Even though the entropy of the system itself decreases, the much larger increase in the entropy of the surroundings (caused by the large quantity of heat released) makes the total entropy change strongly positive, so the reaction is spontaneous – consistent with the fact that hydrogen does indeed burn in oxygen.

This is also the key to understanding how an endothermic reaction can ever be spontaneous: it must be accompanied by a sufficiently large increase in the entropy of the system to outweigh the decrease in the entropy of the surroundings (caused by heat flowing into the system, away from the surroundings). Since neither ΔH(system) nor ΔS(system) alone can reliably predict whether a reaction will be spontaneous, a single combined function – Gibbs free energy – is far more useful in practice.

Gibbs Free Energy, ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°

Starting from the total entropy criterion for spontaneity and substituting the expression for ΔS(surroundings):

ΔS(total) = ΔS(system) − ΔH(system) ÷ T > 0

Multiplying through by −T (which reverses the inequality, since T is always positive) gives a new quantity called the Gibbs free energy change, ΔG:

ΔG(system) = ΔH(system) − TΔS(system) = −TΔS(total)

Because ΔS(total) must be positive for a spontaneous change, and the two are related by a simple negative sign and a multiplication by the (always positive) absolute temperature, ΔG(system) must be negative for a spontaneous change. Gibbs free energy combines enthalpy and entropy into one single quantity that can be used to predict spontaneity directly from data about the system alone, without needing to calculate the entropy change of the surroundings separately every time.

Why both ΔH and ΔS matter, not ΔH aloneEnthalpy change by itself cannot predict spontaneity, because it ignores entropy changes in the surroundings entirely; entropy change of the system by itself cannot predict spontaneity either, because it ignores the (often much larger) entropy change in the surroundings caused by heat transfer. Gibbs free energy change, ΔG, is the single function that correctly accounts for both effects together, which is why it – not ΔH and not ΔS(system) in isolation – is the definitive criterion for whether a reaction is thermodynamically favourable under given conditions.
Sign of ΔG° and spontaneity
  • ΔG° < 0: the reaction is spontaneous (thermodynamically favourable) as written, under standard conditions.
  • ΔG° > 0: the reaction as written is non-spontaneous under standard conditions – but the reverse reaction is spontaneous, since reversing a reaction simply reverses the sign of ΔG°.
  • ΔG° = 0: the system is at equilibrium; there is no net tendency to proceed in either direction.
A negative ΔG° tells us a reaction is thermodynamically favourable, but it gives no information at all about how fast that reaction will actually occur – that depends on the activation energy and is a question of kinetics, not thermodynamics. A spontaneous reaction with a high activation energy barrier may need to be heated, or supplied with a catalyst, before it proceeds at any noticeable rate, even though ΔG° is negative throughout.

Two routes to calculating ΔG°

ΔG° at 298 K can be calculated directly from tabulated standard Gibbs energies of formation, ΔGf°, using exactly the same type of cycle used for ΔHf° (and, as for ΔHf°, ΔGf° of an element in its standard state is zero by definition):

ΔG° = ΣΔGf°(products) − ΣΔGf°(reactants)

This route, however, only gives ΔG° at 298 K, since tabulated ΔGf° values are specific to that temperature. To find ΔG at any other temperature, the equation ΔG = ΔH − TΔS must be applied directly, using ΔH° and ΔS° (which barely change with temperature, and so can be treated as constant) together with the actual temperature of interest.

Worked example: ΔG° for the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate at 298 K

Using ΔH° = +178 kJ mol−1 and ΔS° = +160.8 J K−1 mol−1 (both calculated above) for CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g), calculate ΔG° at 298 K.

Since ΔH° is in kJ and ΔS° is in J, convert ΔS° to kJ K−1 mol−1 first: 160.8 J K−1 mol−1 = 0.1608 kJ K−1 mol−1.

ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° = 178 − (298 × 0.1608) = 178 − 47.9 = +130 kJ mol−1. Since ΔG° is positive, the decomposition of calcium carbonate is non-spontaneous at 298 K – limestone is thermodynamically stable at room temperature, which matches everyday experience.

The Four ΔH/ΔS Sign Combinations and the Crossover Temperature

Because ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° combines a temperature-independent term (ΔH°) with a term that scales directly with temperature (TΔS°), whether a reaction is spontaneous can depend on temperature – and the way it depends on temperature is fixed entirely by the signs of ΔH° and ΔS°. There are four possible combinations.

ΔH°ΔS°ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°Spontaneity
negativepositivealways negative (both terms favourable)spontaneous at all temperatures
positivenegativealways positive (both terms unfavourable)never spontaneous
negativenegativenegative at low T, positive at high Tspontaneous only at low temperature
positivepositivepositive at low T, negative at high Tspontaneous only at high temperature

In the two temperature-dependent cases, there is a specific crossover temperature, Tspontaneous, at which ΔG° is exactly zero (the boundary between spontaneous and non-spontaneous). Setting ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° = 0 and rearranging:

Tspontaneous = ΔH° ÷ ΔS°
Worked example: crossover temperature for calcium carbonate (ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0 – spontaneous only at high T)

Using ΔH° = +178 kJ mol−1 = +178 000 J mol−1 and ΔS° = +160.8 J K−1 mol−1 for the decomposition of CaCO3, calculate the temperature above which decomposition becomes spontaneous.

Tspontaneous = 178 000 ÷ 160.8 = 1107 K (about 834 °C). Below 1107 K, ΔG° is positive and CaCO3 is stable; above 1107 K, ΔG° becomes negative and decomposition is spontaneous – consistent with the fact that limestone is thermally decomposed industrially (to make quicklime, CaO) in kilns operated at around 1000–1100 °C.

Worked example: crossover temperature for the Haber process (ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0 – spontaneous only at low T)

For N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g), ΔH° = −92 kJ mol−1 and ΔS° = −198 J K−1 mol−1 (calculated earlier). Calculate the crossover temperature, and ΔG° at 298 K and at 800 K.

Tspontaneous = −92 000 ÷ (−198) = 465 K. Below 465 K the reaction is spontaneous; above 465 K it is not.

At 298 K: ΔG° = −92 − [298 × (−0.198)] = −92 + 59.0 = −33.0 kJ mol−1 (negative, spontaneous, since 298 K < 465 K). At 800 K: ΔG° = −92 − [800 × (−0.198)] = −92 + 158.4 = +66.4 kJ mol−1 (positive, non-spontaneous, since 800 K > 465 K).

This explains an otherwise puzzling feature of the industrial Haber process: although the reaction is thermodynamically favoured only at low temperature, industrial plants actually operate at around 400–450 °C (670–720 K) – a temperature chosen as a practical compromise, since a low temperature that maximizes the equilibrium yield would also make the reaction rate impractically slow; a catalyst (iron) is used specifically to make the reaction fast enough at a moderate, more favourable-for-rate temperature, even though a higher temperature somewhat reduces the thermodynamically attainable yield.

The −TΔS° term as a ‘temperature tap’It is useful to think of absolute temperature, T, as a dial that controls how much the entropy term, −TΔS°, contributes to ΔG° relative to the fixed enthalpy term, ΔH°. At very low T, the entropy term is small, so ΔG° ≈ ΔH°, and spontaneity is governed almost entirely by whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. As T increases, the entropy term grows in magnitude, and eventually dominates ΔG° completely; at very high T, ΔG° ≈ −TΔS°, and spontaneity is governed almost entirely by the sign of ΔS°.

ΔG° and the Equilibrium Constant, K

Every one of the worked examples so far has implicitly assumed a reaction goes entirely to completion, but real reactions settle at a position of equilibrium. ΔG° is in fact directly related to the equilibrium constant, K, of a reaction, by the equation:

ΔG° = −RT ln K  (R = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1, T in kelvin)
How the sign and magnitude of ΔG° map onto K
  • A large, negative ΔG° corresponds to a large, positive value of ln K, and hence a very large K (K >> 1): the equilibrium lies strongly towards products.
  • ΔG° = 0 corresponds to ln K = 0, so K = 1: an equilibrium mixture with comparable, appreciable amounts of both reactants and products.
  • A large, positive ΔG° corresponds to a large, negative ln K, and hence a very small K (K << 1): the equilibrium lies strongly towards reactants.
In short: the more negative ΔG° is, the larger K is, and the further the reaction proceeds towards products at equilibrium – this single equation is what directly links thermodynamics (energetics) to equilibrium (the extent of reaction).
Units: ΔG must be in J mol−1, not kJ mol−1, when used with RSince R is given in J K−1 mol−1, ΔG° must first be converted from the usual kJ mol−1 into J mol−1 (multiply by 1000) before it is substituted into ΔG° = −RT ln K, or the calculated value of K (or of ΔG°, if working in the other direction) will be wrong by a factor of 1000 in the exponent – an enormous error, since K depends exponentially on ΔG°.
Worked example: calculating K from ΔG°

The esterification reaction CH3COOH(aq) + C2H5OH(aq) → CH3COOC2H5(aq) + H2O(aq) has ΔG° = −4.38 kJ mol−1 at 298 K. Calculate K.

Convert ΔG° to J mol−1: −4.38 kJ mol−1 = −4380 J mol−1.

Rearranging ΔG° = −RT ln K gives ln K = −ΔG° ÷ RT = −(−4380) ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 4380 ÷ 2476.4 = 1.77.

K = e1.77 = 5.9. Since K > 1 (though not dramatically so), the equilibrium mixture contains somewhat more ester and water than unreacted acid and alcohol, but appreciable amounts of all four species remain present – consistent with esterification being a classic, reversible, incomplete equilibrium reaction rather than one that runs to completion.

Worked example: calculating ΔG° from K

At 298 K, the equilibrium constant for 2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g) is K = 1.7 × 1012. Calculate ΔG° for this reaction.

ΔG° = −RT ln K = −(8.31 × 298) × ln(1.7 × 1012) = −2476.4 × 28.16 = −69 739 J mol−1 = −69.7 kJ mol−1.

This large, negative ΔG° corresponds to the enormous value of K, confirming that the oxidation of NO to NO2 lies overwhelmingly towards products at equilibrium – which is exactly what allows NO emitted from vehicle engines to be so efficiently (and rapidly, once nucleation begins) converted into NO2 in the atmosphere.

Guiding Question Revisited

What determines the direction of chemical change?
  • Entropy, S, measures the number of accessible microstates, or degree of dispersal, of a system; it is always positive and is measured in J K−1 mol−1, never kJ. Entropy increases with the number of particles (especially moles of gas), on going solid → liquid → gas, on dissolving, with rising temperature, and with molecular complexity.
  • ΔS°(reaction) = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants); for the Haber process this comes out strongly negative (−198 J K−1 mol−1), since 4 mol of gaseous reactants form only 2 mol of gaseous product.
  • The second law of thermodynamics requires the total entropy of system plus surroundings to increase for any spontaneous change; ΔS(surroundings) = −ΔH(system) ÷ T, which is why exothermic reactions are more commonly spontaneous than endothermic ones.
  • Gibbs free energy, ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°, combines both effects into one criterion: ΔG° < 0 means spontaneous as written, ΔG° > 0 means non-spontaneous as written (spontaneous in reverse), and ΔG° = 0 means the system is at equilibrium.
  • The sign combination of ΔH° and ΔS° determines how spontaneity depends on temperature: (−,+) always spontaneous; (+,−) never spontaneous; (−,−) spontaneous only below a crossover temperature T = ΔH°÷ΔS°; (+,+) spontaneous only above that crossover temperature.
  • ΔG° = −RT ln K links thermodynamics directly to the position of equilibrium: a more negative ΔG° corresponds to a larger equilibrium constant, K, and a reaction lying further towards products.
