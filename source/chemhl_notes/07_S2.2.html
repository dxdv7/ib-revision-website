Guiding Question

What determines the covalent nature and properties of a substance?

Covalence is the idea that atoms can combine by sharing pairs of valence electrons rather than transferring them outright, as happens in ionic bonding. The American chemist Gilbert Lewis first proposed this idea in the early 20th century and devised the Lewis formula, a notation for showing exactly how electrons are arranged so that each atom gains a full valence shell. At Higher Level, this sub-topic builds a complete toolkit for predicting and explaining covalent structure in far more depth than at SL: Lewis formulas and the octet rule, single/double/triple and coordination bonds, the VSEPR model and molecular shape, bond and molecular polarity, covalent network structures, intermolecular forces and chromatography — and then, going well beyond SL, resonance structures (including the historically important case of benzene), expanded octets on five- and six-domain central atoms, formal charge as a tool for choosing between competing Lewis formulas, and finally the orbital-level explanation of bonding itself through sigma and pi bonds and hybridization — the single biggest new idea in this sub-topic, since it explains why VSEPR angles like 109.5° and 120° actually arise from atomic orbitals that are not naturally arranged that way at all.

Roadmap of this sub-topicOctet rule & Lewis formulas → multiple bonds & coordination bonds → VSEPR shapes (2–4 domains) → bond & molecular polarity → covalent network structures → intermolecular forces & chromatography → HL: resonance & benzene → HL: expanded octets (5–6 domains) → HL: formal charge → HL: sigma/pi bonds → HL: hybridization. Each stage depends on the one before: you cannot assign hybridization without first knowing the number of electron domains, and you cannot judge electron domains without a correct Lewis formula.

Covalent Bonding and the Octet Rule

When two non-metal atoms react, each is trying to gain a full valence shell like the nearest noble gas. Rather than transferring electrons completely, non-metals achieve this by sharing a pair of electrons. The shared pair sits in the region of space between the two nuclei and, being a region of negative charge, is attracted simultaneously to both positively charged nuclei. This mutual attraction of two nuclei to one shared electron pair is what holds the atoms together.

Covalent bond (definition)A covalent bond is the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the positively charged nuclei of the two bonded atoms. A group of atoms held together entirely by covalent bonds is a molecule.
Diagram: energy profile of covalent bond formationA graph of potential energy (vertical axis) against the separation distance between two approaching hydrogen nuclei (horizontal axis). Far apart, energy is defined as zero. As the atoms approach, energy falls as each nucleus starts to attract the other atom's electron. The curve reaches a minimum — the most stable point — at a separation of 75 pm, which defines the H–H bond length; pushing the atoms closer than this causes energy to rise steeply again as the two positive nuclei begin to repel each other directly. The covalent bond is therefore the distance at which nuclear attraction to the shared electrons is exactly balanced by internuclear repulsion.
Octet rule (definition)The octet rule is the tendency of atoms to gain a valence shell containing a total of eight electrons (through a combination of their own and shared electrons), matching the stable electron arrangement of a noble gas. Hydrogen is a built-in exception, since its single shell holds a maximum of only two electrons (a "duet").

Two chlorine atoms illustrate the rule well: each starts with seven valence electrons, and by sharing one pair, each ends up with a share of eight electrons overall — one bonding pair plus three unshared non-bonding (lone) pairs. Lone pairs take no part in the bond itself but, as this sub-topic will show, strongly influence the shape of more complex molecules. The octet rule also explains the unreactivity of the noble gases: apart from helium, every noble gas atom already has a complete octet of its own electrons, so there is no driving force to share electrons and form covalent bonds.

Why covalent bonds form between similar atoms but ionic bonds do notTwo atoms of the same or similar element attract valence electrons with very similar strength, so neither can pull an electron completely away from the other — sharing is the only option. Ionic bonding, by contrast, requires a large difference in electron-attracting power between a metal and a non-metal so that electrons can be transferred completely rather than shared.
Nature of science — limitations of the octet ruleScientific rules are descriptive generalizations that work most of the time, not universal laws, so knowing the exceptions matters as much as knowing the rule. Recognized exceptions include: hydrogen (2-electron duet); small central atoms such as beryllium and boron that form an incomplete octet; some larger central atoms (period 3 and below) that form an expanded octet of more than eight electrons, using accessible d-orbitals (covered later in this sub-topic); some transition elements; and free radicals (species with an unpaired, odd electron).

Drawing Lewis Formulas

A Lewis formula shows every valence electron — bonding and non-bonding — in a covalently bonded species. Electron pairs can be shown as two dots, two crosses, a mix of both, or a single dash for a bonding pair.

Step 1: total valence electrons = Σ (valence electrons per element × number of atoms of that element)
Step 2: draw the skeletal structure, joining the central atom to each outer atom with one shared pair
Step 3: complete the octet on the outer atoms first, then the central atom, using leftover electrons (forming double/triple bonds if there are not enough electrons for single bonds alone)
Step 4: check the total electrons drawn equals the number calculated in Step 1
Worked exampleDraw the Lewis formula of CCl4.
Step 1: valence electrons = (4 × 1) for C + (7 × 4) for the four Cl = 4 + 28 = 32.
Step 2: carbon sits at the centre, singly bonded to each of four chlorines (4 bonding pairs = 8 electrons so far).
Step 3: each chlorine already has one bonding pair; add three lone pairs to each Cl to complete its octet (4 × 3 × 2 = 24 electrons). Carbon ends with four bonding pairs and no lone pairs.
Step 4: total = 8 + 24 = 32, matching Step 1. Confirmed correct.

Lewis formulas of ions

For an ion, calculate valence electrons exactly as for a molecule, then add one electron per unit of negative charge or subtract one per unit of positive charge. Enclose the finished structure in square brackets with the overall charge shown outside.

Worked exampleDraw the Lewis formula of OH−.
Valence electrons = 6 (O) + 1 (H) + 1 (for the 1− charge) = 8. Skeleton O–H uses one bonding pair (2 electrons); the remaining 6 electrons form three lone pairs on oxygen. Oxygen ends with a complete octet (1 bonding pair + 3 lone pairs = 8 electrons) while hydrogen keeps its 2-electron duet; the whole structure sits inside square brackets with a 1− charge shown outside.

The incomplete octet

Small central atoms with very few valence electrons — especially beryllium and boron — are too small to form enough bonds to complete a full octet. BeCl2 (2 + 7 × 2 = 16 valence electrons) places only 4 electrons (2 bonding pairs) around Be, with no lone pairs; BF3 (3 + 7 × 3 = 24 valence electrons) places only 6 electrons (3 bonding pairs) around B. Common elements that form an incomplete octet are hydrogen, beryllium, boron, and aluminium.

Single, Double and Triple Covalent Bonds

When single bonds cannot supply enough electrons to complete every atom's octet, atoms share more than one electron pair, forming a multiple bond: a double bond shares two pairs (four electrons); a triple bond shares three pairs (six electrons).

Worked exampleDraw the Lewis formula of O2.
Valence electrons = 6 × 2 = 12. A single O–O bond plus three lone pairs on each O would use all 12 electrons but leave each oxygen with only 6 electrons — short of an octet. Converting one lone pair from each oxygen into a second shared pair (forming a double bond) gives each oxygen one double bond (4 electrons shared) plus two lone pairs (4 electrons) = 8 electrons each. Total electrons shown = 12, matching the calculation.

Nitrogen, N2, needs a triple bond to satisfy both octets: each N ends with one lone pair plus a share in three bonding pairs (2 + 6 = 8 electrons). This triple bond is extremely difficult to break, explaining why atmospheric N2, despite making up about 78% of the air, is so unreactive that living organisms rarely use it directly.

Bond length and bond strength

Key relationshipFor bonds between the same pair of elements, as the number of shared pairs increases: bond length decreases and bond strength (bond enthalpy) increases. Triple < double < single in length; triple > double > single in strength — but a double bond is not exactly twice as strong as a single bond, since it is made of two different types of bond (one sigma, one pi — see later in this sub-topic), the pi component being weaker.

The same pattern appears for the two different carbon–oxygen bonds within one molecule of ethanoic acid, CH3COOH: the C–O single bond (143 pm, 358 kJ mol−1) is longer and weaker than the C=O double bond (122 pm, 804 kJ mol−1). Down group 17 (F–F, Cl–Cl, Br–Br, I–I), bonds generally lengthen and weaken as atomic radius increases, placing the shared pair further from both nuclei. F2 is a notable outlier, weaker than expected, because its lone pairs are forced unusually close together by fluorine's small size, causing strong lone pair–lone pair repulsion.

Coordination (Dative) Bonds

Coordination bond (definition)A coordination bond (dative covalent bond) is a covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair originate from the same atom, rather than one from each. The donor atom supplies both electrons; the acceptor atom receives a share in them.

An arrow drawn along the bond axis, pointing from donor to acceptor, is sometimes used to show a coordination bond. Crucially, once formed, a coordination bond is completely indistinguishable from any other covalent bond of the same type — identical in length and strength. The distinction matters only for explaining how the bond originated.

Coordination bonding in H3O+ and NH4+In H3O+: a water molecule donates one of its lone pairs to an incoming H+ ion (a bare proton with no electrons), forming a new O–H bond drawn with an arrow from oxygen's lone pair to the new hydrogen. The resulting ion has oxygen bonded to three hydrogens with one lone pair remaining, overall charge 1+. In NH4+: ammonia donates its lone pair to an incoming H+, forming a fourth N–H bond; nitrogen ends bonded to four hydrogens with no lone pairs, overall charge 1+. In both ions, once formed, every N–H or O–H bond is identical — there is no way to identify which one was originally coordinate.
Coordination bonding in carbon monoxide, COCO has a triple bond between C and O. Two of the three shared pairs form in the ordinary way (one electron from each atom); the third is a coordination bond, with both electrons donated by oxygen (which has a lone pair to spare), shown with an arrow from O to C. This coordination bond lets carbon — with only 4 valence electrons of its own — complete a full octet (1 lone pair + 3 shared pairs = 8 electrons). Oxygen also ends with 1 lone pair + 3 shared pairs = 8 electrons. All three C–O bonds in the finished molecule are identical.

Coordination bonds also explain how stable molecules combine: ammonia's nitrogen lone pair can be donated into boron's empty space in BF3 (which has only an incomplete octet of 6 electrons), giving both atoms a complete octet in the product H3N→BF3. Similarly, gaseous AlCl3 exists as dimers, Al2Cl6, in which two chlorine atoms each donate a lone pair to the aluminium of the neighbouring molecule, bridging the two halves and giving every atom a complete octet.

The VSEPR Model: Electron Domains

Once a molecule's Lewis formula is known, the Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) model predicts its three-dimensional shape from the simple idea that electron pairs in the valence shell carry the same charge, repel each other, and so spread out as far apart as possible.

Electron domain"Electron pair" is a simplification: a double or triple bond behaves as a single repelling unit, since all its bonding pairs point the same way. The better term is electron domain: any region of electron density around a central atom — a single bond, a double bond, a triple bond, or a lone pair. It is the total number of electron domains, not the total number of electron pairs, that fixes the geometry.
Worked exampleHow many electron domains surround the central atom in (a) NH3, (b) CO2, (c) O3 (ozone)?
(a) 3 N–H single bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 domains (3 bonding, 1 non-bonding).
(b) 2 C=O double bonds, each counted as one domain, + 0 lone pairs = 2 domains (2 bonding, 0 non-bonding).
(c) 1 O–O single bond + 1 O=O double bond + 1 lone pair = 3 domains (2 bonding, 1 non-bonding).
The VSEPR model, summarized• Electron domains repel and spread as far apart as geometrically possible.
• The total number of domains fixes the electron domain geometry.
• The molecular geometry (the named shape) depends only on the positions of the bonded atoms — a lone pair is invisible in the final shape, though it still occupies space and pushes the other domains around.
• Lone pairs and multiple bonds cause slightly more repulsion than an ordinary single bonding pair (lone pairs are not shared between nuclei; multiple bonds contain extra pairs), giving the order: non-bonding pair > multiple bond > single bond.

Because of this order, a lone pair or multiple bond on the central atom compresses bond angles slightly below the "ideal" value predicted from domain count alone. You are not expected to memorize the exact size of each distortion, only to recognize when an angle should be slightly larger or smaller than the basic prediction.

VSEPR Shapes: Two and Three Electron Domains

Two domains — linear

Two electron domains minimize repulsion at 180° to each other, giving a linear shape.

Linear molecules: BeCl2, CO2, C2H2BeCl2: Cl–Be–Cl in one straight line, 180°. CO2: O=C=O in one straight line, 180°. Ethyne, C2H2: all four atoms, H–C≡C–H, lie on one straight line, each H–C–C angle 180°.

Three domains — trigonal planar (all bonding) or bent (with a lone pair)

Three domains spread at roughly 120° in one plane — trigonal planar electron domain geometry. With all three bonding, the molecular shape is also trigonal planar.

Trigonal planar molecules: BH3, AlCl3, methanal HCOHBH3: three H atoms spread symmetrically around B in one plane, each H–B–H angle exactly 120°. AlCl3: three Cl atoms spread symmetrically around Al, each Cl–Al–Cl angle exactly 120°. Methanal, HCOH: central C double-bonded to O and singly bonded to two H, all in one plane; the C=O double bond's extra repulsion opens the O=C–H angle to slightly more than 120° while closing the H–C–H angle to slightly less than 120°.

If one of three domains is a lone pair, the electron domain geometry stays trigonal planar but the molecular geometry becomes bent (V-shaped), with the angle compressed below 120°.

Ozone, O3 — bent from three domainsCentral O has three domains: one O–O single bond, one O=O double bond, one lone pair, in a rough triangle. Because one position is a lone pair (not an atom), the shape traced by the three atoms is bent, with the O–O–O angle compressed to about 117°, below 120°, by the extra repulsion of the lone pair and the double bond.

VSEPR Shapes: Four Electron Domains

Four domains point towards the four corners of a regular tetrahedron, each separated by about 109.5° — the tetrahedral electron domain geometry. With all four bonding, the molecular geometry is also tetrahedral.

Step-by-step procedure for any molecule1. Draw the Lewis formula.
2. Count electron domains on the central atom (each multiple bond = one domain; plus lone pairs).
3. Domain count → electron domain geometry (2→linear, 3→trigonal planar, 4→tetrahedral, and — covered later in this sub-topic — 5→trigonal bipyramidal, 6→octahedral).
4. Use only the bonded domains to name the molecular geometry.
5. Adjust the angle downward slightly wherever a lone pair or multiple bond is present.
Nature of science — how useful is VSEPR?VSEPR reliably predicts the correct general shape and approximate bond angle of almost any simple molecule from nothing more than a Lewis formula, remarkable given how simple its core assumption is. Its main limitation is that it explains bond-angle distortions only qualitatively (larger/smaller than ideal); explaining their exact size, and explaining why the angles take the values they do at all, requires the orbital-level models (hybridization) covered later in this sub-topic.

Bond Polarity and Electronegativity

Not every covalent bond shares electrons equally. When the bonded atoms differ in electronegativity, the shared pair spends more time nearer the more electronegative atom.

Electronegativity (definition)Electronegativity is a measure of an atom's ability to attract a shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond, expressed on the dimensionless Pauling scale (about 0.7 to 4.0). It increases across a period and decreases down a group, so it increases towards fluorine, the most electronegative element.

When bonded atoms differ in electronegativity, the bond has a bond dipole: the more electronegative atom becomes partially negative, δ−, and the less electronegative atom partially positive, δ+. This is shown either by writing δ+/δ− over the atoms, or by a small arrow along the bond pointing towards the more electronegative atom (a vector).

ΔEN = |EN of more electronegative atom − EN of less electronegative atom|

As a rough guideline (there is no sharp official cut-off, since electronegativity difference is a continuous spectrum): ΔEN below about 0.5 is non-polar (pure) covalent; roughly 0.5–1.8 is polar covalent; above about 1.8 is predominantly ionic.

Worked exampleClassify H–F (EN H = 2.2, EN F = 4.0), H–Cl (EN Cl = 3.2), and Na–Cl (EN Na = 0.9).
H–F: ΔEN = |4.0 − 2.2| = 1.8 — right at the polar/ionic boundary, classified as strongly polar covalent.
H–Cl: ΔEN = |3.2 − 2.2| = 1.0, comfortably polar covalent, less polar than H–F.
Na–Cl: ΔEN = |3.2 − 0.9| = 2.3, above 1.8, classified as ionic.

The only truly non-polar bonds (ΔEN = 0) are between two atoms of the same element, as in F2, H2, O2, Cl2 — sometimes called pure covalent. Every other bond has some polarity, even if slight: the C–H bond, universal in organic chemistry, is treated as essentially non-polar even though carbon (EN 2.6) is slightly more electronegative than hydrogen (EN 2.2).

The bonding continuumPolar covalent bonds sit between pure covalent (ΔEN = 0) and pure ionic (very large ΔEN) bonding. The more polar a covalent bond, the more ionic character it has — which is why HCl, a covalent molecule in the gas phase, ionizes completely in water to give H+ and Cl− and conducts electricity in that state. There is no sharp dividing line between "covalent" and "ionic"; chemists describe bonding as "predominantly" one or the other.

Molecular Polarity

Whether the whole molecule is polar depends on two things together: (1) which polar bonds it contains, and (2) how those bonds are arranged in space (the molecular geometry).

The cancellation principleIf a molecule's bond dipoles are all equal (same elements) and symmetrically arranged, they point outward in exactly opposing directions and cancel — the molecule is non-polar despite containing polar bonds. If the dipoles are unequal or asymmetrically arranged, they do not fully cancel, leaving a net dipole, and the molecule is polar.
Non-polar despite polar bonds: CO2, BF3, CCl4CO2: two identical C=O dipoles point outward from carbon 180° apart, cancelling exactly. BF3: three identical B–F dipoles point outward at 120°; by vector addition, three equal arrows spaced evenly around a point sum to zero. CCl4: four identical C–Cl dipoles point towards the four tetrahedral corners, again cancelling by symmetry.
Polar molecules: CH3Cl, NH3, H2OCH3Cl: the single, strongly polar C–Cl dipole has no equal and opposite partner (the other three bonds are weakly polar C–H), leaving a clear net dipole towards Cl. NH3: the three N–H dipoles point somewhat towards N, but the trigonal pyramidal shape (not a flat triangle, due to the lone pair) means they do not cancel, leaving a net dipole pointing up towards the lone pair. H2O: the two O–H dipoles point towards O, but the bent shape means they cannot point in exactly opposite directions, leaving a substantial net dipole towards O.
Worked comparison: cis- and trans-1,2-dichloroetheneBoth isomers have two identical C–Cl bonds. In trans, the two Cl atoms sit on opposite sides of the C=C bond, so their dipoles point in nearly opposite directions and largely cancel — non-polar (or only weakly polar). In cis, both Cl atoms are on the same side, so the dipoles reinforce rather than cancel — a clear net dipole, polar. Geometry, not just the presence of polar bonds, decides overall polarity.

Covalent Network Structures: Carbon and Silicon

Most covalent substances form small, discrete molecules. A few elements instead form a covalent network structure (giant molecular/macromolecular structure): a crystalline lattice where covalent bonding repeats indefinitely, so the whole crystal is effectively one enormous molecule.

Silicon and silicon dioxide

Silicon, directly below carbon in group 14, forms an analogous giant lattice: each Si bonded tetrahedrally to four others. Silicon dioxide, SiO2 (silica/quartz), alternates Si and O: each Si bonded to four O, each O bridging two Si.

The structure of quartz, SiO2A 3-D lattice where each silicon sits at the centre of a tetrahedron of four oxygens (109.5°), each oxygen bridging to a second silicon, linking tetrahedra into one continuous network. SiO2 is only an empirical (ratio) formula — the real structure is one enormous lattice, not a finite molecule.
Why carbon forms C–C chains but silicon bonds mainly to oxygenCarbon's smaller atomic radius gives the C–C bond a much higher bond enthalpy (≈346 kJ mol−1) than the Si–Si bond (≈226 kJ mol−1). This ability of carbon to form strong, stable chains and rings with itself (catenation) underlies all of organic chemistry. Silicon, in contrast, forms an especially strong Si–O bond (≈466 kJ mol−1), so silicon is found mainly in silicate minerals rather than long silicon chains.

Pure silicon is a poor conductor at low temperature, but conductivity rises sharply with temperature as thermal energy frees electrons from the lattice (leaving a mobile positive "hole"); deliberately adding trace impurities (doping, e.g. with arsenic or boron) enhances this further, the basis of the modern electronics industry.

Intermolecular Forces

Covalent bonds hold atoms within a molecule together; molecules also attract each other through weaker intermolecular forces (inter- = between; intra- = within). The type and strength present depends on the size and polarity of the molecules, and largely determines a covalent substance's physical properties.

Key relative strength orderingFor molecules of comparable molar mass: London (dispersion) forces < dipole–dipole forces < hydrogen bonding. All intermolecular forces are considerably weaker (roughly 15–20 times) than covalent or ionic bonds.

London (dispersion) forces

Non-polar molecules, such as Cl2, have no permanent dipole, since electrons are pulled equally by both atoms. But electrons form a constantly shifting cloud, and at any instant may be denser over one end than the other — a brief instantaneous (temporary) dipole.

London dispersion forces between two Cl2 moleculesTwo Cl2 molecules, electron clouds evenly spread, no dipole. At some instant, the left molecule's cloud shifts denser onto its right chlorine, giving it an instantaneous dipole (δ− right, δ+ left). This repels the nearby right-hand molecule's cloud, pushing its density to its far side and inducing a matching dipole. The attraction between the δ− end of the left molecule and the induced δ+ end of the right molecule is a London dispersion force; these instantaneous dipoles appear and disappear continuously, in every orientation, throughout a sample.

London forces are the weakest intermolecular force but the only one between non-polar molecules, and are present to some degree between every pair of molecules. Strength increases with molecular size (more electrons → larger, more easily distorted cloud): F2 (−188°C), Cl2 (−34°C), Br2 (59°C), I2 (185°C) — boiling points rising steadily down group 17 with increasing Mr. The same trend appears in the alkanes.

Dipole–dipole attraction

Polar molecules such as HCl have a permanent dipole. Neighbouring polar molecules line up so the δ+ end of one attracts the δ− end of the next: dipole–dipole attraction. Because these dipoles are permanent, this force is consistently stronger than London forces: propane (M = 44, non-polar) boils at 229 K, while methoxymethane (M = 46, polar) boils higher, at 249 K, despite near-identical molar mass.

Dipole–induced dipole attraction

In a mixture of polar and non-polar molecules, a polar molecule's permanent dipole can temporarily distort the electron cloud of a nearby non-polar molecule, inducing a dipole in it — a weaker attraction than ordinary dipole–dipole.

Van der Waals' forces (umbrella term)"Van der Waals' forces" covers London dispersion, dipole–induced dipole, and dipole–dipole forces together — any intermolecular attraction that does not involve full ionic charge or an actual covalent/hydrogen bond.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bond (definition)A hydrogen bond is a particularly strong dipole–dipole attraction that occurs when hydrogen, covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (only F, O, or N), is also attracted to a lone pair on an F, O, or N atom of a neighbouring molecule. Hydrogen's small size and lack of other shielding electrons leave it with an unusually concentrated, exposed partial positive charge.

Comparing hydrides down groups 14–17, boiling point rises with Mr, but NH3, HF, and H2O are striking anomalies with far higher boiling points than their mass predicts — explained by hydrogen bonding. Isomers CH3OCH3 (no O–H, cannot H-bond, boils at −23°C) and CH3CH2OH (has O–H, H-bonds, boils at +79°C) have identical M = 46 g mol−1, isolating the effect of hydrogen bonding.

Why ice floatsEach water molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds (two as donor via its hydrogens, two as acceptor via its lone pairs). In liquid water, fewer than four form on average as molecules tumble past each other; in ice, every molecule is locked into the maximum of four, holding molecules a fixed distance apart in a rigid, open tetrahedral lattice with empty space at its centre. This open lattice is less densely packed than the liquid, so ice floats — the opposite of almost every other substance.

Physical Properties from Intermolecular Forces

Knowing which intermolecular force(s) act lets us predict a substance's physical properties.

Worked exampleRank CH3CH2CH3 (propane, M=44), CH3CHO (ethanal, M=44), CH3CH2OH (ethanol, M=46) by increasing boiling point.
Molar masses are nearly identical, so London forces are similar for all three. Propane is non-polar (London only); ethanal has a polar C=O bond (London + dipole–dipole); ethanol has an O–H bond (London + hydrogen bonding). Applying London < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding: CH3CH2CH3 < CH3CHO < CH3CH2OH.

Giant covalent structures behave very differently: changing state breaks actual covalent bonds throughout the lattice, giving extremely high melting/boiling points (diamond remains solid until roughly 4000°C). Volatility (tendency to vaporize) falls as intermolecular forces strengthen.

Solubility follows "like dissolves like": non-polar solutes dissolve in non-polar solvents (London forces only); polar/ionic solutes dissolve in polar solvents such as water (dipole–dipole/hydrogen bonding), though this weakens in larger molecules where the polar bond is a small part of a mostly non-polar structure. Giant covalent substances are essentially insoluble in any solvent.

Electrical conductivity: covalent substances contain no free ions and generally cannot conduct as solids or pure liquids, unless they ionize in solution (HCl in water conducts because it splits into H+ and Cl−). Among giant structures, graphite and graphene conduct well (mobile delocalized electrons); silicon and the fullerenes show weak semiconductor behaviour; diamond does not conduct at all.

Chromatography and Rf Values

Chromatography separates mixture components based on their relative attraction (through intermolecular forces) to a stationary phase and a mobile phase. Stronger attraction to the stationary phase means slower movement; stronger attraction to the mobile phase means faster movement.

In paper chromatography, the stationary phase is chromatography paper, which holds roughly 10% water via hydrogen bonds to the –OH groups of its cellulose. A solvent (mobile phase) rises by capillary action, carrying sample components at different rates. A small sample is spotted at the origin (marked in pencil); the solvent eventually reaches the solvent front.

Rf = distance moved by the component ÷ distance moved by the solvent

Both distances are measured from the origin. Rf is always between 0 and 1, with no units, and is characteristic of a compound under fixed conditions (same solvent, paper, temperature), allowing comparison with reference data.

Worked exampleOrigin-to-solvent-front distance = 8.0 cm; a spot has travelled 6.0 cm. Rf = 6.0 ÷ 8.0 = 0.75.

Thin layer chromatography (TLC) uses the same principle but with a thin layer of silica or alumina (both containing polar –OH groups that hydrogen bond with sample components) coated on a rigid backing.

HL: Resonance Structures

Covalent bonds are not always confined to one fixed position. In some species, bonding electrons are shared between more than one possible bonding position in a process called delocalization, which gives extra stability to the molecule or ion.

Resonance structures (definition)Resonance structures occur when more than one valid Lewis formula can be drawn for a species, differing only in the position of a double bond (the skeletal arrangement of atoms stays the same). The true structure is a blend, or resonance hybrid, of all the contributing structures, with the delocalized electrons spread equally between the possible bonding positions.

Ozone, O3, is the classic example: one valid Lewis formula places the double bond on the left O–O, another places it on the right. Experimental data show ozone's two O–O bonds are actually identical, with length and strength intermediate between a single and a double bond — direct evidence that the true structure is the resonance hybrid of both contributing structures, not a flickering alternation between them.

Resonance is not rapid flippingResonance structures are a notation problem, not a physical one: the molecule does not switch back and forth between two structures. (Analogy: describing a "zorse," a cross between a zebra and a horse, by drawing a zebra and a horse with a double-headed arrow between them does not mean the animal alternates between being a zebra and a horse one minute to the next — it is a genuine, fixed hybrid.) The number of resonance structures for a species equals the number of different possible positions for its double bond(s).
Worked exampleDraw the resonance structures of the carbonate ion, CO32−.
Valence electrons = 4 + (6 × 3) + 2 = 24. The Lewis formula has a central carbon with one C=O double bond and two C–O single bonds (each singly bonded O carrying 3 lone pairs, the doubly bonded O carrying 2 lone pairs). Because the double bond could equally be placed on any of the three oxygens, there are three equivalent resonance structures; the true structure is a hybrid in which all three C–O bonds are identical, each of length and strength intermediate between a single and a double bond.

Other common examples with delocalized electrons include the nitrate ion, NO3− (3 equivalent resonance structures, like carbonate), the nitrite ion, NO2− (2 equivalent structures), and the methanoate ion, HCOO− (2 equivalent structures, giving two identical, intermediate C–O bonds — unlike methanoic acid, HCOOH, which has only one possible Lewis formula and so one distinct C=O and one distinct C–O bond, with no resonance). The same reasoning explains why the ethanoate ion, CH3COO−, has two equal, intermediate carbon–oxygen bonds, while ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, with only one possible Lewis formula, has one distinct C=O and one distinct C–O bond.

Resonance also explains physical observations beyond bond length and strength: ozone, O3, with its delocalized, intermediate-strength O–O bonds, is dissociated by longer-wavelength (lower energy) UV light (<330 nm) than molecular oxygen, O2, with its full, undelocalized double bond (<242 nm) — ozone's bonds are, on average, easier to break than a true double bond.

HL: Benzene — A Classic Case of Resonance

Benzene, C6H6, has six carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal ring, each also bonded to one hydrogen, in a trigonal planar arrangement with 120° bond angles. A simple Lewis formula alternates single and double C–C bonds around the ring (the 1865 structure proposed by August Kekulé, 1,3,5-cyclohexatriene) — but because the double bonds could equally be placed in the opposite alternating pattern, two equivalent resonance structures exist, and the true structure is their hybrid: a ring of six identical, intermediate carbon–carbon bonds, usually drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside it to represent the delocalized electrons.

Benzene does not behave like a typical unsaturated moleculeBenzene's 1:1 carbon-to-hydrogen ratio suggests high unsaturation, yet it shows none of the reactivity expected of a triply unsaturated ring with alternating single and double bonds: it has no isomers (as the symmetrical Kekulé structure predicted), and it strongly resists the addition reactions that typical alkenes undergo readily, favouring substitution reactions instead.

Four lines of evidence confirm the delocalized (resonance hybrid) model over the simple alternating-bond Kekulé model:

As X-ray diffraction and calorimetry provided more precise quantitative data, the model was revised to the modern delocalized resonance hybrid — a clear example of science progressing by refining or abandoning a model when new data demand it.

HL: Molecules with an Expanded Octet

When the central atom is from period 3 or below, its valence shell has accessible d-orbitals at an energy close to that of the p-orbitals. Promoting an electron (for example, from 3p to an empty 3d orbital) allows additional electron pairs to form, giving a central atom more than eight electrons — an expanded octet, with five or six electron domains.

Five electron domains — trigonal bipyramidal

Five domains position themselves in a trigonal bipyramidal arrangement, with bond angles of 90° and 120°. With all five bonding (e.g. PCl5), the molecular shape is also trigonal bipyramidal. Because lone pairs cause more repulsion, they always occupy the less crowded equatorial positions (in the 120° plane) rather than the more crowded axial positions.

Valence electrons = 5 + (7 × 6) + 1 = 48. Six bonding domains give an octahedral molecular geometry with all F–P–F bond angles equal to 90°.

Molecular polarity with five and six domains

The same cancellation principle applies: PCl5 and SF6 are non-polar, since all attached atoms are identical and there are no lone pairs on the central atom to break the symmetry. If the attached atoms differ, polarity depends on symmetry: SBrF4 has a net dipole (polar), while PCl3F2, with the two different atom types arranged symmetrically, can have its dipoles cancel (non-polar). Lone pairs can also break or preserve symmetry: ClF3 is polar (its T-shape is asymmetric), whereas XeF4 is non-polar (its two lone pairs sit on exactly opposite sides, preserving the square planar symmetry, so all four Xe–F dipoles still cancel).

HL: Formal Charge

Following the standard rules for drawing Lewis formulas can sometimes produce more than one plausible structure for the same skeletal arrangement of atoms — especially for species that can expand their octet. Sulfur dioxide, SO2, is a classic case: one structure has a single S–O bond and one S=O double bond (with sulfur's octet unexpanded, at 8 electrons); another has two S=O double bonds (with sulfur's octet expanded to 10 electrons). These are not resonance structures of each other, since they contain different numbers of bonds — so a tool other than resonance is needed to judge which is the more stable, preferred structure.

Formal charge (definition)Formal charge (FC) is a bookkeeping value assigned to each atom in a Lewis formula, calculated by pretending every bond is purely covalent (ignoring any real electronegativity difference) and splitting every bonding pair exactly equally between the two bonded atoms.
FC = V − (S + L)

Plain-language explanation: V is the number of valence electrons the free, unbonded atom would normally have (from its group number). L is the number of non-bonding (lone pair) electrons the atom owns outright in the structure. S is the number of covalent bonds (shared electron pairs) that atom forms — counted once per bond, since formal charge gives the atom credit for only half of each shared pair (one electron per bond). Low formal charges (close to zero) mean little artificial "charge transfer" has been imposed by the drawing, which usually corresponds to the most stable, preferred Lewis structure.

Worked example — SO2Structure A (one S–O single bond, one S=O double bond, 1 lone pair on S): S has 3 bonds and 1 lone pair (2 electrons), FC(S) = 6 − (3 + 2) = +1. The double-bonded O has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs (4 electrons), FC = 6 − (2 + 4) = 0. The single-bonded O has 1 bond and 3 lone pairs (6 electrons), FC = 6 − (1 + 6) = −1. Formal charges: S = +1, one O = 0, other O = −1.
Structure B (two S=O double bonds, 1 lone pair on S, expanded octet of 10 electrons on S): S has 4 bonds and 1 lone pair, FC(S) = 6 − (4 + 2) = 0. Each double-bonded O has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs, FC = 6 − (2 + 4) = 0 for both oxygens.
Structure B, with a formal charge of zero on every atom, is the preferred Lewis structure for SO2.
Worked example — XeO3Structure A (one Xe=O double bond, two Xe–O single bonds, 1 lone pair on Xe): FC(Xe) = 8 − (4 + 2) = +2; the double-bonded O: FC = 6 − (2 + 4) = 0; each single-bonded O: FC = 6 − (1 + 6) = −1. Sum = +2 + 0 − 1 − 1 = 0, consistent with neutral XeO3, but the charges are large.
Structure B (three Xe=O double bonds, 1 lone pair on Xe, an expanded octet of 14 electrons on Xe): FC(Xe) = 8 − (6 + 2) = 0; each double-bonded O: FC = 6 − (2 + 4) = 0. All formal charges are zero.
Structure B, with every formal charge equal to zero, is strongly preferred over structure A.

Note that the formal charges in any single structure must always add up to the overall charge on the species (zero for a neutral molecule, or the ionic charge for an ion) — this is a useful check on your arithmetic.

Rules for choosing between non-equivalent Lewis structuresBecause formal charge ignores real electronegativity differences, it is a useful but imperfect tool. The preferred structure is generally the one with: (1) the lowest formal charges overall (closest to zero), and (2) any negative formal charge placed on the more electronegative atom, if more than one structure ties on rule (1).
Worked example — nitrous oxide, N2OStructure (i), N≡N–O (a dative/coordinate N–O bond): the terminal N (triple bond, 1 lone pair) has FC = 5 − (3 + 2) = 0; the central N (triple bond to the other N plus a single bond to O = 4 bonds, no lone pair) has FC = 5 − (4 + 0) = +1; O (single bond, 3 lone pairs) has FC = 6 − (1 + 6) = −1.
Structure (ii), N=N=O: the terminal N (double bond, 2 lone pairs) has FC = 5 − (2 + 4) = −1; the central N (two double bonds = 4 bonds, no lone pair) has FC = 5 − (4 + 0) = +1; O (double bond, 2 lone pairs) has FC = 6 − (2 + 4) = 0.
Both structures have the same spread of formal charges (0, +1, −1, just on different atoms), so rule (1) alone cannot distinguish them. Applying rule (2): structure (i) places the negative formal charge on oxygen, the more electronegative atom, so structure (i) is the preferred Lewis formula for N2O.
Formal charge explains why BF3 keeps an incomplete octetYou might expect boron's incomplete octet in BF3 (only 6 electrons around B) to be "fixed" by converting one B–F single bond into a B=F double bond, giving boron a full octet. But doing so gives boron a formal charge of −1 and that fluorine a formal charge of +1 — placing a positive formal charge on fluorine, the most electronegative element of all, which is highly unfavourable. The structure with an incomplete octet on boron but a formal charge of zero on every atom is therefore still the preferred Lewis formula for BF3, even though it does not satisfy the octet rule.
Formal charge vs oxidation numberFormal charge and oxidation number are opposite modelling assumptions for the same problem of assigning charge within a structure. Formal charge treats every bond as purely covalent, splitting each shared pair exactly equally. Oxidation number treats every bond as purely ionic, assigning both electrons of a shared pair entirely to the more electronegative atom. Neither one perfectly describes the real charge on an atom, but each is a useful simplification in its own context — an example of scientists applying Occam's razor, preferring whichever simple model has enough explanatory power for the task at hand.

HL: Sigma and Pi Bonds

The VSEPR model predicts angles such as 109.5° and 120°, but atomic orbitals are not naturally arranged this way: s orbitals are spherical, and p orbitals are dumbbell-shaped, oriented at 90° to each other. Explaining how these orbitals combine to produce molecules with the angles VSEPR predicts requires looking at covalent bonds at the level of overlapping atomic orbitals.

In simple valence bond terms, a covalent bond forms when two atomic orbitals, each containing one electron, combine into a new, lower-energy molecular orbital. There are two possible ways the orbitals can overlap, giving two distinct types of covalent bond.

Sigma (σ) bond (definition)A sigma bond forms by the head-on (end-on) overlap of two atomic orbitals along the bond axis — the imaginary line directly connecting the two nuclei — concentrating electron density directly between the two nuclei. Sigma bonds can form from the overlap of s orbitals, p orbitals (end-on), or hybrid orbitals (see the next section) in any combination. Every single covalent bond is a sigma bond, and the first bond in any double or triple bond is always a sigma bond.
Formation of sigma bonds(a) Two s orbitals (spherical clouds) overlapping head-on, as in H + H → H2: a single region of overlapping electron density lies directly on the line between the two nuclei. (b) Two p orbitals (dumbbell-shaped) overlapping end-on along their shared axis, as in F + F → F2. (c) An s orbital and a p orbital overlapping end-on, as in H + F → HF. In every case, the region of greatest electron density lies directly along the bond axis, between the two nuclei.
Pi (π) bond (definition)A pi bond forms by the lateral (sideways-on) overlap of two parallel p orbitals, concentrating electron density into two separate regions, above and below (or in front of and behind) the bond axis, rather than directly on it. A pi bond only forms alongside an existing sigma bond between the same two atoms — in other words, pi bonds exist only within double or triple bonds, never on their own.
Formation of a pi bondTwo p orbitals on neighbouring atoms, both oriented perpendicular to the bond axis and parallel to each other, overlap sideways rather than end-on. The resulting molecular orbital has two lobes of electron density, one above the bond axis and one below it (or one in front and one behind, depending on orientation) — two distinct regions, but together representing only one bond, holding only one pair of electrons, which can be found anywhere within either lobe.
Counting sigma and pi bonds in multiple bondsA single bond = 1 sigma bond.
A double bond = 1 sigma bond + 1 pi bond.
A triple bond = 1 sigma bond + 2 pi bonds (the two pi bonds lie in two different planes, both containing the bond axis, perpendicular to each other).

Pi bonds are weaker than sigma bonds, since their electron density lies further from the direct line between the nuclei and so is less strongly attracted to both nuclei at once; this is why a double bond is not exactly twice as strong as a single bond — it is made of one strong sigma bond plus one weaker pi bond. Pi electrons, being more loosely held, also break more easily during chemical reactions, which is why molecules containing C=C double bonds are generally more reactive than those with only single bonds.

Carbon monoxide, CO, is a useful inorganic example: its triple bond consists of one sigma bond and two pi bonds, exactly like any other triple bond, even though one of the three shared pairs is formed as a coordination bond (both electrons donated by oxygen) rather than in the usual way — the type of orbital overlap (and therefore whether a shared pair counts towards a sigma or a pi bond) is completely independent of how the electrons were originally contributed.

HL: Hybridization

Carbon's ground-state electron configuration, 1s22s22px12py1, has only two singly occupied orbitals — yet carbon consistently forms four covalent bonds in its compounds. This mismatch is resolved by two linked ideas: excitation and hybridization.

Excitation

Excitation (in this context)Excitation is the promotion of one electron from a filled 2s orbital into an empty 2p orbital, which costs a small amount of energy but creates four singly occupied orbitals (one 2s, three 2p) available for bonding, instead of only two. The energy cost of excitation is more than repaid by the extra energy released when four bonds form instead of two.
Excitation of carbonGround-state carbon: the 2s orbital shown fully occupied by a pair of electrons (arrows pointing in opposite directions), and two of the three 2p orbitals each occupied by a single electron (arrows pointing the same direction, per Hund's rule), with one 2p orbital empty. Excited-state carbon: one electron from the 2s orbital has been promoted into the previously empty 2p orbital, leaving all four orbitals (one 2s, three 2p) singly occupied, each available to form a bond.

Hybridization itself

Even after excitation, the four available orbitals are still not identical: one 2s orbital (lower energy, spherical) and three 2p orbitals (slightly higher energy, dumbbell-shaped, at 90° to each other). If bonds formed directly from these unequal, oddly-angled orbitals, methane's four C–H bonds would not be identical — but experimentally they are. The resolution is that the orbitals mix together to form a new set of orbitals, identical to each other, with properties intermediate between their parent orbitals.

Hybridization (definition)Hybridization is the mixing of a set of atomic orbitals within one atom to produce an equal number of new hybrid orbitals, equal to one another in energy and shape, but different from (and intermediate between) the original atomic orbitals. Hybrid orbitals allow for greater overlap with a neighbouring atom's orbital, forming stronger sigma bonds than the unhybridized parent orbitals could.
A useful analogyMixing one bucket of white paint with three buckets of red paint gives four buckets of identical, equally dark pink paint — mixing one s orbital with three p orbitals gives four identical sp3 hybrid orbitals, closer in character to p orbitals than s orbitals (just as pink made from 1 part white to 3 parts red is closer to red). Mixing one bucket of white with two of red gives three buckets of a slightly lighter pink — mixing one s orbital with two p orbitals gives three sp2 hybrid orbitals. Mixing one bucket of white with one of red gives two buckets of the lightest pink — mixing one s orbital with one p orbital gives two sp hybrid orbitals.

Crucially, the overlap of a hybrid orbital with any other orbital (s, p, or another hybrid orbital) always forms a sigma bond — hybrid orbitals, being end-on directional lobes, never form pi bonds. Any p orbital left unhybridized keeps its original dumbbell shape and is the only type of orbital that can go on to form a pi bond.

sp3 hybridization — four single bonds

When carbon forms four single bonds, one s and all three p orbitals mix to give four equal sp3 hybrid orbitals, oriented towards the four corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart. Each overlaps end-on with another atomic orbital to form a sigma bond.

Worked example — methane, CH4Carbon's four sp3 hybrid orbitals each overlap with the 1s orbital of a hydrogen atom, forming four identical C–H sigma bonds arranged tetrahedrally (109.5°). There are no unhybridized p orbitals left on carbon, and no pi bonds, consistent with methane containing only single bonds.

sp2 hybridization — one double bond

When carbon forms a double bond (plus two single bonds), one s and two of the three p orbitals mix to give three equal sp2 hybrid orbitals, oriented in one plane at 120° to each other (trigonal planar). This leaves one p orbital completely unhybridized, oriented perpendicular to the plane of the three sp2 orbitals.

Worked example — ethene, C2H4Each carbon is sp2 hybridized: two of its three sp2 orbitals overlap with hydrogen 1s orbitals (two C–H sigma bonds), and the third sp2 orbital overlaps end-on with the other carbon's sp2 orbital (one C–C sigma bond), giving each carbon a trigonal planar arrangement of three sigma bonds at 120°. The one unhybridized p orbital remaining on each carbon, perpendicular to this plane, overlaps sideways with the matching p orbital on the other carbon, forming one pi bond. The C=C double bond is therefore one sigma bond (from two overlapping sp2 orbitals) plus one pi bond (from two overlapping unhybridized p orbitals).

sp hybridization — one triple bond

When carbon forms a triple bond (plus one single bond), one s and only one of the three p orbitals mix to give two equal sp hybrid orbitals, oriented at exactly 180° to each other (linear). This leaves two p orbitals completely unhybridized, perpendicular both to each other and to the line of the two sp orbitals.

Worked example — ethyne, C2H2Each carbon is sp hybridized: one sp orbital overlaps with a hydrogen 1s orbital (one C–H sigma bond), and the other sp orbital overlaps end-on with the other carbon's sp orbital (one C–C sigma bond), giving each carbon a linear arrangement of two sigma bonds at 180°. The two unhybridized p orbitals remaining on each carbon, mutually perpendicular, overlap sideways with their counterparts on the other carbon, forming two separate pi bonds at 90° to each other. The resulting electron density from the two pi bonds forms a continuous cylinder of negative charge wrapped around the C≡C axis, making the triple bond especially attractive to electron-seeking (electrophilic) reactants. The C≡C triple bond is therefore one sigma bond plus two pi bonds.

Hybridization and lone pairs

Hybrid orbitals are not reserved only for bonding electrons — a non-bonding (lone) pair can also occupy a hybrid orbital. In ammonia, NH3, nitrogen is sp3 hybridized: three of its four sp3 orbitals form N–H sigma bonds, while the fourth holds the lone pair. In water, oxygen is likewise sp3 hybridized, with two sp3 orbitals forming O–H sigma bonds and the other two each holding a lone pair.

Relating hybridization to electron domains and shape

The three-way relationshipnumber of electron domains ↔ molecular/electron-domain shape ↔ hybridization of the central atom. Counting electron domains from the Lewis formula tells you both the shape (via VSEPR) and the hybridization (since both depend on exactly how many orbitals must combine): 2 domains ↔ linear ↔ sp hybridization; 3 domains ↔ trigonal planar ↔ sp2 hybridization; 4 domains ↔ tetrahedral ↔ sp3 hybridization.

Worked example — urea, H2N–C(=O)–NH2Draw the Lewis formula first: a central carbon double-bonded to one oxygen and singly bonded to two nitrogen atoms, each nitrogen also bonded to two hydrogens and carrying one lone pair.
Carbon has three electron domains (1 C=O double bond + 2 C–N single bonds) — trigonal planar, so carbon is sp2 hybridized, with bond angles of approximately 120°.
Each nitrogen has four electron domains (2 N–H bonds + 1 N–C bond + 1 lone pair) — tetrahedral electron domain geometry, so each nitrogen is sp3 hybridized, with H–N–H and H–N–C angles compressed slightly below 109.5° by the lone pair.

Hybridization in benzene

Each of benzene's six carbon atoms is sp2 hybridized, forming three sigma bonds (two C–C and one C–H, or two C–H... each carbon actually forms two C–C sigma bonds to its ring neighbours and one C–H sigma bond) at 120°, giving the whole ring a flat, planar sigma-bond framework. This leaves one unhybridized p orbital per carbon, standing perpendicular to the plane of the ring. Instead of pairing up into three separate, localized pi bonds (as a simple alternating Kekulé structure would suggest), all six p orbitals overlap sideways with both of their neighbours simultaneously, spreading the six pi electrons out evenly around the whole ring. This produces the delocalized pi-electron system — pictured as two donut-shaped rings of electron density, one above and one below the plane of the carbon atoms — that is responsible for benzene's special stability (resonance energy) and its resistance to addition reactions.

Nature of science — hybridization as a modelHybridization has a genuine mathematical foundation in quantum mechanics (combining atomic wave functions to produce new wave functions), but this underlying mathematics is complex, so hybridization is usually taught and visualized as a simpler "mixing" model. The model is validated not by being a literal physical description of what electrons do, but by how successfully it explains and predicts real molecular geometries that match experimental observation — the same standard used to judge the VSEPR model itself. (An alternative framework, molecular orbital theory, sometimes describes delocalized pi systems, such as benzene's, even more accurately, though its mathematical details go beyond this course.) Expanded-octet species with five or six electron domains are sometimes loosely described using five or six hybrid orbitals (sp3d, sp3d2), but modern computational studies increasingly favour alternative bonding models (such as three-centre, four-electron bonding) over genuine d-orbital participation for most of these species — a reminder that even a widely taught model may later be refined as understanding deepens.

Guiding Question Revisited

  • A covalent bond is the electrostatic attraction between a shared electron pair and the positively charged nuclei of the bonded atoms; sharing one, two, or three pairs forms a single, double, or triple bond, each shorter and stronger than the last. A coordination (dative) bond is a covalent bond in which both shared electrons come from the same atom, but is otherwise identical to any other covalent bond once formed.
  • The octet rule is a powerful guideline for drawing Lewis formulas, but has recognized exceptions (hydrogen, incomplete octets on small atoms, expanded octets, radicals, some transition metals).
  • The VSEPR model predicts molecular shape from the repulsion between electron domains: 2 → linear, 3 → trigonal planar, 4 → tetrahedral, and, with an expanded octet, 5 → trigonal bipyramidal, 6 → octahedral; lone pairs and multiple bonds compress the ideal bond angle slightly.
  • Bond polarity arises from electronegativity differences; molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and molecular geometry, since symmetrically arranged equal dipoles cancel.
  • Carbon and silicon form covalent network structures (diamond, graphite, graphene, fullerenes, silicon, silicon dioxide) whose extreme properties arise from extended covalent lattices rather than weak intermolecular forces.
  • Intermolecular forces — London dispersion, dipole–induced dipole, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding, increasing in that order — govern melting/boiling point, volatility, solubility, and conductivity; chromatography exploits differing attractions to a stationary and mobile phase, quantified by Rf.
  • HL: Resonance structures arise when more than one valid Lewis formula exists for the same skeleton of atoms; the true structure is a hybrid with delocalized electrons and intermediate bond lengths/strengths, dramatically illustrated by benzene's exceptional stability and unreactivity.
  • HL: Central atoms from period 3 downward can expand their octet beyond eight electrons using d-orbitals, giving five-domain (trigonal bipyramidal) and six-domain (octahedral) species with their own characteristic distorted shapes.
  • HL: Formal charge (FC = V − (S + L)) is a bookkeeping tool for choosing between competing, non-equivalent Lewis formulas: the structure with formal charges closest to zero, and negative charge on the more electronegative atom, is generally preferred.
  • HL: Covalent bonds form from overlapping atomic or hybrid orbitals: head-on overlap gives a sigma bond (present in every bond), sideways overlap of parallel p orbitals gives a pi bond (present only within double/triple bonds).
  • HL: Hybridization — the mixing of atomic orbitals into new, equivalent hybrid orbitals (sp, sp2, sp3) — explains why VSEPR's predicted angles (180°, 120°, 109.5°) actually arise, and links directly to the number of electron domains and the resulting molecular geometry.
