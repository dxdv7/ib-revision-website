Guiding Question

How can the rate of a reaction be controlled?

The rate, or kinetics, of a chemical reaction refers to the speed at which reactants are converted into products. Studies of reaction rate are of prime industrial importance, since they reveal how quickly products form and what conditions give the most efficient, economic process – and sometimes how to slow an unwanted reaction down, such as the destruction of stratospheric ozone. At SL, collision theory explains qualitatively why concentration, pressure, surface area, temperature and catalysts all affect rate. At HL, this chapter goes much further: rate can be expressed mathematically as a rate equation, rate = k[A]m[B]n, in which the orders of reaction, m and n, and the rate constant, k, must be found experimentally. The temperature dependence of k is described quantitatively by the Arrhenius equation, which allows the activation energy of a reaction to be calculated from experimental data. Finally, kinetic data provide crucial evidence about a reaction's mechanism – the sequence of elementary steps by which reactants actually become products.

Rate of Reaction: Definition, Gradients and Tangents

Rate of reactionThe rate of a chemical reaction is the increase in concentration of products, or the decrease in concentration of reactants, per unit time.
rate of reaction = Δ[P] / Δt   or   −Δ[R] / Δt     units = mol dm−3 s−1

Δ means “change in”. By convention, rate is always reported as a positive value; because [R] decreases over time, a minus sign is placed in front of the reactant expression to cancel the naturally negative value of Δ[R].

Concentration–time curvesTwo sketch graphs with time on the horizontal axis. Product concentration, [P], starts at zero and rises as a curve that becomes less steep, levelling off as the reaction finishes. Reactant concentration, [R], starts at a maximum and falls as a curve that also becomes less steep, levelling off toward zero (or toward whatever concentration remains). Both curves show that the rate of change is greatest at the start of the reaction and decreases as the reaction proceeds.

Because these graphs are curves, their gradient is not constant, so rate can only be quoted for a particular instant in time. This is found by drawing a tangent to the curve at the chosen time and measuring its gradient. A longer tangent line, drawn as far as is reasonably possible in both directions, gives a more accurate gradient, since both the concentration change (y) and the time change (x) used in the calculation are larger.

Worked example

A tangent drawn to a curve of [R] against time at t = 120 s shows a change of −2.40 mol dm−3 over 283 s. Calculate the rate at t = 120 s.

rate = 2.40 mol dm−3 / 283 s = 8.48 × 10−3 mol dm−3 s−1
Worked example: rate changes during a reaction

For the same reaction, a tangent at t = 0 s gives a change of −1.92 mol dm−3 over 67 s, and a tangent at t = 90 s gives a change of −1.18 mol dm−3 over 164 s.

rate at t = 0 s = 1.92 / 67 = 2.9 × 10−2 mol dm−3 s−1
rate at t = 90 s = 1.18 / 164 = 7.2 × 10−3 mol dm−3 s−1

The initial rate is roughly four times greater than the rate at 90 s, confirming that rate falls sharply as reactant concentration is used up. Because of this variation, the initial rate (the tangent at t = 0) is commonly used to compare different reactions, or the same reaction under different conditions, on a fair basis – and, as seen later in this chapter, initial rate data are central to the experimental determination of a rate equation.

“Draw a graph” versus “sketch a graph”Drawing a graph is based on actual data, so scales must be chosen appropriately and data points and units clearly marked. Sketching shows a trend in the variables without reference to specific data. In both cases, axes must be clearly labelled, with the dependent variable on the y-axis and the independent variable on the x-axis.

Experimental Techniques for Measuring Rate

Concentration is rarely measured directly. Instead, a related signal is monitored, such as volume, mass, colour or conductivity.

Continuous monitoring methods

Gas volume: convenient when a product is a gas. A gas syringe, or displacement of water from an inverted burette (only valid for a gas of low solubility), gives a graph of volume against time. Example: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g).

Mass loss: if a gas escapes, standing the flask on a balance gives a continuous decrease in mass. Unsuitable for hydrogen, which is too light to give a measurable change. Example: CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + CO2(g) + H2O(l).

Colorimetry/spectrophotometry: if a reactant or product is coloured (absorbing in the visible region, roughly 320–800 nm), a colorimeter measures absorbance, which increases as the coloured species' concentration increases (and can be converted into concentration via a calibration curve). Example: CV+ + OH− → CVOH, followed by the decrease in absorbance as the coloured crystal violet cation is consumed.

Titration: samples are withdrawn at intervals and titrated against a standard solution. Because titration itself takes time, a quenching agent is added to each sample the instant it is withdrawn, stopping the reaction and giving a “freeze-frame” snapshot. Example: H2O2(aq) + 2H+(aq) + 2I−(aq) → I2(aq) + 2H2O(l), with I2 titrated against Na2S2O3 and Na2CO3 used to quench by neutralizing the acid.

Conductivity: total ionic conductivity changes if the total concentration or charge of ions in solution changes. Example: BrO3−(aq) + 5Br−(aq) + 6H+(aq) → 3Br2(aq) + 3H2O(l), where 12 mol of ions on the reactant side fall to 0 on the product side.

Non-continuous methods: clock reactions

When a continuous signal is impractical, the time taken to reach a fixed, observable end point is measured instead – for example, the disappearance of a magnesium ribbon, or the classic “disappearing cross” reaction, Na2S2O3(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + SO2(aq) + H2O(l) + S(s), where precipitated sulfur gradually obscures a cross viewed through the solution. This method gives only an average rate over the interval measured.

Time as independent versus dependent variableIn the five continuous techniques, time is the independent variable and the signal is measured as it changes. In a clock reaction, a fixed end point is chosen and the time to reach it is measured, making time the dependent variable.
Balanced equations give no rate informationThe balanced equation of a reaction gives no information about its rate. Rate information can only come from experimental (empirical) data – a point developed much further later in this chapter.

Collision Theory and the Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

The kinetic molecular theory states that particles move randomly because of their kinetic energy. Not all particles have the same kinetic energy at a given instant; instead they show a range of values, whose average is directly related to the substance's absolute temperature.

Temperature and kinetic energyTemperature in kelvin (K) is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.
The Maxwell–Boltzmann energy distribution curveA graph of number of particles (vertical axis) against kinetic energy, E (horizontal axis). The curve starts at the origin, rises steeply to a single peak at a relatively low energy, then falls away more gradually in a long tail toward higher energies, never quite reaching zero. The curve is asymmetric (skewed), not a symmetrical bell shape. The area enclosed under the whole curve represents the total number of particles in the sample.

When reactants are mixed, their kinetic energy causes collisions that may break existing bonds and form new ones, so that products form. This is collision theory. Not every collision is “successful”: two factors determine this – the energy of collision and its geometry.

Collision theorySpecies react as a result of collisions of sufficient energy and proper orientation. Particles must collide with kinetic energy greater than the activation energy and with the correct collision geometry.
Activation energy, EaThe minimum value of kinetic energy that particles must have before they are able to react. Particles with lower energy may still collide, but unsuccessfully.

Activation energy behaves like a “pass mark”: only particles with energy above it can react. The rate of reaction therefore depends on the proportion of particles with kinetic energy greater than Ea.

Activation energy on the Maxwell–Boltzmann curveThe same curve, with a vertical line marking Ea positioned along the tail, to the right of the peak. The area under the curve to the left of this line (from zero to Ea) represents particles without enough energy to react; the smaller area under the tail, to the right of the line, represents the (much smaller) proportion that do have enough energy.
Reaction energy profilesTwo potential-energy-against-reaction-coordinate diagrams. In (a), an endothermic reaction, reactants sit lower on the left, the curve rises to a peak (the transition state) in the middle, and products sit higher on the right; the height from reactants to the peak is Ea, and the smaller height from reactants to products is ΔH (positive). In (b), an exothermic reaction, reactants sit higher on the left, the curve rises to a peak labelled Ea above the reactants, then falls to products lower on the right; ΔH is negative, since products sit below reactants.
Effect of collision geometryTwo reactant molecules approaching each other. In (a), the orientation is wrong, so after collision the molecules simply separate – an ineffective collision. In (b), the molecules approach with the correct orientation so their reactive parts meet directly, and products form – an effective collision.

Collision theory in summary: the rate of a reaction depends on the frequency of collisions between particles possessing both kinetic energy greater than Ea and appropriate collision geometry.

Factors That Influence the Rate of Reaction

Any factor that increases the frequency of successful collisions increases the rate.

Temperature

Maxwell–Boltzmann curves at two temperaturesTwo curves for the same sample at 300 K and 310 K, equal in area (same total number of particles). The 310 K curve is lower, broader, and shifted to the right compared with the taller, narrower 300 K curve. A vertical line marks Ea further along the tail of both curves; the area beyond this line is visibly larger for the 310 K curve, showing a greater proportion of particles able to react.

As temperature rises, collision frequency increases somewhat, but far more importantly, the proportion of particles with energy exceeding Ea increases sharply, so the frequency of successful collisions – and hence rate – increases. Many reactions approximately double their rate for every 10°C rise, though this is only a rule of thumb, as later sections of this chapter will show quantitatively.

Concentration

Increasing concentration brings particles closer together on average, increasing collision frequency and so the frequency of successful collisions. As a reaction proceeds, reactant concentration falls and rate decreases, producing the characteristic curved concentration–time graphs seen earlier.

Pressure

For gaseous reactions, increasing pressure compresses the gas, effectively increasing its concentration and so increasing collision frequency.

Surface area

In heterogeneous reactions (reactants in different phases), subdividing a solid into smaller particles increases its total surface area, allowing more contact and collision with the other reactant. Replacing marble chips with powder in the reaction with hydrochloric acid gives much more vigorous effervescence. On an industrial scale, this effect can be dangerous: fine dust powders (coal dust in mines, flour in mills) have an enormous surface area, allowing explosively rapid combustion.

Catalysts and Rate

CatalystA substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without itself undergoing permanent chemical change. Most catalysts work by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
Reaction profile with and without a catalystReactants on the left, products on the right, with ΔH the same vertical distance between them for both routes. The uncatalyzed path rises to a high peak (large Ea); the catalyzed path rises to a noticeably lower peak (smaller Ea). Both curves share the same start and end points, so ΔH is unchanged – only the height of the peak differs.
Maxwell–Boltzmann curve showing the effect of a catalystA single curve at one fixed temperature. Two vertical lines mark Ea (uncatalyzed), further along the tail, and Ea (catalyzed), closer to the peak. The area beyond the catalyzed line is visibly larger than beyond the uncatalyzed line, even though the curve's shape has not changed – only the position of the energy threshold has moved lower, increasing the proportion of molecules with sufficient energy to react without any change in temperature.

Catalysts lower the activation energy of the forward and reverse reactions by an equal amount, so they do not shift the position of equilibrium or alter the yield – they simply let both reactions reach the same equilibrium position faster.

Homogeneous versus heterogeneous catalystsA homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants (e.g. gaseous chlorine radicals catalyzing ozone breakdown). A heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase (e.g. solid vanadium(V) oxide catalyzing gaseous SO2 + O2). The detailed mechanisms of each are not required at this level.

Biological catalysts are enzymes. Catalytic converters use transition metal catalysts (platinum, palladium, rhodium) to convert toxic exhaust gases into less harmful ones: 2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO2(g) + N2(g), reducing pollutant emissions by around 90% without loss of engine performance. Catalysis underpins Green Chemistry: catalysts replace stoichiometric reagents, enhance selectivity, and – being effective in small, reusable quantities – reduce chemical waste and increase atom economy.

HL preview: a catalyst can appear in the rate equationAlthough a catalyst does not appear in the overall balanced equation, it can appear in the experimentally determined rate equation, if it participates in the rate-determining step of the mechanism. This apparent paradox is resolved later in this chapter, when reaction mechanisms are studied in detail.

HL: Rate Expressions and the Order of Reaction

Experiment shows that the rate of almost every reaction depends on reactant concentrations raised to specific powers. For a general reaction A + B → products:

rate = k[A]m[B]n
Order of reactionThe order of reaction with respect to a reactant is the power, m or n, to which its concentration is raised in the rate equation. The overall order of reaction is the sum of the individual orders, m + n. The rate constant, k, is fixed for a given reaction at a given temperature.

Crucially, m and n are not necessarily the same as the stoichiometric coefficients in the balanced equation – they can only be found by experiment.

Worked example

The reaction 2H2(g) + 2NO(g) → 2H2O(g) + N2(g) is second order with respect to NO and first order with respect to H2. Give the rate equation and overall order.

rate = k[NO]2[H2]     overall order = 2 + 1 = 3 (third order)

Note that the coefficients in the balanced equation (2 and 2) do not match the experimental orders (2 and 1).

ReactionOrder wrt each reactantOverall order
H2(g) + I2(g) → 2HI(g)H2, 1st; I2, 1st2nd
2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g)H2O2, 1st1st
S2O82−(aq) + 2I−(aq) → 2SO42−(aq) + I2(aq)S2O82−, 1st; I−, 1st2nd
2N2O5(g) → 4NO2(g) + O2(g)N2O5, 1st1st

There is no predictable link between the coefficients of the balanced equation and these experimentally determined orders.

The initial rates method

The order with respect to each reactant is found by running several experiments with different starting concentrations of one reactant while holding all others constant, and measuring the initial rate each time (using the tangent-at-t = 0 technique described earlier).

  • If changing [A] has no effect on rate, the reaction is zero order with respect to A.
  • If rate changes in direct proportion to [A] (doubling [A] doubles the rate), the reaction is first order with respect to A.
  • If rate changes with the square of the change in [A] (doubling [A] quadruples the rate, tripling [A] gives a nine-fold increase), the reaction is second order with respect to A.

HL: Graphical Representations of Order – Zero, First and Second Order

Concentration–time and rate–concentration graphs allow the order with respect to a reactant, A, to be identified visually. Concentration–time graphs do not clearly distinguish first from second order; rate–concentration graphs do.

Zero order: rate = k[A]0 = k

Zero-order graphsConcentration–time: a straight line sloping downward (constant rate), with gradient equal to −k. Rate–concentration: a horizontal straight line at height k, showing rate is independent of [A].

First order: rate = k[A]

First-order graphsConcentration–time: an exponential decay curve, falling steeply at first then levelling off, with a constant half-life – the time for concentration to halve is always the same, however far the reaction has progressed. Rate–concentration: a straight line through the origin, with gradient k.
Worked example: constant half-life

Radioactive decay is a first-order process. Iodine-131 has a half-life, t½, of 8 days. Calculate the mass of 131I remaining after 24 days, starting from 0.680 g.

24 days = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 half-lives. Each half-life halves the remaining mass:

mass remaining = 0.680 × (½)3 = 0.680 ÷ 8 = 0.0850 g

Second order: rate = k[A]2

Second-order graphsConcentration–time: a curve, steeper than the first-order curve at the start but levelling off even more quickly. Rate–concentration: a parabola (curving upward), characteristic of the square function, with gradient initially zero and increasing as [A] increases.
How half-life itself behaves differently for each orderFor a first-order reaction, t½ is constant throughout – the signature graphical feature used above. For a zero-order reaction, successive half-lives get shorter as the reaction proceeds (the same constant rate acts on an ever-smaller amount of reactant). For a second-order reaction, successive half-lives get longer (the rate itself falls as concentration falls, so a smaller concentration takes disproportionately longer to halve).
A real first-order example

The decomposition of an oxidized fullerene derivative, C60O3, in toluene solution (C60O3 → C60O + O2) can be followed by its absorbance, which is directly proportional to [C60O3]. A graph of rate (found from the gradient of the absorbance–time curve) against absorbance gives a straight line through the origin, confirming that rate ∝ [C60O3]1 – a first-order reaction, rate = k[C60O3].

HL: The Rate Constant, k – Units and Calculations

The units of k depend on the overall order of the reaction, since rate always has units of concentration × time−1.

Secondrate = k[A]2 or k[A][B]mol−1 dm3 s−1

k is temperature-dependentThe rate constant, k, has a single fixed value for a given reaction only at a given, fixed temperature. Increasing temperature always increases k (explored quantitatively via the Arrhenius equation, below). Do not confuse the lowercase rate constant, k, with the uppercase equilibrium constant, K.
Worked example: calculating k

A reaction has rate equation rate = k[A]2[B]. When [A] = [B] = 2.50 × 10−2 mol dm−3, the rate is 7.75 × 10−5 mol dm−3 min−1. Calculate k, including units.

k = rate / ([A]2[B]) = 7.75 × 10−5 / (2.50 × 10−2)3 = 4.96 mol−2 dm6 min−1

The units follow from the overall third order of the reaction (m + n = 2 + 1 = 3), using minutes since that is the time unit of the given rate.

Zero order in practice: catalytic decomposition of ammonia

2NH3(g) → N2(g) + 3H2(g), catalyzed by heated platinum, is zero order overall. The rate depends only on the (very small, fixed) number of NH3 molecules attached to the saturated catalyst surface at any moment, not on the much larger bulk concentration of NH3 gas – so increasing [NH3] has no effect on rate.

HL: Reaction Mechanisms, the Rate-Determining Step and Molecularity

Most reactions occur as a sequence of simple elementary steps, collectively called the reaction mechanism. Individual steps usually cannot be observed directly, so a mechanism is a theory, supported (but never proven) by kinetic and stoichiometric evidence.

Intermediate versus transition state versus catalystAn intermediate is a species produced in one step and consumed in a later step; it exists for a finite time and could, in principle, be detected or isolated. A transition state exists only fleetingly, while bonds are actively breaking and forming, and can never be isolated. A catalyst is, by contrast, present at the start of the mechanism (often regenerated at the end), is not a product of an earlier step, and speeds up the reaction by opening a lower-Ea pathway.

Molecularity

Molecularity is the number of reactant particles in a single elementary step: unimolecular (one particle), bimolecular (two particles), or termolecular (three particles). Because the simultaneous collision of three particles with sufficient energy and correct orientation is statistically very improbable, termolecular elementary steps are rare; mechanisms generally propose a sequence of unimolecular and bimolecular steps instead.

The rate-determining step (RDS)The rate-determining step is the slowest elementary step in a mechanism – equivalently, the step with the highest activation energy. Products can only appear as fast as this step allows, so the RDS determines the rate equation for the overall reaction.

Because the RDS is a single elementary step, collision theory tells us its rate equation directly from its molecularity: each reactant's concentration appears in the rate equation for that step, raised to the power of its coefficient in that step (not in the overall equation).

Elementary stepMolecularityRate equation for that step
A → productsUnimolecularrate = k[A]
2A → productsBimolecularrate = k[A]2
A + B → productsBimolecularrate = k[A][B]
Worked example: RDS is the first step

2NO2Cl(g) → 2NO2(g) + Cl2(g) is believed to proceed by: step 1 (slow, RDS) NO2Cl(g) → NO2(g) + Cl(g); step 2 (fast) NO2Cl(g) + Cl(g) → NO2(g) + Cl2(g).

Since the RDS is the first step, the overall rate equation is simply that of step 1:

rate = k[NO2Cl]     first order overall

Note that the balanced equation has a coefficient of 2 for NO2Cl, yet the experimental order is only 1 – direct evidence that stoichiometric coefficients cannot be assumed to give reaction orders.

Worked example: RDS is not the first step – eliminating an intermediate

2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g) is believed to proceed by: step 1 (fast) NO(g) + NO(g) → N2O2(g); step 2 (slow, RDS) N2O2(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g).

The rate equation for step 2 is rate = k[N2O2][O2]. But N2O2 is an intermediate, not a reactant, so it cannot appear in the final rate equation. Because the fast first step produces N2O2 bimolecularly from NO, [N2O2] depends on [NO]2, which is substituted in:

rate = k[NO]2[O2]     third order overall
Worked example: a catalyst appearing in the rate equation

The acid-catalyzed iodination of propanone, CH3COCH3(aq) + I2(aq) → CH2ICOCH3(aq) + HI(aq), is catalyzed by H+(aq). Experiment shows the rate equation is:

rate = k[CH3COCH3][H+]     (zero order with respect to I2)

This is only possible because the rate-determining step is the acid-catalyzed enolization of propanone (which does not involve I2 at all); the reaction of enol with I2 is a subsequent, fast step. The catalyst, H+, is consumed in the RDS and regenerated later in the mechanism, so it appears in the rate equation even though it is entirely absent from the overall balanced equation – resolving the paradox introduced earlier in this chapter.

Worked example: SN1 mechanism – a reactant missing from the rate equation

(CH3)3CCl(aq) + OH−(aq) → (CH3)3COH(aq) + Cl−(aq) proceeds by: step 1 (slow, RDS) (CH3)3CCl → (CH3)3C+ + Cl−; step 2 (fast) (CH3)3C+ + OH− → (CH3)3COH.

rate = k[(CH3)3CCl]     first order overall, independent of [OH−]

Even though OH− appears in the overall balanced equation, it plays no part in the (unimolecular) rate-determining step, so it does not appear in the rate equation at all.

Energy profile for a two-step reactionA potential-energy-against-reaction-coordinate diagram with reactants on the left and products on the right. Two maxima (peaks) represent the two transition states, with a dip between them representing the intermediate. If the first peak is higher than the second, the first step has the greater activation energy and is the rate-determining step; the activation energy of the overall reaction is taken as that of this rate-determining step.
Zero order with respect to a reactantIf a reactant does not appear in the rate equation (zero order with respect to it), that reactant plays no part in the rate-determining step – even though it is required overall for the reaction to occur. Conversely, if a species does appear in the rate equation, it (or something derived from it) must take part in the rate-determining step.

HL: The Arrhenius Equation

Since increasing temperature does not change reactant concentrations, its effect on reaction rate must act entirely through the rate constant, k. Svante Arrhenius showed that the fraction of molecules with energy exceeding Ea at temperature T is proportional to e−Ea/RT, and therefore so is k itself:

k = Ae−Ea/RT

This is the Arrhenius equation. R is the gas constant (8.31 J K−1 mol−1), T is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and A is the Arrhenius factor (also called the frequency factor or pre-exponential factor) – a constant for a given reaction that accounts for the frequency of collisions with the correct orientation. A has the same units as k.

The linear (logarithmic) form

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides gives a straight-line equation:

ln k = −Ea/R × (1/T) + ln A     (compare y = mx + c)
The Arrhenius plotA graph of ln k (y-axis) against 1/T (x-axis) gives a straight line. Its gradient equals −Ea/R (so the line slopes downward, since Ea and R are both positive), and its y-intercept equals ln A.
Worked example: finding Ea graphically

Rate constants for a first-order reaction were measured at five temperatures:

k / s−1T / °C
2.88 × 10−4320
4.87 × 10−4340
7.96 × 10−4360
1.26 × 10−3380
1.94 × 10−3400

Converting T to kelvin and plotting ln k against 1/T gives a straight line of gradient −9518.65 K.

Ea = −(gradient) × R = 9518.65 K × 8.31 J K−1 mol−1 = 79 100 J mol−1 = 79.1 kJ mol−1

Note the unit conversion: the gradient method gives Ea directly in J mol−1 (since R is in J K−1 mol−1); dividing by 1000 converts this to the more usual kJ mol−1.

Worked example: finding A from the intercept

For a first-order reaction, an Arrhenius plot gave a y-intercept of −1.20 (the units of ln k are not written, since a logarithm is dimensionless, but A itself carries the units of k). Calculate A.

intercept = ln A, so A = e(intercept) = e−1.20 = 0.301 s−1

(A takes the units of k; here, s−1, since the reaction is first order.)

HL: Calculating Activation Energy From Two Data Points

Ea can also be calculated without a full graph, using the rate constant at just two temperatures. Writing the linear Arrhenius equation at T1 (giving k1) and at T2 (giving k2), and subtracting one equation from the other to eliminate ln A, gives:

ln(k2/k1) = −Ea/R × (1/T2 − 1/T1)
Worked example: activation energy of HI decomposition

2HI(g) → H2(g) + I2(g) has rate constant k1 = 3.52 × 10−7 mol−1 dm3 s−1 at 283°C, and k2 = 3.95 × 10−2 mol−1 dm3 s−1 at 508°C. Calculate Ea.

Convert to kelvin: T1 = 283 + 273 = 556 K; T2 = 508 + 273 = 781 K.

ln(3.95 × 10−2 / 3.52 × 10−7) = −Ea/8.31 × (1/781 − 1/556)
ln(1.122 × 105) = −Ea/8.31 × (−5.182 × 10−4)
11.63 = Ea/8.31 × 5.182 × 10−4

Note the sign handling: (1/T2 − 1/T1) is negative, since T2 > T1 makes 1/T2 the smaller term; this negative sign cancels the leading minus sign in front of Ea/R, leaving a positive quantity equal to the (positive) left-hand side, ln(k2/k1), as it must, since k2 > k1.

Ea = 11.63 × 8.31 / (5.182 × 10−4) = 1.865 × 105 J mol−1 ≈ 187 kJ mol−1
Why the “doubles every 10°C” rule is only a rule of thumbThe Arrhenius equation shows that the effect of a given temperature rise on k depends on the actual value of Ea. The familiar doubling-every-10°C pattern holds only for reactions with Ea in the fairly narrow range around 50 kJ mol−1 typical of many everyday reactions. A reaction with an unusually large Ea is far more sensitive to temperature change than this rule suggests, while one with an unusually small Ea is far less sensitive – which is why the rule cannot be relied upon as a general law.

Investigating Rate of Reaction: Variables and Errors

When planning a rate investigation, all variables other than the one being tested must be controlled. For example, investigating the effect of temperature on HCl(aq) + Mg(s) requires the same acid concentration and the same size of magnesium strip in every trial, as well as consistent details of the measurement technique itself (for example, keeping a light sensor and light source in the same position in every trial).

Systematic and random error on a graphThree scatter plots of a dependent variable against an independent variable, each with a trend line. In the first (“perfect”), every point sits exactly on the line. In the second (“systematic”), all points sit consistently to one side of the expected trend line, which is itself displaced from its true position. In the third (“random”), points scatter on both sides of the trend line with no consistent pattern.

Systematic errors make all measurements consistently higher or lower than the true value, causing inaccuracy. Random errors, from equipment limitations, environmental changes, misread values or too few repeats, scatter points on both sides of the true value, causing imprecision. The fit of a trend line can be evaluated using the coefficient of determination, R2, between 0 and 1, with 1 indicating a perfect fit.

Guiding Question Revisited

How can the rate of a reaction be controlled?
  • Rate is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time, mol dm−3 s−1, found from the gradient of a tangent to a concentration–time (or volume, mass, absorbance) graph; it can be measured using gas volume, mass loss, colorimetry, titration with quenching, conductivity, or clock reactions.
  • Collision theory: species react via collisions with energy greater than the activation energy, Ea, and correct orientation. Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution curves explain how temperature and catalysts change the proportion of successful collisions.
  • Concentration, pressure, surface area, temperature and catalysts all increase rate by increasing the frequency of successful collisions; catalysts do this by providing a lower-Ea pathway, without changing ΔH or the position of equilibrium.
  • HL: the rate equation, rate = k[A]m[B]n, and the orders m and n, must be found experimentally (usually via the initial rates method) and bear no necessary relation to the stoichiometric coefficients of the overall equation.
  • HL: zero-, first- and second-order reactions give characteristic concentration–time and rate–concentration graph shapes; only a first-order reaction has a constant half-life.
  • HL: the rate constant, k, is temperature-dependent, and its units depend on the overall order of reaction (mol dm−3 s−1 for zero order, s−1 for first order, mol−1 dm3 s−1 for second order).
  • HL: reactions proceed via a mechanism of elementary steps; the slowest, rate-determining step fixes the overall rate equation. Its stoichiometry (not the overall equation's) sets the orders of reaction, which is why intermediates are eliminated from, and catalysts can appear in, the final rate equation.
  • HL: the Arrhenius equation, k = Ae−Ea/RT, and its linear form, ln k = −Ea/R × (1/T) + ln A, allow Ea and A to be found either graphically (from an Arrhenius plot) or from rate constants at just two temperatures.
