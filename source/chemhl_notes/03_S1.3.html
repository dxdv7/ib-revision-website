Guiding Question

How can we model the energy states of electrons in atoms?

An atom’s chemical behaviour is controlled by how its electrons are arranged. Since electrons cannot be observed directly inside an atom, chemists instead study the light that atoms give out (or take in) and use that evidence to build a model of where the electrons are and what energies they have. At Higher Level this sub-topic goes further than the overview most students first meet: it covers emission spectra and what they prove about quantized energy; the detailed rules — the Aufbau principle, Hund’s rule and the Pauli exclusion principle — used to build the electron configuration of any atom or ion up to atomic number 36; and, uniquely at Higher Level, how the convergence limit of a line spectrum and the fine structure of successive and first ionization energies give direct, quantitative experimental evidence for the existence of main energy levels, sublevels and even the pairing of electrons within an orbital.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Visible light is only a small slice of a much larger family of waves called the electromagnetic spectrum, which ranges from long-wavelength, low-energy radio waves to short-wavelength, high-energy gamma rays. Every type of electromagnetic radiation travels through a vacuum at the same speed, the speed of light, c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1, and different types are distinguished only by their wavelength and frequency.

Wavelength and frequencyWavelength (λ) is the distance between two successive crests (or troughs) of a wave, measured in metres. Frequency (f) is the number of complete waves passing a fixed point in one second, measured in hertz (Hz, equivalent to s−1). Shorter wavelengths correspond to higher frequencies, and vice versa.
c = fλ

Here c is the speed of light (a constant, 3.00 × 108 m s−1), f is the frequency of the wave, and λ is its wavelength. Because c never changes, wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional: doubling the frequency of a wave halves its wavelength, and vice versa.

Worked example

A wave of red light has a wavelength of 700 nm (700 × 10−9 m). Calculate its frequency.
Rearranging c = fλ gives f = c ÷ λ. f = (3.00 × 108) ÷ (700 × 10−9) = 4.29 × 1014 Hz. This is the frequency of the light wave.

Moving across the electromagnetic spectrum from radio waves through microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and finally gamma rays, wavelength steadily decreases while frequency and energy steadily increase. Within visible light, red light has the longest wavelength (and lowest frequency and energy) and violet light has the shortest wavelength (and highest frequency and energy). Infrared radiation has a longer wavelength than red light, and ultraviolet radiation has a shorter wavelength than violet light.

The electromagnetic spectrumA horizontal bar running from left to right, labelled with decreasing wavelength (and increasing energy) from left to right: radio waves, microwaves, infrared (IR), then a narrow visible-light band (red through orange, yellow, green, blue, to violet), then ultraviolet (UV), X-rays, and finally gamma rays at the far right with the shortest wavelength and highest energy. An arrow beneath the bar points right and is labelled “energy increasing”; wavelength values printed along the top of the bar shrink from around 108 m on the radio-wave end down to around 10−16 m on the gamma-ray end.

White light, such as sunlight, is really a mixture of all the visible wavelengths together. Passing white light through a glass prism, or through water droplets to form a rainbow, spreads it out into a continuous spectrum, in which the colours blend smoothly into one another with no gaps.

Emission and Absorption Spectra

When atoms absorb energy — for example from an electric spark passed through a gas, or from strong heating in a flame — an electron can be promoted from its normal, lowest-energy arrangement (the ground state) up to a higher-energy arrangement (an excited state). Excited states are unstable, so the electron quickly falls back down to a lower energy level. As it does so, it releases the extra energy as a single packet of electromagnetic radiation called a photon.

Ground state and excited stateThe ground state is the lowest-energy, most stable electron arrangement of an atom. An excited state is any arrangement in which one or more electrons occupy a higher energy level than in the ground state. Excited atoms are unstable and return to the ground state by emitting photons.
Emission spectrum vs absorption spectrumAn emission spectrum is produced when excited atoms fall from a higher to a lower energy level, releasing photons; it appears as bright coloured lines on a dark background. An absorption spectrum is produced when atoms in a lower energy level absorb specific wavelengths of radiation passed through them, promoting electrons to higher levels; it appears as dark lines at specific positions on an otherwise continuous, bright background. Both types of spectrum contain lines at exactly the same set of wavelengths for a given element, because both correspond to the same set of possible energy transitions.
Origin of absorption and emission spectraTwo energy-level diagrams side by side. On the left, a sample of atoms in a low energy level absorbs incoming radiation, some atoms jumping up to a higher energy level; the transmitted radiation, when passed through a spectrometer, shows a continuous rainbow band interrupted by a few dark absorption lines at the wavelengths that were absorbed. On the right, a sample of atoms already in a high energy level falls back down to the low energy level, releasing radiation of exactly the same wavelengths as were absorbed on the left; passed through a spectrometer, this shows a plain dark background with a few bright, coloured emission lines at those same wavelengths.

Since every element has its own unique set of energy levels, each element produces its own unique pattern of spectral lines — effectively a barcode that can be used to identify it. This is how several elements (including rubidium, caesium, thallium and indium) were first discovered and named from their characteristic flame colours, and it is also how astronomers work out which elements are present in distant stars: dark absorption lines in the Sun’s spectrum, for example, reveal the presence of hydrogen and helium in its outer atmosphere.

Line Spectra as Evidence for Quantized Energy

Continuous spectrum vs line spectrumA continuous spectrum (such as the rainbow produced by passing white light through a prism) contains every wavelength across a range, blending smoothly into one another with no gaps. A line emission spectrum contains light of only a few specific, discrete wavelengths, seen as separate coloured lines with dark gaps between them.

The fact that an atom’s emission spectrum consists of separate lines, rather than a continuous smear of colour, is powerful evidence that an electron in an atom cannot have just any energy — it can only occupy certain fixed, discrete energy levels. If energy levels could take any value at all, an atom could emit photons of every possible energy, giving a continuous spectrum instead of a line spectrum. Because each line corresponds to an electron falling between two specific, fixed energy levels, the electron can only ever gain or lose energy in fixed-size steps, never continuously.

Quantization of energyThe energy of an electron in an atom is said to be quantized: it can only take one of a set of discrete, fixed values, never anything in between. This can be pictured as a staircase — a person on a staircase can stand on one particular step or another, but never at a height between two steps.

The energy released as a photon when an electron falls from a higher energy level to a lower one is exactly equal to the difference in energy between those two levels, and this in turn determines the frequency of the light emitted, through the Planck equation (covered below). Because the emitted photon’s energy is fixed by the gap between two allowed levels, only certain frequencies (certain colours) of light can ever appear in the spectrum — producing the pattern of separate lines observed experimentally. Later in this sub-topic we will see how the precise frequencies at which these lines converge can be used to calculate an ionization energy directly from spectral data.

The Planck Equation

Light can be described either as a wave (characterized by its frequency and wavelength) or as a stream of particle-like packets of energy called photons. The energy carried by each photon is directly proportional to the frequency of the radiation.

Ephoton = hf

Here Ephoton is the energy of one photon (in joules), h is the Planck constant (a fixed value, given in the data booklet, approximately 6.63 × 10−34 J s), and f is the frequency of the radiation (in hertz). Because higher frequency corresponds to higher photon energy, ultraviolet photons carry more energy than infrared photons, and gamma-ray photons carry more energy still.

When an electron falls from a higher energy level to a lower one inside an atom, the photon that is emitted carries away exactly the energy the electron has lost:

ΔEelectron = Ephoton = hf
Worked example

An electron falls between two energy levels in an atom, emitting a photon of frequency 5.00 × 1014 Hz. Using h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s, calculate the energy of the emitted photon, and hence the size of the energy change of the electron.
Ephoton = hf = (6.63 × 10−34) × (5.00 × 1014) = 3.32 × 10−19 J. Since ΔEelectron = Ephoton, the electron’s energy has decreased by 3.32 × 10−19 J as it fell to the lower level.

Worked example: combining both equations

A spectral line in an emission spectrum has a wavelength of 486 nm (486 × 10−9 m). Calculate the energy of the corresponding photon.
First find the frequency using c = fλ: f = c ÷ λ = (3.00 × 108) ÷ (486 × 10−9) = 6.17 × 1014 Hz. Then find the photon energy using E = hf: E = (6.63 × 10−34) × (6.17 × 1014) = 4.09 × 10−19 J. This two-step method — wavelength to frequency, then frequency to energy — is the standard way of converting a measured spectral line into the energy gap it represents, and is the basis of the ionization-energy calculations later in this sub-topic.

The Hydrogen Emission Spectrum

The simplest possible atom, hydrogen, has just one electron, and its line emission spectrum played a crucial role in developing atomic theory. In the (now outdated, but still useful) Bohr model, this electron is pictured as orbiting the nucleus in circular paths of fixed radius, each corresponding to a fixed energy level, labelled with an integer n (n = 1 is the lowest energy level, the ground state; n = 2, 3, 4… are progressively higher energy levels). Absorbing energy promotes the electron to a higher n; falling back down to a lower n releases a photon.

Hydrogen energy levels and the visible seriesA ladder of horizontal lines representing energy levels n = 1 (at the bottom) up through n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and higher, with the gaps between successive levels becoming steadily smaller as n increases, until the levels merge into a continuum at very high n. Several downward arrows are drawn from higher levels (n = 3, 4, 5, 6…) converging on n = 2, representing the transitions that produce the visible emission lines of hydrogen; the arrow from n = 3 to n = 2 is the longest wavelength (lowest energy, red) visible line, and arrows from higher n produce progressively shorter wavelength (higher energy, blue/violet) visible lines that crowd closer together as n increases.

Transitions ending at different energy levels fall in different regions of the electromagnetic spectrum: transitions that end at n = 1 (the ground state) release the most energy and appear in the ultraviolet region; transitions ending at n = 2 produce the visible lines; transitions ending at n = 3 or higher produce infrared lines. (The individual names given to these three series of lines are not required for this course.)

Convergence of spectral linesThe lines of the hydrogen emission spectrum are spaced further apart at low energy and crowd closer together (converge) at higher energy, eventually merging into a continuum. This happens because the energy levels themselves are spaced further apart at low n and become progressively closer together as n increases, so the energy gaps between adjacent levels — and hence the spacing between the corresponding spectral lines — shrink at higher energies.
Ionization and the convergence limitAs n increases without limit (n → ∞), the energy levels converge on a single value: the point at which the electron is no longer bound to the nucleus at all. The transition from n = 1 to n = ∞ corresponds to completely removing the electron from a ground-state atom, i.e. ionization: H(g) → H+(g) + e−. The ionization energy of an element is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state. The frequency at which the lines converge (the “convergence limit”) can therefore be used to calculate the ionization energy of hydrogen directly from experimental spectral data — the full method is developed later in this sub-topic.

The Bohr model successfully predicts the wavelengths of every line in the hydrogen spectrum, which was a major achievement, but it fails for any atom with more than one electron, because it does not account for the repulsion between electrons or their true wave-like behaviour, and it could not explain the relative intensities of the different lines. A more complete model was needed.

Beyond Bohr: Wave–Particle Duality and the Schrödinger Model

Light shows properties of both waves (it diffracts, or spreads out, when passing through a narrow slit) and particles (it can knock electrons out of a metal surface, behaving like a stream of discrete photons). Neither the wave model nor the particle model on its own fully explains light’s behaviour; both are needed together. Quantum theory extends this same dual wave/particle description to matter: a beam of electrons passed through a thin sheet of graphite produces a diffraction pattern of rings, just as a wave would, showing that electrons themselves have wave-like properties.

Electron diffractionA beam of electrons is fired from an electron gun through a thin sheet of graphite and strikes a luminescent screen beyond it. Instead of forming a single bright spot (as expected if electrons behaved only as tiny particles travelling in straight lines), the screen shows a pattern of concentric bright and dark rings, the same kind of interference pattern produced when a wave passes through a small aperture — direct evidence that electrons have wave-like character.
Heisenberg’s Uncertainty PrincipleIt is impossible to know, at the same time and with perfect precision, both the exact position and the exact momentum of a particle such as an electron. Any attempt to measure an electron’s position disturbs its motion. As a result, an electron’s path (trajectory) around the nucleus cannot be mapped out precisely, unlike the fixed circular orbits imagined in the Bohr model; only a probability description of where the electron is likely to be found is possible.

Erwin Schrödinger combined the ideas of wave behaviour and probability into a mathematical model in which the electron is described by a wave equation, whose solutions are called atomic orbitals. Unlike a Bohr orbit (a fixed circular path), an atomic orbital is a three-dimensional region of space, and it only tells us the probability of finding the electron at different points around the nucleus. Crucially, unlike the Bohr model, the Schrödinger wave equation can be applied, at least approximately, to atoms with more than one electron.

Atomic orbitalA region of space around an atomic nucleus in which there is a 90% probability of finding a given electron. Orbitals of higher energy are, on average, larger, with the electron more likely to be found further from the nucleus.
Bohr model vs Schrödinger (wave mechanical) modelThe Bohr model treats the electron as a particle following a precise, fixed circular path (an “orbit”) at a definite distance from the nucleus, and it only works for hydrogen (one electron). The Schrödinger model treats the electron as a wave, described only in terms of the probability of finding it within a three-dimensional region (an “orbital”), consistent with the Uncertainty Principle, and it can be extended (at least approximately) to atoms with many electrons.

Electron Spin and the Pauli Exclusion Principle

Refining the model further, each electron behaves as though it is spinning on its own axis, in one of two possible directions, conventionally drawn as an upward arrow (↑) or a downward arrow (↓). Two electrons occupying the same orbital can only do so if they spin in opposite directions; this reduces the repulsion between their negative charges enough for them to share the same small region of space. If two electrons occupied the same orbital with the same spin, they would be indistinguishable in every respect — which is impossible, since they are two separate electrons.

Pauli exclusion principleAn atomic orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons, and if two electrons do occupy the same orbital, they must have opposite spins. No two electrons in the same atom can have an identical set of quantum descriptions (the same orbital and the same spin).

Orbital Shapes: s and p Orbitals

Different orbitals have different characteristic shapes and sizes. You are expected to recognise the shapes of s and p orbitals (not d or f orbitals).

The 1s orbitalA single, simple spherical cloud centred on the nucleus. The density of shading (or dots) is greatest close to the nucleus and fades gradually outward, representing the probability of finding the electron at each distance; the electron may be found anywhere within this roughly spherical volume, with no preferred direction.
The three 2p orbitalsThree identical dumbbell (figure-of-eight) shaped lobes, each consisting of two rounded lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus, meeting at a point at the nucleus itself. The three 2p orbitals, labelled 2px, 2py and 2pz, are oriented at right angles to one another along the x, y and z axes respectively. Because all three have exactly the same size, shape and energy, they are described as degenerate orbitals.

A 2s orbital has the same overall spherical symmetry as a 1s orbital, but it is larger: an electron in a 2s orbital is, on average, further from the nucleus and has higher energy than an electron in a 1s orbital. Orbitals in higher sublevels (d, f) exist too and hold electrons in heavier atoms, but their detailed shapes are not required — only their names, letters and electron capacities matter for this course.

Sublevel labels and the quantum number lEach type of sublevel is identified by a letter, s, p, d or f, which corresponds to a value of the azimuthal (angular momentum) quantum number, l: l = 0 for s, l = 1 for p, l = 2 for d, and l = 3 for f. The number of degenerate orbitals within any sublevel is given by (2l + 1): one s orbital (2 × 0 + 1 = 1), three p orbitals (2 × 1 + 1 = 3), five d orbitals (2 × 2 + 1 = 5), and seven f orbitals (2 × 3 + 1 = 7). This confirms, from first principles, the electron capacities used throughout this sub-topic.

Energy Levels, Sublevels and Orbitals

Electrons are organized into a hierarchy: main energy levels (shells), which are divided into sublevels, which are themselves made up of one or more individual orbitals.

Energy level, sublevel, orbitalThe main energy level (or shell) is given an integer n = 1, 2, 3…. Each main energy level is divided into sublevels, labelled s, p, d and f, of successively higher energy. Each sublevel is made up of one or more individual orbitals of equal (degenerate) energy, each of which can hold a maximum of two electrons.

Maximum electron capacities of the sublevels and main energy levels up to n = 4.

Number of orbitals in level n = n2
Maximum number of electrons in level n = 2n2

Here n is the main energy level number. The nth level is divided into exactly n sublevels (so the 1st level has 1 sublevel, the 4th level has 4 sublevels); the total number of orbitals across all those sublevels is n2; and because each orbital holds a maximum of two electrons, the maximum number of electrons the whole level can hold is 2n2. Individually, an s sublevel always has 1 orbital (2 electrons), a p sublevel always has 3 orbitals (6 electrons), a d sublevel always has 5 orbitals (10 electrons), and an f sublevel always has 7 orbitals (14 electrons).

Worked example

Calculate the maximum number of electrons that the n = 3 energy level can hold, and check this against the individual sublevel capacities.
Using the formula: maximum electrons = 2n2 = 2 × 32 = 2 × 9 = 18. Checking against the sublevels present in n = 3 (3s, 3p, 3d): 2 (from 3s) + 6 (from 3p) + 10 (from 3d) = 18. Both methods agree: the third energy level holds a maximum of 18 electrons.

Relative energies of the sublevelsA vertical energy-level diagram with energy increasing up the page. Starting from the bottom: 1s, then 2s, then 2p (slightly above 2s), then 3s, then 3p, then 4s, then 3d (drawn just above 4s, since the 3d sublevel falls below 4s in energy only for atoms with atomic number greater than 20), then 4p. A note beside the 3d/4s pair states that their relative energy ordering is unusually close and changes once electrons begin to occupy the 3d sublevel. A further note states that this diagram is a simplification: the precise relative energies of all the sublevels actually depend on the atomic number, since they arise from a balance between nuclear attraction and inter-electron repulsion, both of which change as protons and electrons are added.

Building Up Electron Configurations: the Aufbau Principle

Aufbau principle(German for “building up”.) Electrons fill the available orbitals of an atom starting with the lowest energy orbital first, before occupying any higher-energy orbital.
Hund’s ruleWhen two or more orbitals of the same sublevel (degenerate orbitals, such as the three 2p orbitals) are available, electrons occupy separate orbitals, with parallel spins, before any orbital is doubly occupied. This arrangement minimizes the repulsion between electrons, since electrons in separate, spatially distinct orbitals stay further apart on average than two electrons crammed into the same orbital.

These rules, together with the Pauli exclusion principle, can be applied using “arrow-in-box” orbital diagrams: each orbital is drawn as a box, and each electron as an arrow (pointing up or down for its spin) placed inside a box.

Orbital diagrams for the first five elementsFive short rows of boxes, one for each of hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium and boron. Hydrogen shows a single box (1s) with one upward arrow. Helium shows the same 1s box with two arrows, one up and one down (paired, opposite spins). Lithium adds a second box (2s) containing one upward arrow. Beryllium fills that 2s box with a second, opposite-spin arrow. Boron adds a row of three separate boxes (the three 2p orbitals), with a single upward arrow in the first 2p box and the other two 2p boxes left empty.
Orbital diagrams for carbon and nitrogen (Hund’s rule in action)Carbon’s three 2p boxes each show: one upward arrow in the first box, one upward arrow in the second box, and the third box left empty — the two 2p electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel (both upward) spins rather than pairing up in one box. Nitrogen’s three 2p boxes each contain a single upward arrow, one in every box, giving three unpaired, parallel-spin electrons, one in each of the three degenerate 2p orbitals.

Continuing across period 2: oxygen begins to pair the 2p electrons (one 2p orbital now holds two, opposite-spin electrons, while the other two 2p orbitals each still hold one unpaired electron), fluorine has two of the three 2p orbitals paired, and neon completes the 2p sublevel with all three orbitals doubly occupied (six electrons in total).

Electron configuration of sulfur (Z = 16): 1s22s22p63s23p4
Worked example

Deduce the ground-state electron configuration of a sulfur atom.
Sulfur has atomic number 16, so a neutral atom has 16 electrons. Filling the orbitals from lowest to highest energy: 2 electrons fill 1s (1s2), 2 more fill 2s (2s2), 6 more fill 2p (2p6) — that accounts for 10 electrons so far — 2 more fill 3s (3s2), leaving 4 electrons for 3p (3p4), since 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 4 = 16. The full configuration is 1s22s22p63s23p4.

The Filling Order and the 3d/4s Anomaly

Because sublevel energies get closer together at higher n, the true filling order is not simply 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s… Instead, the 4s sublevel is slightly lower in energy than 3d for potassium and calcium, so 4s fills before 3d.

Aufbau filling order (up to Z = 36):
1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p
The diagonal filling-order ruleSublevels are written out in rows by main energy level (row 1: 1s; row 2: 2s, 2p; row 3: 3s, 3p, 3d; row 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f; and so on), with each sublevel positioned in a column according to its letter (s, p, d, f). A series of diagonal arrows, each running from the top right of one row down to the bottom left of the next, passes through the sublevels in the exact order they are filled: 1s, then 2s, then (down-diagonal) 2p, 3s, then (down-diagonal) 3p, 4s, then (down-diagonal) 3d, 4p, 5s, and so on. Following these diagonals reproduces the experimentally observed filling order without needing to memorize it directly.
Filling order vs the order configurations are writtenElectrons fill the 4s sublevel before the 3d sublevel (Aufbau order: …3p, 4s, 3d, 4p…). However, once the 3d sublevel starts to fill (from scandium onward), it actually drops below 4s in energy, so by convention the electron configuration of any element after calcium is written with 3d listed before 4s (e.g. vanadium is written 1s22s22p63s23p63d34s2, not …4s23d3). This is also why, when transition metals lose electrons to form positive ions, the 4s electrons are always removed before any 3d electrons — because once the 3d sublevel is occupied, its electrons push the 4s electrons to a higher effective energy.
Vanadium (Z = 23): 1s22s22p63s23p63d34s2, condensed form [Ar]3d34s2
Worked example

State the full electron configuration of vanadium (Z = 23) and deduce the number of unpaired electrons.
Vanadium has 23 electrons. Filling in Aufbau order: 1s22s22p63s23p64s23d3 (2+2+6+2+6+2+3 = 23), conventionally re-written with 3d before 4s: 1s22s22p63s23p63d34s2. The three 3d electrons occupy three separate d orbitals with parallel spins (Hund’s rule), so there are 3 unpaired electrons.

Rather than writing out every inner sublevel each time, a condensed (noble-gas core) configuration can be used: the electron configuration of the previous noble gas is replaced by its symbol in square brackets, and only the outer (valence) electrons are written out explicitly. For example, since argon’s configuration is 1s22s22p63s23p6, vanadium’s condensed configuration is [Ar]3d34s2. A condensed configuration is not an acceptable answer if a question specifically asks for the “full” electron configuration.

The Chromium and Copper Exceptions

Two elements in period 4 do not follow the Aufbau order exactly, because half-filled and completely filled d sublevels are unusually stable (they minimize electron–electron repulsion by spreading electrons out as evenly as possible).

Chromium and copper exceptionsChromium (Z = 24) has the configuration [Ar]3d54s1 rather than the “expected” [Ar]3d44s2, because a half-filled d sublevel (five separate, unpaired d electrons) is more stable. Copper (Z = 29) has the configuration [Ar]3d104s1 rather than the “expected” [Ar]3d94s2, because a completely filled d sublevel is more stable.
Orbital diagrams for chromium and copperTwo sets of boxes for the 3d and 4s sublevels. For chromium: the 4s box contains a single upward arrow, and each of the five separate 3d boxes contains one upward arrow — six singly occupied orbitals in total, with no pairing anywhere. For copper: the 4s box contains a single upward arrow, and each of the five 3d boxes contains two arrows, one up and one down (fully paired) — a completely filled 3d sublevel plus one lone 4s electron.

Because the 3d and 4s sublevels are so close in energy for these elements, chromium’s configuration would actually be exactly what Hund’s rule predicts if 3d and 4s had identical energy: six singly occupied orbitals (one 4s and five 3d) minimizes repulsion far more effectively than pairing two electrons together in 4s while leaving 3d less than half full.

Electron Configurations of Ions

Positive ions (cations) form when an atom loses one or more electrons from its outermost (highest-energy) occupied sublevel. Negative ions (anions) form when an atom gains one or more electrons, which are added into the next available orbital following the same Aufbau/Hund rules used for neutral atoms.

Forming cations and anionsCations: remove electrons starting with the highest-energy occupied sublevel. Anions: add electrons into the lowest-energy available empty orbital(s), following Hund’s rule where more than one orbital of equal energy is available.
Which sublevel loses electrons first: main group vs transition metalsFor a typical p-block or s-block atom, electrons are simply removed from the highest n, highest-energy sublevel present (e.g. aluminium: Al is 1s22s22p63s23p1; Al3+ is 1s22s22p6, having lost all three of its 3s and 3p electrons in succession — first the single 3p electron, then the two 3s electrons). For a transition metal, even though the configuration is written with 3d before 4s, the 4s electrons are always removed first when forming an ion, because once occupied, the 3d sublevel drops below 4s in energy (e.g. Cr is [Ar]3d54s1, and Cr3+ is [Ar]3d3, having lost the single 4s electron and two of the five 3d electrons).
Worked example

Deduce the ground-state electron configuration of the Fe3+ ion.
Iron (Z = 26) has the configuration 1s22s22p63s23p63d64s2 (26 electrons total: 2+2+6+2+6+6+2 = 26). Forming Fe3+ means removing 3 electrons: first both 4s electrons, then one 3d electron, giving 1s22s22p63s23p63d5. Note that the 4s electrons are removed before any 3d electrons, even though 3d was written before 4s in the neutral atom’s configuration.

For anions, electrons simply continue filling the next available sublevel: sulfur (1s22s22p63s23p4) gains two electrons to complete its 3p sublevel, giving the sulfide ion S2− (1s22s22p63s23p6), which has the same electron configuration as the noble gas argon. Several common ions of neighbouring elements share an electron configuration with the nearest noble gas in this way (for example Na+, F− and O2− all share neon’s configuration).

Electron Configuration and the Periodic Table

The layout of the periodic table directly reflects the pattern in which sublevels fill, because an element’s position depends on the highest-energy sublevel occupied by its valence electrons.

Blocks of the periodic tableThe s block (groups 1 and 2, plus helium) contains elements whose valence electrons occupy an s sublevel. The p block (groups 13–18, except helium) contains elements with valence electrons in p orbitals. The d block (the transition metals) contains elements with valence electrons filling d orbitals. The f block (the lanthanides and actinides, usually shown as a separate strip below the main table) contains elements filling f orbitals.
Block structure of the periodic tableThe standard periodic table grid, shaded into four connected regions: a narrow two-column block on the far left (s block, groups 1–2), a wide central block (d block, the ten columns of transition metals), a six-column block on the right (p block, groups 13–18), and a separate two-row strip beneath the main table (f block, the lanthanides and actinides). Each period (row) number corresponds to the highest main energy level (n) reached by that row’s valence electrons. Hydrogen and helium are noted as awkward to classify: although their configurations formally place them in the s block, their chemistry is not typical of group 1 or group 2 elements.

Because of this direct link, an element’s electron configuration can be read straight off its position in the periodic table, and vice versa: the period number gives the highest main energy level occupied, the block tells you which type of sublevel is being filled, and the position within the block tells you how many electrons occupy that sublevel. For example, iodine sits in group 17, period 5, in the p block, giving the condensed configuration [Kr]4d105s25p5 — seven valence electrons in total (2 in 5s, 5 in 5p), consistent with its position seven columns into the p block.

The number of electrons in an atom’s outermost occupied main energy level are called its valence electrons, and these are mainly responsible for how the atom bonds with other atoms. The number of valence electrons follows a repeating (periodic) pattern down each group of the table, which underlies many of the recurring chemical trends explored elsewhere in the course.

Calculating Ionization Energy from the Convergence Limit

This section covers material unique to Higher Level: using experimental spectral data to calculate an actual numerical value for an ionization energy, providing direct quantitative support for the energy-level model developed above.

First ionization energyThe first ionization energy of an element is the minimum energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms in their ground state. For hydrogen: H(g) → H+(g) + e−, with the electron removed from the 1s orbital. Once removed, the electron is considered to be at an infinite distance from the nucleus, corresponding to the n = ∞ energy level.

The lines in the ultraviolet series of the hydrogen spectrum (all transitions ending at n = 1) converge at higher frequency. Because this convergence limit corresponds to the transition n = 1 → n = ∞, i.e. ionization, measuring the frequency at which the lines converge allows the ionization energy to be calculated directly, without needing to reach n = ∞ experimentally (which is impossible, since the lines become infinitely close together and merge into a continuum before that point).

Finding the convergence limit graphicallyA table of frequencies, f, for successive lines in the ultraviolet series of hydrogen (transitions from n = 2, 3, 4, 5… down to n = 1), together with Δf, the difference in frequency between each line and the next. As n increases, f increases but Δf steadily shrinks toward zero (the lines are converging). A graph is plotted of f (y-axis) against Δf (x-axis); the points lie close to a straight line, and this line is extrapolated back to the point where Δf = 0. The frequency value at this intercept is the convergence limit, fconvergence, corresponding to the n = 1 → ∞ transition.
Ephoton (per atom) = hfconvergence
Ionization energy (per mole) = hfconvergence × NA
Worked example

Graphical extrapolation of the lines in the ultraviolet series of hydrogen gives a convergence limit of fconvergence = 32.3 × 1014 Hz. Using h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s and NA = 6.02 × 1023 mol−1, calculate the ionization energy of hydrogen.
First find the energy of the photon emitted by one atom at the convergence limit: Ephoton = hf = (6.63 × 10−34) × (32.3 × 1014) = 2.14 × 10−18 J.
This is the energy needed to ionize one atom, so for one mole of atoms: IE = Ephoton × NA = (2.14 × 10−18) × (6.02 × 1023) = 1.29 × 106 J mol−1 = 1290 kJ mol−1.
This is close to the data booklet value of 1312 kJ mol−1, with the small difference arising from the uncertainty of extrapolating a best-fit line from experimental data.

This same graphical method — plotting frequency (or wavelength) of each line against the difference between successive lines, and extrapolating to zero difference — can be applied to the spectrum of any element or ion to find its ionization energy directly from experimental measurements, giving strong, independent, quantitative support for the energy-level model of the atom.

Successive Ionization Energies: Evidence for Energy Levels and Sublevels

Removing electrons from an atom one at a time, in sequence, produces a set of successive ionization energies. These provide some of the strongest evidence for the existence of main energy levels and sublevels, because of the characteristic jumps that appear in the data as electrons are removed from progressively deeper, lower-energy shells.

Successive ionization energiesThe first ionization energy removes an electron from a neutral atom (X(g) → X+(g) + e−); the second ionization energy removes an electron from the resulting 1+ ion (X+(g) → X2+(g) + e−); and so on. Successive ionization energies always increase, because each electron is being removed from an ion of progressively greater positive charge, which attracts the remaining electrons more strongly.

Within a given main energy level, successive ionization energies increase only gradually, because the electrons being removed are all roughly the same distance from the nucleus. However, a large jump occurs whenever an electron must be removed from the next energy level down (closer to the nucleus), because that electron experiences much less shielding from inner electrons and is held far more strongly by the nuclear charge.

Worked example: carbon

The successive ionization energies of carbon (Z = 6, configuration 1s22s22p2), in kJ mol−1, are: 1086, 2352, 4619, 6220, 37 820, 47 280. Explain the pattern.
The first four ionization energies (1086, 2352, 4619, 6220) correspond to the removal, one at a time, of the four n = 2 valence electrons (the two 2p electrons, then the two 2s electrons); these increase steadily but only modestly, since all four electrons are shielded from the nucleus by the same inner 1s2 core. The huge jump to the fifth ionization energy (37 820, roughly six times the fourth value) occurs because this electron must be removed from the 1s sublevel, much closer to the nucleus and shielded by no inner electrons at all. This 4-then-2 split in the data (a big jump after the 4th electron) is direct evidence that carbon has 4 electrons in an outer (n = 2) level and 2 electrons in an inner (n = 1) level.

A closer look at the first few ionization energies of a period 3 element such as aluminium (1s22s22p63s23p1) reveals even finer structure. The data show a 2, 8, 3 pattern overall (matching the 2 + 8 + 3 = 13 electrons in aluminium’s three occupied main levels), with a large jump between the 3rd and 4th ionization energies (as the n = 3 electrons are exhausted and removal moves to n = 2) and a second large jump between the 11th and 12th (as removal moves from n = 2 to n = 1).

Worked example: sub-shell structure within a level

Within aluminium’s 8 electrons in the n = 2 level, a further, smaller jump appears between the 9th and 10th ionization energies overall. Explain why.
The 4th to 9th ionization energies remove the six 2p electrons one at a time (Al3+ is 1s22s22p6, and successive removals empty the 2p sublevel down to Al8+, 1s22s22p1, then Al9+, 1s22s2). The 10th ionization energy must then remove an electron from the lower-energy 2s sublevel (Al9+ → Al10+). Because 2s electrons are, on average, closer to the nucleus and less shielded than 2p electrons, this 10th ionization energy is noticeably larger than the steady increase seen across the 2p removals, confirming that the n = 2 level is itself divided into two sublevels, 2s and 2p, of different energy.

Removing from a doubly occupied orbital vs a singly occupied orbitalWithin a sublevel that is only partly filled, the increase between two successive ionization energies can itself vary in size depending on whether the electron removed comes from a doubly occupied orbital (paired, and therefore experiencing extra electron–electron repulsion that makes it slightly easier to remove) or a singly occupied orbital (unpaired, with no partner to repel it, and therefore slightly harder to remove). For example, in the sequence of ionizations that empty aluminium’s 2p6 sublevel, the step that removes an electron from a doubly occupied 2p orbital (leaving three singly occupied, parallel-spin orbitals, by Hund’s rule) shows a smaller increase than the very next step, which must then remove an electron from one of those singly occupied orbitals.

This same reasoning explains an important general feature of successive ionization data across the whole periodic table: the overall pattern of large jumps (between main energy levels) and smaller, more irregular steps (within a sublevel, reflecting pairing) allows the group number of an unknown element to be deduced directly from a table or graph of its successive ionization energies, since the position of the first large jump reveals the number of valence electrons.

Worked example: deducing a group number

An element has first four ionization energies (kJ mol−1) of 738, 1450, 7730 and 10 550. Deduce the group of the periodic table to which it belongs.
There is a small, steady increase from the 1st to the 2nd ionization energy (738 to 1450), but a very large jump to the 3rd (1450 to 7730, more than a five-fold increase). This shows that only 2 electrons can be removed relatively easily, before the 3rd electron must be pulled from a full, inner energy level. An element with exactly 2 easily removed valence electrons belongs to group 2 of the periodic table (this data in fact corresponds to magnesium).

Trends in First Ionization Energy Across Periods and Down Groups

The same energy-level and sublevel model also explains the overall pattern, and the specific discontinuities, seen when the first ionization energies of consecutive elements are plotted against atomic number.

General trends in first ionization energyFirst ionization energy generally increases from left to right across a period, because the nuclear charge increases while the outer electrons remain in the same main energy level (and so are not significantly better shielded); the increasing attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons makes them progressively harder to remove. First ionization energy decreases down a group, because each successive element’s outermost electron occupies a new main energy level, further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons, and so is more easily removed despite the larger nuclear charge.

Superimposed on this overall trend are two small, regular discontinuities (dips) that repeat in every period, and both are direct evidence for the existence of sublevels of different energy and for the effect of electron pairing.

The group 2 → group 13 dip (s to p)First ionization energy drops slightly on moving from a group 2 element to the group 13 element immediately after it (for example beryllium to boron, or magnesium to aluminium). Group 2 elements have the outer configuration ns2, so their first ionization energy removes an s electron; group 13 elements have the outer configuration ns2np1, so their first ionization energy removes a p electron instead. Because a p sublevel is at a slightly higher energy than the s sublevel of the same main level (p electrons are, on average, slightly further from the nucleus and less tightly held), the p electron is easier to remove, producing the dip.
The group 15 → group 16 dip (paired vs unpaired p electrons)First ionization energy also drops slightly on moving from a group 15 element to the group 16 element immediately after it (for example nitrogen to oxygen, or phosphorus to sulfur). Group 15 elements have the configuration ns2npx1npy1npz1 — three unpaired electrons, one in each p orbital (Hund’s rule). Group 16 elements have the configuration ns2npx2npy1npz1, so the electron removed on ionization comes from a doubly occupied p orbital. Because this electron experiences extra repulsion from its paired partner in the same orbital, it is easier to remove than an electron from a singly occupied (unpaired) orbital, producing the dip.
First ionization energy vs atomic number, periods 1–3A graph of first ionization energy (y-axis, kJ mol−1) against atomic number (x-axis) from Z = 1 to Z = 20. The overall shape is a repeating sawtooth: a steady rise across each period from the group 1 element (a clear local minimum) up to the noble gas at the end of the period (a clear local maximum), followed by a sharp drop to the next period’s group 1 element. Within each rising period, two small dips interrupt the otherwise steady increase: one between the group 2 and group 13 elements, and a second, usually slightly smaller, between the group 15 and group 16 elements.

Together, the overall periodic sawtooth pattern (evidence for distinct main energy levels, since ionization energy resets sharply at the start of every new period) and the two small, regularly repeating dips within each period (evidence for s and p sublevels of different energy, and for the effect of electron pairing within a sublevel) provide some of the clearest experimental support for the entire electron-configuration model developed in this sub-topic.

Successive Ionization Energies of Transition Elements

A further application of successive ionization energy data, unique to Higher Level, is explaining why transition metals commonly show several different, closely spaced oxidation states, in contrast to the single fixed oxidation state typical of an s-block metal.

Calcium vs titanium: contrasting patternsCalcium ([Ar]4s2) shows only the +2 oxidation state in its compounds. Its first two ionization energies (removing the two 4s electrons) are relatively low and similar in size, but there is then a very large jump to the third ionization energy, because the third electron must be removed from the full, inner 3p sublevel. This large jump makes the 3+ (and higher) oxidation states energetically inaccessible, so calcium is restricted to +2.
Titanium ([Ar]3d24s2) shows the +2, +3 and +4 oxidation states. Because the 3d and 4s sublevels are very close in energy, its first four ionization energies (removing the two 4s electrons and then the two 3d electrons) increase only gradually and relatively smoothly from one to the next, with no single very large jump until a fifth electron would have to be removed from the inner 3p sublevel. This gradual increase across several accessible ionization energies is why titanium (and transition metals generally) can display multiple, energetically similar oxidation states, unlike a typical s- or p-block element.
Successive ionization energies of calcium and titaniumA graph of ionization energy (y-axis) against ionization number (x-axis, 1 to 6) for calcium and titanium plotted together. Calcium’s line rises gently from the 1st to the 2nd ionization energy, then jumps sharply and steeply upward to the 3rd, staying high for the 4th, 5th and 6th. Titanium’s line instead rises gradually and fairly evenly across the 1st, 2nd, 3rd and 4th ionization energies (all comparatively low and close together), before itself jumping sharply upward at the 5th ionization energy. The gentler, more gradual slope of titanium’s first four points, compared with calcium’s sharp jump after only two, visually illustrates why titanium supports more oxidation states than calcium.

This pattern generalizes across the d block: variable oxidation states are common precisely because several successive 3d (and 4s) electrons can be removed at broadly comparable energy costs, whereas s- and p-block elements typically show one dominant, low-energy ionization step (or a small, fixed number of them) followed immediately by a very large jump into an inaccessible inner shell.

Guiding Question Revisited

How can we model the energy states of electrons in atoms?

  • Electromagnetic radiation can be described by a wave model (c = fλ) or a particle model (E = hf); both are needed to explain its full behaviour, and the same wave–particle duality applies to electrons.
  • A line emission spectrum, rather than a continuous one, is direct evidence that electron energies in an atom are quantized; the Planck equation links the frequency of each line to the energy gap between two allowed levels.
  • The Bohr model explains the hydrogen spectrum but fails for multi-electron atoms; the Schrödinger (wave mechanical) model describes electrons probabilistically as atomic orbitals, consistent with the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle.
  • The Aufbau principle, Hund’s rule and the Pauli exclusion principle together determine the ground-state electron configuration of any atom or ion up to Z = 36, with chromium and copper as notable exceptions and with 4s electrons always removed before 3d electrons when transition metal ions form.
  • The periodic table’s block structure (s, p, d, f) directly reflects which sublevel is being filled, linking electron configuration to periodic position.
  • Unique to Higher Level: the convergence limit of a line spectrum can be used to calculate an ionization energy directly (IE = hfconvergence × NA), and the detailed pattern of successive and first ionization energies — the large jumps between main levels, the smaller jumps between sublevels, and the small dips caused by sublevel energy differences and electron pairing — provides quantitative experimental evidence for the entire energy-level model, and explains why transition metals show multiple accessible oxidation states while calcium and other s-block metals do not.
