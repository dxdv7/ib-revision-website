Guiding Question

What role do bonding and structure have in the design of materials?

Throughout history, humans have transformed raw materials into more useful ones: smelting copper ore in the Bronze Age, extracting iron in the blast furnaces of the Industrial Revolution. As our models of chemical bonding have become more sophisticated, our ability to predict — and deliberately design — the properties of materials has advanced with them. Innovations such as superconductors, breathable fabrics and liquid crystals exist only because scientists could apply detailed bonding theory to their synthesis. Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding were introduced earlier as three separate categories, but this sub-topic builds a single, unified picture: the bonding triangle, which treats these three models as extremes of one continuum. It then applies that picture to two important classes of real material — alloys and polymers — and, at Higher Level, extends the polymer story to condensation polymers, the class that includes nylon, polyester fabrics and the proteins and nucleic acids of living cells.

Bonding as a Continuum: the Bonding Triangle

Real materials rarely show pure ionic, pure covalent or pure metallic bonding. Most substances lie somewhere between these three extremes and show a blend of characteristics — a pattern already seen with polar covalent bonds, which show partial ionic character. A more realistic model represents bonding as a continuum using a triangle, with each vertex representing 100% of one bonding type: ionic, covalent or metallic. Any element or compound can be plotted at a position inside, or on an edge of, the triangle according to how much of each bonding type it shows.

The bonding triangleAn equilateral triangle with three labelled vertices: “ionic” at the top, “metallic” at the bottom left, and “covalent” at the bottom right. The horizontal axis along the base represents average electronegativity, increasing from left (low, metallic) to right (high, covalent). The vertical axis (height) represents electronegativity difference, increasing from zero at the base to a large value at the top (ionic) vertex. Substances near the top behave as ionic compounds; those near the bottom left behave as metals or alloys; those near the bottom right behave as covalent, usually molecular, substances; and those in between, such as “polar covalent” partway between the covalent and ionic corners, show blended, intermediate properties.

A substance’s position is calculated from electronegativity values (given in the data booklet) using two combined quantities:

x-axis: average electronegativity, Σχ = (χA + χB) ÷ 2
y-axis: electronegativity difference, Δχ = χA − χB

where χA and χB are the Pauling electronegativity values of the two bonded elements (A is normally taken as the more electronegative element, so Δχ is positive). Because the two “bonded” atoms in an element are identical, every element has Δχ = 0 and so lies on the horizontal base of the triangle — metals towards the low-electronegativity (left) end, non-metals towards the high-electronegativity (right) end. Compounds with a large electronegativity difference plot near the top (ionic); compounds with a small difference and high average electronegativity plot in the bottom right corner (covalent).

Worked example: Cs, F2 and CsFLocate caesium, fluorine and caesium fluoride in the bonding triangle, given χ(Cs) = 0.8 and χ(F) = 4.0.
Cs: average = 0.8, difference = 0.8 − 0.8 = 0.0 → extreme left of the base (100% metallic; caesium has the lowest electronegativity of any element).
F2: average = 4.0, difference = 0.0 → extreme right of the base (100% covalent).
CsF: average = (4.0 + 0.8) ÷ 2 = 2.4, difference = 4.0 − 0.8 = 3.2 → the very top of the triangle (100% ionic) — in fact the usual reference point for the most ionic compound possible, pairing the most electropositive metal with the most electronegative non-metal.
Worked example: diamond, silicon dioxide and bronzeLocate diamond (carbon, χ = 2.6), silicon dioxide (Si χ = 1.9, O χ = 3.4) and bronze (an alloy of Cu χ = 1.9 and Sn χ = 2.0).
Diamond: average = 2.6, difference = 0.0 (an element, so it lies on the base, well into the covalent region).
SiO2: average = (1.9 + 3.4) ÷ 2 = 2.65, difference = 3.4 − 1.9 = 1.5 → substantial covalent character with some ionic character, consistent with SiO2 being a giant covalent (not simple molecular) structure.
Bronze (Cu/Sn): average = (1.9 + 2.0) ÷ 2 = 1.95, difference = 2.0 − 1.9 = 0.1 → very close to the base, deep in the metallic corner, consistent with bronze being an alloy.

Using the Bonding Triangle to Predict Properties

Once a substance’s position is known, its likely properties can be predicted from the corner it lies closest to. Substances near the ionic vertex are expected to have high melting points and to conduct electricity only when molten or dissolved. Substances near the metallic vertex are expected to conduct electricity as solids and to be malleable. Substances near the covalent vertex are expected to be non-conductors and, if simple molecular, to have low melting and boiling points.

The period 3 oxides illustrate this well. Moving across period 3, each element’s electronegativity increases and gets closer to that of oxygen (χ = 3.4), so Δχ between the element and oxygen steadily falls, and the oxides become progressively less ionic and more covalent.

OxidesBondingProperties
Na2O, MgO, Al2O3Ionic (metallic oxides)High melting/boiling points; conduct electricity when molten
SiO2Giant covalent (oxide of a metalloid)High melting/boiling point; does not conduct electricity
P4O10, SO2, Cl2OCovalent (non-metallic oxides)Low melting/boiling points; do not conduct electricity

The bonding triangle also exposes the limits of treating “metal + non-metal = ionic” as a hard rule. Aluminium chloride, AlCl3, contains a metal bonded to a non-metal, which might suggest a typically ionic, high-melting solid, yet its melting point (192°C, sublimes) is far below that of a genuinely ionic chloride such as NaCl (801°C).

Worked example: NaCl vs AlCl3Compare the bonding character of NaCl and AlCl3 using electronegativity data (χ(Na) = 0.9, χ(Al) = 1.6, χ(Cl) = 3.2).
NaCl: average = (0.9 + 3.2) ÷ 2 = 2.05, difference = 3.2 − 0.9 = 2.3 → upper-centre of the triangle, approximately 75% ionic.
AlCl3: average = (1.6 + 3.2) ÷ 2 = 2.4, difference = 3.2 − 1.6 = 1.6 → close to the ionic/covalent border, approximately 50% covalent.
The IB syllabus does not require a numerical percentage-ionic-character calculation here — the triangle position is used qualitatively. AlCl3’s much greater covalent character explains its far lower melting point and poor electrical conductivity even in the liquid state, compared with the strongly ionic NaCl.
Composite materials vs a blended position in the triangleA composite (e.g. reinforced concrete or fibreglass) is a heterogeneous mixture of at least two separate materials, each keeping its own individual properties and its own separate position in the bonding triangle (ionic concrete reinforced with metallic steel rebar, for instance). This differs from a single compound with an intermediate bonding character, which has one blended position because its electrons are genuinely shared between ionic and covalent character throughout the whole structure.

HL: the Limits of a Simple Binary Classification

Using only the two bonded elements’ electronegativities, as in the previous section, works well for simple binary compounds, but it is still only a first approximation, and two further examples show where it can mislead.

Worked example: LaBr3 vs SbBr3Lanthanum and antimony both form tribromides with a similar formula (LaBr3 and SbBr3), yet they have very different bonding. Using approximate data-booklet electronegativities χ(La) = 1.1, χ(Sb) = 2.05, χ(Br) = 2.96:
LaBr3: average = (1.1 + 2.96) ÷ 2 = 2.03, difference = 2.96 − 1.1 = 1.86 → well towards the ionic vertex. Lanthanum has an electronegativity similar to the group 2 metals, so LaBr3 behaves as a typical ionic solid: a crystalline lattice that is hard but brittle, since a shearing force brings like-charged ions into contact and the layers repel apart.
SbBr3: average = (2.05 + 2.96) ÷ 2 = 2.505, difference = 2.96 − 2.05 = 0.91 → much closer to the covalent region, since antimony (a metalloid) has an electronegativity much closer to that of bromine. SbBr3 behaves as a simple covalent molecular substance with a much lower melting point.
Two compounds with the same general formula, Mχ3, can therefore sit in very different positions in the bonding triangle, and have correspondingly different properties.
When even the triangle is not enough: MgB2Magnesium diboride, MgB2, plots at a position with substantial metallic and covalent character (using χ(Mg) ≈ 1.3 and χ(B) ≈ 2.0, it sits roughly midway between the metallic and covalent corners). In reality its structure is more subtle than any single point on the triangle can show: the boron atoms form covalently bonded hexagonal sheets, similar to the layers in graphite, while Mg2+ ions and delocalized electrons sit between these sheets providing metallic conductivity. This layered hybrid structure is also an unusually effective superconductor at low temperature — a property that cannot be predicted just from a single electronegativity-based position. The bonding triangle is a very useful simplification, but, like any classification system, it cannot capture every structural detail of every real material.

Alloys

AlloyAn alloy is a homogeneous mixture containing at least one metal, combined with one or more other metals or non-metals (such as carbon), held together throughout by metallic bonding.

Alloys are made by mixing metals together while molten, so the different types of atom can mix freely, and then allowing the mixture to solidify as a single, uniform solid. Because metallic bonding relies on a “sea” of delocalized electrons attracting a lattice of cations in all directions (non-directional bonding), the lattice can readily accommodate cations of different sizes without the strict directional requirements of ionic or covalent bonding — this is exactly what makes alloying possible.

Structure of an alloyA regular grid of large, identically sized positive metal ions (a pure metal) is shown beside a second grid in which smaller cations of a different metal are inserted into some of the gaps between the larger cations. In both grids, a shaded background of small negative symbols represents the sea of delocalized electrons flowing freely around every positive ion and holding the whole structure together, regardless of which size of cation each electron happens to be near.

Introducing atoms of a different size into the regular lattice of a pure metal disrupts its orderly rows of identical ions. In a pure metal, these rows slide over each other relatively easily under an applied force, which is why pure metals are often soft and easily bent. In an alloy, the irregular packing caused by differently sized ions jams against itself and makes it much harder for layers to slide past each other, so alloys are typically harder, stronger and often more corrosion-resistant than the pure metals they are made from. Steel, for example — iron alloyed with under 2% carbon plus small amounts of manganese and other elements — can be around 1000 times stronger than pure iron.

Why alloys resist deformationTwo side-by-side diagrams show a metal lattice under an applied force. In the pure metal, uniform rows of identical circles slide smoothly past one another when the force is applied, changing the metal’s shape easily. In the alloy, larger and smaller circles of two different elements are mixed within the same layers; when the same force is applied, the irregular sizes jam against each other and resist sliding, so the alloy holds its shape far more strongly.

Because alloys are mixtures rather than compounds, they have no fixed composition and cannot be written with a single chemical formula: “steel” names a whole family of iron-carbon(-and other element) mixtures of varying proportions, not one substance. Since no new chemical bonds are broken or formed between the different metal elements during mixing (it is a purely physical process), each component of an alloy keeps typical metallic properties such as electrical and thermal conductivity, and, where iron is present, magnetism.

Polymers

Polymer, monomerA polymer (or macromolecule) is a very large molecule built from many repeating subunits, called monomers, joined together by covalent bonds. Polymers vary enormously in the identity of their monomer and in the length and branching of the resulting chain, giving a huge range of possible properties.

Because a polymer chain can contain many thousands of repeat units, its structure is normally drawn as a single repeating unit in brackets, with open bonds at each end to show the chain continues, and a subscript n outside the bracket representing the large, variable number of repeat units in an actual chain.

General repeating-unit notationA single monomer unit is drawn with a central carbon atom bonded to two identical side groups labelled X. This unit is then shown enclosed in large square brackets, with a short bond line extending outward from each side of the bracket to show the chain continues indefinitely in both directions, and a small subscript n placed just outside the bottom-right corner of the bracket to represent the large, repeating number of these units making up the full chain.

Polymers occur throughout the living world: proteins, starch and DNA are all natural polymers, and understanding how their structure relates to their biological function has driven major advances in medicine, from vaccines to new drugs. Synthetic polymers first became widely available in the 1930s once raw materials from the petroleum industry were accessible, rapidly giving rise to modern plastics.

Plastics and the Environment

Plastics are valued for being lightweight, unreactive, water-resistant and, in some cases, very strong — exactly why they are used everywhere in packaging, construction, clothing, transport and machinery. Unfortunately, the same unreactivity and water resistance that make plastics useful also make most of them non-biodegradable: they cannot be broken down by natural biological processes and so persist in the environment, sometimes for centuries, contributing heavily to pollution on land and in the oceans.

Biodegradable plasticsA biodegradable compound can be broken down by microorganisms into simple products already found in nature (typically carbon dioxide and water) and so does not accumulate as pollution. Biodegradable plastics are often plant-based (e.g. made from corn starch, lignin or cellulose); others are petroleum-based but contain catalysts that assist their breakdown. Compostable plastics break down under the specific warm, moist, microbially active conditions of a compost pile.

Reducing plastic pollution is not solved by any single strategy: better recycling, greater use of biodegradable materials, and — most importantly — reducing overall production and single-use consumption of plastic all play complementary roles.

Addition Polymers

Addition polymerAn addition polymer forms when the double bond in each monomer molecule breaks, creating two new bonding positions that link the monomer to its neighbours on either side, building up a long chain. No other product is formed — the polymer is the only product of an addition polymerization reaction.

Alkenes, which contain a carbon–carbon double bond, are reactive at that double bond and so readily act as monomers for addition polymerization. As the double bond in each monomer opens up, its two carbon atoms each gain a new single bond, one to each of its neighbouring monomer units in the growing chain.

Forming an addition polymer from etheneThree separate ethene molecules are drawn side by side, each with its carbon–carbon double bond turned to point vertically so that the two hydrogen atoms on each carbon point out horizontally to the left and right, like a person standing with arms outstretched. Curved arrows show each double bond opening into a single bond, with a new single bond forming between the right-hand carbon of one monomer and the left-hand carbon of its neighbour, joining the three units end-to-end into a continuous zig-zag chain, with open bonds extending off each end to show the process continues indefinitely.
MonomerPolymerRepeating unit
Ethene, CH2=CH2Poly(ethene)(–CH2–CH2–)n
Propene, CH2=CHCH3Poly(propene)(–CH2–CH(CH3)–)n
Chloroethene, CH2=CHClPoly(chloroethene), PVC(–CH2–CHCl–)n

To find the repeating unit from a monomer, change the C=C double bond into a C–C single bond and place the resulting unit in brackets with subscript n; to find the monomer from a given repeating unit, reverse the process — identify the two carbon atoms that connect to neighbouring units and convert that single bond back into a double bond.

Atom Economy of Addition Polymerization

Atom economyAtom economy measures what proportion of the total mass of reactants ends up in the desired product, based on the reaction’s stoichiometry.
% atom economy = (molar mass of desired product ÷ molar mass of all reactants) × 100

In addition polymerization, the only product is the polymer itself — every atom present in the monomers ends up in the chain, with no by-product. This means addition polymerization always has an atom economy of exactly 100%.

Worked exampleShow that the polymerization of ethene (C2H4, M = 28 g mol−1) to form poly(ethene), (–C2H4–)n, has 100% atom economy.
Reactant mass: n monomers of C2H4, total molar mass = 28n g mol−1.
Product mass: the polymer chain also has molar mass 28n g mol−1, since every atom from the monomers is incorporated with nothing left over.
% atom economy = (28n ÷ 28n) × 100 = 100%.
Common mistakeA 100% atom economy does not mean an addition polymerization process has no environmental impact. A reaction yield below 100% still leaves unreacted monomer, and the solvents, catalysts and high temperatures/pressures often used in industrial polymerization all carry their own environmental costs that must be assessed separately from atom economy.

HL: Condensation Polymers

Condensation polymerA condensation polymer forms when a functional group on one monomer reacts with a functional group on a neighbouring monomer to form a new covalent bond, with a small molecule (commonly H2O, HCl or NH3) released for every bond formed.

To act as a monomer for condensation polymerization, a molecule must have two reactive functional groups, one at each “end,” so that it can form a new bond with a neighbour on both sides — much as a person can only join a human chain once they have two free hands available to link with the people on either side of them.

Addition polymers vs condensation polymersAddition polymers form from monomers with a carbon–carbon double bond that simply opens to link neighbours; the polymer is the only product, so atom economy is always 100%. Condensation polymers form from monomers bearing two separate functional groups that react with those of their neighbours; a small molecule is released for every bond formed, so the polymer is not the only product and atom economy is always below 100%.

Polyesters

When one monomer carries two carboxylic acid (–COOH) groups and the other carries two alcohol (–OH) groups, an ester linkage forms between them with the loss of a water molecule at each linkage. Because the chain extends in both directions through repeated ester links, the resulting polymer is called a polyester.

Forming a polyester linkTwo monomers are drawn as boxes (representing the rest of each molecule) with their functional groups sticking outward: the left-hand box has a –COOH group projecting from each side, and the right-hand box has a –OH group projecting from each side. An arrow shows the acid group on the right side of the left box reacting with the alcohol group on the left side of the right box, forming a new –CO–O– ester link between the two boxes and releasing one H2O molecule, while a –COOH group and a –OH group remain free on the outer, unreacted sides, ready to link with further neighbouring monomers.

Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (HOOC–C6H4–COOH) and ethane-1,2-diol (HO–CH2–CH2–OH) react together in this way to give the repeating unit (–CO–C6H4–CO–O–CH2–CH2–O–)n. This polyester, polyethylene terephthalate (PET), is widely used in synthetic fibres, plastic bottles and other containers. Other polyesters include the natural polymers found in fats and oils, and the phosphodiester links that hold together the nucleic acids DNA and RNA.

Worked example: atom economy of forming PETBenzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid, C8H6O4, has M = 166 g mol−1; ethane-1,2-diol, C2H6O2, has M = 62 g mol−1. Calculate the atom economy of forming one repeating unit of PET, C10H8O4 (M = 192 g mol−1).
Total reactant mass = 166 + 62 = 228 g mol−1.
Each repeating unit contains two ester linkages, so two molecules of H2O (2 × 18 = 36 g mol−1) are released per repeating unit formed: 228 − 36 = 192 g mol−1, matching the given product mass.
% atom economy = (192 ÷ 228) × 100 = 84.2%.
This is well below the 100% of addition polymerization, because mass is lost from the reactants as water with every linkage formed.

Polyamides

When one monomer carries two carboxylic acid (–COOH) groups and the other carries two amine (–NH2) groups, an amide linkage forms between them, again with the loss of a water molecule at each linkage. The resulting polymer is a polyamide.

The most common form of nylon, 6,6-nylon, is named because both of its monomers contain six carbon atoms: 1,6-diaminohexane, H2N–(CH2)6–NH2, and hexanedioic acid, HOOC–(CH2)4–COOH. These combine to give the repeating unit (–NH–(CH2)6–NH–CO–(CH2)4–CO–)n. Kevlar®, a much stiffer and stronger polyamide used in body armour and reinforced tyres, is made instead from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid and 1,4-diaminobenzene, giving the repeating unit (–NH–C6H4–NH–CO–C6H4–CO–)n; the rigid, flat benzene rings in place of flexible –(CH2)n– chains allow Kevlar® chains to pack and hydrogen-bond together unusually tightly, giving the polymer its exceptional strength.

Amino acids and proteinsA polyamide can also form from a single type of monomer if each molecule carries one carboxylic acid group and one amine group — this describes an amino acid. Proteins are natural polyamides built by condensation reactions between amino acid monomers, releasing one H2O molecule for every amide (peptide) bond formed.
Worked example: deducing monomers from a repeating unitPoly(ethylene furanoate), PEF, is a bio-based polyester whose repeating unit contains a five-membered furan ring (four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom in the ring) in place of the benzene ring found in PET, with a –CO– group attached at each of the ring’s two substitutable carbons, an –O– linking to a –CH2–CH2– chain, and a second –O– closing the repeat. Deduce the two monomers used to make PEF.
To reverse a condensation polymerization, find each ester link (–CO–O–) in the repeating unit and break it, adding back one H2O molecule across the broken bond: the carbon side regains a –COOH group and the oxygen side regains a –OH group.
Breaking both ester links in the PEF repeating unit regenerates furan-2,5-dicarboxylic acid (the furan ring bearing a –COOH group at each of its two substitutable positions) and ethane-1,2-diol, HO–CH2–CH2–OH — the same diol used to make PET.

Hydrolysis: the Reverse of Condensation

Just as condensation polymerization releases a small molecule for every bond formed, breaking a condensation polymer back down into its monomers requires that same small molecule — usually water — to be added back across each bond broken. This reverse reaction is called hydrolysis.

HydrolysisHydrolysis is a reaction in which a covalent bond in a molecule is broken by the addition of a water molecule across that bond, regenerating the two functional groups that originally combined to form it.

Hydrolysis reactions are essential to living organisms: digestion breaks down dietary polyamides (proteins) and polysaccharides such as starch into their constituent monomers, which can then be absorbed and used by the body, while decomposition and the mobilization of stored food reserves (for example, in a germinating seed) rely on the same chemistry. Both condensation and hydrolysis reactions in living systems are catalysed by biological catalysts called enzymes.

Guiding Question Revisited

  • Bonding in real materials is best described as a continuum between ionic, covalent and metallic extremes, represented by the bonding triangle, whose axes are calculated from the average electronegativity and electronegativity difference of the bonded elements.
  • A substance’s position in the triangle allows its properties to be predicted; classifying a compound as simply “ionic” or “covalent” from its component elements alone can be misleading, as AlCl3, LaBr3 vs SbBr3, and MgB2 all show.
  • Alloys are homogeneous mixtures containing at least one metal, held together by non-directional metallic bonding, which allows differently sized cations to be incorporated into a single lattice, typically making the alloy stronger, harder and more corrosion-resistant than the pure metal.
  • Polymers are macromolecules built from repeating monomer units joined by covalent bonds; they include both natural polymers (proteins, starch, DNA) and synthetic polymers (plastics).
  • Addition polymers form when the double bond in an alkene monomer opens to create new bonding positions, joining monomers into a chain with no other product formed, giving 100% atom economy.
  • At Higher Level, condensation polymers form when monomers with two functional groups each react to form new covalent bonds, releasing a small molecule (typically H2O) for every bond formed; polyesters form from diacids and diols, and polyamides form from diacids and diamines (or from amino acids), always with an atom economy below 100%.
  • Condensation polymers can be broken back down into their monomers by hydrolysis, the reverse reaction, in which a water molecule is added across each bond broken.
  • The widespread use of plastics brings major practical benefits but also significant environmental costs, since most plastics are not biodegradable and accumulate in the environment.
