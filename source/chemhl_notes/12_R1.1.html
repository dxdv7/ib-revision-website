Guiding Question

What can be deduced from the temperature change that accompanies chemical or physical change?

Every chemical reaction is accompanied by an energy change, and the law of conservation of energy tells us that this energy is never created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed. Our bodies depend on the energy changes of respiration; modern life depends on the energy released when fuels burn. Much of this chapter is about a single practical skill: how a measured temperature change, usually of a known mass of water, can be converted into a quantitative enthalpy change for a reaction, and how the inevitable imperfections of a real experiment – heat loss, incomplete combustion, the heat capacity of the apparatus itself – explain why an experimental value almost always differs from the accepted literature value.

Heat, Temperature and Systems

Energy is a measure of the ability to do work, and can be transferred in different ways, including heat, light, sound and electricity, and stored in different forms of potential energy such as chemical potential energy. It is essential to distinguish between heat and temperature.

Heat versus temperatureHeat is a process of energy transfer that occurs as a result of a temperature difference; it is not a property of an object itself. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in an object, and is a property of that object. When heat is transferred to a system, the average kinetic energy of its particles, and therefore its temperature, increases.

More heat energy is needed to raise the temperature of the water in a swimming pool by 1 K than for the water in a kettle, because the swimming pool contains far more water molecules; the same amount of heat is shared among a larger number of particles, giving a smaller average increase in kinetic energy and hence a smaller temperature rise.

System and surroundings

The system is the sample or reaction vessel of interest; the surroundings are, in theory, everything else in the universe. This distinction lets chemists describe energy transfers that occur in test tubes, polystyrene cups, industrial plants and living cells alike.

Open, closed and isolated systemsAn open system can exchange both matter and energy with its surroundings – for example, a reaction in an open test tube, where gases and heat can escape freely. A closed system can exchange energy, but not matter, with its surroundings. An isolated system cannot exchange either matter or energy with its surroundings.

Although energy can be exchanged between a closed system and its surroundings, the law of conservation of energy means that the total energy of the system plus surroundings cannot change: any energy lost by the system is gained by the surroundings, and vice versa.

Enthalpy and the Sign of ΔH

EnthalpyThe chemical potential energy of a system is called its enthalpy, H, a name derived from the Greek for ‘heat inside’. A system acts like a reservoir of chemical potential energy. Changes in enthalpy are denoted ΔH.

When heat is added to a system from the surroundings, the enthalpy of the system increases, and ΔH is positive. When heat is released from the system to the surroundings, the enthalpy of the system decreases, and ΔH is negative.

Two horizontal levels are shown, labelled ‘before’ on the left and ‘after’ on the right, joined by an upward step. The ‘after’ level is higher than the ‘before’ level, and an arrow labelled ‘heat energy’ points from a box labelled ‘surroundings’ into a box labelled ‘system’, showing heat flowing in and the potential energy of the system rising.
Enthalpy change when heat is released from a system (ΔH negative)The same style of diagram, but now the ‘after’ level is lower than the ‘before’ level, and the arrow labelled ‘heat energy’ points from the box labelled ‘system’ out to the box labelled ‘surroundings’, showing heat flowing out and the potential energy of the system falling.
Exothermic versus endothermic reactionsAn exothermic reaction gives out heat to the surroundings; ΔHreaction is negative. Most chemical reactions, including most combustion reactions and all neutralization reactions, are exothermic. An endothermic reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings; ΔHreaction is positive, and the products have more stored potential energy than the reactants.

When a reaction occurs in aqueous solution, its enthalpy change is best observed through the temperature change of the water acting as the solvent. In an exothermic reaction, heat released by the reaction is first transferred to the solvent, so the temperature of the water increases; heat is then gradually transferred from the water to the wider surroundings as the system returns to its initial temperature. The net result is a transfer of heat from the system to the surroundings, and the enthalpy of the system decreases. In an endothermic reaction, heat needed by the reaction is taken from the water, so the temperature of the aqueous solvent decreases.

Temperature–time profile of an exothermic reaction in solutionA graph of temperature on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis. The line starts flat at room temperature, rises sharply once the reactants are mixed to reach a maximum temperature, and then falls gradually back towards room temperature as heat is lost to the wider surroundings – showing that heat is first transferred to the water (causing the rise) and then from the water to the surroundings (causing the slow decline).

Energy Profiles, Stability and Activation Energy

There is a natural direction for change: reactions tend to proceed in the direction of lower potential energy, or greater stability, in the same way that a person slipping on a ladder falls down rather than up. Methane is expected to burn because the products, carbon dioxide and water, are more stable (lower in potential energy) than the reactant mixture of methane and oxygen.

Energy profile of an exothermic reactionA graph with potential energy, H, on the vertical axis and reaction coordinate on the horizontal axis. The reactants are drawn as a horizontal level on the left at a higher potential energy, and the products as a horizontal level on the right at a lower potential energy, with a curve rising to a peak (the transition state) before falling to the product level. The vertical drop from reactants to products is labelled ΔH, and is negative because the products are lower in energy than the reactants.
Energy profile of an endothermic reactionThe same style of graph, but with the products level drawn higher than the reactants level, again joined by a curve rising to a peak before descending slightly to the still-elevated product level. The vertical rise from reactants to products is labelled ΔH, and is positive because the products are higher in energy than the reactants.

In both profiles, the height of the curve above the reactants level, up to the peak, is the activation energy, Ea – the minimum kinetic energy the reactants need in order to react, since some bonds in the reactants must be broken before new bonds in the products can form. A lighted match provides the activation energy needed for methane to react with oxygen.

Stability is relative, not absoluteStability is always relative to a particular comparison, not an absolute property. Hydrogen peroxide, for example, is stable with respect to its elements (hydrogen and oxygen), but unstable relative to its decomposition products, water and oxygen. The sign of ΔH is a useful guide to the likely direction of change, but not a complete one: some endothermic reactions do occur spontaneously, and ΔH gives no information about the rate of a reaction – a thermodynamically favourable reaction may still occur too slowly to observe if its activation energy is high.

Standard Enthalpy Changes: Formation and Combustion

Standard conditionsThe standard enthalpy change, ΔH°, is measured under standard conditions: a pressure of 100 kPa, a concentration of 1 mol dm−3 for all solutions, and all substances in their standard states. A temperature of 298 K is usually specified, although temperature is not technically part of the definition of standard state.

A thermochemical equation is a balanced chemical equation, including state symbols, together with the enthalpy change for the reaction as written. State symbols must always be included, because the enthalpy change of a reaction depends on the physical states of the reactants and products.

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)    ΔH°reaction = −890 kJ mol−1

This is shorthand for: one mole of gaseous methane reacts with two moles of gaseous oxygen to give one mole of gaseous carbon dioxide and two moles of liquid water, releasing 890 kJ of heat energy under standard conditions.

Two particular standard enthalpy changes are used so often that they are given their own symbols and will be developed fully in the next chapter, Reactivity 1.2, where they become the basis of entire energy cycles.

Standard enthalpy change of formation, ΔHf°The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is formed from its elements in their standard states, under standard conditions. Because no chemical change occurs when an element in its standard state is ‘formed’ from itself, ΔHf° of any element in its standard state is zero (for example, ΔHf°[O2(g)] = 0, but ΔHf°[O3(g)] ≠ 0, since ozone is not the standard state of oxygen).
Standard enthalpy change of combustion, ΔHc°The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely combusted in excess oxygen, under standard conditions. Combustion reactions are generally exothermic, so ΔHc° values are generally negative.

Worked example

Write the thermochemical equation that corresponds to the standard enthalpy of formation of ethanol, given ΔHf°(C2H5OH) = −278 kJ mol−1.

Ethanol, C2H5OH, is built from the elements C(graphite), H2(g) and O2(g) in their standard states. Balancing carefully for one mole of product (fractional coefficients are allowed, since ΔHf° always refers to one mole of the substance formed):

2C(graphite) + 3H2(g) + ½O2(g) → C2H5OH(l)  ΔH° = −278 kJ mol−1

Calculating Enthalpy Changes from Temperature Changes

The increase in temperature, ΔT, when an object is heated depends on the mass of the object, the heat added, and the nature of the substance. The specific heat capacity, c, is the heat needed to raise the temperature of a unit mass of a substance by 1 K.

heat added (q) = mass (m) × specific heat capacity (c) × temperature change (ΔT)
q (J) = m (g) × c (J g−1 K−1) × ΔT (K)

Here, q is the heat energy transferred, in joules; m is the mass of the substance being heated or cooled, usually the mass of water in a calorimetry experiment, in grams; c is the specific heat capacity of that substance, in J g−1 K−1; and ΔT is the change in temperature, in kelvin (a temperature change of 1 K is identical to a change of 1°C, so there is no need to convert units of temperature change when using this equation). For water, c = 4.18 J g−1 K−1, a value given in the data booklet.

Worked example

How much heat is released when 10.0 g of copper, with specific heat capacity 0.385 J g−1 °C−1, is cooled from 85.0°C to 25.0°C?

q = m × c × ΔT = 10.0 g × 0.385 J g−1 °C−1 × (−60.0°C) = −231 J (the value is negative because the copper has lost heat).

Measuring Enthalpy Changes of Combustion

The enthalpy change of combustion, ΔHc, of a liquid fuel such as ethanol can be determined by burning it beneath a metal calorimeter containing a known mass of water. Copper is often used for the calorimeter because it conducts heat well. The heat given out by the flame heats the water, and the heat absorbed by the water is calculated from its temperature change and mass; assuming that all the heat released is transferred to the water, the heat absorbed by the water is taken to equal the heat released by combustion.

Apparatus for measuring the enthalpy of combustion of a liquid fuelA copper calorimeter containing a known mass of water sits on a clamp stand, with a thermometer inserted into the water to measure its temperature. Below the calorimeter is a burner containing the liquid fuel, with a wick that produces a flame directly under the calorimeter. A draught shield and insulating card surround the apparatus to reduce heat loss to the surroundings and to shield the flame from air currents.
ΔHc = −m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) ÷ n(fuel)

Here, m(H2O) is the mass of water heated, c(H2O) is the specific heat capacity of water, ΔT(H2O) is the temperature rise of the water, and n(fuel) is the amount, in moles, of fuel burnt (found from its mass and molar mass). The negative sign is included because the enthalpy change of the reaction is opposite in sign to the temperature change of the water: ΔT is positive for an exothermic reaction, but ΔH itself is negative.

Worked example

Calculate the enthalpy of combustion of ethanol from the following data, assuming all the heat from the reaction is absorbed by the water: mass of water in copper calorimeter = 200.00 g; temperature increase of water = 13.00°C; mass of ethanol burnt = 0.45 g.

M(C2H5OH) = (12.01 × 2) + (6 × 1.01) + 16.00 = 46.08 g mol−1; n(C2H5OH) = 0.45 / 46.08 = 0.00977 mol

ΔHc = −(200.00 g × 4.18 J g−1 °C−1 × 13.00°C) ÷ 0.00977 mol = −10 868 J ÷ 0.00977 mol

= −1 112 000 J mol−1 ≈ −1100 kJ mol−1 (to 2 significant figures, limited by the precision of the mass of ethanol)

The data booklet value is −1367 kJ mol−1. The experimental value is less exothermic because: not all the heat produced is transferred to the water; some heat is absorbed by the copper calorimeter itself and some passes to the surroundings; combustion may be incomplete owing to limited available oxygen (rather than complete, as assumed by the literature value); and the experiment was not performed under standard conditions.

Qualitative evidence for incomplete combustionEvidence of incomplete combustion, such as black soot on the underside of the calorimeter or a yellow, sooty flame, should always be recorded as qualitative data alongside the quantitative temperature measurements, since it helps explain why an experimental ΔHc value is less exothermic than the literature value.

Measuring Enthalpy Changes of Reactions in Solution

The enthalpy change of a reaction occurring in aqueous solution can be found by carrying out the reaction inside an insulated container, such as a polystyrene cup, and measuring the resulting temperature change of the water acting as the solvent. Polystyrene is used because it is a very good thermal insulator with a low heat capacity, which maximizes the fraction of heat transferred between the reaction and the water rather than lost to the surroundings.

Correcting for heat loss by extrapolation

One of the largest sources of error in a polystyrene-cup experiment is heat loss to the surroundings as soon as the temperature rises above room temperature. Consider the exothermic displacement reaction Cu2+(aq) + Zn(s) → Cu(s) + Zn2+(aq): once zinc powder is added to copper(II) sulfate solution, the temperature rises to a maximum and then falls approximately linearly as heat is lost to the surroundings.

Temperature–time graph with extrapolation for heat lossA graph of temperature on the vertical axis against time on the horizontal axis. The line is flat at room temperature until zinc is added; after this point it rises steeply to a recorded maximum temperature and then declines slowly and linearly as heat is lost to the surroundings. A dashed straight line is drawn back through this linear cooling section, extrapolated backward to the exact time at which the zinc was added; the temperature where this dashed line crosses that time is higher than the recorded maximum, and represents the maximum temperature that would have been reached if the reaction had occurred instantaneously with no heat loss.
ΔHreaction = −m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) ÷ n(limiting reactant)

Assuming no heat loss from the system, all the heat produced passes from the reaction to the water, the solution is dilute enough that its volume equals the volume of water present, and water has a density of 1.00 g cm−3, the molar enthalpy change of the reaction can be calculated by dividing the heat released by the amount, in moles, of the limiting reactant.

Worked example: a displacement reaction

Calculate the molar enthalpy change for the reaction between zinc and copper(II) sulfate, given 100 cm3 of 1.00 mol dm−3 CuSO4(aq), a corrected (extrapolated) maximum temperature of 70.0°C and an initial temperature of 20.0°C, with excess zinc added so that CuSO4 is the limiting reactant.

n(CuSO4) = 1.00 mol dm−3 × 0.100 dm3 = 0.100 mol

ΔHreaction = −(100 g × 4.18 J g−1 K−1 × 50.0 K) ÷ 0.100 mol = −20 900 J ÷ 0.100 mol = −209 000 J mol−1 = −209 kJ mol−1

Enthalpy of neutralization

The enthalpy change of a neutralization reaction between an acid and a base in solution can be measured the same way, again using q = mcΔT with the total volume of solution (assumed to have the density and specific heat capacity of water) as the mass of water heated.

Worked example: enthalpy of neutralization and percentage error

Sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid solutions, both 1.00 mol dm−3, were mixed with a total volume of 120.0 cm3. The largest temperature increase occurred using V(NaOH) = 80.0 cm3 and V(H2SO4) = 40.0 cm3, with an initial temperature of 25.0°C rising to a maximum of 33.5°C. The literature value for the enthalpy of neutralization is −57.5 kJ mol−1.

Heat produced, assuming 120.0 cm3 of solution contains 120.0 g of water and all the heat produced passes into the water: q = m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) = 120.0 g × 4.18 J g−1 K−1 × 8.5 K = 4264 J.

Amount of NaOH reacted: n(NaOH) = 1.00 mol dm−3 × (80.0 ÷ 1000) dm3 = 0.0800 mol. Heat produced per mole of NaOH: ΔHreaction = −4264 J ÷ 0.0800 mol = −53 300 J mol−1 = −53.3 kJ mol−1

Percentage error = |(−57.5) − (−53.3)| ÷ |−57.5| × 100% = 4.2 ÷ 57.5 × 100% = 7% (to 1 significant figure). The calculated value assumes no heat loss from the system, that all the heat is transferred to the water, and that the solution contains exactly 120 g of water; there are also uncertainties in the measured temperature, volume and concentration values.

Enthalpy of dissolution

The same technique measures the enthalpy change when a solid dissolves in water – some dissolution processes are exothermic (e.g. anhydrous CaCl2), while others, such as the one below, are endothermic.

Worked example: an endothermic enthalpy of dissolution

5.35 g of solid ammonium chloride, NH4Cl, is added to 100.00 g of water in a polystyrene cup. The temperature falls from 25.55°C to 21.79°C. Calculate the molar enthalpy of dissolution.

M(NH4Cl) = 14.01 + (4 × 1.01) + 35.45 = 53.50 g mol−1; n(NH4Cl) = 5.35 / 53.50 = 0.100 mol

ΔT(H2O) = 21.79 − 25.55 = −3.76 K (the water cools, since the dissolution process absorbs heat from it)

ΔHsoln = −m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT(H2O) ÷ n(NH4Cl) = −(100.00 g × 4.18 J g−1 K−1 × (−3.76 K)) ÷ 0.100 mol

= −(−1572 J) ÷ 0.100 mol = +15 700 J mol−1 = +15.7 kJ mol−1 (positive, confirming the process is endothermic), reasonably close to the literature value of about +15.2 kJ mol−1.

A common error to avoidWhen reporting an enthalpy change calculated from calorimetry data, always include the correct units (usually kJ mol−1) and the correct sign: forgetting the negative sign for an exothermic reaction, or the positive sign for an endothermic one, is one of the most common errors made in this type of calculation.

HL: Systematic and Random Errors in Calorimetry

A calorimetry result is only as reliable as the experimental design behind it, and it is useful to separate the sources of discrepancy into two distinct categories, since each demands a different kind of fix.

Systematic versus random errorA systematic error biases every reading in the same direction by roughly the same amount, so the mean of many repeats is still wrong; it must be identified and corrected by changing the method, not by repeating the measurement. A random error scatters readings unpredictably above and below the true value, usually owing to the limited resolution of an instrument or small, unavoidable variations in technique; averaging repeated trials reduces its effect on the mean.
Source of error in a calorimetry experimentTypeEffect and remedy
Heat loss to the surroundings during the reaction or combustionSystematicAlways makes the experimental ΔH less exothermic (or less endothermic) than the true value; reduced by insulation, a lid, and corrected for by extrapolating the cooling curve back to the time of mixing
Heat absorbed by the calorimeter itself, not just the waterSystematicAlways makes the experimental value for an exothermic reaction appear less exothermic; corrected by including the calorimeter’s own heat capacity in the calculation (see below) or using a calorimeter with a very low heat capacity, such as polystyrene
Incomplete combustion (soot, yellow flame)SystematicAlways makes ΔHc less exothermic, since some of the fuel’s chemical energy is never released as heat; reduced by ensuring an adequate air or oxygen supply
Limited precision of the thermometer scale (e.g. ±0.1°C)RandomScatters individual temperature readings slightly above and below the true value; reduced by using a thermometer or data logger with finer graduations, or by repeating the experiment and averaging
Small variations in the exact moment of mixing or stirringRandomIntroduces run-to-run scatter in the measured ΔT; reduced by practice, standardized technique, and repeat trials

Accounting for the heat capacity of the calorimeter

The simple equation q = m(H2O) × c(H2O) × ΔT assumes that only the water absorbs heat. In practice, the calorimeter itself (and the thermometer, lid, and stirrer) also warms up, and more careful experiments account for this using the calorimeter’s own heat capacity, Ccal, in J K−1 (determined separately, for example by supplying a known quantity of electrical energy and measuring the resulting temperature rise).

q = [m(H2O) × c(H2O) + Ccal] × ΔT
Worked example

A copper calorimeter has a heat capacity of 155 J K−1, determined by a separate electrical calibration. It contains 150.0 g of water. When 0.0512 mol of a liquid fuel is burnt beneath it, the temperature of the water and calorimeter rises by 11.2°C. Calculate the molar enthalpy of combustion of the fuel, accounting for the heat capacity of the calorimeter.

q = [150.0 g × 4.18 J g−1 K−1 + 155 J K−1] × 11.2 K = [627 + 155] J K−1 × 11.2 K = 782 J K−1 × 11.2 K = 8758 J

ΔHc = −q ÷ n(fuel) = −8758 J ÷ 0.0512 mol = −171 000 J mol−1 = −171 kJ mol−1

Ignoring the calorimeter’s heat capacity (using only the water) would have given q = 150.0 × 4.18 × 11.2 = 7022 J and ΔHc = −137 kJ mol−1 – a substantially less exothermic (and less accurate) value, since some of the heat that actually warmed the calorimeter would otherwise be missed entirely.

Enthalpy Cycles: A First Look at Hess's Law

Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2(l), can be formed directly from hydrogen and oxygen, or it can decompose into water and oxygen. Because H2O2(l) can be converted into H2O(l) either directly or by way of its elements, these enthalpy changes are linked.

Enthalpy cycle linking hydrogen, oxygen, hydrogen peroxide and waterA potential energy diagram with three horizontal levels. The highest level, on the left, is labelled H2(g) + O2(g). A downward arrow labelled ΔH1 leads from this level to a middle level labelled H2O2(l). A further downward arrow labelled ΔH2 leads from the middle level down to the lowest level, labelled H2O(l) + ½O2(g). A third arrow, drawn directly from the highest level down to the lowest level, is labelled ΔH3, representing the same overall change reached in a single step.

Since the direct route (ΔH3) and the two-step indirect route (ΔH1 followed by ΔH2) both start from the same initial state and end at the same final state, the overall enthalpy change must be the same by either route:

ΔH3 = ΔH1 + ΔH2

This relationship is an example of Hess's law: the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the pathway taken between the same initial and final states. Hess's law follows from the law of conservation of energy introduced earlier in this chapter, and is explored in full in the next chapter, Reactivity 1.2, where it becomes the basis for calculating enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly, including from bond enthalpies, formation and combustion data, and Born–Haber cycles.

Guiding Question Revisited

What can be deduced from the temperature change that accompanies chemical or physical change?
  • A system is the reaction container and its contents; the surroundings are everything outside the system. Exothermic reactions release heat energy to the surroundings (ΔH negative); endothermic reactions absorb heat energy from the surroundings (ΔH positive).
  • Heat is a process of energy transfer, while temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles; the heat transferred is related to a temperature change through the specific heat capacity, c, using q = mcΔT for a pure substance.
  • Heat changes in the laboratory are usually determined from the temperature change of a known mass of water: heated in a copper calorimeter for enthalpy of combustion experiments, or acting as the solvent in an insulated polystyrene-cup calorimeter for reactions in solution (displacement, neutralization and dissolution alike).
  • A thermochemical equation gives the balanced equation for a reaction, with state symbols, together with its corresponding enthalpy change, measured under standard conditions; the standard enthalpy changes of formation, ΔHf°, and combustion, ΔHc°, are two particularly important examples, each referring to one mole of the substance concerned.
  • An energy profile diagram plots potential energy against reaction coordinate, showing reactants above products for an exothermic reaction and below products for an endothermic reaction, with the activation energy, Ea, as the height of the energy barrier above the reactants.
  • Systematic errors (heat loss, the calorimeter’s own heat capacity, incomplete combustion) bias every result in the same direction and must be corrected by improving the method, for example by extrapolating a cooling curve or including a calorimeter heat-capacity term; random errors scatter results and are reduced by repeating measurements and averaging.
  • Hess's law – that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the pathway taken, provided the initial and final states are the same – can be introduced through simple enthalpy cycles, and is developed fully in the next chapter.
