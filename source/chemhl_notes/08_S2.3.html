Guiding Question

What determines the metallic nature and properties of an element?

The names of entire historical periods — the Bronze Age, the Iron Age — testify to how central metals have been to human civilization, and their importance has only grown: the gold-plated beryllium mirrors of the James Webb Space Telescope, floating 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, depend on exactly the same bonding that made bronze tools useful six thousand years ago. Because metals show a set of properties quite unlike those of ionic or covalent substances, they need their own explanatory model: a lattice of cations surrounded by a “sea” of delocalized electrons. This sub-topic uses that metallic model to explain the characteristic properties of metals and to explain trends in metallic bond strength across the periodic table. At Higher Level we go further and look in detail at the transition elements of the d-block, whose ability to delocalize d-electrons as well as s-electrons gives them some of the most extreme physical properties of any metals.

The Metallic Bond

Metals sit on the left-hand side and in the centre of the periodic table. Their atoms have relatively few electrons in their outer (valence) shell and low ionization energies, so in the elemental state — where no other element is present to accept any lost electrons — those valence electrons do not stay associated with any one nucleus. Instead they become delocalized, spreading out across the whole structure, while the metal atoms themselves become positively charged cations arranged in a regular, repeating lattice.

Metallic bondA metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of cations and the delocalized electrons that surround them, moving freely through the whole structure rather than belonging to any single ion.
The metallic modelA regular, repeating grid of positively charged circles, each marked with a + sign, arranged in neat rows and columns to represent a lattice of metal cations, seen as if in cross-section. Scattered through every gap between the cations are many small, unmarked dots representing delocalized electrons, drawn with no fixed position and no particular association with any cation, conveying that the whole “sea” of electrons can move freely in any direction through the entire lattice.

A useful analogy is a tight-knit neighbourhood where children move freely between houses rather than staying with only their own parents, so that the constant mixing creates a strong bond between every family in the street. In the same way, the delocalized electrons in a metal are shared among the whole lattice of cations rather than belonging to any one of them, and it is the electrostatic attraction between the entire sea of electrons and the entire lattice of cations that holds the metallic structure together.

Explaining the Properties of Metals

Every characteristic physical property of a metal can be traced back to the mobility of its delocalized electrons and the non-directional nature of the attraction holding the lattice together.

Malleable vs ductileBoth describe a metal’s ability to change shape without breaking. Malleable specifically means being hammered, pressed or rolled into sheets or other shapes, while ductile means being drawn or stretched out into a thin wire. Both rely on the same underlying cause: because metallic bonding is non-directional, layers of cations can slide past one another while the electron sea simply reshapes around the new arrangement, keeping the structure bonded throughout.

Alloys: a First Look

Because the delocalized electrons in a metallic lattice are not associated with any particular pair of ions, a metal’s properties can be modified by mixing it, while molten, with other metallic or non-metallic elements. The resulting mixture, called an alloy, keeps its metallic bonding throughout, since the delocalized electrons continue to attract the cations — including any foreign atoms sitting within the lattice — from every direction.

Steel is a familiar example, made by adding small amounts of carbon to molten iron. Although the carbon atoms occupy positions within the iron lattice, the iron cations still experience electrostatic attraction to the delocalized electrons in all directions, so the metallic bond is preserved. Because the carbon atoms disrupt the even layers of the pure iron lattice, it becomes harder for layers of ions to slide past one another, giving steel a much greater tensile strength than pure iron — one reason it is so widely used in construction. The features of metallic bonding that make alloying possible in general are examined in full depth in the following sub-topic, Structure 2.4.

Factors Affecting the Strength of a Metallic Bond

Not every metal bonds equally strongly. The strength of a metallic bond — and hence properties such as melting point — depends on three related factors.

Factors determining metallic bond strengthA metallic bond is stronger, and the metal has a higher melting point, when: (1) the cation carries a greater positive charge; (2) more delocalized electrons per atom are contributed to the electron sea; and (3) the cation has a smaller ionic radius. All three factors increase the electrostatic force of attraction between the cations and the sea of delocalized electrons.

These factors can be compared directly for sodium (group 1, electron configuration 1s22s22p63s1) and magnesium (group 2, electron configuration 1s22s22p63s2). Sodium contributes one delocalized electron per atom and forms the singly charged Na+ ion, while magnesium contributes two delocalized electrons per atom, forms the doubly charged Mg2+ ion, and has a slightly smaller ionic radius than Na+. All three factors therefore predict that magnesium should have the stronger metallic bonding.

Comparing sodium and magnesium latticesTwo lattice diagrams are shown side by side. On the left, a lattice labelled sodium shows ions marked Na+ with a moderate scattering of delocalized electron dots between them. On the right, a lattice labelled magnesium shows Mg2+ ions drawn slightly smaller and packed slightly closer together, with roughly twice as many electron dots filling the gaps, representing the two delocalized electrons per atom that magnesium contributes compared with sodium’s one.
Worked example

Sodium melts at 98°C and magnesium melts at 650°C. Explain this difference in terms of the metallic model.
Melting point directly measures how much thermal energy is needed to overcome the electrostatic attraction holding a lattice together, so a far higher melting point indicates far stronger metallic bonding. Magnesium’s much higher melting point is consistent with the prediction above: Mg2+ carries double the charge of Na+, contributes twice as many delocalized electrons per atom, and has a smaller ionic radius, so the electrostatic attraction between its cations and the electron sea is considerably stronger.

Trends in Metallic Bond Strength Across the Periodic Table

Trend down a groupMetallic bond strength generally decreases down a group. The cation’s charge stays the same, but its ionic radius increases (an extra occupied energy level), which increases the distance between the delocalized electrons and the nucleus and weakens the electrostatic attraction.
Worked example

Sodium melts at 98°C, potassium at 63°C, and rubidium at 39°C. Explain this trend.
Moving down group 1 from Na to K to Rb, each element has one more occupied main energy level, giving a larger cation radius, even though every group 1 cation carries a +1 charge and contributes one delocalized electron per atom. The larger radius places the delocalized electrons further, on average, from the nucleus, weakening the attraction and giving progressively weaker metallic bonding, consistent with the steadily falling melting points.

Trend across a periodMetallic bond strength generally increases from left to right across a period of s- and p-block metals. Cation charge increases (e.g. +1 for group 1, +2 for group 2, +3 for group 13), the number of delocalized electrons contributed per atom increases, and ionic radius decreases slightly, so the electrostatic attraction strengthens.

Because metals react by losing their valence electrons, the same factors that determine metallic bond strength can be used to predict relative reactivity: a metal that holds its delocalized electrons less tightly (weaker metallic bonding) tends to react more readily.

Melting point trend vs reactivity trendMelting point and metallic bond strength rise and fall together. Reactivity moves in the opposite direction: a metal whose valence electrons are held only loosely (weak metallic bonding, low melting point, large cation, low charge) loses those electrons more easily in a reaction, making it more reactive.

HL: Transition Elements Have Delocalized d-Electrons

Transition elementA transition element is an element whose atoms have an incomplete d sublevel, or which can give rise to a cation with an incomplete d sublevel.
Why zinc and scandium are special casesZinc is excluded from the transition elements: both the Zn atom (...3d104s2) and its only common ion, Zn2+ (...3d10), have a completely full 3d sublevel, so neither satisfies the definition. Scandium is debated: the Sc atom (...3d14s2) does have an incomplete d sublevel, but its only common ion, Sc3+ (...3d0), has an empty rather than incomplete d sublevel — so whether scandium counts as a true transition element depends on whether the atom or the ion is used as the test.

Like other metals, the bonding in transition elements is explained by the metallic model: a lattice of cations surrounded by delocalized electrons. However, because the outer sublevels of a transition metal atom are very close in energy (for example, the 3d and 4s sublevels of the period 4 transition elements), these metals are also able to delocalize some of their d-electrons into the electron sea in addition to their s-electrons. This additional delocalization is what gives transition elements some of their most distinctive properties.

High Melting Points

The attraction between a particularly large number of delocalized electrons and a correspondingly large positive charge on the metal cations produces very strong metallic bonding and high melting points across the transition block. Rhenium, for example, has a melting point of about 3186°C, one of the highest melting points of any element in the periodic table.

Unlike the smooth, predictable trends seen across a period of s- and p-block metals, the melting points of the transition elements show no simple, steadily increasing or decreasing pattern.

Melting points across period 4 (Sc to Cu)A line graph with the period 4 transition elements, Sc through Cu, along the horizontal axis and melting point in °C (ranging up to about 1900) on the vertical axis. The line climbs from scandium up to a peak around vanadium and chromium, then drops sharply to a pronounced dip at manganese, climbs steeply back up through iron, cobalt and nickel to a second, slightly lower peak, and finally drops away again at copper, which falls even lower than manganese to become the lowest point on the whole graph.

This irregular, zig-zagging pattern (rather than the single clean trend seen in the s-block) arises because the usual simple picture — comparing only cation charge and radius — is far less reliable here: transition metal cations across the period have fairly similar ionic radii to one another, and the number of electrons any particular transition element delocalizes does not increase steadily and predictably with atomic number in the way it does for s-block cations. This makes trends across the d-block considerably harder to predict than trends across the s- and p-blocks.

High Electrical Conductivity

The large number of delocalized electrons available in the metallic structure of a transition element also makes these metals particularly good electrical conductors. Copper, for instance, is the metal most commonly used in electrical cables because of this high conductivity combined with its relatively low cost and ease of shaping.

Silver: the best conductor, but not the most usedSilver is the single most electrically conductive metal of all, yet it is used only in small amounts, and usually only inside electronic devices rather than for visible or structural wiring. Two practical factors explain this: silver is far more expensive to produce in bulk than copper, and its surface readily tarnishes through reaction with trace sulfur compounds in air, forming a layer of silver sulfide that is a poor conductor and would degrade the connection’s performance over time if exposed.
s/p-block trends vs d-block trendsAcross a period of s- and p-block metals, melting point and metallic bond strength change smoothly and predictably as charge, delocalized electron count and radius all shift together in the same direction. Across the d-block, by contrast, cation radii stay relatively similar and the number of delocalized electrons varies in a more complicated way from element to element, so melting points rise and fall irregularly rather than following one steady trend — even though every transition element still shows very strong metallic bonding overall, and generally higher melting points than the neighbouring s- and p-block metals.

Metal Extraction and Environmental Considerations

Extracting metals from their ores is a major industrial activity worldwide, but it carries real environmental costs: the destruction of landscapes through strip mining, the build-up of waste rock and processing residue (“tailings”) in spoil tips, and the release of toxic substances that can leach out of waste material and pollute soil and water supplies. Because metals are a valuable but finite resource, maximizing the reuse and recycling of metal objects, and avoiding unnecessary metal waste, are important ways of reducing these impacts.

Guiding Question Revisited

  • A metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of cations and a sea of delocalized electrons that belong to no single ion.
  • This model explains the characteristic properties of metals: electrical and thermal conductivity (electron mobility), malleability and ductility (non-directional bonding), high melting points (strong electrostatic attraction), and lustre (reflection of light by surface electrons).
  • Metallic bond strength — and so melting point and reactivity — depends on the cation’s charge, the number of delocalized electrons per atom, and the cation’s ionic radius: bond strength increases across a period and decreases down a group, with reactivity trending in the opposite direction.
  • At Higher Level, transition elements are defined by an incomplete d sublevel in the atom or in a derived cation; because their outer d and s sublevels are close in energy, they can delocalize d-electrons as well as s-electrons.
  • This extra delocalization gives transition elements particularly high melting points and excellent electrical conductivity, but because cation radii and delocalized electron counts do not change in a simple, steady way across the d-block, melting point trends across the transition elements are far less predictable than across the s- and p-blocks.
