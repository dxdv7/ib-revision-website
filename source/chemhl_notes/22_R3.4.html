Guiding Question

What happens when reactants share their electron pairs with others?

When one reactant shares an electron pair with another, a coordination (dative covalent) bond forms. This is a common theme across acid–base chemistry, transition metal chemistry and organic chemistry. This sub-topic focuses on two organic examples: nucleophilic substitution of halogenoalkanes and electrophilic addition of alkenes, together with the mechanistic detail — SN1 and SN2 substitution, Markovnikov’s rule, and electrophilic substitution of benzene — that is assessed only at Higher Level.

Nucleophiles (Recap)

A nucleophile is a reactant that forms a bond to its reaction partner (the electrophile) by donating both electrons of the new bond. Nucleophiles tend to be electron rich: they have one or more lone pairs and may carry a negative charge.

Examples of nucleophilesNeutral: H2O, NH3, ROH (alcohols), RNH2 (amines). Anions: OH−, F−, Cl−, Br−, I−, CN−, R− (carbanions).

The name nucleophile means ‘nucleus-loving’: being electron rich, nucleophiles are attracted to positive charges, such as an atomic nucleus or a region of partial positive charge.

Coordination bond formationWhen a nucleophile (labelled Nuc, shown with a lone pair) reacts with an electrophile (labelled A, electron deficient), a curly arrow is drawn starting at the lone pair on the nucleophile and pointing towards the electrophile. This arrow shows both bonding electrons moving from the nucleophile to form a brand-new covalent bond between Nuc and A in the product. Because both electrons of this new bond originated from the nucleophile alone, the new bond is specifically called a coordination bond (or dative covalent bond).

Nucleophilic Substitution of Halogenoalkanes (Recap)

In a nucleophilic substitution reaction, a nucleophile donates an electron pair to form a new bond to a substrate, while another bond in the substrate breaks, releasing a leaving group.

Halogenoalkanes are polar: the halogen is more electronegative than carbon, so it pulls the shared C–X electron density towards itself. This leaves the halogen with a partial negative charge (δ−) and the carbon atom bonded to it with a partial positive charge (δ+), making that carbon electron deficient and attractive to nucleophiles.

CH3Cl + OH− → CH3OH + Cl−  (chloromethane → methanol)
C3H7Br + OH− → C3H7OH + Br−  (1-bromopropane → propan-1-ol)

Because the halogen can be substituted by many different nucleophiles, halogenoalkanes are very useful intermediates for converting one class of organic compound into another in synthesis pathways. Halogens make good leaving groups because they form relatively weak bonds to carbon, and their high electronegativity makes the attached carbon electron deficient and susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

General mechanism, R–X + Nu:− → R–Nu + X−The substrate R–X is drawn with the carbon atom bonded to the leaving group X (a halogen). A curly arrow starts at a lone pair on the nucleophile, Nu, and points towards that carbon atom, showing the nucleophile donating an electron pair to form the new C–Nu bond. At the same time, a second curly arrow starts on the C–X bond (the bonding pair) and points towards X, showing that bond breaking heterolytically so that both electrons leave with the departing group, forming X−. The bond that forms (C–Nu) and the bond that breaks (C–X) both involve the same carbon atom — the one originally bonded to the leaving group. For example, in the reaction of hydroxide with chloromethane: a curly arrow runs from a lone pair on the O of OH− to the carbon of CH3Cl (the δ+ carbon), while a second curly arrow runs from the C–Cl bond to the Cl atom, releasing it as Cl− and forming CH3OH.

If the nucleophile is a neutral species such as water, the immediate product carries a positive charge, because the O atom has donated a lone pair without losing its own H atoms. This intermediate then loses a proton (H+) to give a neutral alcohol product. If the leaving group was a halide ion, it can combine with this released H+ to form a hydrogen halide.

Overall: H2O + RX → ROH + HX

Heterolytic Fission (Recap)

Heterolytic fission is the breaking of a covalent bond such that both bonding electrons end up on one of the two fragments, forming a pair of oppositely charged ions. (‘Hetero-’ means ‘different’, reflecting the unequal split of the bonding electrons between the two products, in contrast to the equal split in the homolytic fission of Reactivity 3.3.) When the bond is between two different atoms, the more electronegative atom keeps both electrons and becomes the negative ion.

Cl–Cl → Cl+ + Cl−
H–Cl → H+ + Cl−
Homolytic vs. heterolytic fissionHomolytic fission (Reactivity 3.3) splits the bonding electron pair equally, one electron to each fragment, producing two neutral radicals, each with an unpaired electron; this is shown using a single-barbed curly arrow (a ‘fish hook’) for each unpaired electron that moves. Heterolytic fission sends both bonding electrons to one fragment, producing two oppositely charged ions; this is shown using one full, double-barbed curly arrow, since a whole electron pair moves together.
Heterolytic fission of H–ClA single curly (double-barbed) arrow starts on the H–Cl bonding pair and points towards the Cl atom, showing both electrons moving onto chlorine. This leaves Cl as Cl− (now holding both electrons, plus its original lone pairs) and H as H+ (having lost its only electron). The tail of the arrow always shows where the electron pair starts (the bond), and the head shows where it ends up (the more electronegative atom, or more generally the site of greater electron deficiency).

The reverse of heterolytic fission is the formation of a coordination bond: a nucleophile donates an electron pair to an electrophile, shown with the same style of double-barbed curly arrow, running from the nucleophile’s lone pair to the electrophile.

Electrophiles (Recap)

An electrophile is a reactant that forms a bond to its reaction partner (the nucleophile) by accepting both bonding electrons from that partner. Electrophiles tend to be electron deficient, or contain an electron-deficient region, so they often carry a positive charge or partial positive charge.

Examples of electrophilesNeutral: HX (hydrogen halides), X2 (halogens), H2O, halogenoalkanes (RX). Cations: H+, NO2+, NO+, CH3+, R+ (carbocations).
Nature of science: inclusive definitionsDefinitions in science must be broad enough to be inclusive. Describing electrophiles simply as ‘electron deficient’ would wrongly exclude molecules such as bromine, Br2, which is not itself electron deficient but still acts as an electrophile in addition reactions — its bond becomes polarized as it approaches an electron-rich region such as a carbon–carbon double bond.

Electrophilic Addition of Alkenes (Recap)

Alkenes, general formula CnH2n, are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing a carbon–carbon double bond. The C=C double bond has a high electron density, making alkenes attractive to electrophiles. The pi (π) bond is relatively easily broken, creating two new bonding positions on the carbon atoms; this allows alkenes to undergo addition reactions, in which an electrophile X–Y adds across the double bond so that X and Y each bond to one of the two former double-bond carbons, giving a saturated product.

Addition of water (hydration). Water is a poor electrophile on its own, so hydration of an alkene requires a strong acid catalyst (e.g. concentrated sulfuric acid in the laboratory). The reaction converts an alkene into an alcohol:

CH2=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH2OH  (conc. H2SO4 catalyst; ethene → ethanol)

Addition of halogens. Halogens (X2) react rapidly with alkenes at room temperature to give dihalogeno compounds, with one halogen atom adding to each of the two former double-bond carbons, accompanied by loss of colour of the halogen:

CH3CH=CH2 + Br2 → CH3CHBrCH2Br  (propene → 1,2-dibromopropane)
Bromine water test – alkanes vs. alkenesAlkenes decolorize bromine water rapidly at room temperature in the dark, via electrophilic addition, because the electron-rich C=C double bond attracts the Br2 electrophile without needing UV light. Alkanes only react with bromine water via a radical substitution mechanism (Reactivity 3.3), which requires UV light to initiate it — so alkanes do not decolorize bromine water in the dark.

Addition of hydrogen halides. Hydrogen halides (HCl, HBr, HI) react rapidly with alkenes in solution at room temperature to give halogenoalkanes:

CH2=CH2 + HCl → CH3CH2Cl  (ethene → chloroethane)
Reactivity order: HI > HBr > HClThis order reflects the decreasing strength of the hydrogen–halogen bond down group 17: the H–I bond is the weakest (the large, diffuse iodine orbital gives poorer orbital overlap), so it is broken most easily and HI reacts fastest, while H–Cl is the strongest of the three and reacts most slowly.

Because alkenes can react with a range of electrophiles to give alcohols, halogenoalkanes and dihalogenoalkanes, and these products can be converted into many further classes of compound, alkenes are described as very useful ‘starting molecules’ in organic synthesis pathways, for example in the industrial production of many plastics.

HL: The SN2 Mechanism – Primary Halogenoalkanes

At Higher Level, the mechanisms of nucleophilic substitution are examined in detail for both primary and tertiary halogenoalkanes. Primary halogenoalkanes, such as chloroethane, have at least two hydrogen atoms attached to the carbon of the C–X bond. Because the carbon is only lightly shielded by small hydrogen atoms, it is relatively open to attack.

SN2 mechanismThe nucleophile (e.g. OH−) approaches the electron-deficient carbon from the side directly opposite the leaving group (a ‘backside attack’). As the nucleophile begins to bond, the carbon passes through an unstable transition state in which it is weakly, partially bonded to both the incoming nucleophile and the departing leaving group simultaneously. The C–X bond then finishes breaking heterolytically as the C–Nu bond finishes forming, in a single, continuous step with no intermediate: bond formation and bond breaking happen at the same time. This is called a concerted mechanism.
The SN2 transition stateThe nucleophile (e.g. the O of OH−) approaches the carbon from 180° opposite the leaving group X. In the transition state, shown in square brackets with an overall negative charge, the carbon is drawn with five groups arranged around it: the three original substituents roughly in a plane, and the partially-formed Nu···C and partially-broken C···X bonds shown as dashed lines pointing in opposite directions along the same axis. As the reaction proceeds past this transition state, the three original substituents flip through the plane of the carbon to the opposite side, like an umbrella turning inside out in the wind.

Because the mechanism depends on the concentration of both the halogenoalkane and the nucleophile, it is a bimolecular reaction, second order overall:

rate = k[halogenoalkane][nucleophile]

This mechanism is therefore fully described as SN2: substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular (second order).

Inversion of configurationBecause the nucleophile attacks from the side opposite the leaving group, the three-dimensional arrangement of the other three groups is inverted in the product, exactly as an umbrella blowing inside out reverses its shape. If the carbon under attack is a chiral centre, the SN2 mechanism is stereospecific: bond formation and bond breaking are linked in a single transition state, so the stereochemistry of the product is completely determined by the stereochemistry of the reactant. A single, optically pure enantiomer of a chiral halogenoalkane (Structure 3.2) reacting entirely by SN2 therefore gives a single enantiomer of product — the inverted one — with 100% inversion of configuration, and the product is optically active.

HL: The SN1 Mechanism – Tertiary Halogenoalkanes

Tertiary halogenoalkanes, such as 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, have three alkyl groups attached to the carbon of the C–X bond. These bulky alkyl groups create steric hindrance, physically blocking a nucleophile from approaching the carbon from the opposite side to the leaving group, so the SN2 pathway is strongly disfavoured.

SN1 mechanismInstead, the reaction proceeds in two distinct steps. In the first, slow step, the C–X bond breaks heterolytically on its own, with no nucleophile involved, and the halide ion leaves entirely before any new bond forms. This generates a planar carbocation intermediate, with the positive charge centred on the carbon that lost the leaving group. In the second, fast step, a nucleophile (e.g. OH−, or the leaving halide ion itself in a competing reverse reaction) attacks this carbocation, forming the new C–Nu bond and completing the substitution.

Because the slow first step (the rate-determining step) involves only the halogenoalkane breaking apart, with no nucleophile taking part, the reaction is unimolecular, first order overall:

rate = k[halogenoalkane]

This mechanism is therefore fully described as SN1: substitution, nucleophilic, unimolecular (first order).

Why tertiary substrates favour SN1The carbocation intermediate is stabilized by the three alkyl groups attached to it: each alkyl group has an electron-donating (positive) inductive effect, pushing electron density towards the positively charged carbon and spreading out (delocalizing) the charge. A tertiary carbocation, with three such stabilizing groups, is therefore considerably more stable than a secondary carbocation (two groups) or a primary carbocation (one group): tertiary > secondary > primary in carbocation stability. This greater stability allows the carbocation intermediate to persist long enough for the second step to occur, which is why SN1 is favoured for tertiary (and, to a lesser extent, secondary) substrates, while a primary carbocation is too unstable for SN1 to compete with SN2.
RacemizationThe carbocation intermediate is planar (the positively charged carbon is sp2 hybridized), so the incoming nucleophile in the second step is equally likely to attack from either face of this plane. If the original substrate was a single, optically pure enantiomer, the product is therefore a near 50:50 mixture of both enantiomers — a racemic mixture — and the SN1 mechanism is described as not stereospecific. (In practice the halide or solvent on the side the leaving group departed from can partially shield that face from attack, so real SN1 products are often not perfectly 50:50, but the mixture remains close to racemic and the optical activity of the original pure enantiomer is largely lost.)

HL: Secondary Halogenoalkanes and the Influence of the Leaving Group

It is not possible to be precise about the mechanism of nucleophilic substitution in secondary halogenoalkanes: experimental data shows that they typically react through a mixture of both SN1 and SN2 pathways simultaneously, since a secondary carbon is sterically intermediate between the open primary case and the hindered tertiary case, and its carbocation is intermediate in stability as well.

Separately from the mechanism, the identity of the halogen itself (the leaving group) affects the rate of substitution. For halogenoalkanes of the same carbon skeleton, the observed order of reactivity is:

iodoalkanes > bromoalkanes > chloroalkanes > fluoroalkanes

This factor affects the rate of both SN1 and SN2 reactions, independently of which mechanism operates.

HL: Comparing SN1 and SN2

Four factors together determine whether a given nucleophilic substitution proceeds mainly by SN1 or mainly by SN2: the degree of substitution (and resulting steric hindrance) at the substrate carbon, the stability of the carbocation that would form, the strength and concentration of the nucleophile, and the polarity of the solvent.

Why solvent polarity mattersA polar protic solvent (one with O–H or N–H bonds, such as water or ethanol) surrounds and stabilizes both the carbocation intermediate and the departing halide ion through hydrogen bonding and dipole interactions, lowering the activation energy for the slow, ion-forming first step of SN1 and so favouring that pathway. The same strong solvation, however, surrounds the nucleophile itself, partly shielding it and reducing its reactivity in a direct SN2 attack; SN2 is therefore relatively favoured in a less polar (or polar aprotic) solvent, where the nucleophile remains less solvated and more available to attack the substrate carbon directly.
Worked example

Predict whether 2-bromo-2-methylpropane and bromoethane are each more likely to react with hydroxide ions via SN1 or SN2, and justify your answer.

2-Bromo-2-methylpropane is a tertiary halogenoalkane: the three methyl groups cause significant steric hindrance, blocking backside attack, while also strongly stabilizing the resulting tertiary carbocation through their positive inductive effect. Both factors favour SN1.

Bromoethane is a primary halogenoalkane: the carbon bonded to the leaving group carries only small hydrogen atoms and one alkyl group, leaving it open to backside attack, and any carbocation that might form (primary) would be poorly stabilized. Both factors favour SN2.

HL: Stereochemistry of Nucleophilic Substitution

The stereochemical outcome of a nucleophilic substitution reaction provides strong experimental evidence for which mechanism, SN1 or SN2, is actually operating — this links directly to the treatment of chirality and optical isomerism in Structure 3.2.

SN2: single inverted productBecause bond formation and bond breaking occur simultaneously, in a single transition state, from the side opposite the leaving group, an SN2 reaction starting from a single pure enantiomer gives a single product: the enantiomer with the opposite spatial arrangement at that carbon (though not necessarily the opposite R/S descriptor, since this also depends on the priority of the incoming group). The product is optically active, with 100% inversion of configuration, and (if the starting material was optically pure) the product is also optically pure.
SN1: racemic productBecause the carbocation intermediate is planar and symmetric about that plane, the nucleophile in the second step attacks from either face with almost equal probability, destroying the original stereochemical information. An SN1 reaction starting from a single pure enantiomer therefore gives (approximately) a 50:50 mixture of both enantiomers, which is optically inactive overall, since the optical rotations of the two enantiomers cancel.
Using a polarimeter to identify the mechanismA polarimeter measures the optical rotation of a solution. If an optically pure chiral halogenoalkane is reacted with a nucleophile and the product solution shows full optical activity (fully inverted from the original), the reaction proceeded essentially entirely by SN2. If the product solution shows no net optical activity (or only a small amount), the reaction proceeded essentially entirely by SN1. A partial loss of optical activity indicates that both mechanisms are occurring together, as is typical for a secondary halogenoalkane.

HL: The Electrophilic Addition Mechanism in Detail

At Higher Level, the electrophilic addition of alkenes is examined as a full two-step mechanism, rather than simply as an overall equation.

The carbon atoms of the C=C double bond are sp2 hybridized, giving a planar, trigonal (120°) arrangement that leaves the double bond relatively open to attack. The π bond represents a region of electron density above and below the plane of the sigma-bonded framework; because its electrons are less tightly held than those of a σ bond, the π bond is weaker and breaks more easily.

Step 1: the π bond attacks the electrophileThe electron-rich π bond behaves, in effect, as the nucleophile: its electron pair is attracted towards an electrophile (or towards the electron-deficient end of a polarizable molecule such as Br2, which becomes polarized as it approaches the alkene). A curly arrow runs from the middle of the C=C double bond towards the electrophile, forming a new σ bond from one of the former double-bond carbons to the electrophile. At the same time, a second curly arrow shows the electrophile’s own bond breaking heterolytically (e.g. the Br–Br bond, or the H–X bond of a hydrogen halide), sending both electrons onto the departing atom and forming an anion (e.g. Br−). This slow step leaves a positive charge on the other former double-bond carbon: a carbocation intermediate.
Step 2: the carbocation is attacked by a nucleophileThe carbocation formed in step 1 is highly reactive and is rapidly attacked by a nucleophile — often the anion released in step 1 (e.g. Br−), or a solvent molecule such as water. A curly arrow runs from a lone pair on this nucleophile to the positively charged carbon, forming the second new σ bond and giving the final, saturated addition product. Because this step simply involves an existing lone pair attacking an existing positive centre, it is fast, with a low activation energy, compared with the slow first step.
Worked example: bromine addition to ethene

Describe the two-step mechanism for the reaction of ethene with bromine.

Step 1 (slow): as the non-polar Br2 molecule approaches the electron-rich C=C bond of ethene, it becomes polarized (δ+ on the nearer Br atom). The π electrons attack this δ+ bromine, forming a new C–Br bond, while the Br–Br bond breaks heterolytically, releasing Br− and leaving a positive charge on the other carbon: a carbocation intermediate, +CH2CH2Br.

Step 2 (fast): the bromide ion released in step 1 acts as a nucleophile, attacking the positively charged carbon of the carbocation and forming the second C–Br bond.

Overall: CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2BrCH2Br (1,2-dibromoethane).

Evidence for the carbocation intermediateIf ethene reacts with bromine in the presence of added chloride ions, Cl−, the product mixture contains not only 1,2-dibromoethane but also 1-bromo-2-chloroethane — even though no Cl2 was ever added. This is strong evidence for a genuine carbocation intermediate in step 1: once formed, this intermediate reacts with whatever nucleophile is available in step 2, Br− or Cl−, giving a mixture of products. (No dichloro product is ever formed, confirming that the initial electrophilic attack came only from Br2, never from Cl2, which was never present.) The same two-step mechanism, with H–X or H3O+ breaking heterolytically in step 1 in place of Br2, accounts for the analogous additions of hydrogen halides and of acid-catalysed water to alkenes.

HL: Markovnikov’s Rule

The reactions considered so far all involved symmetrical alkenes, which give only a single possible addition product. An unsymmetrical alkene — one with a different number of alkyl substituents on each double-bond carbon — can form two different carbocation intermediates, depending on which carbon the electrophile adds to first, and so has two theoretically possible products. In practice, only one of these forms as the major product.

Worked example: HBr + propene

Explain why 2-bromopropane, not 1-bromopropane, is the major product when propene reacts with hydrogen bromide.

In step 1, the electrophilic H of HBr can add to either carbon of the C=C bond. If H adds to the central carbon, the resulting carbocation is on the terminal (CH2) carbon: a primary carbocation, stabilized by only one adjacent alkyl group’s inductive effect. If H instead adds to the terminal carbon, the resulting carbocation is on the central carbon: a secondary carbocation, stabilized by two adjacent alkyl groups.

Because a secondary carbocation is more stable than a primary carbocation (more inductive stabilization, spreading the positive charge further), the pathway through the secondary carbocation is strongly favoured. In step 2, Br− then attacks this secondary carbocation, giving 2-bromopropane as the major product (1-bromopropane, from the disfavoured primary carbocation pathway, forms only as a very minor product).

Markovnikov’s ruleIn the addition of an unsymmetrical reagent H–X to an unsymmetrical alkene, the hydrogen atom adds to the carbon already bonded to the greater number of hydrogen atoms (equivalently: the more electropositive part of the reagent bonds to the less substituted carbon). This is simply a consequence of the reaction proceeding through the more stable of the two possible carbocation intermediates — the product predicted by this rule is called the Markovnikov product. Markovnikov’s rule can be extended to any asymmetric electrophile adding to an asymmetric alkene, not only to the hydrogen halides.
Worked example: BrCl + propene

Predict and explain the major product of the addition of the interhalogen compound Br–Cl to propene.

Chlorine is more electronegative than bromine, so the Br–Cl bond is polarized Brδ+–Clδ−; the electrophilic end is bromine. If Brδ+ adds to the terminal carbon, the resulting carbocation is secondary (on the central carbon); if Brδ+ adds to the central carbon, the resulting carbocation is primary (on the terminal carbon). The secondary carbocation pathway is favoured, so Cl− (the nucleophile released) ends up bonded to the central carbon and Br to the terminal carbon, giving 1-bromo-2-chloropropane as the major product.

Nature of science: predictable models and their limitsMarkovnikov’s rule is a highly successful predictive model based on carbocation stability, but it describes only the major product of a typical reaction, not an absolute rule. Specialized catalysts and reaction conditions have since been developed that deliberately favour the ‘non-Markovnikov’ (anti-Markovnikov) product instead, illustrating how a well-understood mechanism can be exploited to extend, rather than simply confirm, the predictive reach of a model.

HL: Electrophilic Substitution of Benzene

Benzene, C6H6, contains a ring of six carbon atoms with a delocalized system of six π electrons spread evenly above and below the ring (Structure 2.2). This delocalization lowers repulsion between the π electrons and gives benzene an unusually high stability (resonance stabilization) compared with a hypothetical ring containing three localized, alternating C=C double bonds.

Why benzene undergoes substitution, not additionDespite being highly unsaturated, benzene does not behave like a typical alkene. Addition across one of its ‘double bonds’ would destroy the delocalized ring system and its associated stabilization, so this pathway is strongly disfavoured energetically. Instead, benzene reacts so as to preserve its aromatic ring: an electrophile is substituted for one of the ring hydrogens, and the stable, delocalized ring system survives intact in the product. This is confirmed experimentally: unlike an alkene, benzene does not decolorize bromine water, showing that it does not undergo the addition reaction typical of C=C double bonds.
General electrophilic substitution mechanismAn electrophile, E+, is attracted to the electron-rich delocalized ring. In the slow, rate-determining step, a pair of π electrons from the ring attacks E+, forming a new C–E σ bond and temporarily disrupting the delocalized system: this produces an unstable, non-aromatic carbocation intermediate, in which the positive charge is spread (delocalized) over several of the remaining ring carbons, and in which the entering group E and the hydrogen originally on that carbon are both temporarily bonded to the same ring carbon. In the fast second step, this C–H bond breaks heterolytically: the pair of electrons from the C–H bond moves back into the ring, regenerating the full, stable delocalized system and releasing H+. Because this restores the aromatic stabilization, the second step strongly favours loss of H+ over loss of E+, which is why the overall reaction results in substitution rather than simply reversing back to the reactants.
Reaction profile for electrophilic substitution of benzeneA plot of energy (vertical axis) against extent of reaction (horizontal axis) shows the reactants (benzene + E+) at a relatively low energy. The curve rises through a first, high-energy transition state to a local minimum representing the non-aromatic carbocation intermediate (higher in energy than both the reactants and the final products, since the ring’s delocalization stabilization has been temporarily lost). The curve then rises again through a second transition state before falling to the products (substituted benzene derivative + H+), which sit lower in energy than the intermediate, since the stable aromatic ring has been restored. The first step, with the higher of the two activation energies, is rate-determining.
Worked example: nitration of benzene

Describe how benzene is converted into nitrobenzene, including how the electrophile is generated.

Benzene is heated with a mixture of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid (a ‘nitrating mixture’) at around 50°C. Sulfuric acid, the stronger acid, protonates nitric acid; the protonated species then loses a molecule of water, generating the electrophile, the nitronium ion, NO2+:

HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4−

The nitronium ion, a strong electrophile, is attacked by a pair of π electrons from the benzene ring, forming a non-aromatic carbocation intermediate with the –NO2 group and the departing H both attached to the same ring carbon. Loss of H+ from this intermediate regenerates the aromatic ring, giving nitrobenzene, C6H5NO2. The released H+ recombines with HSO4− to reform H2SO4, which is therefore a catalyst for the overall reaction:

C6H6 + HNO3 → C6H5NO2 + H2O  (conc. H2SO4 catalyst)

Guiding Question Revisited

What happens when reactants share their electron pairs with others?
  • Donation of an electron pair from an electron-rich species to an electron-deficient species forms a coordination bond; curly arrows show the movement of electron pairs in these reactions.
  • A nucleophile forms a bond by donating a pair of electrons to its reaction partner, the electrophile, which accepts that pair.
  • In nucleophilic substitution, a leaving group departs as another bond breaks heterolytically while the nucleophile bonds to the substrate; heterolytic fission produces two oppositely charged ions, in contrast to the two radicals formed by homolytic fission (Reactivity 3.3).
  • HL: primary halogenoalkanes react mainly via SN2 (one concerted step, bimolecular, backside attack, 100% inversion of configuration), while tertiary halogenoalkanes react mainly via SN1 (two steps via a planar carbocation intermediate, unimolecular, racemization); secondary halogenoalkanes react via a mixture of both.
  • HL: steric hindrance, carbocation stability, nucleophile strength/concentration and solvent polarity together determine which mechanism, SN1 or SN2, predominates for a given substrate.
  • Alkenes undergo electrophilic addition reactions in which the carbon–carbon π bond breaks, converting the alkene into a saturated product; HL: this proceeds via a two-step mechanism through a carbocation intermediate.
  • HL: for unsymmetrical alkenes, the major product follows Markovnikov’s rule, since the reaction proceeds through the more stable of the two possible carbocation intermediates.
  • HL: benzene undergoes electrophilic substitution, not addition, because substitution preserves its resonance-stabilized delocalized ring system; the nitration of benzene using the electrophile NO2+ is a key example.
