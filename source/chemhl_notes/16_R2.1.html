R2.1.1 – Chemical Equations and Mole Ratios

Guiding questionHow are chemical equations used to calculate reacting ratios, and how can those ratios be used to quantify and improve the efficiency of a chemical process?

Stoichiometry (from the Greek stoicheion, element, and metron, measure) describes the reacting ratios during chemical reactions. Because matter is conserved during chemical change (the law of conservation of mass), a correctly balanced chemical equation gives the exact mole ratio in which reactants combine and products form, read directly from its stoichiometric coefficients.

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)

This equation tells us that 1 mol CH4 reacts with 2 mol O2 to form 1 mol CO2 and 2 mol H2O. The mole ratio of any two species in the equation can be converted into a reacting ratio by mass (using m = nM), by gas volume (using Avogadro's law), or by solution concentration and volume (using n = cV). This chapter develops all three conversions, and then uses them to quantify how much product a reaction can realistically deliver, through the concepts of limiting reactant, theoretical and percentage yield, and atom economy.

General strategy for every reacting-quantity question
  • Write (or deduce) the balanced equation.
  • Identify only the species named in the question, and read off their mole ratio from the coefficients.
  • Convert the given quantity (mass, volume of gas, or concentration and volume of solution) into moles.
  • Use the mole ratio to find the moles of the species being asked for.
  • Convert back into the units required by the question.

R2.1.2 – Reacting Masses

Moles provide the essential link between the number of particles that react, which is fixed by the equation, and the mass that can actually be measured in the laboratory.

n = m / M   or   m = nM

where n is the amount in moles, m is the mass in grams, and M is the molar mass in g mol−1. Values of the relative atomic mass, Ar, given in the data booklet to two decimal places, should be used throughout these calculations.

Worked example

Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced from the complete combustion of 1.00 g of methane, CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g).

Only CH4 and CO2 are needed. The mole ratio is 1 : 1, so the reacting mass ratio is M(CH4) : M(CO2) = 16.05 : 44.01.

g CH4 / g CO2 = 16.05 / 44.01 = 1.00 / x

x = (1.00 × 44.01) / 16.05 = 2.74 g CO2.

Worked example

Iodine chloride, ICl, is made by 2I2 + KIO3 + 6HCl → 5ICl + KCl + 3H2O. Calculate the mass of I2 (M = 253.80 g mol−1) needed to prepare 28.60 g of ICl (M = 162.35 g mol−1).

Mole ratio I2 : ICl = 2 : 5, so mass ratio = (2 × 253.80) : (5 × 162.35) = 507.60 : 811.75.

x = (507.60 × 28.60) / 811.75 = 17.9 g I2.

R2.1.2 – Reacting Volumes of Gases

Avogadro's law states that equal volumes of all gases, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules (V ∝ n). This means the ratio of reacting gas volumes, all measured under the same conditions, is identical to the mole ratio given by the equation — no conversion to mass is needed at all.

Worked example

10 cm3 of nitrogen reacts with excess hydrogen: N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). What volume of ammonia is produced (same temperature and pressure throughout)?

Mole ratio N2 : NH3 = 1 : 2, so the volume ratio is also 1 : 2. 10 cm3 N2 gives 20 cm3 NH3 (and consumes 30 cm3 H2).

At standard temperature and pressure (STP) — 0 °C (273 K) and 100 kPa — one mole of any gas occupies the same molar volume, Vm = 22.7 dm3 mol−1, a value given in the data booklet.

n = V / Vm
Worked example

What volume of oxygen at STP is needed to completely burn 1.00 mol of butane: 2C4H10(g) + 13O2(g) → 8CO2(g) + 10H2O(g)?

Mole ratio C4H10 : O2 = 2 : 13, so 1.00 mol butane needs 6.50 mol O2.

V = 6.50 mol × 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 148 dm3.

STP is not the thermodynamic ‘standard state’Standard temperature and pressure (0 °C, 100 kPa), used only for gas molar volume, is a completely different convention from the thermodynamic standard state (298 K, 100 kPa) used for enthalpy data (Reactivity 1.1). Do not confuse the two when a question gives a temperature.

HL – The Ideal Gas Equation Applied to Stoichiometry

The molar volume of 22.7 dm3 mol−1 applies only at STP. When a reacting gas is measured at some other temperature and pressure, the ideal gas equation must be used instead to convert between volume and amount in moles.

PV = nRT

where P is pressure in Pa, V is volume in m3, n is amount in mol, T is temperature in K, and R is the gas constant, 8.31 J K−1 mol−1 (given in the data booklet). Rearranging for the quantity required, exactly as in any other reacting-quantity question, the balanced equation is first used to find the moles of gas needed, and the ideal gas equation then converts this to a volume (or vice versa).

Worked example

Propane burns completely: C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(g). Calculate the volume of oxygen, at 350 K and 150 kPa, required to completely combust 2.00 mol of propane.

Mole ratio C3H8 : O2 = 1 : 5, so n(O2) = 5 × 2.00 = 10.0 mol.

Rearranging PV = nRT for V, with P = 150 × 103 Pa:

V = nRT / P = (10.0 × 8.31 × 350) / (150 × 103) = 0.194 m3 = 194 dm3
Choosing which gas law to useIf a gas-stoichiometry question states, or implies, STP conditions (273 K, 100 kPa), use n = V/Vm with Vm = 22.7 dm3 mol−1. If any other temperature or pressure is specified, use the ideal gas equation, PV = nRT, converting all quantities into SI units (Pa, m3, K) before substituting.

R2.1.2 – Solution Stoichiometry: Concentration and Dilution

The mole ratio of an equation can also be used to determine the concentration of a reactant or product in solution.

n = cV

where n is the amount in moles, c is the concentration in mol dm−3, and V is the volume in dm3. A standard solution is a solution of accurately known concentration.

Dilution calculations

Diluting a solution adds solvent but does not change the amount, in moles, of solute present. Since n = cV is the same immediately before and immediately after dilution:

c1V1 = c2V2

where c1 and V1 are the concentration and volume of the original (stock) solution, and c2 and V2 are the concentration and volume after dilution with water.

Worked example

What volume of 2.00 mol dm−3 stock HCl(aq) is needed to prepare 500.0 cm3 of 0.150 mol dm−3 HCl(aq) by dilution with water?

c1V1 = c2V2, so V1 = c2V2 / c1 = (0.150 × 500.0) / 2.00 = 37.5 cm3 of stock acid, made up to 500.0 cm3 with water in a volumetric flask.

R2.1.2 – Titration Calculations

Acid–base titration apparatusA known volume of one solution is measured accurately using a pipette and transferred into a conical flask, with a few drops of indicator added. The other solution is placed in a burette, a calibrated glass tube with a tap, clamped above the flask so it can deliver precise, small volumes into it. The indicator changes colour sharply at its end point, signalling the equivalence point — where the two solutions have reacted exactly in the ratio given by the balanced equation. The volume released from the burette to reach the end point is the titre; several concordant titres (agreeing within about 0.05–0.10 cm3) are averaged for a reliable result.
Volumetric-analysis strategy
  • Write a balanced equation for the reaction, if not already given.
  • Identify the reactant whose volume and concentration are both known, and use n = cV to find its moles.
  • Use the mole ratio in the equation to find the moles of the other reactant.
  • Use c = n/V to find the unknown concentration (or n = cV to find an unknown volume or mass).
Worked example

25.00 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 NaHCO3(aq) is titrated with dilute H2SO4(aq): 2NaHCO3(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l) + 2CO2(g). 15.20 cm3 of acid is required. Calculate the concentration of the acid.

n(NaHCO3) = cV = 0.100 mol dm−3 × (25.00/1000) dm3 = 2.50 × 10−3 mol.

From the mole ratio, 2n(NaHCO3) = n(H2SO4), so n(H2SO4) = 1.25 × 10−3 mol.

c = n/V = (1.25 × 10−3 mol) / (15.20/1000 dm3) = 0.0822 mol dm−3.

HL – Back Titration

A back titration is carried out in reverse: a known excess of a standard reagent is added to react completely with a sample, and the unreacted excess is then determined by titration against a second standard solution. Subtracting the unreacted amount from the total amount originally added gives the amount that reacted with the sample. Back titration is used whenever a direct titration is impractical — because the sample is impure, insoluble, reacts too slowly, or has no sharp, easily detected end point of its own.

Worked example: purity of an impure carbonate sample

An impure sample of calcium carbonate of mass 2.50 g is added to 50.00 cm3 of 1.000 mol dm−3 HCl(aq), a known excess. Once the reaction is complete, the leftover (unreacted) HCl requires 20.00 cm3 of 0.500 mol dm−3 NaOH(aq) to reach the equivalence point in a titration. Calculate the percentage by mass of CaCO3 in the sample.

Reaction with the sample: CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)  (mole ratio 1 : 2)

Back titration: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)  (mole ratio 1 : 1)

First calculate the total amount of HCl added, a known excess:

n(HCl total) = (50.00/1000) dm3 × 1.000 mol dm−3 = 5.00 × 10−2 mol.

n(NaOH) = (20.00/1000) dm3 × 0.500 mol dm−3 = 1.00 × 10−2 mol = n(HCl unreacted), from the 1 : 1 titration ratio.

n(HCl reacted with CaCO3) = (5.00 × 10−2) − (1.00 × 10−2) = 4.00 × 10−2 mol.

From the 1 : 2 mole ratio in the first equation, n(CaCO3) = ½ × (4.00 × 10−2) = 2.00 × 10−2 mol.

M(CaCO3) = 40.08 + 12.01 + (3 × 16.00) = 100.09 g mol−1, so m(CaCO3) = 2.00 × 10−2 × 100.09 = 2.00 g.

% by mass = (2.00 / 2.50) × 100 = 80.0%.

This calculation assumes that every other component of the impure sample is insoluble in, or unreactive with, dilute HCl — an assumption that should be stated whenever a back titration purity result is reported.

Why use a back titration here?A direct titration of the carbonate sample with HCl would be very difficult: solid CaCO3 reacts only slowly and incompletely with acid added dropwise from a burette, and effervescing CO2 gas makes any colour-change end point almost impossible to see clearly. Adding a large, known excess of acid first allows the reaction to go to completion in its own time; titrating only the clear, gas-free leftover acid against NaOH then gives a sharp, easily detected end point.

R2.1.3 – The Limiting Reactant and Theoretical Yield

In most real reactions, reactants are not supplied in exactly the mole ratio given by the balanced equation. The reactant completely used up first, and so determines the quantity of product formed, is the limiting reactant; any reactant not fully used is said to be in excess.

Theoretical yieldThe theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product obtainable, in grams or moles, assuming 100% of the limiting reactant is converted into product.

Identifying the limiting reactant always depends on comparing mole ratios, so if reactant quantities are given in grams, cm3 of gas, or concentration and volume of solution, they must first all be converted to moles.

Worked example

N2 is prepared by 2NH3(g) + 3CuO(s) → N2(g) + 3Cu(s) + 3H2O(g). If 18.1 g NH3 (M = 17.04 g mol−1) reacts with 90.4 g CuO (M = 79.55 g mol−1), determine the mass of N2 (M = 28.02 g mol−1) formed.

n(NH3) = 18.1 / 17.04 = 1.06 mol. n(CuO) = 90.4 / 79.55 = 1.14 mol.

Required mole ratio NH3 : CuO = 2 : 3 = 0.667. Given ratio: 1.06 : 1.14 = 0.93. Since the given ratio exceeds the required ratio, NH3 is relatively in excess, and CuO is the limiting reactant.

Using CuO : N2 = 3 : 1: n(N2) = 1.14 / 3 = 0.380 mol, so m(N2) = 0.380 × 28.02 = 10.6 g.

Steps for limiting reactant problems
  • Write the balanced equation and note the mole ratio of the reactants.
  • Convert the given quantity of each reactant to moles.
  • Compare the given mole ratio of reactants with the ratio of their coefficients in the equation.
  • The reactant present in a smaller ratio than the equation requires is limiting.
  • Use the moles of the limiting reactant, and the mole ratio in the equation, to calculate the amount of product.

R2.1.4 – Percentage Yield

Real reactions rarely proceed with no loss of product, no impurities, no wastage, and no incomplete reaction, so the actual experimental yield obtained is usually different from the theoretical yield calculated from the limiting reactant.

Causes of experimental yield differing from theoretical yield
Experimental yield lower than theoreticalExperimental yield higher than theoretical
Side reactions occurringImpurities present in the collected product
Loss of product during purification or transferProduct not fully dried
Reversible reactions preventing completion (Reactivity 2.3)—
An incomplete reaction (can affect the yield in either direction, depending on the reaction type)
percentage yield = (experimental yield / theoretical yield) × 100
Worked example

The theoretical yield of N2 from a reaction of NH3 and CuO is 10.6 g. An experiment produces 8.35 g N2. Calculate the percentage yield.

percentage yield = (8.35 / 10.6) × 100 = 78.8%.

Percentage yield is important both in evaluating laboratory experiments and in industry, where it is used to assess and improve the efficiency of large-scale processes such as pharmaceutical drug synthesis. Green Chemistry places great importance on increasing yield in order to reduce wastage.

R2.1.5 – Atom Economy and Green Chemistry

Green Chemistry is the sustainable design of chemical products and processes, aiming to minimize the use and generation of substances hazardous to human health and the environment. Percentage yield alone is a limited measure of efficiency, since it only compares what was obtained to what was theoretically possible from the amounts of reactants used — it gives no indication of how much of the reactant mass was always destined to become waste by-products rather than desired product. The atom economy measures exactly this: what proportion of the total mass of the reactants ends up in the desired product.

% atom economy = (molar mass of desired product / molar mass of all reactants) × 100

(This equation is given in Section 1 of the data booklet.) A reaction can have a high percentage yield — almost none of the desired product actually made is lost during the process — while still having a low atom economy, if a large fraction of the reactant mass was always going to end up as an unwanted by-product rather than the desired product. The reverse is also possible: a reaction with excellent atom economy can still give a low percentage yield if side reactions, incomplete conversion, or losses during purification waste some of the desired product once it has formed.

Addition reactions can reach 100% atom economyIn an addition reaction, two reactant molecules combine to form a single product with no other substance released, so every atom from both reactants is necessarily incorporated into the product: the atom economy is 100%. Substitution and elimination reactions, by contrast, always release at least one additional small by-product molecule alongside the main product, so some of the reactant mass is inevitably lost as waste and the atom economy is always below 100%.
Worked example: addition versus substitution routes to the same product

Bromoethane, C2H5Br, can be made by two different routes. Compare their atom economies.

Route 1 (addition): C2H4(g) + HBr(g) → C2H5Br(l). Every atom in the reactants appears in the single product, so the atom economy is 100%.

Route 2 (substitution): C2H6(g) + Br2(l) → C2H5Br(l) + HBr(g). Using M(C2H5Br) = 108.97, M(C2H6) = 30.08 and M(Br2) = 159.80 g mol−1:

% atom economy = 108.97 / (30.08 + 159.80) × 100 = 108.97 / 189.88 × 100 = 57.4%

Both routes could in principle give a high percentage yield of pure bromoethane, but the addition route is far more atom-economical: none of the reactant mass is lost as a by-product, whereas the substitution route always releases one HBr molecule of by-product for every molecule of product formed.

Worked example

Nickel is produced by NiO(s) + C(s) → Ni(s) + CO(g). Calculate the atom economy, to 3 significant figures, given M(Ni) = 58.69, M(NiO) = 74.69 and M(C) = 12.01 g mol−1.

Molar mass of all reactants = 74.69 + 12.01 = 86.70 g mol−1.

% atom economy = (58.69 / 86.70) × 100 = 67.7%.

Other factors used to gauge the overall efficiency and sustainability of a chemical process include its rate (Reactivity 2.2), the quantities of reagents such as solvents and catalysts that are not themselves consumed, and its energy usage and economic efficiency.

HL – Multi-Step Worked Example: Limiting Reactant, Gas Volume and Percentage Yield Combined

Many HL exam questions combine several of the ideas in this chapter into a single multi-step problem. The strategy is always the same: work through the calculation one concept at a time, using the result of each step as the starting point for the next.

Worked example

5.00 g of calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (M = 100.09 g mol−1), is reacted with 40.0 cm3 of 2.00 mol dm−3 hydrochloric acid: CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g).

(a) Identify the limiting reactant. (b) Calculate the theoretical volume of CO2 gas produced at STP. (c) If 0.750 dm3 of CO2 is actually collected, calculate the percentage yield.

(a) n(CaCO3) = 5.00 / 100.09 = 0.0500 mol. n(HCl) = (40.0/1000) dm3 × 2.00 mol dm−3 = 0.0800 mol.

Required mole ratio CaCO3 : HCl = 1 : 2 = 0.500. Given ratio: 0.0500 : 0.0800 = 0.625. As the given ratio exceeds the required ratio, there is relatively more CaCO3 than the equation needs, so HCl is the limiting reactant (and CaCO3 is in excess).

(b) Using the limiting reactant and the mole ratio HCl : CO2 = 2 : 1: n(CO2) = 0.0800 / 2 = 0.0400 mol.

V(CO2) at STP = n × Vm = 0.0400 mol × 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 0.908 dm3 (the theoretical yield, expressed as a gas volume).

(c) percentage yield = (experimental yield / theoretical yield) × 100 = (0.750 / 0.908) × 100 = 82.6%.

Recognizing a multi-step questionA question is signalling a multi-step calculation whenever it gives masses, volumes, or concentrations of more than one reactant (limiting reactant is involved), asks for an answer in a gas volume or solution concentration rather than a mass (a further unit conversion is needed), and/or separately states both a theoretical/calculated quantity and an experimentally obtained quantity (percentage yield is involved). Working through each concept in turn, rather than trying to combine steps, is the most reliable approach.

Guiding Question Revisited

How are chemical equations used to calculate reacting ratios, and how can those ratios be used to quantify and improve the efficiency of a chemical process?
  • A balanced chemical equation gives the mole ratio of reactants and products directly from its stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Mole ratios convert to reacting masses using m = nM, to reacting gas volumes using Avogadro's law (n = V/Vm at STP, or PV = nRT at any other temperature and pressure), and to solution concentrations and volumes, via titration, using n = cV; dilution problems use c1V1 = c2V2.
  • Back titration measures the amount of an impure, insoluble, or otherwise awkward reactant indirectly: a known excess of a standard reagent is added, and the unreacted excess is titrated to find how much actually reacted.
  • The limiting reactant is identified by comparing the given mole ratio of reactants with the ratio of coefficients in the equation; it determines the theoretical yield of product, while any other reactant is left in excess.
  • Percentage yield = (experimental yield / theoretical yield) × 100 compares what was actually obtained with the maximum possible amount.
  • Atom economy = (molar mass of desired product / molar mass of all reactants) × 100 measures how much of the reactant mass ends up in the desired product rather than in waste by-products; addition reactions can reach 100%, while substitution and elimination reactions always fall short of it. Percentage yield and atom economy are independent measures of efficiency, and a process can score well on one while scoring poorly on the other.
