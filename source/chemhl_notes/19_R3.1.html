Guiding Question

What happens when protons are transferred?

Reaction mechanisms describe how reactions proceed at the molecular level. There are four fundamental types of reaction based on the behaviour of subatomic particles: proton transfer reactions (acid–base reactions and neutralization), electron transfer reactions (redox), electron-sharing reactions (radicals) and electron-pair-sharing reactions (electrophiles and nucleophiles). This sub-topic deals with the first of these: proton transfer reactions. At Standard Level, Brønsted–Lowry theory, the pH scale and the qualitative distinction between strong and weak acids and bases provide a descriptive picture of acid–base behaviour. At Higher Level, the equilibrium law introduced in Reactivity 2.3 is applied directly to weak acids and bases, giving quantitative acid dissociation and base dissociation constants (Ka and Kb) that allow the pH of any weak acid or base solution to be calculated, the relative strengths of different acids and bases to be compared precisely, and the behaviour of buffer solutions and titration curves to be explained and predicted mathematically.

Brønsted–Lowry Theory and Conjugate Acid–Base Pairs (Recap)

A hydrogen atom contains just one proton and one electron, so when it ionizes by losing its electron to form H+, all that remains is the proton: H+ and “a proton” can be used interchangeably. When a proton is transferred in a reaction, the species that loses it loses a positive charge, while the species that gains it gains a positive charge.

Brønsted–Lowry theory (Reactivity 3.1.1)A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H+) donor. A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton (H+) acceptor. A proton in aqueous solution can be represented as either H+(aq) or H3O+(aq); the latter, variously called the hydroxonium, oxonium or hydronium ion, is the true form taken by a proton in water, though H+(aq) is used as a convenient simplification throughout this sub-topic.

Donating a proton cannot happen in isolation: there must always be a base present to accept it. For a generic acid HA reacting with a generic base B:

HA + B ↔ A− + BH+

Reading the forward reaction, HA is the acid and B is the base. Reading the reverse reaction, BH+ now acts as an acid (donating H+ back) and A− acts as a base (accepting H+). Acid HA has become base A−, and base B has become acid BH+.

Conjugate acid–base pair (Reactivity 3.1.2)A pair of species differing by a single proton is called a conjugate acid–base pair. In HA + B ↔ A− + BH+, the pairs are HA/A− and B/BH+. To write the conjugate base of an acid, remove one H+ (and reduce the charge by 1). To write the conjugate acid of a base, add one H+ (and increase the charge by 1).
Worked example

Label the conjugate acid–base pairs in CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ CH3COO−(aq) + H3O+(aq), and write the conjugate base of H2CO3 and the conjugate acid of CO32−.
CH3COOH (acid) / CH3COO− (base) is one conjugate pair; H2O (base) / H3O+ (acid) is the other. Removing one H+ from H2CO3 gives its conjugate base, HCO3−. Adding one H+ to CO32− gives its conjugate acid, HCO3−.

Most polyatomic ions act as Brønsted–Lowry bases, accepting H+ to form their conjugate acid (e.g. NO3− → HNO3; SO42− → HSO4−; PO43− → HPO42−). The ammonium ion, NH4+, cannot accept another H+; instead it loses H+ and acts as a Brønsted–Lowry acid, forming its conjugate base NH3.

Base vs. alkaliA Brønsted–Lowry base is any species that can accept a proton. An alkali is a smaller, specific group of soluble bases which dissolve in water to release the hydroxide ion, OH−. Every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali.

Amphiprotic Species (Recap)

Brønsted–Lowry theory describes acids and bases in terms of how they behave in a particular reaction, so the same species can act as an acid in one reaction and a base in another:

CH3COOH + H2O ↔ CH3COO− + H3O+  (water acts as a base)
NH3 + H2O ↔ NH4+ + OH−  (water acts as an acid)

Amphiprotic (Reactivity 3.1.3)A species that can act as both a Brønsted–Lowry acid and a Brønsted–Lowry base is described as amphiprotic. To act as an acid it must be able to dissociate and release H+; to act as a base it must have a lone pair of electrons able to accept H+. An amphiprotic species must therefore possess both a releasable H and a lone pair.
Worked example

Write equations to show HCO3− acting (a) as an acid and (b) as a base.
(a) As an acid, it donates H+: HCO3−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ CO32−(aq) + H3O+(aq).
(b) As a base, it accepts H+: HCO3−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ H2CO3(aq) + OH−(aq).

Amphiprotic vs. amphotericAmphiprotic specifically refers to Brønsted–Lowry theory, where the emphasis is on proton transfer. Amphoteric has a broader meaning, including acid–base behaviour that does not involve proton transfer (explained instead by Lewis theory, discussed later in this chapter). All amphiprotic substances are amphoteric, but not all amphoteric substances are amphiprotic — e.g. aluminium oxide is amphoteric (reacts with both acids and alkalis) but is not amphiprotic.

The pH and pOH Scales (Recap)

Because most acids encountered are weak, [H+] expressed directly in mol dm−3 often has an inconveniently large negative exponent. Søren Sorensen introduced the pH scale in 1909 to give a more convenient, quantitative measure of acid strength.

pH = −log10[H+]      [H+] = 10−pH

pH is inversely related to [H+]: a higher [H+] gives a lower pH. Because the scale is logarithmic, a change of one pH unit represents a 10-fold change in [H+]. The number of decimal places in a calculated pH should equal the number of significant figures in the original concentration, since the digit(s) left of the decimal point come from the exponential part of the number and are not significant.

The same idea can be applied to [OH−], giving the pOH scale (Reactivity 3.1.9):

pOH = −log10[OH−]      [OH−] = 10−pOH

pOH shares every feature of the pH scale: pOH numbers are normally positive and have no units, pOH is inversely related to [OH−], and a change of one pOH unit is a 10-fold change in [OH−]. Taking negative logarithms of Kw = [H+][OH−] gives the key relationship linking the two scales:

pH + pOH = pKw    (pKw = 14.00, so pH + pOH = 14.00 at 298 K)
Worked example

Lemon juice has a pH of 2.90 at 298 K. Calculate its [H+], pOH and [OH−].
[H+] = 10−2.90 = 1.26 × 10−3 mol dm−3.
pOH = 14.00 − 2.90 = 11.10.
[OH−] = 10−11.10 = 7.94 × 10−12 mol dm−3 (check: Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1.26 × 10−3 × 7.94 × 10−12 = 1.00 × 10−14, consistent).

The pH/pOH scale at 298 KA single horizontal number line running from 0 to 14, labelled pH along the top and pOH along the bottom in the opposite direction (pOH 14 sits below pH 0, and pOH 0 sits below pH 14). Strong acids occupy the low-pH end, weak acids and the neutral point (pH 7, pOH 7) sit in the middle, and weak and then strong alkalis occupy the high-pH end. As pH rises from left to right, pOH falls from left to right, always summing to 14.00 at any point on the line.

Kw, the Ion Product Constant of Water (Recap)

Water itself ionizes very slightly: H2O(l) ↔ H+(aq) + OH−(aq). Because [H2O] is essentially constant, it is combined into a modified equilibrium constant, Kw.

Kw = [H+][OH−]    (at 298 K, Kw = 1.00 × 10−14)

In pure water, [H+] = [OH−] = √Kw, giving [H+] = 1.00 × 10−7 mol dm−3 and pH = 7.00 at 298 K.

Classifying solutions at 298 K (Reactivity 3.1.5)Acidic: [H+] > [OH−], pH < 7. Neutral: [H+] = [OH−], pH = 7. Alkaline: [H+] < [OH−], pH > 7. Given either [H+] or [OH−], the other is found from Kw = [H+][OH−].

The dissociation of water is endothermic, so increasing temperature shifts the equilibrium right, increasing Kw and decreasing the pH of pure water; decreasing temperature has the reverse effect. Pure water remains neutral at any temperature, since [H+] = [OH−] always — pH 7.00 corresponds to neutral only at 298 K, so temperature should always be stated alongside a pH measurement.

pKwJust as Kw can be converted to pKw = −log10Kw (pKw = 14.00 at 298 K), the same logarithmic treatment is applied to the acid and base dissociation constants introduced later in this chapter, giving pKa and pKb.

Strong and Weak Acids and Bases (Recap)

Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases exist in aqueous solution as equilibrium mixtures of the undissociated form and the ions. The position of this equilibrium defines the strength of the acid or base (Reactivity 3.1.6):

HA(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ A−(aq) + H3O+(aq)

Strong vs. weakA strong acid or base dissociates/ionizes almost fully, its equilibrium lying essentially completely to the right (a single forward arrow is used). A weak acid or base dissociates/ionizes only partially, its equilibrium lying well to the left (an equilibrium sign must be used). Do not confuse strong/weak (an inherent property, the extent of dissociation) with concentrated/dilute (moles of solute per unit volume) — a strong acid can be dilute, and a weak acid can be concentrated.
Common strong and weak acids/basesStrong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, H2SO4. Strong bases: the group 1 hydroxides, LiOH, NaOH, KOH. Weak acids (examples): CH3COOH and other carboxylic acids, H2CO3, H3PO4, HF, HCN. Weak bases (examples): NH3, amines such as CH3NH2 and C2H5NH2. There are only a handful of common strong acids and bases, so any acid or base not on this short list is very likely weak.

Strong acids/bases have a high concentration of the ionized (product) form at equilibrium and so have large equilibrium constants; weak acids/bases have a low concentration of ionized product and so have much smaller equilibrium constants — the HL sections that follow quantify this directly through Ka and Kb. At the same molar concentration, strong acids/bases can be distinguished from weak ones by electrical conductivity (strong acids/bases conduct more strongly), rate of reaction (faster with stronger acids) and pH (lower pH for a stronger acid at equal concentration).

HL: The Acid and Base Dissociation Constants, Ka and Kb

Unlike strong acids and bases, weak acids and bases do not dissociate fully, so their ion concentrations cannot be deduced directly from the initial concentration — they depend on the extent of dissociation, which is governed by an equilibrium. Applying the equilibrium law of Reactivity 2.3 to a generic weak acid HA dissociating in water:

HA(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ H3O+(aq) + A−(aq)

Because [H2O] is essentially constant, it is combined into a modified equilibrium constant, the acid dissociation constant, Ka (Reactivity 3.1.10):

Ka = ([H+][A−]) / [HA]

Similarly, for a generic weak base B ionizing in water, the base dissociation constant, Kb, is defined as:

B(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ BH+(aq) + OH−(aq)     Kb = ([BH+][OH−]) / [B]
Ka and Kb measure acid/base strengthKa and Kb are equilibrium constants, so each has a fixed value for a particular acid or base at a specified temperature, independent of concentration. The higher the value of Ka (or Kb), the greater the extent of dissociation, and so the stronger the acid (or base). Because strong acids and bases dissociate essentially completely, Ka and Kb are not normally quoted for them — the concept is most useful for weak acids and bases.
Worked example

Write the Ka expression for CH3COOH(aq) and the Kb expression for NH3(aq).
CH3COOH(aq) ↔ CH3COO−(aq) + H+(aq), so Ka = ([CH3COO−][H+]) / [CH3COOH].
NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ NH4+(aq) + OH−(aq), so Kb = ([NH4+][OH−]) / [NH3].

Worked example: calculating Ka from pH and concentration

Calculate Ka at 298 K for a 0.0100 mol dm−3 solution of ethanoic acid that has a pH of 3.4 at this temperature.
From the pH: [H+] = 10−3.4 = 4.0 × 10−4 mol dm−3. From the 1:1 stoichiometry of the dissociation, [CH3COO−] = [H+] = 4.0 × 10−4 mol dm−3 at equilibrium, and [CH3COOH]eqm = 0.0100 − 4.0 × 10−4 ≈ 0.0100 mol dm−3 (the small amount dissociated is negligible compared with the initial concentration).

Ka = ([CH3COO−][H+]) / [CH3COOH] = (4.0 × 10−4)2 / 0.0100 = 1.6 × 10−5.

HL: pKa, pKb and the Relationship Ka × Kb = Kw

Because Ka and Kb values are typically very small and span a wide range, they are converted into their negative base-10 logarithms, exactly as [H+] is converted to pH:

pKa = −log10Ka     pKb = −log10Kb
Interpreting pKa and pKb (Reactivity 3.1.10)pKa and pKb are inversely related to Ka and Kb: a smaller pKa means a larger Ka and so a stronger acid (more fully dissociated); a smaller pKb means a stronger base. A change of one unit in pKa or pKb represents a tenfold change in Ka or Kb. Like pKw, pKa and pKb must always be quoted at a specified temperature, since they derive from temperature-dependent equilibrium constants.

For a conjugate acid–base pair HA/A−, the Ka expression for the acid and the Kb expression for its conjugate base can be multiplied together:

HA(aq) ↔ H+(aq) + A−(aq)   Ka = ([H+][A−]) / [HA]
A−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ HA(aq) + OH−(aq)   Kb = ([HA][OH−]) / [A−]

Ka × Kb = ([H+][A−]/[HA]) × ([HA][OH−]/[A−]) = [H+][OH−] = Kw, since [HA] and [A−] cancel exactly.

Ka × Kb = Kw      pKa + pKb = pKw = 14.00 (at 298 K)

This relationship (Reactivity 3.1.11) holds for any conjugate acid–base pair in aqueous solution, and shows quantitatively that the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base, and vice versa — consistent with the qualitative rule that acid–base equilibria favour the weaker conjugate.

Worked example

Ka of ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, is 1.8 × 10−5 at 298 K. Calculate pKa of CH3COOH, and Kb and pKb of its conjugate base, CH3COO−.

pKa = −log10(1.8 × 10−5) = 4.74.

Kb = Kw / Ka = (1.00 × 10−14) / (1.8 × 10−5) = 5.6 × 10−10.

pKb = −log10(5.6 × 10−10) = 9.26 (check: pKa + pKb = 4.74 + 9.26 = 14.00, confirming the relationship).

Why this matters for hydrolysis of saltsBecause every conjugate base has its own Kb (and every conjugate acid its own Ka) derivable from Ka × Kb = Kw, even the anion or cation of a simple salt can act as a weak base or weak acid in water: a salt of a weak acid and a strong base gives a solution with pH > 7 (the anion hydrolyses, e.g. CH3COO−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ CH3COOH(aq) + OH−(aq)), while a salt of a strong acid and a weak base gives pH < 7 (the cation hydrolyses, e.g. NH4+(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ NH3(aq) + H3O+(aq)). A salt of a strong acid and strong base gives pH = 7, since neither ion hydrolyses appreciably.

HL: Calculating the pH of a Weak Acid Solution

Given Ka and the initial concentration of a weak acid, an ICE table (as in Reactivity 2.3) can be used to find [H+] at equilibrium and hence the pH. Let the initial concentration of acid HA be C, and let x be the concentration that dissociates:

HAH+A−
initial / mol dm−3C00
change / mol dm−3−x+x+x
equilibrium / mol dm−3C − xxx

Ka = x2 / (C − x). Because weak acids dissociate only slightly, Ka is usually very small compared with C, so x is negligible compared with C and the approximation C − x ≈ C can be made, avoiding a quadratic equation:

Ka ≈ x2 / C   ⇒   [H+] = x ≈ √(Ka × C)
Validity of the approximationThe approximation [H+] ≈ √(Ka × C) is valid only when the degree of dissociation is small. As a rule of thumb, it can safely be used whenever the calculated [H+] turns out to be less than about 5% of the initial concentration C (equivalently, when C / Ka is large, typically greater than about 400–500). This condition should be checked after the calculation; if it fails, the full quadratic Ka = x2/(C − x) must be solved instead, as in Reactivity 2.3.
Worked example

A 0.75 mol dm−3 solution of ethanoic acid has Ka = 1.8 × 10−5 at 298 K. Calculate its pH.

Since Ka ≪ C, the approximation [CH3COOH]eqm ≈ 0.75 mol dm−3 is valid.

[H+] = √(Ka × C) = √(1.8 × 10−5 × 0.75) = √(1.35 × 10−5) = 3.7 × 10−3 mol dm−3.

Check: 3.7 × 10−3 is only 0.49% of 0.75, well under 5%, so the approximation is confirmed valid.

pH = −log10(3.7 × 10−3) = 2.43.

Why this differs from a strong acid calculationFor a strong acid of the same concentration, full dissociation would be assumed and [H+] = C directly, giving a much lower (more acidic) pH. For 0.75 mol dm−3 HCl, pH = −log10(0.75) = 0.12, compared with pH 2.43 for 0.75 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid — the weak acid is far less acidic at the same concentration because only a small fraction has dissociated.

HL: Calculating the pH of a Weak Base Solution

The same method applies to a weak base B, initial concentration C, ionizing as B(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ BH+(aq) + OH−(aq):

Kb ≈ x2 / C   ⇒   [OH−] = x ≈ √(Kb × C)

valid under the same condition as for a weak acid: the degree of ionization must be small (calculated [OH−] less than about 5% of C). Once [OH−] is found, pOH = −log10[OH−], and pH = 14.00 − pOH at 298 K.

Worked example

A 0.20 mol dm−3 aqueous solution of ammonia has Kb = 1.8 × 10−5 at 298 K. Calculate its pH.

Since Kb ≪ C, [NH3]eqm ≈ 0.20 mol dm−3.

[OH−] = √(Kb × C) = √(1.8 × 10−5 × 0.20) = √(3.6 × 10−6) = 1.9 × 10−3 mol dm−3 (0.95% of 0.20, so the approximation is valid).

pOH = −log10(1.9 × 10−3) = 2.72.

pH = 14.00 − 2.72 = 11.28.

Summary of the four pH/pOH calculation routesStrong acid, concentration C: [H+] = C (assume full dissociation). Weak acid, Ka known: [H+] ≈ √(KaC). Strong base, concentration C: [OH−] = C (or C multiplied by the number of OH− per formula unit). Weak base, Kb known: [OH−] ≈ √(KbC). In every case, convert the resulting [H+] or [OH−] to pH using pH = −log10[H+] directly, or via pOH and pH + pOH = 14.00.

HL: Relative Strength of Acids and Bases from Ka/Kb and pKa/pKb

Because Ka and Kb are direct measures of the equilibrium position of dissociation, they allow the strengths of different weak acids (or different weak bases) to be compared quantitatively, not just qualitatively as “weak”. A larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a more fully dissociated, stronger acid; a larger Kb (smaller pKb) means a stronger base.

AcidFormulaKa / mol dm−3pKa
hydrofluoric acidHF6.8 × 10−43.17
methanoic acidHCOOH1.8 × 10−43.75
ethanoic acidCH3COOH1.8 × 10−54.76
propanoic acidC2H5COOH1.3 × 10−54.87
hydrocyanic acidHCN6.2 × 10−109.21

All values at 298 K. Reading down the table, Ka falls and pKa rises, so acid strength decreases: HF > HCOOH > CH3COOH > C2H5COOH > HCN. Lengthening the hydrocarbon chain of a carboxylic acid slightly weakens the acid (ethanoic > propanoic), while HCN is by far the weakest acid listed.

BaseFormulaKb / mol dm−3pKb
ammoniaNH31.8 × 10−54.75
methylamineCH3NH24.6 × 10−43.34
ethylamineC2H5NH24.5 × 10−43.35

All values at 298 K. Methylamine and ethylamine are both stronger bases than ammonia (larger Kb, smaller pKb), because the electron-donating alkyl group increases the availability of the lone pair on nitrogen for accepting H+.

Worked example

Using the table above, deduce which is the stronger acid, HF or HCN, and state the relative strength of their conjugate bases, F− and CN−.
HF has the smaller pKa (3.17 vs 9.21) and so is the stronger acid; HCN is a much weaker acid. Since acid strength and conjugate base strength are inversely related, F− (conjugate base of the stronger acid) is the weaker base, while CN− (conjugate base of the much weaker acid HCN) is the stronger base.

Why Ka/Kb are not used for strong acids/basesStrong acids and bases dissociate essentially completely, so their equilibrium lies so far to the right that Ka or Kb would be enormous (and experimentally very difficult to measure precisely) — the concept of a dissociation constant is only informative, and only normally quoted, for weak acids and bases where an equilibrium mixture of measurable composition actually exists.

HL: Buffer Solutions

Buffer solution (Reactivity 3.1.16)A buffer solution is one that resists change in pH on the addition of small amounts of acid or base. An acidic buffer maintains a pH below 7; a basic buffer maintains a pH above 7 — though neither is restricted to a particular pH value, and buffers can be designed to maintain almost any pH.

An unbuffered solution can show large pH swings on addition of only a few drops of acid or base (e.g. pure water at pH 7 can jump to pH 4 or pH 10 on addition of just 0.1 cm3 of dilute acid or base). Biological systems depend on buffers because enzyme activity is highly sensitive to pH: human blood is buffered within a very narrow range around pH 7.4, and ocean water is buffered by the carbonate/hydrogencarbonate system.

A buffer solution is made from one of two combinations:

  • Acidic buffer: a weak acid together with a soluble salt of its conjugate base (e.g. CH3COOH(aq) and NaCH3COO(aq)).
  • Basic buffer: a weak base together with a soluble salt of its conjugate acid (e.g. NH3(aq) and NH4Cl(aq)).

In an acidic buffer, the weak acid HA dissociates only slightly (HA(aq) ↔ H+(aq) + A−(aq), equilibrium far left), while its soluble salt dissociates fully, supplying a high concentration of A−. The mixture therefore contains substantial reservoirs of both HA and A−, ready to react with added base or acid respectively:

  • Added acid (H+) is removed by the conjugate base: A−(aq) + H+(aq) → HA(aq).
  • Added base (OH−) is removed by the weak acid: HA(aq) + OH−(aq) → A−(aq) + H2O(l).

A basic buffer works analogously, with the weak base B neutralizing added acid (B(aq) + H+(aq) → BH+(aq)) and its conjugate acid BH+ neutralizing added base (BH+(aq) + OH−(aq) → B(aq) + H2O(l)).

Why buffers require a weak, not strong, conjugate pairBecause the components of a buffer must respond reversibly to both added H+ and added OH−, they must exist as an equilibrium mixture containing appreciable concentrations of both the acid and base forms. A strong acid or base ionizes essentially completely, leaving (almost) none of the undissociated form present to react with added base (for a strong acid) or added acid (for a strong base) — so a buffer can only be made from a weak acid/base conjugate system.
Worked example

State whether each mixture, dissolved in 1.00 dm3 of water, forms a buffer solution: (a) 0.20 mol NaHCO3 and 0.20 mol Na2CO3; (b) 0.20 mol NH3 and 0.10 mol HCl; (c) 0.20 mol CH3COOH and 0.20 mol HCl.
(a) HCO3− and CO32− form a conjugate acid–base pair, both present — this is a buffer.
(b) NH3 and HCl react to form 0.10 mol NH4Cl, leaving 0.10 mol NH3 unreacted: the mixture now contains NH3 and NH4+, a conjugate pair — this is a buffer.
(c) This mixture contains two different acids (one weak, one strong) and no conjugate base reservoir — this is not a buffer.

Because Ka and Kb are unaffected by concentration, and because dilution changes the concentrations of both the acid and base buffer components by the same factor (leaving their ratio, and hence the pH, unchanged), diluting a buffer does not change its pH. Dilution does, however, reduce the absolute amount of acid or base the buffer can neutralize before its pH changes significantly — its buffering capacity — since this depends on the actual concentrations of the buffer components, not their ratio.

HL: Buffer pH – The Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation

For an acidic buffer made of weak acid HA and its salt MA, two approximations can be made: the dissociation of the weak acid is negligible, so [HA]eqm ≈ [HA]initial; and the salt is fully dissociated, so [A−]eqm ≈ [salt]initial. Substituting into the Ka expression:

Ka = [H+][A−] / [HA]  ⇒  [H+] = Ka × ([acid] / [salt])

Taking negative logarithms of both sides gives the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation (Reactivity 3.1.17):

pH = pKa + log10([A−] / [HA])    i.e.    pH = pKa + log10([salt] / [acid])

For a basic buffer of weak base B and its salt BHX, the parallel equation is pOH = pKb + log10([salt]/[base]), with pH then found from pH = 14.00 − pOH.

The pH of a buffer depends on two things onlyThe pH of a buffer solution depends on: (1) the pKa (or pKb) of its weak acid or base component, which sets the pH the buffer is centred on; and (2) the ratio of the concentrations of the conjugate base and acid (or conjugate acid and base) actually present, which fine-tunes the pH around that centre. When [salt] = [acid] (or [salt] = [base]), the ratio is 1, log10(1) = 0, and pH = pKa (or pOH = pKb) exactly.
Worked example

Calculate the pH at 298 K of a buffer prepared by mixing 25 cm3 of 0.10 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, with 25 cm3 of 0.10 mol dm−3 sodium ethanoate, NaCH3COO. Ka(CH3COOH) = 1.8 × 10−5 at 298 K.

pKa = −log10(1.8 × 10−5) = 4.74. Since equal volumes of equal concentrations are mixed, [acid] = [salt], so [salt]/[acid] = 1.

pH = pKa + log10(1) = 4.74 + 0 = 4.74.

Worked example: an unequal ratio

A buffer is prepared containing 0.30 mol dm−3 methanoic acid, HCOOH (pKa = 3.75), and 0.15 mol dm−3 sodium methanoate, HCOONa. Calculate the pH.

pH = pKa + log10([salt]/[acid]) = 3.75 + log10(0.15/0.30) = 3.75 + log10(0.50) = 3.75 + (−0.30) = 3.45.

Note that because [salt] < [acid] here, the pH is lower than pKa — the buffer is slightly more acidic than an equimolar mixture of the same acid and salt would be.

Choosing an acid to make a buffer of a target pHTo prepare a buffer at a specific pH, choose a weak acid (or base) whose pKa (or pKb) is close to the target pH (or pOH), then adjust the ratio of salt to acid (or salt to base) using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation to fine-tune the exact value. An equimolar mixture of the acid and its salt can be prepared directly, or by partially neutralizing the acid with a strong base so that exactly half of it is converted to salt.

HL: Acid–Base Titration Curves

A titration (Reactivity 3.1.8/3.1.13) monitors pH continuously (by pH meter or data-logger) as a base is added from a burette to an acid in a conical flask (or vice versa), producing a pH curve against volume of titrant added. The following four cases all use 0.10 mol dm−3 monoprotic solutions, with 50.0 cm3 of acid in the flask and base added from the burette, so that equivalence occurs at 50.0 cm3 of titrant added in every case.

Strong Acid–Strong Base

Strong acid–strong base pH curveA graph of pH (0–14, vertical axis) against volume of NaOH added (0–75 cm3, horizontal axis). The curve begins flat at a low pH around 1 (the pH of the strong acid alone), rises only gradually as base is added, then shoots almost vertically upward in a very sharp jump centred on 50 cm3 — from about pH 3 to about pH 11 — before flattening out again at a high pH close to 13 (the pH of the excess strong base). The equivalence point sits halfway up this near-vertical jump, at pH 7.

Because the salt formed (e.g. NaCl) contains ions from a strong acid and a strong base, neither hydrolyses, so the pH at equivalence is exactly 7. This titration gives by far the largest, sharpest jump in pH of the four cases.

Weak Acid–Strong Base

Weak acid–strong base pH curveA graph with the same axes. The curve starts at a fairly high initial pH (higher than for a strong acid of the same concentration), rises only slowly and stays relatively flat through a long, gently sloping region extending roughly to the mid-point of the volume added (the buffer region, where the flask contains a mixture of the weak acid and its conjugate base salt), then shows a jump in pH at equivalence from about pH 7 to pH 11 — smaller than the strong–strong jump — before flattening out at a high pH (that of the excess strong base).

At equivalence the flask contains only the salt of a weak acid and a strong base: the anion hydrolyses (e.g. CH3COO−(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ CH3COOH(aq) + OH−(aq)), releasing OH−, so the pH at equivalence is greater than 7.

The half-equivalence point: finding pKa experimentallyAt exactly half the volume needed to reach equivalence (the half-equivalence point), exactly half of the original weak acid has been converted to its conjugate base salt, so [acid]eqm = [salt]eqm in the flask. By the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log10(1) = pKa at this point. Reading the pH directly off the curve at the half-equivalence volume therefore gives an experimental value for the pKa of the weak acid — one of the most useful applications of a titration curve.
Worked example

25.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 CH3COOH(aq) is titrated with 0.100 mol dm−3 NaOH(aq). The equivalence point occurs at 25.0 cm3 of NaOH added. At 12.5 cm3 of NaOH added, the measured pH is 4.74. State what this tells us, and identify the value obtained.

12.5 cm3 is exactly half of the 25.0 cm3 needed for equivalence, so this is the half-equivalence point, where [CH3COOH] = [CH3COO−] in the flask. Therefore pH = pKa, so pKa(CH3COOH) = 4.74 (consistent with Ka = 1.8 × 10−5).

Strong Acid–Weak Base

Strong acid–weak base pH curveA graph, now with weak base (e.g. NH3) added from the burette to a strong acid in the flask. The curve starts at a low pH around 1 (strong acid), rises gradually, passes through a buffer region as equivalence approaches, shows a jump at equivalence from about pH 3 to pH 7 — again smaller than the strong–strong case — then flattens out at a fairly low pH (that of excess weak base, well below the pH 13–14 reached with a strong base).

At equivalence the salt formed contains the conjugate acid of a weak base: the cation hydrolyses (e.g. NH4+(aq) + H2O(l) ↔ NH3(aq) + H3O+(aq)), releasing H+, so the pH at equivalence is less than 7. A parallel half-equivalence analysis applies here too, giving pOH = pKb of the weak base at that point.

Weak Acid–Weak Base

Weak acid–weak base pH curveA graph in which the pH rises steadily and smoothly from a fairly high starting value (weak acid) to a fairly low final value (weak base) relative to the other three cases, with no sharp, near-vertical jump anywhere near the equivalence volume — just a gentle, almost featureless change in slope through the middle of the curve.

Because both the acid and base are weak, several equilibria compete simultaneously and there is no large jump in pH to mark equivalence clearly; the pH at equivalence cannot be predicted simply and depends on the relative Ka and Kb values of the two reactants. A pH curve (or indicator) is not a reliable way to locate the equivalence point for this combination — other techniques, such as conductimetric titration, are used instead.

HL: Acid–Base Indicators

Indicators are weak acids (or bases) (Reactivity 3.1.14)An acid–base indicator is itself a weak acid (or weak base) whose undissociated and dissociated forms have distinctly different colours. Using the generic indicator HInd: HInd(aq) ↔ H+(aq) + Ind−(aq), colour A (HInd) ≠ colour B (Ind−).

By Le Châtelier’s principle, increasing [H+] shifts this equilibrium left (favouring colour A, HInd), while decreasing [H+] shifts it right (favouring colour B, Ind−). The dissociation constant of the indicator is:

Ka (indicator) = ([H+][Ind−]) / [HInd]

At the exact midpoint of the colour change, [HInd] = [Ind−], so these terms cancel, leaving Ka = [H+], i.e. pH = pKa of the indicator at its colour-change midpoint. This is called the indicator’s end point. Because the human eye can distinguish the pure colour of one form from a mixture only once the ratio of concentrations reaches about 10:1, the observable colour change actually spans roughly ±1 pH unit around pKa, giving each indicator a characteristic end point range rather than a single sharp value.

Choosing the right indicator (Reactivity 3.1.15)An indicator is suitable for a particular titration only when its end point range coincides with (lies within) the steep, near-vertical part of the pH curve around the equivalence point — not necessarily pH 7. Choosing an indicator therefore requires three steps: (1) identify the strong/weak nature of the acid and base being titrated; (2) deduce the approximate pH at equivalence from this; (3) select an indicator whose end point range matches that pH.

Worked example

Explain why phenolphthalein, but not methyl orange, is a suitable indicator for the titration of ethanoic acid with sodium hydroxide.

This is a weak acid–strong base titration, so the pH at equivalence is greater than 7 (around 8–9, due to hydrolysis of the ethanoate ion). Phenolphthalein has an end point range of 8.2–10.0, which coincides with the sharp part of the pH curve near equivalence, so it changes colour at the correct point. Methyl orange has an end point range of 3.1–4.4, which lies in the gently sloping buffer region of this curve, well before equivalence — it would change colour far too early to signal the correct end point.

End point vs. equivalence pointThe equivalence point is the exact stoichiometric point where acid and base have reacted in the mole ratio of the balanced equation (a mathematical/graphical concept). The end point is the pH at which the chosen indicator visibly changes colour during the actual experiment. A well-chosen indicator has an end point range that brackets the equivalence point, so the observed end point closely approximates the true equivalence point.

Lewis Acid–Base Theory: A Broader View

Brønsted–Lowry theory describes acid–base behaviour purely in terms of proton transfer, which requires the acid to possess a releasable H and the base to possess a lone pair. The American chemist Gilbert Lewis proposed a broader theory, based instead on the movement of an electron pair, that includes Brønsted–Lowry behaviour as a special case while also extending to species that have no proton to donate or accept at all.

Lewis acid–base theoryA Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor. A Lewis base is an electron-pair donor. Every Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction is also a Lewis acid–base reaction (the base donates a lone pair to form a new bond to H+, which accepts it), but Lewis theory also covers many reactions with no proton transfer at all.

For example, boron trifluoride, BF3, has no hydrogen to donate as a proton, so it cannot be a Brønsted–Lowry acid at all — yet boron has an incomplete octet (only six electrons around it) and so readily accepts a lone pair from a species such as ammonia:

BF3 + :NH3 → F3B–NH3

Here BF3 is a Lewis acid (it accepts the electron pair into a new covalent bond) and NH3 is a Lewis base (it donates the electron pair), even though no proton is transferred. Similarly, the formation of a complex ion such as [Cu(NH3)4]2+ from Cu2+(aq) and NH3(aq) is a Lewis acid–base reaction: Cu2+, a metal cation with empty orbitals, is the Lewis acid, and each NH3 ligand, donating a lone pair to form a dative covalent bond, is a Lewis base.

Why a broader theory was neededBrønsted–Lowry theory cannot classify the reaction of BF3 with NH3, nor the formation of transition metal complex ions, as acid–base reactions at all, since no proton changes hands in either case. Lewis theory resolves this by focusing on the more fundamental process common to both proton transfer and these other reactions: the formation of a new covalent bond from a donated electron pair. This is why Lewis theory, alongside Brønsted–Lowry theory, is considered to have “stood the test of time” — each is the right tool for a different, well-defined range of chemical behaviour.

Guiding Question Revisited

What happens when protons are transferred?
  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor and a Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor; conjugate acid–base pairs differ by a single proton, and amphiprotic species such as H2O and HCO3− can act as either.
  • pH = −log10[H+] and pOH = −log10[OH−]; pH + pOH = 14.00 at 298 K, since Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1.00 × 10−14 at this temperature.
  • Strong acids/bases dissociate almost fully; weak acids/bases dissociate only partially, establishing an equilibrium.
  • HL: the acid and base dissociation constants, Ka and Kb (and their logarithmic forms, pKa and pKb), quantify the strength of weak acids and bases; for any conjugate pair, Ka × Kb = Kw and pKa + pKb = 14.00 at 298 K.
  • HL: the pH of a weak acid or base solution can be calculated from [H+] ≈ √(KaC) or [OH−] ≈ √(KbC), valid when dissociation is small (checked against the 5% rule of thumb).
  • HL: a buffer solution, made from a weak acid/base and its conjugate salt, resists pH change; its pH is given by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log10([salt]/[acid]).
  • HL: titration curves have characteristic shapes for strong–strong, weak acid–strong base, strong acid–weak base and weak–weak combinations; the half-equivalence point of a weak acid–strong base titration gives pH = pKa.
  • HL: acid–base indicators are weak acids that change colour at pH = pKa; a suitable indicator for a titration has an end point range matching the steep part of the pH curve at equivalence.
  • Lewis theory broadens the definition of acids and bases to electron-pair acceptors and donors, extending acid–base behaviour to species such as BF3 that have no proton to transfer.
