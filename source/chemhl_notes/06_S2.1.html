Guiding Question

What determines the ionic nature and properties of a compound?

All atoms are electrically neutral, since the number of protons equals the number of electrons. If an electron is transferred from one atom, M, to another atom, X, the two atoms become charged particles called ions: M becomes a positively charged cation and X becomes a negatively charged anion. Because the resulting ions carry opposite charges, they are pulled together by electrostatic attraction. This attraction between oppositely charged ions is the ionic bond. At Higher Level, we go beyond simply predicting ion charge from group number: we examine the energetics of ion formation in detail, using successive ionization energies to explain exactly which ions can and cannot form, and we quantify lattice enthalpy and the degree of covalent character shown by real ionic compounds.

Electron transfer forms oppositely charged ionsTwo neutral atoms, M and X, are shown side by side. An arrow shows a single electron moving from M to X. After the transfer, M is drawn as a smaller sphere labelled M+ and X as a larger sphere labelled X−, with curved attraction lines linking the two oppositely charged spheres to show the electrostatic pull between them. In general M is a metal (found on the left of the periodic table) and X is a non-metal (found on the right).

A single pair of ions is rarely found in isolation. Instead, huge numbers of cations and anions arrange themselves into a repeating, three-dimensional lattice, with every positive ion surrounded by negative neighbours and vice versa. It is this large-scale, orderly packing of ions — not just the attraction between one pair — that gives ionic compounds their characteristic crystalline structure and physical properties. The ionic character of a bond is closely related to how far apart the two elements sit in the periodic table: the further apart, the more ionic the compound tends to be.

Why Metals Form Cations and Non-Metals Form Anions

Cation and anionA cation is a positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses one or more electrons. An anion is a negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains one or more electrons. A useful memory aid: CATion is PAWsitive, aNion is Negative.

It is always the outer (valence) electrons that are lost or gained in ion formation, because they are furthest from the nucleus and least strongly held. The atomic number, and therefore the identity of the element, never changes during ionic bonding — only electrons move.

Effective Nuclear Charge

Although the actual nuclear charge of an atom increases by one unit for every proton added going across a period, the outer electrons do not experience this full charge. Inner-shell electrons shield the outer electrons from the nucleus and repel them, so the outer electrons only feel a reduced pull called the effective nuclear charge. For period 3 elements, the ten inner electrons of the neon core shield the nucleus almost completely, so the effective nuclear charge felt by the valence electrons is roughly equal to the group number (for example, sodium, with 11 protons and 10 shielding electrons, has an effective nuclear charge of about +1; chlorine, with 17 protons and the same shielding core, has an effective nuclear charge of about +7).

Effective nuclear charge: across a period vs down a groupAcross a period, effective nuclear charge increases, because one proton is added to the nucleus each time but the number of inner shielding electrons stays the same. Down a group, effective nuclear charge stays roughly constant (about +1 for group 1, for example), because the increase in nuclear charge is offset by an equal increase in the number of inner shielding electrons — both the nuclear charge and the shielding electron count increase by 8 between successive group 1 elements such as sodium and potassium.

This pattern explains ion formation directly. Metals on the lower left of the periodic table have low effective nuclear charge and low ionization energy, so they lose electrons easily to form cations. Non-metals on the upper right have high effective nuclear charge and small atomic radii, so they attract additional electrons strongly and form anions readily. The most vigorous ionic reactions occur between elements from opposite extremes of the table, such as caesium (bottom of group 1, lowest ionization energy) and fluorine (top of group 17, smallest atomic radius and strongest electron-attracting power).

The noble gases are the great exception. Their outer shells are already full and their ionization energies are very high, so they have no tendency to lose electrons, and any extra electron would have to enter a completely new, unshielded energy level where it would experience an effective nuclear charge of zero. This lack of reactivity means the noble gas electron configuration behaves like an energetic “finishing line”: metal atoms lose electrons to reach the electron configuration of the noble gas that precedes them, and non-metal atoms gain electrons to reach the configuration of the noble gas that follows them. For example, when sodium reacts with chlorine, the sodium atom loses its single 3s electron to reach the electron configuration of neon, while the chlorine atom gains that electron to reach the electron configuration of argon. The electron transfer is sometimes pictured as a "harpoon" thrown from the metal atom to the non-metal atom, after which the resulting electrostatic attraction pulls the two ions together.

Group 14 elements such as carbon and silicon have four valence electrons; losing or gaining four electrons is too costly in energy, so these elements do not readily form simple ions and instead form covalent bonds. As negative charge on an ion increases, extra electrons experience greater electron–electron repulsion and a lower effective nuclear charge, which is why an ion such as Si4− is not feasible even though the group number would suggest it. Hydrogen is unusual: it usually loses its single 1s electron to form H+ (like a group 1 metal), but can occasionally gain an electron to form the hydride ion, H− (like a halogen).

HL: The Energetics of Ion Formation

Predicting ion charge from group number alone works well for most main-group elements, but the underlying reason is energetic: an ion only forms if the energy cost of removing (or adding) each successive electron is small enough to be repaid by the lattice energy released when the resulting ions come together. Successive ionization energies reveal exactly where this cost becomes prohibitive, and explain precisely why, for example, sodium forms Na+ but never Na2+.

Successive ionization energies of sodium, magnesium, aluminium and siliconA bar chart with "number of electrons removed" on the horizontal axis (1 to 5) and ionization energy in kJ mol−1 on the vertical axis (up to about 18 000), showing four separate lines for Na, Mg, Al and Si. Each line rises gently for the first few electrons removed, then jumps sharply upward by a huge amount at a specific point: for sodium, the jump occurs immediately after the 1st electron is removed; for magnesium, after the 2nd; for aluminium, after the 3rd; and for silicon, after the 4th. In every case, the sharp jump occurs at the point where the next electron to be removed would have to come from the full, stable neon core rather than from the valence shell.

For sodium ([Ne]3s1), the single 3s electron is removed fairly easily (first ionization energy about 496 kJ mol−1), but the second ionization energy – which would have to remove an electron from the complete neon core – is enormously larger (about 4560 kJ mol−1). This huge jump makes the formation of Na2+ energetically unfeasible in any ordinary chemical reaction, so sodium always forms Na+ and never Na2+. The same pattern explains why magnesium forms Mg2+ but not Mg3+, why aluminium forms Al3+ but not Al4+, and why silicon – whose own first ionization energy is already comparatively high, and which would need to lose all four of its valence electrons to reach a noble-gas core – does not form a simple Si4+ ion in its compounds at all, forming covalent bonds instead.

General rule: successive ionization energies limit ion chargeAn element forms the cation corresponding to the loss of all of its valence electrons, provided this can be achieved without removing an electron from an inner, noble-gas-like core. As soon as the next electron to be removed comes from a full inner shell, the ionization energy required increases so sharply that forming that more highly charged ion becomes energetically unrealistic.

A parallel limitation applies to the formation of anions. As increasingly negative charge builds up on an anion, each additional electron experiences greater electron–electron repulsion from the electrons already present and a progressively lower effective nuclear charge, making it harder to add. This is why group 17 elements readily form singly charged halide ions, group 16 elements form doubly charged ions less readily (since the outer electrons of oxygen or sulfur only experience an effective nuclear charge of about +6), and group 15 elements form triply charged ions least readily of the three (an effective nuclear charge of only about +5) – and why a quadruply charged anion such as Si4− is never formed.

Transition Metals and Variable Oxidation States

Unlike the metals of groups 1, 2 and 13, transition metals often form more than one stable ion, because the energies of their outer electrons are close together. Iron commonly forms Fe2+ and Fe3+; copper commonly forms Cu+ and Cu2+. Other elements outside the transition block also show more than one common ion despite their periodic table position: lead and tin (group 14) form stable 2+ and 4+ ions, and silver reliably forms Ag+.

Because these elements can form ions of different charge, systematic (IUPAC) names use a Roman numeral in brackets after the element name to state the oxidation number unambiguously. FeO is iron(II) oxide and Fe2O3 is iron(III) oxide; Cu2O is copper(I) oxide and CuO is copper(II) oxide. This Roman-numeral naming is only necessary when an element commonly shows more than one oxidation state — sodium, for instance, is simply called sodium oxide (not "sodium(I) oxide") because +1 is its only common state.

Oxidation state vs oxidation numberAn oxidation state is written with a sign and an Arabic numeral (e.g. +2). An oxidation number is shown as a Roman numeral in brackets after the element's name or symbol (e.g. iron(II)). The two terms are often used interchangeably.

HL: Explaining the maximum oxidation state of a transition metal

Successive ionization energies also explain exactly how many oxidation states a given transition metal can realistically reach. Consider calcium, chromium and manganese. Calcium's third ionization energy is vastly larger than its first two, because the third electron must be removed from a full, noble-gas-like argon core – so Ca2+ forms readily but Ca3+ does not. Chromium and manganese behave very differently, because their outer 3d and 4s electrons occupy a whole run of similar energy levels: their successive ionization energies increase steadily, in a roughly linear pattern, for several electrons in a row, with no single huge jump until many electrons have already been removed.

Successive ionization energies of calcium, chromium and manganeseA graph of ionization energy in kJ mol−1 (vertical axis, up to about 20 000) against the number of electrons removed (horizontal axis, 1 to 8), showing three lines. The calcium line rises only gently for the first two electrons, then jumps sharply upward after the second electron is removed, showing that only Ca2+ is energetically accessible. The chromium and manganese lines both rise in a much more gradual, almost straight-line pattern across several electrons in a row, before each shows its own sharp upward jump much further along the horizontal axis – for chromium, the jump occurs after the sixth electron is removed, and for manganese, after the seventh.

Because chromium's ionization energies increase only gradually up to the sixth electron removed, with a large jump only occurring when the seventh electron (from an inner, more stable configuration) must be removed, chromium can reach a maximum oxidation state of +6, as seen in the chromate and dichromate ions, CrO42− and Cr2O72−. Manganese's gradual rise continues one electron further, with its large jump occurring only when the eighth electron must be removed, giving it a maximum oxidation state of +7, as seen in the permanganate ion, MnO4−.

High oxidation states are not simple ionic chargesIt is more accurate to describe chromium(VI) and manganese(VII) using the language of oxidation state rather than true ionic charge. A hypothetical Mn7+ ion, with such an enormous concentrated positive charge packed into a very small volume, would have an extremely high charge density. Such a small, highly charged cation would strongly polarize any neighbouring anion, distorting its electron cloud so severely that the bonding electrons end up substantially shared rather than fully transferred. In practice, species like CrO3, Cr2O72− and MnO4− are bonded with substantial covalent character between the metal and oxygen, rather than being simple ionic lattices of bare M6+ or M7+ cations and O2− anions. As a general rule, ions with a charge greater than about 3+ typically show significant covalent character for exactly this reason.

Formulas and Names of Ionic Compounds

An ionic compound is always electrically neutral overall: the total positive charge from the cations exactly balances the total negative charge from the anions. Finding the formula of an ionic compound is therefore an exercise in balancing charge, using the smallest whole-number ratio of ions possible. Sodium chloride, Na+Cl−, needs equal numbers of each ion and is written NaCl. Magnesium fluoride needs two F− ions to balance each Mg2+ ion, giving MgF2.

Worked exampleDeduce the formula of the compound formed between aluminium and oxygen.
1. Identify the ions: aluminium (group 13) forms Al3+; oxygen (group 16) forms O2−.
2. Find the lowest common multiple of the charges: 6. This needs 2 × Al3+ (giving 6+) and 3 × O2− (giving 6−).
3. Write the formula using these numbers as subscripts, dropping the charges: Al2O3.

By convention, the cation is always named/written first, followed by the anion, and the anion's ending is usually changed to "-ide" (chlorine becomes chloride, oxygen becomes oxide, nitrogen becomes nitride). Naming an ionic compound is simply the reverse process: state the cation name, then the anion name.

Polyatomic Ions

Some common ions are made of more than one atom bonded covalently together, with the group as a whole carrying an overall charge; these are called polyatomic ions. Many of the common polyatomic anions are the conjugate bases of familiar acids, formed when an H+ ion is removed from the parent acid; the number of hydrogen atoms in the acid's formula is a useful guide to the charge of the resulting anion. It is worth learning the names, formulas and charges of the following polyatomic ions, since they appear constantly in chemical formulas and equations.

Common mistakeIonic compound formulas always show the simplest whole-number ratio of ions, never a multiple of it. Magnesium oxide is MgO, never Mg2O2. Also, never call an ionic compound a "molecule" — the correct term is formula unit, since a lattice has no fixed, discrete number of ions.

The Ionic Lattice and Its Physical Properties

The sodium chloride latticeA three-dimensional grid of alternating spheres represents the NaCl lattice. Smaller spheres (Na+) and larger spheres (Cl−) alternate in all three directions, so that every Na+ ion is surrounded by six nearest-neighbour Cl− ions, and every Cl− ion is surrounded by six nearest-neighbour Na+ ions. The pattern repeats indefinitely in a regular, repeating unit (the unit cell), which is why ionic solids form crystals with flat faces and regular geometric shapes. The number of oppositely charged ions surrounding a given ion in the lattice is called its coordination number; both ions in NaCl have a coordination number of 6, consistent with the 1:1 ratio in its formula.

Because a lattice can grow indefinitely and has no fixed total number of ions, the formula of an ionic compound is an empirical formula — it states only the simplest ratio of ions present, referred to as the formula unit.

HL: Confirming the formula from the unit cell

The repeating unit cell of the NaCl lattice can itself be used to confirm the formula NaCl, by carefully counting how much of each ion on the boundary of the cell actually belongs to it (since ions on a face, edge or corner are shared between neighbouring cells).

Counting ions in the NaCl unit cellA cube-shaped unit cell with a chloride ion at each of the 8 corners and at the centre of each of the 6 faces, and a sodium ion at the centre of each of the 12 edges and one additional sodium ion at the very centre of the cube. A corner ion, shared between 8 neighbouring unit cells, contributes only 1/8 of an ion to this cell; a face-centre ion, shared between 2 cells, contributes 1/2; an edge-centre ion, shared between 4 cells, contributes 1/4; and the single body-centre ion belongs entirely (1/1) to this cell alone.

Adding up the chloride contributions: 8 corners × 1/8 = 1 Cl−ion, plus 6 faces × 1/2 = 3 Cl− ions, giving 4 Cl− ions in total. Adding up the sodium contributions: 12 edges × 1/4 = 3 Na+ ions, plus 1 body-centre ion counted in full = 1 Na+ ion, giving 4 Na+ ions in total. The unit cell therefore contains 4 Na+ ions for every 4 Cl− ions, a 1:1 ratio, which confirms the formula unit NaCl.

Lattice Enthalpy

Lattice enthalpyThe lattice enthalpy (ΔH⊖lat) is the enthalpy change when one mole of a solid ionic lattice is separated into its gaseous ions. It is always positive, since energy must be supplied to overcome the electrostatic attraction holding the lattice together, and it is used as a direct measure of the strength of the ionic bond in a compound.
Na+(g) + Cl−(g) → NaCl(s) ΔH = −790 kJ mol−1
NaCl(s) → Na+(g) + Cl−(g) ΔH⊖lat = +790 kJ mol−1

The first equation shows that 790 kJ of energy is released when one mole of gaseous sodium ions and one mole of gaseous chloride ions come together to form the solid lattice; this favourable energy release is what drives ionic compounds to form. The lattice enthalpy is defined as the reverse of this process, so it carries the opposite (positive) sign.

HL: The electrostatic basis of lattice enthalpy

The ionic model assumes that a lattice is built from perfectly spherical ions that interact only through electrostatic (Coulombic) forces. On this basis, the energy needed to separate one mole of ion pairs from the lattice can be approximated by an equation of the form:

ΔH⊖lat = Knm / (r+ + r−)

K is a constant that depends on the geometry of the particular lattice and accounts for the very large number of ion–ion interactions that must be summed across the whole structure; n and m are the magnitudes of the charges on the cation and anion; and r+ and r− are the ionic radii of the cation and anion. This equation has two direct, testable consequences.

Trends in lattice enthalpyLattice enthalpy increases as the product of the ionic charges, nm, increases (greater charge means stronger electrostatic attraction, since ΔH⊖lat is directly proportional to nm). Lattice enthalpy decreases as the sum of the ionic radii increases (larger ions cannot approach as closely, weakening the attraction, since ΔH⊖lat is inversely proportional to r+ + r−).

Doubling either the cation charge (as in MgF2 compared with NaF) or the anion charge (as in Na2O compared with NaF) increases the melting point, because it increases the product of the charges, nm. When both charges are doubled simultaneously, as in MgO compared with NaF, the product nm increases fourfold (from 1 × 1 to 2 × 2), which is reflected directly in MgO's much larger lattice enthalpy (+3795 kJ mol−1 compared with +923 kJ mol−1 for NaF) and its correspondingly much higher melting point.

Worked example

Explain, using lattice enthalpy, why magnesium oxide (ΔH⊖lat = +3795 kJ mol−1) has a far higher melting point than sodium fluoride (ΔH⊖lat = +923 kJ mol−1). Mg2+ and O2− ions both carry twice the charge of Na+ and F−, and are also similar in size, so the product of the ionic charges, nm, is four times larger for MgO (2 × 2 = 4) than for NaF (1 × 1 = 1). Because ΔH⊖lat is directly proportional to nm, this makes the electrostatic attraction between the ions in the MgO lattice far stronger, requiring a much larger amount of energy to separate them – exactly what is reflected in MgO's much larger lattice enthalpy and its much higher melting point (3125 K for MgO compared with 1266 K for NaF).

The trend in ionic radius explains the melting points of the sodium halides, which fall steadily from NaF (1266 K) to NaCl (1074 K) to NaBr (1020 K) to NaI (934 K), since the halide ion gets larger down the group (increasing r−) while its charge stays the same, weakening the electrostatic attraction and lowering the lattice enthalpy.

When the equation's prediction fails: aluminium compoundsThe lattice enthalpy equation predicts that aluminium oxide, Al2O3 (charges 3+ and 2−, so nm = 6), should have a far higher melting point than magnesium oxide, MgO (charges 2+ and 2−, so nm = 4). In reality, Al2O3 melts at 2345 K, substantially lower than MgO's 3125 K, even though its ionic charge product is larger. This is because the small, highly charged Al3+ ion has such a high charge density that it strongly polarizes the neighbouring O2− ions, pulling their electron density toward itself and introducing significant covalent character into the bonding. The purely ionic model, which assumes perfectly spherical, undistorted ions, therefore becomes less accurate for compounds of small, highly charged cations such as Al3+.

Melting Point, Boiling Point and Volatility

Because a large amount of energy is needed to pull the ions in a lattice apart, ionic compounds are crystalline solids at room temperature with high melting and boiling points, and low volatility (a low tendency to vaporize).

Solubility

Dissolving of an ionic lattice in waterA crystal surface of alternating Na+ and Cl− ions is shown surrounded by polar water molecules. Each water molecule is drawn bent, with a small δ+ symbol on each hydrogen atom and a small δ− symbol on the oxygen atom. Water molecules orient their oxygen (δ−) ends toward Na+ ions and their hydrogen (δ+) ends toward Cl− ions. As ions at the crystal surface become surrounded by these oriented water molecules, they are pulled away from the lattice into solution, a process called hydration.

Water is a polar molecule: the oxygen atom carries a small negative charge and the hydrogen atoms carry small positive charges. These partial charges attract the ions at the surface of an ionic crystal away from the lattice, surrounding (hydrating) them and allowing the solid to dissolve: NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq). This same principle extends to other polar solvents, giving the general rule "like dissolves like": ionic compounds tend to be soluble in polar solvents such as water, but insoluble in non-polar solvents such as hexane or oil, since non-polar molecules have no partial charges to attract the ions away from the lattice. As with most generalizations there are exceptions — calcium carbonate, for instance, is an ionic compound that does not dissolve appreciably in water.

Electrical Conductivity

Whether an ionic compound conducts electricity depends entirely on whether its ions are free to move as mobile charge carriers. In the solid state, the ions are fixed in place within the lattice, so solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity. When melted or dissolved in water, however, the ions become mobile and are free to carry charge, so ionic compounds do conduct electricity in the molten or aqueous state. This property is economically significant: the electrolytic extraction of aluminium from molten aluminium oxide, for example, requires maintaining the very high temperatures needed to melt the compound, which is a major cost in the process.

Brittleness

Why ionic solids are brittleTwo diagrams show a layer of an ionic lattice before and after a shear force is applied. Before: rows of alternating + and − ions sit directly above rows of opposite charge, so all neighbouring ions attract each other. After a sideways force displaces the top layer by one ion-spacing: ions of the same charge now sit directly above one another. The like-charged ions strongly repel each other and the layers fly apart, shattering the crystal.

A shearing force can shift one layer of ions relative to the layer beneath it, so that ions of the same charge end up next to each other instead of ions of opposite charge. The resulting repulsion between like-charged ions splits the crystal apart, which is why ionic solids are typically hard but brittle.

The Bonding Continuum and Electronegativity

Not every compound formed from a metal and a non-metal behaves as a perfect ionic model would predict. Ionic bonding can be viewed as an extreme case of a polar covalent bond, in which the bonding electrons are not merely shared unequally but are considered to belong almost entirely to the more electronegative atom. Electronegativity measures an atom's ability to attract a shared pair of bonding electrons, and the difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms (ΔχP, on the Pauling scale) gives a way of estimating how ionic (or how covalent) a bond really is.

% ionic character ≈ (ΔχP ÷ 3.2) × 100%

where ΔχP is the electronegativity difference between the two bonded elements, and 3.2 is the electronegativity difference of caesium fluoride, CsF, the compound generally taken as being 100% ionic (from the most electropositive metal and the most electronegative non-metal). A difference of about 1.6 corresponds to roughly 50% ionic character, and a difference greater than about 1.8 is usually described as predominantly ionic.

Worked exampleWhich pair of elements is most likely to form an ionic bond: (A) B and F, (B) Si and O, (C) N and Cl, or (D) K and Cl?
Electronegativity differences: (A) 4.0 − 2.0 = 2.0; (B) 3.4 − 1.9 = 1.5; (C) 3.2 − 3.0 = 0.2; (D) 3.2 − 0.8 = 2.4.
Option D has the largest electronegativity difference, so KCl is the most ionic pairing.

NaCl and MgCl2 behave as classic ionic solids with high melting points and high electrical conductivity when molten, but AlCl3 has a much lower melting point and only poor conductivity in the liquid state, showing it has significant covalent character – consistent with the small, highly charged Al3+ ion strongly polarizing the chloride ions, in the same way it polarizes oxide ions in Al2O3. SiCl4 and PCl5 are essentially covalent, with low melting points and no electrical conductivity at all, since their electronegativity differences from chlorine are too small to support ionic bonding. The ionic model is a very useful simplification, but real bonding across the periodic table forms a continuous spectrum rather than three separate boxes; a more complete two-dimensional classification, using both the electronegativity difference and the average electronegativity of the bonded atoms, is provided by the Van Arkel–Ketelaar triangular bonding diagram, which places every compound at a point somewhere between the purely ionic, purely covalent and metallic corners of a triangle.

Guiding Question Revisited

  • An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom (or group of atoms) loses or gains one or more electrons.
  • Metals lose electrons to form cations; non-metals gain electrons to form anions. Transition metals and some other elements (e.g. Pb, Sn) can form ions of more than one charge.
  • The charge on a main-group ion can usually be predicted from its group number in the periodic table.
  • At Higher Level, successive ionization energies explain precisely why ion charge is limited: an element forms the ion corresponding to losing all of its valence electrons, but not one more, because removing the next electron from a stable inner core requires a prohibitively large jump in ionization energy. The same reasoning explains the maximum oxidation states of transition metals such as chromium (+6) and manganese (+7), and why such high oxidation states show substantial covalent character.
  • Ionic compounds are electrically neutral formula units, with the simplest whole-number ratio of ions balancing total positive and negative charge.
  • Ionic compounds form three-dimensional lattices held together by strong electrostatic attraction (the ionic bond), which is measured by lattice enthalpy. At Higher Level, lattice enthalpy can be expressed quantitatively as ΔH⊖lat = Knm/(r+ + r−): it increases with the product of the ionic charges and decreases with the sum of the ionic radii.
  • The lattice structure explains the characteristic properties of ionic compounds: high melting and boiling points, low volatility, brittleness, solubility in polar solvents but not non-polar solvents, and electrical conductivity only when molten or in aqueous solution.
  • The distinction between ionic and covalent bonding is a continuum, not a strict divide; the electronegativity difference between bonded atoms indicates the degree of ionic character, and compounds of small, highly charged cations (such as Al3+) deviate most noticeably from the predictions of the simple ionic model.
