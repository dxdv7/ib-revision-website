Guiding Question

How do the nuclei of atoms differ?

Every element on the periodic table is built from atoms that share the same basic ingredients — protons, neutrons and electrons — but combine them in different amounts. The atomic number, the number of protons in the nucleus, is what actually gives an element its identity. This sub-topic looks at how the model of the atom developed historically, how atoms and ions are described using proton, neutron and electron counts, how isotopes explain why elements have non-whole-number relative atomic masses, and — at Higher Level — how a mass spectrometer is used to measure isotopic masses and abundances directly.

The Development of the Atomic Model

The atomic model used in chemistry today did not appear all at once — it developed over roughly two centuries as new experimental evidence accumulated, with each new model replacing or refining the one before it.

Nature of science: from speculation to evidenceThe idea that matter is made of elements and atoms dates back to the 4th century BCE, but it was purely speculative, with little supporting evidence. Scientists adopt a sceptical attitude toward claims and evaluate them using evidence; a key step for chemistry came with Robert Boyle’s Sceptical Chymist (1661), which argued that scientific knowledge must be justified by evidence from practical investigation, and which proposed the modern concept of an element as a substance that cannot be broken down into anything simpler.
Dalton’s atomic theory (early 1800s)John Dalton noticed that hydrogen and oxygen always combine in fixed mass proportions, and proposed to explain this that: all matter is made of tiny indivisible particles called atoms; atoms cannot be created or destroyed; atoms of the same element are identical in every way; atoms of different elements differ; and atoms combine in small whole-number ratios to form compounds. Dalton was also the first to assign symbols to the different elements, the forerunner of the modern notation system. Dalton’s theory was not accepted immediately — many contemporaries, including the influential physicist Lord Kelvin, dismissed atoms as a useful fiction rather than physical reality. Over time, as supporting evidence accumulated, this changed to widespread acceptance; such a shift in the dominant scientific view is called a paradigm shift, and is characteristic of how scientific understanding evolves.
Thomson’s “plum pudding” modelA circular blob represents the atom as a whole, filled with a uniform, evenly shaded positively charged “pudding” or sponge-like substance. Scattered throughout this positive background are small negatively charged spheres representing electrons, spaced out somewhat like plums or raisins embedded in a pudding, with the total negative charge of the electrons exactly balancing the total positive charge of the background so the atom overall is neutral.

J. J. Thomson discovered that all metals emit the same type of negatively charged particle (the electron, originally called a “cathode ray”) when a high voltage is applied, showing that atoms are not truly indivisible and must contain smaller charged particles common to every element. This discovery depended on newly invented powerful vacuum pumps and advances in the understanding of electricity and magnetism — a reminder that new technology is often the driver of new scientific discovery. Since the atom overall has no net charge, Thomson proposed his “plum pudding” model: negative electrons embedded within a diffuse, positively charged substance filling the whole atom.

Rutherford’s gold foil experimentA source emits a narrow beam of positively charged alpha particles toward an extremely thin sheet of gold foil, with a circular fluorescent detecting screen surrounding the foil to register where particles land after passing through or near it. The vast majority of the alpha particles pass straight through the foil with little or no deflection, striking the screen directly behind the foil in a tight, undeflected spot. A very small number of particles are deflected through large angles, and an even tinier number bounce almost straight back toward the source, striking the screen close to where the beam originated.
Interpreting the gold foil resultsIf Thomson’s model had been correct, alpha particles should have either passed straight through the diffuse positive “sponge” or become stuck in it; large-angle deflections were not expected at all. Because most alpha particles passed straight through, Rutherford concluded that an atom is mostly empty space. Because a small number were deflected by large angles, and a very few bounced almost straight back, he concluded that each atom contains a tiny, extremely dense, positively charged region — the nucleus — that repels the positive alpha particles when they pass close to it. Only a very concentrated positive charge could produce such large deflections in such a small fraction of particles; when Rutherford’s team first reported small-angle deflections, he reportedly asked them to check whether any particles had bounced straight back, an intuitive leap with little logical justification at the time but which turned out to be correct.
Bohr model of the hydrogen atomA single positively charged proton is drawn at the centre as the nucleus. A negatively charged electron is shown travelling in a fixed circular path (orbit, or energy level) around the nucleus, similar to a planet orbiting the Sun, held in its orbit by the electrostatic force of attraction between the opposite charges. The circular orbit is drawn much, much larger in radius than the tiny central proton, illustrating that the atom’s volume is overwhelmingly empty space: the nuclear radius is of the order of 10−15 m while the atomic radius is of the order of 10−10 m, a difference of about 100 000 times.

Niels Bohr refined the nuclear model by picturing electrons as moving in fixed circular orbits (energy levels) around the nucleus, similar to a miniature solar system. A hydrogen atom, the simplest atom, is made of just one proton and one electron, with no neutron needed since there is no repulsion between multiple protons to overcome — a neutron here would be essentially redundant. Atoms of every other element need neutrons in the nucleus alongside the protons: a strong short-range attractive force (the strong nuclear force) acts between all nucleons (protons and neutrons) and is powerful enough to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between the mutually repelling positive protons, holding the nucleus together.

Subatomic Particles

Every atom is built from three types of subatomic particle. Protons and neutrons are collectively called nucleons because both are found in the nucleus; they have almost identical mass, and together they account for almost all of the mass of an atom. Electrons occupy the space outside the nucleus and have a mass so small it is considered negligible in most calculations.

Why the nucleus carries almost all the massSince the mass of an electron is negligible compared to a proton or neutron, essentially the entire mass of an atom comes from the protons and neutrons packed into its tiny nucleus, even though the nucleus occupies only a minuscule fraction of the atom’s total volume.
HL extension: quarks and antiparticlesThe description of protons, neutrons and electrons given above is sufficient to understand the whole of chemistry, but it is not the complete physical picture. The electron is indeed a fundamental particle, but protons and neutrons are themselves each made up of three more fundamental particles called quarks. Every particle also has a corresponding antiparticle of identical mass but opposite charge: the positron is the antiparticle of the electron, carrying the same mass but a charge of +1. When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate each other, converting their combined mass entirely into energy released as high-energy photons called gamma rays. This annihilation is the basis of positron-emission tomography (PET) scanning: a patient is injected with a tracer compound containing a positron-emitting radioisotope; each emitted positron travels only around 1 mm before colliding with an electron in the body, and the pair of gamma-ray photons produced by the annihilation are detected to build up a three-dimensional image of where the tracer has accumulated (for example, in rapidly dividing cancer cells).
Occam’s razorChemistry generally treats atoms as built from just three particles — protons, neutrons and electrons — even though quarks and antiparticles are known to exist. This reflects the principle of Occam’s razor: a model should be as simple as possible while still having enough explanatory power for its purpose. The three-particle model explains essentially everything needed for chemical behaviour, so going further is unnecessary for this level of study.

HL Extension: How Small and How Dense is the Nucleus?

Experiments show that the radius R of a nucleus is related to its mass number A by R = 1.2 × 10−15 × A1/3 m. This relationship can be used to show that the density of nuclear matter is enormous, and essentially the same for every nucleus, regardless of size.

Deriving an expression for nuclear density

Treating the nucleus as a sphere, its volume is V = &frac43;πR3. Substituting R = 1.2 × 10−15A1/3 m gives R3 = (1.2 × 10−15)3 × A = 1.73 × 10−45A m3, so V = &frac43;π × 1.73 × 10−45A ≈ 7.24 × 10−45A m3.
The mass of the nucleus is approximately m = A × u, where u = 1.66 × 10−27 kg is the mass of one nucleon.
Density ρ = m ÷ V = (A × 1.66 × 10−27) ÷ (7.24 × 10−45A). The mass number A cancels completely, leaving ρ ≈ 2.3 × 1017 kg m−3 for every nucleus, regardless of its size. This constant value, far denser than any ordinary solid, supports the model of the nucleus as a tightly packed sphere of nucleons of roughly constant density, similar to an incompressible liquid drop — and confirms just how much emptier the rest of the atom is by comparison.

Atomic Number and Mass Number

The atomic number (symbol Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom, and it is the defining property of an element — every atom of a given element has the same atomic number, and no two different elements share the same atomic number. Because a neutral atom has no overall charge, the number of protons must exactly equal the number of electrons, so the atomic number also gives the number of electrons in a neutral atom.

The mass number (symbol A), sometimes called the nucleon number, is the total number of protons plus neutrons in an atom. Since the mass of an electron is negligible, the mass number effectively gives the whole-number mass of an atom (relative to a proton or neutron each being counted as 1).

Nuclear symbol: AZX, where X is the chemical symbol, A is the mass number (top), and Z is the atomic number (bottom)
number of protons = number of electrons (neutral atom) = Z
number of neutrons = A − Z

Given the nuclear symbol of any neutral atom, the number of protons and electrons are both read directly as Z, and the number of neutrons is found by subtracting Z from A.

Worked example

Identify the subatomic particles present in an atom of 27Al (aluminium, Z = 13).
Number of protons = Z = 13. Number of electrons = Z = 13 (neutral atom). Number of neutrons = A − Z = 27 − 13 = 14. An aluminium atom therefore has 13 protons, 13 electrons and 14 neutrons.

Worked example

Identify the subatomic particles present in an atom of 226Ra (radium, Z = 88).
Number of protons = 88. Number of electrons = 88. Number of neutrons = 226 − 88 = 138. This isotope of radium was first isolated by Marie Curie, who remains the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences (Physics and Chemistry) — all the Curie family’s Nobel Prizes were awarded for work on radioactivity.

Ions

The number of protons and neutrons in an atom never changes during an ordinary chemical reaction — only electrons are gained, lost, or shared. When the number of electrons no longer equals the number of protons, the particle carries an overall electric charge and is called an ion. Forming an ion changes neither the atomic number nor the mass number, since the nucleus itself is unaffected.

Cations and anionsA cation is a positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses one or more electrons (so protons now outnumber electrons). An anion is a negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains one or more electrons (so electrons now outnumber protons). The size of the charge equals the number of electrons lost or gained.
charge on an ion = number of protons − number of electrons
Worked example

Determine the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in 27Al3+ and in 16O2−.
For 27Al3+ (Z = 13): protons = 13, neutrons = 27 − 13 = 14. The 3+ charge means 3 electrons have been lost, so electrons = 13 − 3 = 10.
For 16O2− (Z = 8): protons = 8, neutrons = 16 − 8 = 8. The 2− charge means 2 electrons have been gained, so electrons = 8 + 2 = 10.

Worked example

A particle contains 19 protons, 20 neutrons and 18 electrons. Identify the particle, including its mass number and charge.
The number of protons gives the atomic number, Z = 19, which identifies the element as potassium, K. Mass number A = protons + neutrons = 19 + 20 = 39. Charge = protons − electrons = 19 − 18 = +1. The particle is 39K+.

Isoelectronic speciesDifferent atoms or ions that happen to have the same number of electrons are called isoelectronic. For example, the ion 40Ca2+ (20 protons, 20 neutrons, 18 electrons) and the ion 35Cl− (17 protons, 18 neutrons, 18 electrons) are isoelectronic, since both have exactly 18 electrons, even though they have completely different numbers of protons and neutrons, belong to different elements, and carry different charges.
Atoms vs. ions of the same elementAn atom and an ion of the same element can have drastically different chemical properties because chemical behaviour depends on electrons, not on the nucleus. Sodium metal, Na, is dangerously reactive with water, while the sodium ion, Na+, found in table salt and swallowed with every meal, is completely unreactive and essential to health.

Isotopes

Look up any element’s relative atomic mass on the periodic table and, apart from a handful of exceptions, it is not a whole number — chlorine, for example, has a relative atomic mass of 35.45, even though a single proton or neutron has a relative mass of exactly 1. This is explained by the existence of isotopes.

IsotopesAtoms of the same element (so, by definition, with the same number of protons) that have different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers. Because isotopes of an element have identical numbers of protons and electrons, they have essentially identical chemical properties and occupy the same position in the periodic table (the word “isotope” comes from Greek for “same place”). However, because they differ in mass, isotopes typically have slightly different physical properties, such as density, and melting and boiling points, since heavier particles move more slowly at a given temperature.
Common mistake: physical vs. chemical propertiesA difference in the number of neutrons is not itself a physical property. A physical property (such as density or boiling point) is something that can be measured without changing the chemical composition of a substance; isotopes differ in some of these measurable physical properties as a consequence of their different masses, but their chemical behaviour (how they react) is unaffected.

Chlorine exists naturally as two isotopes, 35Cl and 37Cl, both with 17 protons (giving 18 and 20 neutrons respectively). In a representative sample of 1000 chlorine atoms, about 775 are 35Cl and about 225 are the heavier 37Cl. Because there is more of the lighter isotope, the average mass of a chlorine atom is closer to 35 than to 37.

Relative atomic mass (Ar)The weighted average mass of an atom of an element, taking into account the relative abundance of each of its naturally occurring isotopes, measured relative to an atom of carbon-12 (which is defined as having a relative mass of exactly 12, since 1961, because carbon is common, easy to transport and store as a solid). Because it is a weighted average across isotopes of different mass, Ar is generally not a whole number.
Ar = [ Σ (isotope mass number × % abundance) ] ÷ 100

To find Ar, multiply each isotope’s mass number by its percentage (or fractional) abundance, add these products together, then divide by 100 (if using percentages) to get the weighted average mass of one atom.

Worked example

Calculate the relative atomic mass of chlorine, given that a sample contains 77.5% 35Cl and 22.5% 37Cl.
Considering a sample of 100 atoms: total mass = (35 × 77.5) + (37 × 22.5) = 2712.5 + 832.5 = 3545. Ar = 3545 ÷ 100 = 35.45. This matches the value found on the periodic table.

Worked example

Magnesium exists as three isotopes: 24Mg (78.90% abundance), 25Mg (10.00%) and 26Mg (11.10%). Calculate its relative atomic mass.
Considering 100 atoms: total mass = (24 × 78.90) + (25 × 10.00) + (26 × 11.10) = 1893.6 + 250.0 + 288.6 = 2432.2. Ar = 2432.2 ÷ 100 = 24.32.

Worked example: finding an unknown abundance

Boron exists as two isotopes, 10B and 11B, and has a relative atomic mass of 10.81. Find the percentage abundance of each isotope.
Let x = percentage abundance of 10B, so (100 − x) = percentage abundance of 11B. Total mass of 100 atoms = 10x + 11(100 − x) = 1100 − x. Since Ar = (total mass) ÷ 100: 10.81 = (1100 − x) ÷ 100, so 1081 = 1100 − x, giving x = 19. The abundances are 10B = 19% and 11B = 81%.

Radioisotopes and Tracers

The stability of a nucleus depends on the balance between its numbers of protons and neutrons. A nucleus with an unstable balance is radioactive and decays, emitting radiation, to become more stable.

Radioisotopes as tracersBecause a radioactive isotope (radioisotope) behaves chemically just like a stable isotope of the same element, it can be used as a tracer: its path through a chemical, biological, or industrial process can be followed by detecting the radiation it emits, since the radioisotope reacts and moves exactly as a non-radioactive atom of the same element would. Applications include following nitrogen fertiliser uptake and pollution in plants using 15N, medical PET scans using positron-emitting tracers, and dating carbon-containing materials using the steady decay of 14C.
Carbon dating

While an organism is alive, it continuously exchanges carbon with its environment (for example, through photosynthesis or respiration), so the proportion of radioactive 14C in its tissues stays roughly constant, matching the atmosphere. After the organism dies, this exchange stops, and the 14C already present simply decays away at a steady, known rate, without being replenished. By measuring how much 14C remains in a sample relative to the stable isotope 12C, and knowing the decay rate, the age of carbon-containing remains can be estimated.

Uranium exists mainly as two natural isotopes, mostly 238U with less than 1% 235U. Since 235U is the isotope needed for a controlled nuclear fission reaction, nuclear fuel must be enriched to raise the proportion of 235U to around 3–5%; one method heats solid uranium hexafluoride, UF6, to a gas and allows it to diffuse through a series of pipelines and filters — because the lighter 235UF6 molecules diffuse very slightly faster than the heavier 238UF6 molecules, repeating the diffusion step many thousands of times gradually increases the proportion of 235U in the collected gas.

HL Only: Mass Spectrometry

A mass spectrometer is an instrument that measures the mass and relative abundance of the isotopes present in a sample directly, rather than relying on an already-known relative atomic mass. This gives experimental evidence for the existence and abundance of isotopes, instead of simply assuming it.

How a Mass Spectrometer Works

The sample is first vaporised, so that individual, separated atoms (or molecules) can be analysed. These particles are then ionised by a beam of high-energy electrons, which knocks an electron out of each neutral particle to leave a positively charged ion:

X(g) + e− → X+(g) + 2e−

The resulting positive ions are accelerated by an electric field and then passed through a magnetic field oriented at right angles to their path. This magnetic field deflects each ion’s path by an amount that is inversely proportional to its mass-to-charge ratio (m/z): lighter ions (or ions with a larger charge) are deflected more than heavier ions (or ions with a smaller charge) travelling at the same speed. Since almost all ions produced carry a single positive charge (z = 1), the horizontal axis of the resulting spectrum effectively reads directly as mass. A detector records the abundance of ions arriving at each m/z value, producing a mass spectrum: a series of vertical lines, with m/z on the horizontal axis and relative abundance (often shown as a percentage) on the vertical axis. The number of lines in the spectrum shows the number of isotopes present, the position of each line gives that isotope’s mass number, and the height of each line gives its percentage abundance.

Mass spectrum of galliumA bar graph with “mass/charge” on the horizontal axis and “% abundance” on the vertical axis. Two vertical bars are shown: a taller bar at m/z = 69 reaching up to 60% abundance, and a shorter bar at m/z = 71 reaching up to 40% abundance. This spectrum shows that gallium exists as exactly two isotopes, 69Ga and 71Ga, with 69Ga being the more abundant of the two.
Worked example: reading a mass spectrum

The mass spectrum of gallium shows two peaks: one at m/z = 69 with 60% relative abundance, and one at m/z = 71 with 40% relative abundance. Calculate the relative atomic mass of gallium.
Considering a sample of 100 atoms: total mass = (69 × 60) + (71 × 40) = 4140 + 2840 = 6980. Ar = 6980 ÷ 100 = 69.80.

Worked example: reading a mass spectrum

The mass spectrum of rubidium shows two peaks: m/z = 85 with 77% relative abundance, and m/z = 87 with 23% relative abundance. Calculate the relative atomic mass of rubidium shown by this spectrum.
Considering 100 atoms: total mass = (85 × 77) + (87 × 23) = 6545 + 2001 = 8546. Ar = 8546 ÷ 100 = 85.46.

Worked example

A sample of chromium gives a mass spectrum with four peaks: 4.31% at mass 50, 83.76% at mass 52, 9.55% at mass 53, and 2.38% at mass 54. Calculate the relative atomic mass of chromium to two decimal places.
Total mass of 100 atoms = (50 × 4.31) + (52 × 83.76) + (53 × 9.55) + (54 × 2.38) = 215.50 + 4355.52 + 506.15 + 128.52 = 5205.69. Ar = 5205.69 ÷ 100 = 52.06 (2 d.p.).

Worked example

A sample of iron gives a mass spectrum with three peaks: 5.95% at mass 54, 91.88% at mass 56, and 2.17% at mass 57. Calculate the relative atomic mass of iron to two decimal places.
Total mass of 100 atoms = (54 × 5.95) + (56 × 91.88) + (57 × 2.17) = 321.30 + 5145.28 + 123.69 = 5590.27. Ar = 5590.27 ÷ 100 = 55.90 (2 d.p.).

Worked example

A sample of silicon gives a mass spectrum with three peaks: 92.18% at mass 28, 4.70% at mass 29, and 3.12% at mass 30. Calculate the relative atomic mass of silicon to two decimal places.
Total mass of 100 atoms = (28 × 92.18) + (29 × 4.70) + (30 × 3.12) = 2581.04 + 136.30 + 93.60 = 2810.94. Ar = 2810.94 ÷ 100 = 28.11 (2 d.p.).

Worked example: finding an unknown abundance from Ar

Neon has a relative atomic mass of 20.18 and, to a good approximation, exists as just two isotopes, 20Ne and 22Ne. What percentage abundance of 20Ne does this imply?
Let x = % abundance of 20Ne, so (100 − x) = % abundance of 22Ne. Total mass of 100 atoms = 20x + 22(100 − x) = 2200 − 2x. Since Ar × 100 = 2018: 2200 − 2x = 2018, so 2x = 182, giving x = 91. 20Ne is approximately 91% abundant.

Fragmentation Patterns

When a compound (rather than a single element) is analysed, the high-energy electron collisions during ionisation are often energetic enough to shatter the resulting molecular ion into smaller charged fragments. The resulting fragmentation pattern, a series of peaks at different m/z values corresponding to different pieces of the original molecule, provides evidence about the structure of the compound: a chemist can piece together the fragments to reconstruct the shape of the whole molecule, in the way an archaeologist reconstructs an artefact from its broken pieces. The detailed use of fragmentation patterns to determine structure is developed further elsewhere in the course; here, it is enough to recognise that a compound’s mass spectrum can show several peaks, not just one, because the molecule itself breaks apart during ionisation.

Worked example: combining two isotopic elements in one molecule

Bromochloromethane, CH2BrCl, contains bromine (isotopes 79Br and 81Br, present in equal 50:50 abundance) and chlorine (isotopes 35Cl, 75% abundance, and 37Cl, 25% abundance). Taking carbon as exactly mass 12 and hydrogen as exactly mass 1, calculate the percentage of CH2BrCl molecules with a relative molecular mass of exactly 128.
The C and 2 H atoms always contribute 12 + 1 + 1 = 14 to the total mass, so the total mass is 14 + (mass of Br isotope) + (mass of Cl isotope). Testing each combination: 14 + 79 + 35 = 128; 14 + 79 + 37 = 130; 14 + 81 + 35 = 130; 14 + 81 + 37 = 132. Only the combination of 79Br with 35Cl gives a total mass of 128. The probability of a molecule containing both of these specific isotopes is 0.50 × 0.75 = 0.375, so 37.5% of CH2BrCl molecules have a relative molecular mass of 128.
