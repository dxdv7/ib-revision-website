Guiding Question

What happens when electrons are transferred?

Oxidation and reduction (together, redox) reactions are reactions in which electrons are transferred from one species to another. At Standard Level, this chapter introduced oxidation states, half-equations, the reactivity series, and simple voltaic and electrolytic cells. At Higher Level, these ideas are made quantitative: a numerical standard electrode potential, E°, is assigned to every half-cell, allowing the direction and size of electron transfer to be calculated precisely, linked directly to the Gibbs energy change of a reaction, and extended to systems – such as oxidizing agents like MnO4− and Cr2O72− – that lie entirely outside the simple metal activity series. Quantitative electrolysis similarly allows the exact amount of product formed at an electrode to be calculated from the current and time used.

Oxidation and Reduction (Recap)

OILRIGOxidation Is Loss (of electrons); Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). Because the electrons lost by one species must be gained by another, oxidation and reduction always occur together – this is why such reactions are called redox reactions.

The term oxidation originally meant the gain of oxygen, and reduction the loss of oxygen (the extraction of a metal from its ore). Both definitions were broadened once it was recognized that the same electron transfer underlies reactions that do not involve oxygen at all: magnesium loses two electrons whether it burns in oxygen (2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s)) or reacts with chlorine (Mg(s) + Cl2(g) → MgCl2(s)). Since hydrogen removed from a compound is so often used to form water, oxidation was further extended to mean the removal of hydrogen, and reduction the addition of hydrogen.

For reactions that involve no ions at all, such as H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g), electron loss and gain cannot be described directly, so oxidation state (the charge an atom would have if the compound were fully ionic) is used instead: both elements have an oxidation state of 0 in H2 and Cl2, but in the polar product HCl, H is +1 and Cl is −1. Since chlorine's oxidation state has decreased, chlorine is reduced; since hydrogen's has increased, hydrogen is oxidized.

Oxidation state is the most general of the four, since it applies to every reaction, whether or not ions, oxygen, or hydrogen are involved; it measures a shift in electron density from one atom to another.

DisproportionationA disproportionation reaction is one in which the oxidation state of the same element both increases and decreases. In Cl2(g) + H2O(l) → HCl(aq) + HOCl(aq), one Cl atom is reduced (0 → −1) while the other is oxidized (0 → +1): chlorine is simultaneously oxidized and reduced.

Oxidation States (Recap)

An atom's oxidation state (oxidation number) is the charge it would have if the species it belongs to were composed entirely of ions. Oxidation states are assigned using a consistent set of rules: an atom of an uncombined element is 0; a monatomic ion equals its charge; hydrogen is usually +1 and oxygen usually −2; and the oxidation states within a species must sum to its overall charge (zero for a neutral compound).

Worked example

Assign oxidation states to the metal ion in (a) [Cu(NH3)6]2+ and (b) [Cr(NH3)4Cl2]+.

(a) NH3 is a neutral ligand, so the whole complex's charge is the metal's charge: Cu = +2.

(b) Cl is −1 each, so Cr + (2 × −1) = +1, giving Cr = +3.

In a molecule with more than one atom of the same element in different bonding environments, the oxidation state obtained from the overall formula is an average, and can be a non-integer.

Worked example: a fractional average oxidation state

Assign an oxidation state to carbon in propane, C3H8.

With H = +1: 8(+1) + 3C = 0, so C = −8/3 = −2⅔. This is fractional because it averages three non-equivalent carbons: the two terminal (−CH3) carbons are each −3 (bonded to 3H, 1C), while the central (−CH2−) carbon is −2 (bonded to 2H, 2C). Check: (2(−3) + (−2))/3 = −8/3, as found.

Oxidation numbers appear in IUPAC nomenclature as a Roman numeral in brackets after the relevant element's name, e.g. Fe2O3 is iron(III) oxide, K2Cr2O7 is potassium dichromate(VI), and KMnO4 is potassium manganate(VII).

Oxidizing and Reducing Agents (Recap)

Oxidizing and reducing agentsThe species that is reduced is the oxidizing agent (it removes electrons from, and so oxidizes, the other reactant). The species that is oxidized is the reducing agent (it supplies electrons to, and so reduces, the other reactant).
Worked example

Acidified K2Cr2O7(aq) reacts with FeCl2(aq) to give Fe3+(aq) and Cr3+(aq). Identify the oxidizing and reducing agents.

Fe: +2 → +3 (oxidized), so FeCl2(aq) is the reducing agent. Cr: +6 → +3 (reduced), so K2Cr2O7(aq) is the oxidizing agent.

Common oxidizing agents: O2, F2, Cl2, acidified MnO4−, acidified Cr2O72−, concentrated HNO3, H2O2. Common reducing agents: H2, C, CO, SO2, and reactive metals.

Writing and Balancing Half-Equations, Including in Acidic Solution

A redox equation can be separated into an oxidation half-equation and a reduction half-equation, with electrons shown explicitly. Adding the two, after scaling so the number of electrons matches, cancels the electrons and gives the overall equation.

Steps for balancing a redox equation in acidic solution
  1. Assign oxidation states to find which element is oxidized and which reduced.
  2. Write separate half-equations; balance atoms other than H and O, and add electrons to match the change in oxidation state.
  3. Balance O by adding H2O as needed.
  4. Balance H by adding H+ as needed.
  5. Check each half-equation balances for both atoms and charge.
  6. Find a common multiple of the two electron counts, and multiply each half-equation to match it.
  7. Add the two half-equations, cancelling anything common to both sides (including the electrons).
Worked example: Cu(s) with NO3−(aq) in acidic solution, giving NO(g) and Cu2+(aq)

Cu is oxidized (0 → +2); N is reduced (+5 → +2).

oxidation: Cu(s) → Cu2+(aq) + 2e−

reduction, balanced for O with H2O then for H with H+: NO3−(aq) + 4H+(aq) + 3e− → NO(g) + 2H2O(l)

The lowest common multiple of 2 and 3 electrons is 6: multiply oxidation by 3 and reduction by 2, then add and cancel the 6e−:

3Cu(s) + 2NO3−(aq) + 8H+(aq) → 3Cu2+(aq) + 2NO(g) + 4H2O(l)
Worked example: balancing MnO4− with Fe2+ in acidic solution

Mn: +7 (in MnO4−) → +2 (in Mn2+), a gain of 5 electrons (reduced). Fe: +2 → +3, a loss of 1 electron (oxidized).

oxidation: Fe2+(aq) → Fe3+(aq) + e−

reduction, balancing the 4 O atoms with H2O and the resulting H atoms with H+: MnO4−(aq) + 8H+(aq) + 5e− → Mn2+(aq) + 4H2O(l)

Check the reduction half-equation: atoms – 1 Mn, 4 O, 8 H on the left match 1 Mn, 4 H2O (4 O, 8 H) on the right; charge – left is (−1) + 8(+1) + 5(−1) = +2, right is +2. Balanced.

The oxidation half-equation involves 1 electron and the reduction half-equation 5, so the lowest common multiple is 5: the oxidation equation is multiplied by 5, giving 5Fe2+(aq) → 5Fe3+(aq) + 5e−. Adding this to the reduction half-equation and cancelling the 5e− common to both sides:

5Fe2+(aq) + MnO4−(aq) + 8H+(aq) → 5Fe3+(aq) + Mn2+(aq) + 4H2O(l)

This equation is the basis of a classic redox titration (below): acidified KMnO4(aq) oxidizing Fe2+(aq) to Fe3+(aq).

Redox Titrations (Recap)

Redox titrations find an unknown concentration from the volume of a standard solution needed to react exactly, via electron transfer, with a measured volume of another solution. The calculation uses the mole ratio from the balanced equation, deduced from the half-equations, exactly as in acid–base titrations.

Analysis of iron with manganate(VII)

Acidified KMnO4(aq) oxidizes Fe2+(aq) to Fe3+(aq) while MnO4− is reduced to Mn2+ (the equation derived above). This titration is self-indicating: deep purple MnO4− becomes nearly colourless Mn2+, so the solution stays colourless until the first permanent trace of purple marks the endpoint.

Worked example

All the iron in a 2.000 g tablet was dissolved to Fe2+(aq), requiring 27.50 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 KMnO4(aq) to reach equivalence. Calculate the percentage by mass of iron in the tablet.

n(MnO4−) = cV = 0.100 mol dm−3 × (27.50/1000) dm3 = 0.00275 mol. Ratio MnO4− : Fe2+ = 1 : 5, so n(Fe2+) = 0.01375 mol.

m(Fe) = n × M = 0.01375 mol × 55.85 g mol−1 = 0.768 g. %Fe = (0.768/2.000) × 100 = 38.4%.

The iodine–thiosulfate method

An oxidizing agent (KMnO4, KIO3, K2Cr2O7, or NaOCl) converts excess iodide to iodine, which is titrated against standard Na2S2O3(aq): 2S2O32−(aq) + I2(aq) → 2I−(aq) + S4O62−(aq). Starch indicator, added just before the endpoint, forms a deep blue complex with free I2; the blue disappears sharply as the last I2 is consumed.

Worked example: a back-titration

A 10.00 cm3 sample of bleach (OCl−(aq)) was reacted with excess iodide: OCl−(aq) + 2I−(aq) + 2H+(aq) → I2(aq) + Cl−(aq) + H2O(l). The liberated I2 required 38.65 cm3 of 0.0200 mol dm−3 Na2S2O3(aq). Find [OCl−].

n(S2O32−) = 0.0200 × (38.65/1000) = 7.73 × 10−4 mol. Ratio S2O32− : I2 = 2 : 1, so n(I2) = 3.865 × 10−4 mol. Since OCl− : I2 = 1 : 1, n(OCl−) = 3.865 × 10−4 mol.

[OCl−] = n/V = 3.865 × 10−4 mol / (10.00/1000) dm3 = 0.0387 mol dm−3.

The Reactivity Series and Displacement Reactions (Recap)

More reactive non-metals are stronger oxidizing agents

The halogens act as oxidizing agents, gaining electrons to form halide ions; this ability decreases down group 17: F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2 in oxidizing strength. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its halide solution, e.g. Cl2(aq) + 2I−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + I2(aq), but the reverse combination gives no reaction.

More reactive metals are stronger reducing agents

Metals act as reducing agents by losing electrons; more reactive metals lose them more readily. Zinc added to CuSO4(aq) displaces copper: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s); the reverse (Cu added to Zn2+(aq)) gives no reaction, since copper is too weak a reducing agent to force electrons onto Zn2+. Comparing such displacement reactions builds the activity (reactivity) series: a ranked list of reducing strength.

Reactive metals release H2(g) from dilute acids by reducing H+(aq): 2H+(aq) + Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + H2(g). Metals below hydrogen in the series (copper, silver, gold) do not react with dilute acids, which is part of why they resist corrosion and are valued.

Worked example

Using Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > Cu > Ag (strongest to weakest reducing agent), predict whether 2FeCl3(aq) + 3Mg(s) → 3MgCl2(aq) + 2Fe(s) occurs.

Mg is a stronger reducing agent than Fe, so this reaction will occur.

Voltaic (Galvanic) Cells (Recap)

A voltaic (galvanic) cell physically separates the oxidation and reduction half-reactions of a spontaneous redox reaction, forcing electrons through an external circuit and converting chemical energy into electrical energy. A simple half-cell is a metal strip in a solution of its own ions, e.g. Zn(s) ⇌ Zn2+(aq) + 2e−; the more reactive the metal, the further this equilibrium favours ion formation and the more negative the charge built up on the electrode.

A zinc–copper voltaic cellTwo beakers connected by a salt bridge (a tube of absorbent material soaked in an inert electrolyte such as KNO3(aq)) and by an external wire through a voltmeter. The left beaker holds a zinc strip in ZnSO4(aq); the right holds a copper strip in CuSO4(aq). At the zinc electrode (anode, negative), Zn(s) is oxidized to Zn2+(aq), releasing electrons into the wire; these flow through the wire and voltmeter to the copper electrode (cathode, positive), where Cu2+(aq) is reduced and deposits as Cu(s). Inside the salt bridge, anions drift towards the zinc half-cell and cations towards the copper half-cell, neutralizing the charge build-up in each beaker. The voltmeter reads a steady 1.10 V.
Electron and ion movement
  • The anode is negative (oxidation); the cathode is positive (reduction).
  • Electrons flow from anode to cathode through the external circuit.
  • Anions in the salt bridge migrate from the cathode side to the anode side; cations migrate from the anode side to the cathode side.
  • Without a salt bridge the circuit is incomplete, and no current or voltage is generated.

A cell diagram summarizes a voltaic cell compactly: a single line (|) is a phase boundary, a double line (||) is the salt bridge, the anode is written on the left and the cathode on the right.

Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq) || Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s)

HL: The Standard Hydrogen Electrode and Standard Electrode Potentials

A voltmeter cannot measure the tendency of a single half-cell to be reduced in isolation – only the potential difference generated once it is connected to another half-cell. To compare half-cells on a common scale, all are measured against a single, universally agreed reference: the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE).

The standard hydrogen electrodeA platinum electrode, coated with finely divided ("platinized") platinum to increase surface area and catalyse the reaction, is immersed in a 1.0 mol dm−3 solution of H+(aq), with H2(g) at 100 kPa bubbled continuously over its surface at 298 K. The equilibrium 2H+(aq) + 2e− ⇌ H2(g) is established at the platinum surface. Platinum is chosen because it is inert, does not itself ionize, and catalyses the H+/H2 interconversion, so it conducts electrons without taking part in the redox reaction – the same role platinum plays in any half-cell that does not already contain a conducting metal, such as Fe2+(aq)/Fe3+(aq).
Standard electrode potential, E°By convention, the standard hydrogen electrode is assigned an electrode potential of exactly 0.00 V. The standard electrode potential, E°, of any other half-cell is the emf generated when that half-cell is connected to the SHE by an external circuit and a salt bridge, under standard conditions: all solutions at 1.0 mol dm−3, all gases at 100 kPa, pure substances, and 298 K.

When the SHE is connected to a Cu2+(aq)/Cu(s) half-cell, the voltmeter reads +0.34 V. The positive reading shows electrons flow from the hydrogen half-cell to the copper half-cell: H2(g) is oxidized (anode) and Cu2+(aq) is reduced (cathode), since Cu2+ has a greater tendency to be reduced than H+. When the SHE is connected to a Zn2+(aq)/Zn(s) half-cell, the reading is −0.76 V: electrons flow from the zinc half-cell to the hydrogen half-cell, so Zn(s) is oxidized (anode) and H+(aq) is reduced (cathode) – consistent with zinc's higher reactivity and its ability to displace H2(g) from dilute acids.

E° values are always written for reductionBecause the SHE is always connected to the negative terminal of the voltmeter by convention, every tabulated E° value refers specifically to the reduction half-reaction (hence the alternative name standard reduction potential), even though the species may in fact be oxidized when the half-cell is paired with another. E° values are also independent of the stoichiometric coefficients used to balance a half-equation – they are never scaled up or down to match a particular reaction.

The more positive the E° value, the more readily that half-cell is reduced. Electrons always flow, through the external circuit of a voltaic cell, from the half-cell with the more negative (lower) E° towards the half-cell with the more positive (higher) E°.

This exact data is supplied in an exam data booklet and does not need to be memorized – the skill being tested is reading and applying it correctly.

HL: Calculating Standard Cell Potential, E°cell

The E°cell equation – the highest-risk sign convention in this topic
E°cell = E°(reduction half-cell, cathode) − E°(reduction half-cell, anode)

Both E° values used here are the tabulated reduction potentials, taken exactly as given, including sign – never reversed before subtracting. The half-cell with the higher (more positive) E° is reduced and is the cathode; the half-cell with the lower (more negative) E° is forced into oxidation (even though its tabulated value is for reduction) and is the anode. Subtracting the lower from the higher value this way always gives a positive E°cell for the reaction that actually happens spontaneously. If a value for E°cell comes out negative, the reaction as written is non-spontaneous; the reverse reaction is spontaneous instead, with a cell potential of the same magnitude but positive sign.

Worked example 1: the zinc–copper cell

Calculate E°cell for a voltaic cell built from Zn2+(aq)/Zn(s) (E° = −0.76 V) and Cu2+(aq)/Cu(s) (E° = +0.34 V), and identify the anode and cathode.

Copper has the higher (more positive) E°, so Cu2+(aq) is reduced at the cathode; zinc has the lower E°, so Zn(s) is oxidized at the anode.

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = (+0.34) − (−0.76) = +1.10 V

Overall reaction: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s). The positive E°cell confirms this reaction is spontaneous as written.

Worked example 2: an oxidizing-agent comparison (no metals involved)

A solution of acidified potassium manganate(VII) is mixed with concentrated hydrochloric acid, producing chlorine gas. Identify the stronger oxidizing agent and calculate E°cell, given MnO4−(aq) + 8H+(aq) + 5e− → Mn2+(aq) + 4H2O(l), E° = +1.51 V, and Cl2(g) + 2e− → 2Cl−(aq), E° = +1.36 V.

MnO4−/Mn2+ has the higher E°, so MnO4−(aq) is the stronger oxidizing agent: it is reduced (cathode), while Cl−(aq) is oxidized to Cl2(g) (anode, using the Cl2/Cl− half-equation in reverse).

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = (+1.51) − (+1.36) = +0.15 V

The positive value confirms MnO4−(aq) can indeed oxidize Cl−(aq) to Cl2(g) spontaneously under standard conditions.

Why the "reversed" worked example looks differentSome textbooks present the equation as E°cell = E°half-cell where reduction occurs − E°half-cell where oxidation occurs, which is identical to the cathode-minus-anode form above (the half-cell where reduction occurs is the cathode, and the half-cell where oxidation occurs is the anode) – both describe exactly the same calculation and always give a positive result for the reaction that is actually spontaneous.

HL: Using E° to Predict Spontaneity, Including Beyond the Simple Reactivity Series

Because electrons always tend to flow towards the half-cell with the higher E° value, E° data can predict whether any proposed redox reaction – between metals, non-metals, or polyatomic ions – will occur spontaneously, without needing to memorize an activity series.

Worked example: testing a proposed reaction

Use E° values to decide whether Ni(s) + Mn2+(aq) → Ni2+(aq) + Mn(s) occurs spontaneously, given Ni2+(aq) + 2e− → Ni(s), E° = −0.26 V, and Mn2+(aq) + 2e− → Mn(s), E° = −1.18 V.

Ni2+/Ni has the higher (less negative) E°, so Ni2+(aq) is reduced (cathode) and Mn(s) is oxidized (anode) in the spontaneous direction – the reverse of what the question proposes.

For the proposed direction (Ni(s) oxidized, Mn2+(aq) reduced): E°cell = E°(Mn2+/Mn, "cathode" as proposed) − E°(Ni2+/Ni, "anode" as proposed) = (−1.18) − (−0.26) = −0.92 V. The negative result confirms the proposed reaction is not spontaneous; the reverse reaction, Ni2+(aq) + Mn(s) → Ni(s) + Mn2+(aq), has E°cell = (−0.26) − (−1.18) = +0.92 V and is spontaneous.

Worked example: a non-metal/polyatomic-ion system beyond the reactivity series

Will acidified potassium dichromate(VI) oxidize Fe2+(aq) to Fe3+(aq)? Use Cr2O72−(aq) + 14H+(aq) + 6e− → 2Cr3+(aq) + 7H2O(l), E° = +1.33 V, and Fe3+(aq) + e− → Fe2+(aq), E° = +0.77 V.

Cr2O72−/Cr3+ has the higher E°, so it is reduced (cathode); Fe2+(aq) is oxidized to Fe3+(aq) (anode, using the Fe3+/Fe2+ half-equation in reverse).

E°cell = (+1.33) − (+0.77) = +0.56 V

Positive, so yes: acidified Cr2O72−(aq) spontaneously oxidizes Fe2+(aq) to Fe3+(aq), consistent with its use as an oxidizing titrant for iron(II).

Rate is a separate questionE°cell (and ΔG°, below) answer only "how far?" – whether a reaction is energetically favourable. They give no information on "how fast?" – a reaction predicted to be spontaneous may proceed at an unobservably slow rate if its activation energy is high. E° values also strictly apply only under standard conditions; changing temperature, concentration, or pressure can alter them, occasionally even reversing which direction is spontaneous.

HL: Standard Cell Potential and Gibbs Energy

A positive E°cell corresponds to a spontaneous, energetically favourable reaction – exactly the condition for a negative standard Gibbs energy change, ΔG° (Reactivity 1.4). The two quantities are directly proportional, linked by the total charge transferred:

ΔG° = −nFE°cell

where n is the number of moles of electrons transferred in the balanced overall equation, and F is the Faraday constant, the charge on one mole of electrons, F = 96 485 C mol−1 (often rounded to 96 500 C mol−1). Since ΔG° is normally in J mol−1 (or kJ mol−1, requiring conversion) and E° is in volts (J C−1), the units are consistent: J = (mol)(C mol−1)(J C−1).

The sign linkE°cell positive ↔ ΔG° negative ↔ reaction spontaneous as written. E°cell negative ↔ ΔG° positive ↔ reaction non-spontaneous (the reverse reaction is spontaneous instead). The more positive E°cell, the more negative ΔG°, and the more energetically favourable the reaction – a voltmeter can be thought of as an indirect measure of Gibbs energy change.
Worked example

Calculate ΔG° at 298 K for the zinc–copper voltaic cell, Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s), E°cell = +1.10 V.

From the equation, n = 2 mol of electrons are transferred (Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−; Cu2+(aq) + 2e− → Cu(s)).

ΔG° = −nFE°cell = −(2 mol)(96 485 C mol−1)(1.10 V) = −212 267 J mol−1 ≈ −212 kJ mol−1

The large negative ΔG° confirms a strongly spontaneous, energetically favourable reaction, consistent with the large positive E°cell.

Two routes to the same destinationReactivity 1.4 related ΔG° to spontaneity directly, and Reactivity 3.1 related it to the equilibrium constant via ΔG° = −RT ln K. This chapter adds a third, equivalent route specific to redox reactions: ΔG° = −nFE°cell. All three describe the same underlying energetics of a reaction from different, mathematically consistent angles.

Electrolytic Cells (Recap)

An electrolytic cell reverses a voltaic cell: an external electrical supply drives non-spontaneous oxidation and reduction, converting electrical energy to chemical energy. This process, electrolysis, can break stable compounds into their elements. The substance electrolyzed must be an electrolyte – non-conducting as a solid, but ionically conducting when molten or dissolved.

In a molten salt, only the compound's own ions are present, so exactly one ion type migrates to, and reacts at, each electrode: cations are reduced at the cathode, anions are oxidized at the anode.

Electrolysis of molten sodium chlorideGraphite electrodes dip into molten NaCl, connected to a DC supply. At the cathode (negative), Na+(l) + e− → Na(l): molten sodium collects. At the anode (positive), 2Cl−(l) → Cl2(g) + 2e−: chlorine gas bubbles away. Overall: 2NaCl(l) → 2Na(l) + Cl2(g), splitting a stable compound into its reactive elements.
The Hall–Héroult processAluminium is extracted by electrolyzing Al2O3 dissolved in molten cryolite (Na3AlF6), which lowers the mixture's melting point and raises its conductivity, substantially cutting energy costs. At the graphite-lined cathode: Al3+(l) + 3e− → Al(l) (denser than the electrolyte, so it sinks and is siphoned off). At the graphite anodes: 2O2−(l) → O2(g) + 4e− (this O2 slowly reacts with the hot carbon anodes, which must be replaced regularly).

HL: Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions

Predicting electrolysis products is harder for an aqueous solution than a molten salt, because water itself can be oxidized or reduced at either electrode, competing with the dissolved ions. For a solute M+A−(aq), at the cathode either M+(aq) or H2O(l) can be reduced, and at the anode either A−(aq) or H2O(l) can be oxidized – this is called selective discharge.

Predicting selective discharge from E° data
  1. Identify the ions present and which electrode each migrates to.
  2. Write every possible half-equation (including water's) at each electrode, with its E° value.
  3. At the cathode, the half-reaction with the higher E° is the one that actually occurs (it is reduced preferentially).
  4. At the anode, the half-reaction with the lower E° is the one that actually occurs (it is oxidized preferentially).
  5. Besides E°, the relative concentration of the ions and the nature of the electrode (inert vs reactive) can also affect which product forms.
Worked example: dilute NaOH(aq) (effectively, the electrolysis of water)

Ions present: Na+(aq), OH−(aq). At the cathode, compare Na+(aq) + e− → Na(s), E° = −2.71 V, with 2H2O(l) + 2e− → H2(g) + 2OH−(aq), E° = −0.83 V. Water has the higher E°, so it is reduced preferentially: H2(g) forms at the cathode.

At the anode, compare 2H2O(l) + O2(g) + 4e− → 4OH−(aq), E° = +0.40 V (written as reduction; OH− oxidation is its reverse), with 4H+(aq) + O2(g) + 4e− → 2H2O(l), E° = +1.23 V. The lower E° belongs to the OH−/H2O/O2 couple, so OH−(aq) (effectively, water) is oxidized preferentially: O2(g) forms at the anode.

Overall: 2H2O(l) → 2H2(g) + O2(g), in a 2 : 1 volume ratio of H2 to O2 – this is why dilute NaOH(aq) or H2SO4(aq) is added to pure water before electrolysis: it provides mobile ions to carry the current without itself being consumed, since only water is actually discharged.

Worked example: the effect of concentration – dilute vs concentrated NaCl(aq)

Ions present: Na+(aq), Cl−(aq). The cathode reaction is the same as above: H2O(l) is reduced in preference to Na+(aq) (E° = −0.83 V vs −2.71 V), giving H2(g).

At the anode, compare Cl2(g) + 2e− → 2Cl−(aq), E° = +1.36 V, with O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e− → 2H2O(l), E° = +1.23 V. By E° alone, water's lower value means it should be oxidized preferentially, giving O2(g) – and this is indeed what happens in dilute NaCl(aq). In concentrated brine, however, the very high concentration of Cl−(aq) outweighs the small E° disadvantage, and Cl−(aq) is oxidized instead, giving Cl2(g). This is the basis of the industrial chlor-alkali process, which yields H2(g), Cl2(g), and NaOH(aq) simultaneously.

Worked example: the effect of electrode material – CuSO4(aq) with inert vs copper electrodes

With inert (graphite) electrodes: at the cathode, Cu2+(aq) + 2e− → Cu(s) (E° = +0.34 V) is preferred over reducing water (E° = −0.83 V), depositing copper. At the anode, water (E° = +1.23 V) is preferred over oxidizing SO42−(aq) (a much higher, unfavourable E°), giving O2(g). Overall: 2Cu2+(aq) + 2H2O(l) → 2Cu(s) + O2(g) + 4H+(aq).

With copper electrodes, the cathode reaction is unchanged, but at the anode the copper metal itself is oxidized instead, Cu(s) → Cu2+(aq) + 2e−, since this is far easier than discharging water or SO42−(aq). The net effect is simply the transfer of copper from anode to cathode, with no change in [Cu2+] – the basis of electro-refining, used to purify copper industrially (an impure anode dissolves; pure copper deposits on the cathode, while impurities fall away as sludge).

HL: Quantitative Electrolysis – Faraday's Laws

Since the amount of a substance produced at an electrode depends on exactly how many electrons have been transferred, the mass (or volume, for a gas) of product can be calculated precisely from the current and time for which an electrolytic cell is run.

The calculation chain
Q = It      n(e−) = Q / F      n(product) = n(e−) / (electrons per mole of product, from the half-equation)

where Q is the quantity of charge passed (in coulombs, C), I is the current (in amperes, A), t is the time (in seconds, s), and F is the Faraday constant, 96 485 C mol−1. Once n(product) is known, its mass is found from m = nM, or its gas volume from V = n × Vm (using the molar volume, 22.7 dm3 mol−1 at 273 K and 100 kPa).

Worked example: mass of metal deposited

A current of 2.50 A is passed through CuSO4(aq) using copper electrodes for exactly 1 hour. Calculate the mass of copper deposited at the cathode.

Q = It = 2.50 A × 3600 s = 9000 C

n(e−) = Q/F = 9000 C / 96 485 C mol−1 = 0.0933 mol

From Cu2+(aq) + 2e− → Cu(s), 2 mol of electrons deposit 1 mol of Cu: n(Cu) = 0.0933/2 = 0.0466 mol

m(Cu) = n × M = 0.0466 mol × 63.55 g mol−1 = 2.96 g
Worked example: volume of gas produced

Using the same current and time (Q = 9000 C, n(e−) = 0.0933 mol) passed through dilute H2SO4(aq) with inert electrodes, calculate the volume of O2(g) produced at the anode, measured at 273 K and 100 kPa.

Anode half-equation: 2H2O(l) → O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e−, so 4 mol of electrons produce 1 mol of O2: n(O2) = 0.0933/4 = 0.0233 mol

V(O2) = n × Vm = 0.0233 mol × 22.7 dm3 mol−1 = 0.529 dm3 (529 cm3)
Why the "electrons per mole of product" step always needs the half-equationThe relationship Q = nF gives the moles of electrons transferred, not directly the moles of product. The conversion from moles of electrons to moles of product depends entirely on the stoichiometry of the relevant half-equation (2 e− per Cu, but 1 e− per Ag, or 4 e− per O2), so this half-equation must always be written out first.

Comparing Voltaic and Electrolytic Cells

An Ox and a Red Cat – true in both cell typesOxidation always occurs at the anode, and reduction always occurs at the cathode, in both voltaic and electrolytic cells. What changes between the two is only the polarity (and the energy conversion direction).
Voltaic cellElectrolytic cell
Type of reactionspontaneous (E°cell > 0, ΔG° < 0)non-spontaneous (E°cell < 0, ΔG° > 0, driven by an external supply)
Energy conversionchemical → electricalelectrical → chemical
Electrode of oxidationanodeanode

The polarity swap is the single most common source of confusion in this topic: in a voltaic cell the anode is negative because it is spontaneously releasing electrons; in an electrolytic cell the anode is made positive deliberately, by the external power source, in order to pull electrons away from it and force oxidation to happen there even though it would not occur on its own. A cell exactly at equilibrium (a "dead battery") has E°cell = 0 and ΔG° = 0.

Guiding Question Revisited

What happens when electrons are transferred?
  • Oxidation is electron loss and reduction is electron gain (OILRIG); both can equivalently be tracked using oxidation states, and separated into balanced half-equations, including in acidic solution using H2O and H+ to balance O and H.
  • More reactive non-metals are stronger oxidizing agents and more reactive metals are stronger reducing agents, defining the reactivity series; a voltaic cell forces a spontaneous redox reaction's electrons through an external circuit (anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction).
  • HL: every half-cell has a standard electrode potential, E°, measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (E° = 0.00 V) under standard conditions; ranking these values gives the electrochemical series.
  • HL: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode (both as tabulated reduction values) is always positive for the spontaneous direction of any proposed reaction, including between non-metal/polyatomic-ion systems with no place in a simple metal activity series.
  • HL: ΔG° = −nFE°cell links cell potential directly to Gibbs energy and spontaneity – a positive E°cell always corresponds to a negative ΔG°.
  • Electrolytic cells reverse voltaic cells, using external electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions; in aqueous solution, selective discharge (governed by E°, concentration, and electrode material) determines whether dissolved ions or water itself react at each electrode.
  • HL: Faraday's laws (Q = It, then n(e−) = Q/F) allow the exact mass or volume of any electrode product to be calculated from the current and time used.
