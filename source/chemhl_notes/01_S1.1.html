Guiding Question

How can we model the particulate nature of matter?

Everything you can touch, drink or breathe is built from atoms — particles so small that no human eye, and no ordinary microscope, can see them directly. Because chemists cannot observe atoms directly, they build models: simplified pictures that explain and predict how matter behaves. This sub-topic introduces the particulate model of matter, and uses it to explain how we classify substances, how mixtures are separated, and why matter exists in different physical states with characteristic energy changes between them.

Nature of Science: From Philosophy to Particle Accelerators

One of the earliest questions philosophers asked concerned the divisibility of matter. Could a piece of material be divided again and again, continually, into smaller and smaller pieces, as Aristotle proposed? Or would a limit eventually be reached — a single, indivisible particle — as Democritus argued? The particulate model built on Democritus’s idea has generally stood the test of time, and it lets us explain many aspects of the behaviour of matter despite the particles themselves never being directly visible.

Nature of scienceA model is an artificial representation of a natural phenomenon, built to explain existing observations and to make new predictions. Every model has limitations, and models are revised or replaced as new evidence emerges. We have no proof that matter is not infinitely divisible, but we do have evidence that it becomes increasingly difficult to divide matter into smaller pieces, because dividing a particle requires energy, and the energy required rises sharply as particles get smaller. Evidence for this comes from particle accelerators such as the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN, where beams of ions are collided at enormous energies to produce new, smaller particles. We are still learning how the universe is built from fundamental particles: the Higgs boson, a key elementary particle, was only confirmed experimentally in 2012, even though its existence had been predicted by theoretical models as early as 1964. Progress in science often follows technological development: the discovery of the Higgs boson depended on the invention of powerful particle accelerators, detectors, and sophisticated computing, achieved only through international scientific collaboration.

This chapter develops the simplest and most useful chemical model built on the particulate idea: that matter is made of elements, compounds and mixtures, and that these particles arrange and move differently in the solid, liquid and gaseous states.

Classifying Matter: Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

The first step in understanding chemistry is to sort matter into categories. All matter can be classified as either a pure substance or a mixture. Pure substances are further divided into elements and compounds, while mixtures are divided into homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures.

ElementA pure chemical substance made of atoms that all have the same number of protons in the atomic nucleus. Elements are the primary constituents of matter and cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical means. There are only about 100 known elements, of which 92 occur naturally on Earth, yet their combinations produce the near-infinite variety of substances that make up the universe. An atom is the smallest particle of an element that still shows the characteristic properties of that element.
CompoundA substance formed when atoms of two or more different elements are chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. The physical and chemical properties of a compound are completely different from the properties of the elements it is made from. Sodium chloride (NaCl), for instance, is a harmless white crystalline solid, even though it is made from sodium (a metal that reacts violently with water) and chlorine (a toxic gas): 2Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2NaCl(s).

The chemical formula of a compound combines the symbols of its elements, with subscripts showing how many atoms of each element are present in one unit of the compound. Some common examples: water, H2O; methane, CH4; ammonia, NH3; magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)2; copper sulfate, CuSO4; sulfuric acid, H2SO4; glucose, C6H12O6. (The reasons behind these particular ratios become clear once atomic structure and bonding are studied.)

MixtureMore than one element or compound physically combined, in no fixed ratio, without any chemical bonding between the components. Because a mixture has no fixed composition, it cannot be represented by a single chemical formula, and because there is no chemical bond to break, mixtures can be separated by physical methods. The composition of a mixture can vary continuously — there is a countable number of elements and compounds, each with a distinctive name, but an uncountable, effectively infinite number of possible mixtures.
Compounds vs. mixturesA compound has a fixed ratio of elements, is chemically bonded, has properties completely different from its constituent elements, and can only be separated back into elements by a chemical reaction. A mixture has a variable composition, involves no chemical bonding, and each component keeps its own original properties (nitrogen and oxygen in air still behave exactly as they do when pure — substances burn in air because they react with the oxygen in it just as they would with pure oxygen gas), so a mixture can be separated by purely physical means.

Naming and Symbolising Elements

Each element has its own internationally agreed chemical symbol, a system introduced by the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1814, replacing earlier pictographic alchemical symbols. The letter or letters are usually derived from the element’s English name, but sometimes from another language — for example, Hg for mercury comes from the Latin hydrargyrum, while W for tungsten comes from the German Wolfram.

Case matters: Co vs. COChemistry is an exact subject, and upper- versus lower-case letters are never interchangeable. Co is the one-letter-capitalized symbol for the metallic element cobalt, whereas CO is the formula of carbon monoxide, a poisonous compound of carbon and oxygen. Writing one when the other is meant is a serious, and surprisingly common, error.
The number of elements is not fixed foreverNew elements can still be synthesised or discovered, so the known list is open to revision. It takes time for a claimed new element to be confirmed by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC); until then, a provisional three-letter systematic symbol is used, built from Latin-derived roots for its atomic number’s digits. Element 118, for example, was known for years by the placeholder name ununoctium (Uuo) before being formally confirmed and named oganesson (Og).

Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Mixtures

Mixtures themselves are divided into two types depending on how uniform they are.

Homogeneous mixtureA mixture with uniform composition and properties throughout, so that the separate components cannot usually be distinguished by eye. Air (a mixture of gases, mainly N2 and O2), salt water, and metal alloys such as bronze (copper and tin) or steel are all homogeneous mixtures.
Heterogeneous mixtureA mixture with non-uniform composition, so that its properties differ from one region of the sample to another. Water and oil, or sand and salt, are heterogeneous mixtures; the separate components can usually be seen or distinguished.

Whether a mixture forms a uniform (homogeneous) blend depends on whether the forces of attraction between the different particles in the mixture are similar in strength and nature to the forces between particles of the same substance. This is explored fully once intermolecular and metallic bonding are studied, but the basic rule is that “like dissolves in like”: substances with similar inter-particle attractions mix uniformly, while dissimilar ones tend to separate into visible layers or regions.

Water + ethanol: a volume puzzleMixing 10 cm³ of water with 10 cm³ of more water gives exactly 20 cm³ of water, and the same is true for ethanol with ethanol. Yet mixing 10 cm³ of water with 10 cm³ of ethanol gives slightly less than 20 cm³ of solution. This happens because water and ethanol molecules, once mixed, form new hydrogen bonds with each other that are more efficiently packed than in either pure liquid, so the particles take up a little less total space than the simple sum of the two separate volumes would suggest — a reminder that a homogeneous mixture is not simply “the same two substances sitting next to each other.”

Filtration apparatusA conical filter funnel lined with filter paper sits inside the neck of a beaker or conical flask. The mixture (for example, sand suspended in salt water) is poured into the funnel. The insoluble solid (sand) is trapped on the filter paper as the residue, while the liquid (salt water) passes through the paper and drips down into the collecting vessel below as the filtrate. Afterward, gently heating the filtrate causes the water to evaporate, leaving solid salt crystals behind (crystallisation/recrystallisation).
Distillation apparatusA round-bottomed flask containing the mixture (for example, salt water) sits over a heat source. As the flask is heated, the substance with the lower boiling point (the solvent, water) vaporises first and rises into a sloped side-arm connected to a long glass condenser tube. The condenser is a tube-within-a-tube, with cold water constantly flowing through the outer jacket in the opposite direction to the vapour; this cools the vapour so that it condenses back into a liquid inside the inner tube. The condensed liquid (pure water) drips out of the far end of the condenser into a collecting beaker, while the higher-boiling solute (salt) is left behind in the original flask.
Paper chromatography apparatusSmall spots of the sample solutions (for example, different food dyes) are placed in a line near the bottom edge of a strip of chromatography paper, above the level any solvent will reach (the baseline). The paper is suspended in a closed container with a shallow layer of solvent in the bottom, kept below the baseline. The container is sealed so the atmosphere inside becomes saturated with solvent vapour. As the solvent is drawn up the paper, different dissolved components travel at different speeds depending on how strongly each is adsorbed onto the paper versus how soluble it is in the moving solvent, so a single spot separates into several spots spread out vertically up the paper, each corresponding to one component of the original mixture.
Solvation, filtration, evaporation, distillation, recrystallisation, chromatographySolvation: a solute dissolving into a solvent to form a solution. Filtration: separating an insoluble solid from a liquid or gas using a membrane (filter paper). Evaporation: driving off a solvent as a vapour, leaving a dissolved solid behind. Distillation: separating two liquids (or a solvent from a dissolved solid) using a difference in boiling point, with the vapour re-condensed and collected. Recrystallisation: dissolving an impure solid and allowing pure crystals to re-form as the solution cools or the solvent evaporates, leaving impurities in solution. Paper chromatography: separating dissolved substances based on differing affinities for a stationary phase (the paper) versus a moving solvent.
Yield vs. purity in purificationPurifying the product of a chemical reaction is a genuine practical challenge, because the mixture to be separated also contains unreacted starting materials and unwanted by-products. Maximising the amount of product recovered (the yield) and maximising how pure that product is are not automatically compatible goals: some of the desired product is often lost together with the impurities during a purification step (for example, during recrystallisation, some product stays dissolved in the solvent along with the impurities and is not recovered as crystals).

The Kinetic Molecular Theory

Matter exists in different physical states — solid, liquid and gas — and can change between them depending on temperature and pressure. The kinetic molecular theory is the model used to explain the properties of each state and the transitions between them, in terms of the motion and arrangement of particles.

Kinetic molecular theoryA model stating that matter is made of constantly moving particles, and that the state of a substance (solid, liquid, or gas) at a given temperature and pressure is determined by the strength of the attractive forces between its particles relative to the particles’ average kinetic energy. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. Depending on the chemical nature of a substance, these particles may be atoms (for example Ar(g)), molecules (for example H2O(l)), or ions (for example Na+ and Cl− in aqueous NaCl(aq)); “particle” is used as an inclusive term for all of these.
Particle arrangement in the three statesThree boxes are shown side by side, representing the same substance as a solid, a liquid, and a gas, with an arrow beneath labelled “increasing temperature, increasing average kinetic energy of particles,” pointing from solid through liquid to gas. In the solid box, particles are drawn as circles packed tightly together in a regular, ordered grid, each vibrating only slightly about a fixed position. In the liquid box, the circles are still close together and touching but arranged irregularly, able to slide past one another. In the gas box, the circles are few and far apart, scattered randomly across the whole box, each moving independently in a straight line until it collides with another particle or the container wall.

Liquids and gases are both called fluids because they can flow. Diffusion, the process by which particles of a substance spread out and mix evenly due to their own random motion, occurs mainly in these two fluid states, since particles in a solid are not free to move from place to place. A classic demonstration is releasing bromine liquid, Br2(l), in a sealed gas jar connected to an empty one: the orange-brown bromine vapour is observed spreading (diffusing) until it fills both jars evenly, showing that gas particles move and spread out on their own, without stirring or any external push.

A fourth state: plasmaPlasma exists only under conditions of extremely high temperature and pressure, such as inside stars including our Sun. It is not part of the everyday solid/liquid/gas classification but is often mentioned as a fourth state of matter.
Worked example

Which of the following has the highest average kinetic energy: He(g) at 100°C, H2(g) at 200°C, O2(g) at 300°C, or H2O(g) at 400°C?
Average kinetic energy depends only on temperature, not on the chemical identity or mass of the particles, so the sample at the highest temperature, H2O(g) at 400°C, has the greatest average kinetic energy — regardless of the fact that water molecules are heavier than helium or hydrogen molecules.

Solutions are mixtures of two (or more) components. The less abundant component is the solute and the more abundant is the solvent; the solute can be solid, liquid or gas, but the solvent is generally a liquid. Solutions in water are especially important in chemistry and are given the symbol (aq), short for “aqueous.” For example, the reaction of sodium with water is written: 2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g). It is good practice to always include state symbols in a chemical equation, even if a question does not explicitly ask for them.

Changes of State

As particles gain kinetic energy (usually by heating), they can overcome the inter-particle forces holding them in a more ordered state and change to a less ordered one. These changes occur reversibly, at a fixed temperature and pressure characteristic of each substance, and each of the six changes of state has its own name.

The changes of stateA triangle diagram with “solid” at the top-left corner, “liquid” at the top-right corner, and “gas” at the bottom, connected by six labelled arrows. An arrow from solid to liquid is labelled “melting”; the reverse arrow from liquid to solid is labelled “freezing.” An arrow from liquid to gas is labelled “vaporisation (boiling/evaporation)”; the reverse arrow from gas to liquid is labelled “condensation.” A long arrow running directly from solid to gas (skipping the liquid state entirely) is labelled “sublimation”; the reverse arrow from gas directly to solid is labelled “deposition.”
Sublimation and depositionSublimation is the direct change from solid to gas without passing through the liquid state (for example, solid carbon dioxide, iodine, and ammonium chloride sublime under normal conditions). Deposition is the reverse process, a gas changing directly into a solid, as occurs when frost or hair-ice forms directly from water vapour in cold air.
Melting/freezing point and boiling/condensation pointThe temperature at which a solid melts is exactly the same temperature at which the liquid form of that substance freezes: melting point = freezing point. Likewise, the temperature at which a liquid boils is exactly the same temperature at which its gas condenses: boiling point = condensation point. These can be written as reversible equations, for example X(s) ⇌ X(l) and Y(l) ⇌ Y(g).

A liquid can turn to gas by two different processes. Boiling occurs at one fixed temperature (for a given external pressure) throughout the whole liquid, producing visible bubbles of vapour. Evaporation happens only at the liquid’s surface, occurs over a whole range of temperatures below the boiling point, and speeds up with a larger surface area, a higher temperature, or air movement across the surface.

Freeze-drying: an application of sublimationFreeze-drying is used to preserve food and some pharmaceuticals. The material is first frozen, then warmed gently under very low pressure, causing the ice within it to sublime directly to water vapour without ever passing through the liquid state. This is slower than conventional dehydration, but because the material never becomes liquid, its structure, composition and flavour are far better conserved. The dried product is then stored in a moisture-free, oxygen-free package and can later be reconstituted by adding water.

Vapour Pressure and the Boiling Point

As a liquid is heated, more of its particles have enough energy to escape into the vapour phase, so the vapour pressure above the liquid rises. More precisely, the boiling point is defined as the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid becomes equal to the external (atmospheric) pressure acting on its surface.

Vapour pressure vs. temperature at different external pressuresA graph with temperature (°C) on the horizontal axis and vapour pressure (Pa) on the vertical axis. A single upward-curving line shows how a liquid’s vapour pressure rises steeply as temperature increases. Three horizontal dashed lines cross this curve at different heights, representing three different external pressures: a low external pressure (as found on the summit of Mount Everest), standard atmospheric pressure (1.00 × 105 Pa), and a high pressure (as found inside a sealed pressure cooker). Each horizontal line meets the vapour-pressure curve at a different point, and dropping straight down from that intersection to the temperature axis gives the boiling point at that particular external pressure. The point for low pressure (Everest) gives the lowest boiling point of the three; the point for high pressure (pressure cooker) gives the highest boiling point of the three.
Pressure cooker vs. high altitudeA pressure cooker is a sealed vessel that builds up pressure above atmospheric, which raises the boiling point of water inside it and so cooks food faster. At high altitude (such as on a mountain), atmospheric pressure is lower than at sea level, so water boils at a lower temperature, meaning food takes longer to cook. A butane camping-stove canister stores butane as a liquid because the high pressure inside the sealed canister raises its boiling point above room temperature; opening the valve releases the pressure, so the butane boils and escapes as a burnable gas.
Boiling vs. evaporation, once moreBoiling happens throughout the whole liquid at one fixed temperature (for a given external pressure), producing visible bubbles. Evaporation happens only at the liquid’s exposed surface, over a whole range of temperatures below the boiling point, and is accelerated by a larger surface area, higher temperature, or air movement across the surface — which is why wet laundry dries (fully evaporates) in warm, breezy weather at temperatures far below 100°C.

Predicting a melting point from a trend

Before bromine’s melting point was known, it could be estimated by noticing that both melting and boiling points rise steadily down the halogen group, from fluorine through to iodine. Since bromine lies between chlorine and iodine in the group, its melting point must lie somewhere between −101°C and 114°C, and — because the increase is not a simple straight line but grows more slowly between the lighter halogens — a reasonable estimate places it much closer to chlorine’s value than to iodine’s, in the region of 0°C. The measured value, −7°C, confirms this estimate.

Why melting and boiling points increase down the groupAs the halogen molecules get heavier (more electrons, larger molecules), the attractive London (dispersion) forces between neighbouring molecules become stronger, so more energy is required to separate the molecules into a liquid or a gas. This idea, that increasing molar mass generally strengthens the dispersion forces between non-polar molecules, is developed fully once intermolecular forces are studied.
Worked example

What physical state is iodine in at 200°C?
Iodine’s boiling point is 184°C. Since 200°C is above this boiling point, iodine exists as a gas at 200°C.

Kinetic Energy, Mass and the Rate of Diffusion

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample; particles of different substances at the same temperature all have the same average kinetic energy, but not the same speed, because kinetic energy also depends on mass.

Ek = ½mv2

Here Ek is the kinetic energy of a particle, m is its mass, and v is its speed. Because kinetic energy depends on both mass and the square of speed, a lighter particle must move faster than a heavier particle to have the same kinetic energy.

Worked example

A gas particle of mass 3.0 × 10−26 kg is moving at 600 m s−1. Calculate its kinetic energy.
Ek = ½mv2 = ½ × (3.0 × 10−26) × (600)2 = ½ × (3.0 × 10−26) × 360 000 = 5.4 × 10−21 J. This tiny value is typical of the kinetic energy of a single molecule — a normal sample contains an enormous number of such particles, roughly of the order of Avogadro’s number.

Since particles of different substances at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy, setting ½m1v12 = ½m2v22 and rearranging gives a relationship between the relative masses and relative speeds of two types of particle at the same temperature.

m1 ÷ m2 = v22 ÷ v12

This means that, at a given temperature, lighter particles move faster on average than heavier particles, so lighter particles diffuse more quickly through a space than heavier ones.

Worked example

Compare the average speeds of He atoms (relative mass 4) and CO2 molecules (relative mass 44) at the same temperature.
Using m1 ÷ m2 = v22 ÷ v12 with He as particle 1 and CO2 as particle 2: 4 ÷ 44 = v22 ÷ v12, so v12 ÷ v22 = 44 ÷ 4 = 11, giving v1 ÷ v2 = √11 ≈ 3.3. On average, He atoms move about 3.3 times as fast as CO2 molecules at the same temperature, so helium gas diffuses noticeably faster than carbon dioxide gas.

Comparing two unknown gases by smellIf a mixture of two gases X and Y, both with strong but distinct smells, is released, and the smell of X reaches an observer across the room before the smell of Y, this shows that X diffuses faster than Y. Since diffusion rate depends inversely on mass for particles at the same temperature, it can be deduced that the relative molecular mass of X is smaller than that of Y, even without knowing either gas’s identity directly.

Temperature: the Celsius and Kelvin Scales

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. The Celsius scale is defined relative to the freezing point (0 °C) and boiling point (100 °C) of water, but it is not an absolute scale, since negative temperatures are possible. The kelvin (K) is the SI unit of temperature and is an absolute scale: absolute zero, 0 K (equal to −273.15 °C), is the lowest temperature theoretically possible, the point at which particles have the minimum possible kinetic energy and all particle movement effectively stops.

Kelvin vs. CelsiusA change of 1 °C is exactly the same size as a change of 1 K, which makes converting between the scales straightforward — only the zero point differs, not the size of each degree/kelvin.
T (K) = T (°C) + 273.15

T(K) is the temperature in kelvin and T(°C) is the same temperature in degrees Celsius. Because the two scales have the same-sized unit, this is a simple addition (or, rearranged, subtraction), never a multiplication.

Worked example

Convert 36.85 °C (approximate human body temperature) to kelvin, and convert 0.00 K to degrees Celsius.
T(K) = 36.85 + 273.15 = 310.00 K.
Rearranging, T(°C) = T(K) − 273.15 = 0.00 − 273.15 = −273.15 °C, which is absolute zero.

Although temperature only tells us the average kinetic energy, a real sample contains an enormous number of particles, each with a different individual kinetic energy at any instant (their total kinetic energy is conserved overall, but individual values constantly change as particles collide). The statistical spread of these individual energies at a fixed temperature is called the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, which later helps explain why only some particle collisions in a chemical reaction have enough energy (the activation energy) to react.

Heating Curves and Energy Changes

If a solid is heated at a steady rate and its temperature is tracked over time, the resulting graph reveals exactly when energy is raising the particles’ kinetic energy and when it is instead being used to break inter-particle forces.

Heating curve of a solidA graph of temperature (vertical axis) against energy input or time (horizontal axis), divided into five labelled sections a-b-c-d-e-f. From a to b, the line rises steadily as the solid is heated (temperature increasing, still solid). From b to c, the line is completely flat at the melting point — this plateau is the melting process, with solid and liquid coexisting. From c to d, the line rises again as the liquid is heated (temperature increasing, still liquid). From d to e, the line is flat again, this time at the (higher) boiling point — this plateau is the boiling process, with liquid and gas coexisting, and it is noticeably flatter for longer than the melting plateau because more energy is required to separate particles completely into a gas than to loosen a solid into a liquid. From e to f, the line rises once more as the gas continues to heat up.
Why temperature stays constant during a change of stateDuring melting or boiling, the energy supplied is used entirely to overcome (break) the inter-particle attractive forces, not to increase the particles’ kinetic energy. Since temperature depends only on average kinetic energy, and that is not increasing during the plateau, the temperature itself does not change until the change of state is complete.
Endothermic vs. exothermic changes of stateMelting and boiling are endothermic: energy must be absorbed from the surroundings to separate particles against their attractive forces. Freezing and condensation are exothermic: energy is released to the surroundings as particles move closer together and inter-particle attractions re-form.
Ice cubes in a glass of water

An ice cube at 0°C is placed into a glass of liquid water at 10°C. As the ice melts, the heat released by the surrounding warmer water is used entirely to break the hydrogen bonds holding the ice’s particles in their fixed positions, not to raise the ice’s temperature. This is why the melting ice itself remains at 0°C throughout the melting process, even while it continuously absorbs heat energy from the water around it, and why the overall mixture’s temperature stays close to 0°C until all the ice has melted.
