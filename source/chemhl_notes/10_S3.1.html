Guiding Question

How does the periodic table help us to predict patterns and trends in the properties of elements?

Chemistry is not a random collection of facts about 118 elements memorized in isolation; it is the study of patterns and trends. The periodic table is the single most powerful tool for this task, because it arranges the elements so that similar elements sit close together and predictable trends run across and down the table. First proposed by the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev in 1869, the periodic table groups elements by their chemical and physical properties — a pattern later shown to be a direct consequence of the periodic repetition of electron configurations. Once the position of an element in the table is known, a great deal about its electron configuration, reactivity, bonding and physical properties can be predicted without ever testing it in a laboratory. At Higher Level, this same organizing principle is pushed further into the d block, where an incomplete set of d electrons gives the transition elements a cluster of characteristic properties — variable oxidation states, catalytic activity, magnetism and coloured complex ions — that cannot be predicted from main-group trends alone.

Periods, Groups and Blocks

The periodic law states that, at regular but varying intervals, the chemical elements show an approximate repetition of their properties. Mendeleev arranged the 62 elements known in 1869 into rows and columns by their properties, and — crucially — left gaps for elements that had not yet been discovered, predicting their properties from the surrounding pattern. When elements such as gallium, scandium, germanium and technetium were later isolated, their measured properties matched Mendeleev’s predictions closely, which gave the table immediate scientific credibility. Mendeleev had no knowledge of atomic structure; only after the discovery of the electron (Thomson, 1897) and the nuclear model of the atom (Rutherford and Bohr) could the periodic law be explained. We now know that the periodicity in the elements’ properties arises directly from the periodicity of their electron configurations.

PeriodicityPeriodicity is the regular repetition of the properties of the elements, arising from the pattern in which their electrons fill successive energy levels and sublevels.

In the modern periodic table, elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number, Z (the number of protons in the nucleus), rather than by relative atomic mass as Mendeleev originally used. Because there are no gaps left in the sequence of atomic numbers up to 118, chemists are confident that the search for new naturally occurring elements is essentially complete; any further elements can only be made artificially, by teams of scientists rather than by the lone-chemist isolation that characterized the earliest discoveries.

  • The horizontal rows of the table are called periods, numbered 1 to 7. The period number equals the number of occupied principal electron energy levels (shells) in atoms of that element.
  • The vertical columns are called groups, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in the same group share the same number of valence (outer-shell) electrons and consequently have similar chemical properties.
Metals vs non-metals vs metalloidsMetals occupy the whole left and centre of the table: the s block, the d block, the f block “island,” and a handful of elements at the lower-left of the p block such as aluminium, tin and lead. Metals are shiny, malleable, good conductors of heat and electricity, and tend to lose electrons when they react. Non-metals are confined to the upper right-hand corner of the p block (plus hydrogen). They are typically dull, brittle if solid, poor conductors, and tend to gain electrons when they react. Metalloids — boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony, tellurium and polonium — sit along the staircase itself, between the metals and non-metals. They look and behave physically like metals but react chemically more like non-metals; several, such as silicon and germanium, are semiconductors and form the basis of the electronics industry.
The metal/non-metal staircaseStarting at boron in period 2, a bold diagonal boundary steps down and to the right one element at a time: boron, then between aluminium and silicon, then between germanium and its neighbours, continuing down through arsenic, antimony, tellurium and polonium, finally reaching astatine at the bottom right of the p block. Every element to the lower-left of this staircase is a metal, every element to the upper-right of it is a non-metal, and the seven elements sitting directly on the staircase are the metalloids.

The table can also be divided into four blocks, according to which sublevel holds the highest-energy (last-added) electron in a ground-state atom.

Periodicity and Electron Configuration

An element’s position in the periodic table is a direct readout of its ground-state electron configuration.

Reading the tableThe period number gives the number of occupied principal energy levels (shells) in an atom. The group number (1–18) is related to the number of valence electrons: for the main-group elements (s and p blocks), groups 1–2 have 1–2 valence electrons, and groups 13–18 have 3–8 valence electrons respectively (group 18 has 8, except helium with 2).

For example, sodium (Z = 11) has the configuration 1s22s22p63s1. It sits in period 3 because its highest occupied shell is the third, and in group 1 because it has one electron in that outer shell. Bromine (Z = 35) has the configuration [Ar]3d104s24p5; it is in period 4 (its highest shell is the fourth) and group 17 (its outer 4s and 4p electrons together give 7 valence electrons).

Worked exampleHow many electrons are in the valence energy level of iodine (Z = 53), and what does this tell us about its group?
Iodine is in period 5, so it has the noble-gas core of krypton, [Kr], followed by electrons filling 5s, then 4d, then 5p: [Kr]4d105s25p5.
Counting the outer (n = 5) electrons: 5s25p5 gives 2 + 5 = 7 valence electrons, confirming that iodine belongs to group 17, the halogens.
Group vs period, at a glanceA period is a row (reads across); its number tells you how many shells are occupied. A group is a column (reads down); its number is linked to the number of valence electrons and therefore to chemical behaviour — elements in the same group react in chemically similar ways.

Atomic and Ionic Radius

Many of the trends in atomic and physical properties across the periodic table can be traced back to a single underlying idea: effective nuclear charge, the net positive pull felt by an atom’s outer (valence) electrons once the shielding effect of inner electrons is accounted for. Effective nuclear charge increases across a period (protons are added to the nucleus one at a time while the same inner shells do the shielding) but stays roughly constant down a group (extra protons are added, but so are extra complete inner shells that shield the new outer electron just as effectively).

Atomic radiusThe (covalent) atomic radius, r, is taken as half the distance between the nuclei of two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond. It is not a sharply defined boundary — electrons occupy orbitals that describe a probability distribution with no fixed edge — but this operational definition allows radii to be compared consistently.

Down a group, atomic radius increases: each successive element has one more occupied principal energy level than the one above it, so its outer electrons sit further from the nucleus, even though the nuclear charge is also greater. In group 1, radius increases steadily from lithium (130 pm) to sodium (160 pm) to potassium (200 pm) to rubidium (215 pm) to caesium (238 pm). Across a period, atomic radius decreases: all the elements in a period have the same number of occupied shells, but nuclear charge (and hence effective nuclear charge) increases steadily left to right, pulling the outer electrons progressively closer to the nucleus. Across period 3, radius falls from 160 pm (Na) to 140 pm (Mg), 124 pm (Al), 114 pm (Si), 109 pm (P), 104 pm (S), 100 pm (Cl) — chlorine is only about 60% the size of sodium, even though it has 7 more protons and 7 more electrons.

Forming an ion changes an atom’s radius predictably: positive ions are smaller than their parent atom, because forming a cation removes the entire outer occupied shell (or empties it substantially), so the remaining electrons are held by a now higher effective charge per remaining electron; negative ions are larger than their parent atom, because extra electrons added to the same outer shell increase electron–electron repulsion, forcing the electron cloud to expand. Across period 3, the ionic radii fall into two separate decreasing sequences: Na+ (102 pm) > Mg2+ (72 pm) > Al3+ (54 pm) > Si4+ (40 pm) for the cations, and Si4− (271 pm) > P3− (212 pm) > S2− (184 pm) > Cl− (181 pm) for the anions. There is a large jump between Si4+ and Si4− because the cation has lost its outer (n = 3) shell entirely and is measured by its n = 2 shell, while the anion still has a filled n = 3 shell — it is the difference in the number of occupied shells, not just nuclear charge, that produces this discontinuity. Down a group, ionic radius increases for the same reason as atomic radius.

Worked example: isoelectronic speciesExplain the trend in radius across O2−, F−, Ne, Na+ and Mg2+.
All five species have exactly 10 electrons and the same electron configuration, 1s22s22p6, so electron–electron repulsion is fixed across the series.
Their nuclear charges, however, increase steadily: O (+8), F (+9), Ne (+10), Na (+11), Mg (+12). A larger nuclear charge pulls the same electron cloud in more tightly, so radius decreases steadily along the series: O2− is the largest and Mg2+ is the smallest, even though Mg has more protons and more mass.

Ionization Energy, Electron Affinity and Electronegativity

First ionization energyThe first ionization energy of an element is the energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms of that element in their ground state: X(g) → X+(g) + e−. It is always endothermic, since energy must be supplied to pull a negatively charged electron away from a positively charged nucleus.

Down a group, first ionization energy decreases: the electron removed occupies a shell further from the nucleus in each successive element, and although the actual nuclear charge increases, the effective nuclear charge stays roughly constant because the extra protons are matched by extra inner shielding shells, so the dominant effect is the increased distance, weakening the attraction. Across a period, first ionization energy generally increases, as effective nuclear charge rises steadily left to right and outer electrons are held more tightly. The trend in ionization energy is essentially the mirror image of the trend in atomic radius: both measure the strength of attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons.

First ionization energy vs atomic number: the discontinuities (HL)Plot first ionization energy against atomic number for the first 20 elements. Each noble gas (He, Ne, Ar) sits at a sharp peak; each alkali metal (Li, Na, K) sits at a deep trough immediately afterward. Between each trough and peak the line climbs in a slightly irregular staircase rather than a smooth diagonal, with two small dips recurring in every period: a dip at the group 13 element compared with the group 2 element before it (e.g. boron lower than beryllium), and a second dip at the group 16 element compared with the group 15 element before it (e.g. oxygen lower than nitrogen).

These two discontinuities are HL-specific evidence for the existence of energy sublevels, and the explanation rests on the energy of the electron actually being removed, not on any special stability of filled or half-filled sublevels as such:

  • Dip after group 2 (e.g. Be → B): beryllium’s outer electron is removed from the 2s sublevel, but boron’s outer electron is removed from the 2p sublevel. Because a 2p orbital is at a slightly higher energy than a 2s orbital in the same shell, boron’s outer electron is already less strongly bound, so less energy is needed to remove it, even though boron has one more proton than beryllium.
  • Dip after group 15 (e.g. N → O): nitrogen has the configuration 2s22px12py12pz1, with each of its three 2p electrons alone in its own orbital. Oxygen has one more electron, which must pair up in an already-occupied 2p orbital. The extra inter-electron repulsion between the two paired electrons in that orbital makes oxygen’s fourth 2p electron easier to remove than the explanation from nuclear charge alone would predict, so the first ionization energy drops slightly from N to O.

The same two dips recur in every period (for example, between Mg and Al, and between P and S, in period 3), which is why the overall trend across a period is described as a general increase rather than a perfectly smooth one.

First electron affinityThe first electron affinity of an element is the energy change when one mole of electrons is added to one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions: X(g) + e− → X−(g). Because the added electron is attracted to the positive nucleus, this process is usually exothermic.

Electron affinity becomes generally more exothermic (larger magnitude) across a period, and less exothermic down a group, following the same effective-nuclear-charge argument as ionization energy. The group 17 halogens, with an effective nuclear charge of roughly +7 acting on an almost-complete outer shell, have the most exothermic electron affinities of any group. The noble gases do not generally form stable negative ions and so are not assigned electron affinity values, and beryllium, nitrogen and magnesium are exceptions to the general trend, for essentially the same sublevel reasons as the ionization energy discontinuities above.

ElectronegativityElectronegativity is a measure of the ability of an atom to attract the shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond towards itself. Unlike ionization energy, it is not a directly measurable property of an isolated atom — it describes an atom’s behaviour specifically within a bond — and the concept is not applied to the group 18 elements, which do not generally form covalent bonds.

Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group, for the same underlying reason as ionization energy. Fluorine, at the top right of the table, is the most electronegative element of all (4.0 on the Pauling scale); francium, at the bottom left, has one of the lowest values (about 0.6).

Melting Point Periodicity

Melting point periodicity is governed less by a single smooth trend and more by changes in structure and bonding across a period. Within group 1, melting point decreases down the group (lithium, 181°C, to caesium, 28°C) because the metallic bonds weaken as the atoms get larger: the delocalized outer electrons sit further from the positive ion cores, reducing the electrostatic attraction holding the lattice together. Within group 17, melting point increases down the group (fluorine, a gas, to iodine, a solid) because the molecules get larger and more polarizable, strengthening the London (van der Waals) forces between them.

Melting point across period 3Plot melting point against the period 3 elements Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar. The line rises steadily through the three metals (Na < Mg < Al, as metallic bonding strengthens with more delocalized electrons per atom and higher ionic charge), then shoots up to a tall, sharp spike at silicon, by far the highest point on the graph, reflecting its giant covalent network. Immediately after silicon the line plunges to a very low value at phosphorus (P4 molecules), rises slightly at sulfur (S8 rings, larger and more polarizable than P4), drops again at chlorine (Cl2, a smaller molecule), and reaches its lowest point of all at argon, a single, small, monatomic gas.

Periodicity in Reactivity

The chemical reactivity of an element is governed by how easily its atoms can lose or gain electrons to reach the stable, complete outer-shell arrangement of the nearest noble gas.

The noble gases (group 18) are the least reactive elements because their atoms already have a complete outer energy level, so there is no thermodynamic driving force to lose or gain electrons. Historically called the “inert gases,” this name is now considered inaccurate: xenon, which has the lowest ionization energy in the group, was made to react (forming a compound with fluorine) as early as 1962.

Group 1 reactivity trendReactivity of the alkali metals increases down the group. Each element forms a singly charged M+ ion by losing its one valence electron, and first ionization energy decreases down the group, so the outer electron is lost more and more readily from lithium down to francium.

All the alkali metals react with water to give hydrogen gas and a metal hydroxide: 2M(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2MOH(aq) + H2(g), or, for potassium specifically, 2K(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2K+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) + H2(g). The vigour increases visibly down the group: lithium floats and fizzes gently, keeping its shape; sodium reacts vigorously enough that the heat released melts the unreacted metal into a small silvery ball that darts across the surface; potassium reacts so vigorously that the heat ignites the hydrogen gas itself, producing a lilac flame; caesium reacts explosively.

Group 17 reactivity trendReactivity of the halogens (as oxidizing agents) decreases down the group. Each halogen atom reacts by gaining one electron to complete its outer octet, forming a singly charged X− ion; this pull on an incoming electron is strongest for the smallest atom, fluorine, whose high effective nuclear charge (about +7) acts over the shortest distance. Fluorine is accordingly the most reactive non-metal in the entire periodic table.

The halogens exist as coloured diatomic molecules, X2: fluorine is a very pale yellow gas, chlorine a denser greenish-yellow gas, bromine a dark red-brown liquid giving off orange-brown vapour, and iodine a dark grey-black solid that sublimes to a violet vapour. Relative reactivity can be demonstrated by displacement: a more reactive (higher) halogen displaces a less reactive (lower) halogen from a solution of its halide salt. Chlorine water added to colourless potassium bromide solution turns orange as Cl2(aq) + 2Br−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + Br2(aq); no visible reaction occurs if chlorine water is added to a fluoride solution, because fluorine cannot be displaced by the less reactive chlorine. Halide ions can also be identified by precipitation with silver ions, Ag+(aq) + X−(aq) → AgX(s): silver chloride is a white precipitate, silver bromide cream/pale yellow, and silver iodide a distinctly darker yellow.

Metal and Non-metal Oxides

Metallic and non-metallic character is not a sharp switch but a continuum, and nowhere is this clearer than in the bonding, structure and acid–base behaviour of the period 3 oxides. Whether an oxide is ionic or covalent depends on the electronegativity difference between the element and oxygen (electronegativity 3.4); because electronegativity increases across period 3, the ionic character of the oxides steadily decreases from left to right.

The maximum oxidation state shown by each period 3 element in its oxide equals its group number for groups 1–17 (using the 1–18 numbering), a direct consequence of each atom using all of its valence electrons to bond with the highly electronegative oxygen. Molten conductivity gives direct experimental evidence for the bonding pattern: an oxide only conducts electricity when charged ions are free to move, so the high conductivity of molten Na2O, MgO and Al2O3 confirms their ionic lattices, while the total absence of conductivity in molten P4O10, SO3 and Cl2O7 confirms simple covalent molecules with no free ions.

The metal-to-non-metal oxide continuumIonic, metallic oxides are generally basic; covalent, non-metallic oxides are generally acidic; and oxides with a mixture of ionic and covalent character, such as aluminium oxide, can be amphoteric — capable of reacting as both an acid and a base.

Group 1 and group 2 metal oxides dissolve in water to give alkaline solutions: Na2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq); MgO(s) + H2O(l) → Mg(OH)2(aq). A basic oxide also reacts directly with an acid to form a salt and water, e.g. MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l). Non-metal oxides react with water to give acidic solutions: SO3(l) + H2O(l) → H2SO4(aq) (sulfuric(VI) acid); SO2(g) + H2O(l) → H2SO3(aq) (sulfuric(IV) acid). Aluminium oxide barely dissolves in pure water, but demonstrates amphoteric character directly: Al2O3(s) + 6H+(aq) → 2Al3+(aq) + 3H2O(l) (acting as a base), and Al2O3(s) + 3H2O(l) + 2OH−(aq) → 2Al(OH)4−(aq) (acting as an acid).

Acid rain and ocean acidificationAll rainwater is naturally slightly acidic (pH around 5.6) because atmospheric CO2 dissolves to form carbonic acid. Acid rain refers to precipitation more acidic than this, owing to dissolved sulfur and nitrogen oxides. SO2 (from burning fossil fuels and ore smelting) dissolves to give H2SO3, or is oxidized to SO3 which gives the stronger H2SO4: 2SO2(g) + O2(g) → 2SO3(g). Nitrogen oxides form in combustion engines, N2(g) + O2(g) → 2NO(g), then oxidize further to NO2, which dissolves to give a mixture of nitric(III) and nitric(V) acids: H2O(l) + 2NO2(g) → HNO2(aq) + HNO3(aq). About half of all CO2 released by burning fossil fuels dissolves into the oceans, forming carbonic acid that partly dissociates (CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2CO3(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)), raising ocean surface acidity and inhibiting shell and skeleton growth in many marine organisms.

Oxidation States

Oxidation stateThe oxidation state (or oxidation number) of an atom is the charge that atom would have if the compound it is part of were composed entirely of ions. It is a book-keeping tool for tracking how electrons are distributed between atoms, whether the actual bonding is ionic or covalent.
Four equivalent definitions of oxidationOxidation is: the addition of oxygen; the removal of hydrogen; the loss of electrons; and an increase in oxidation state. (Reduction is the reverse of each.) Magnesium’s oxidation state rises from 0 (in the free element) to +2 (in MgO or MgCl2) whichever non-metal it reacts with: magnesium has been oxidized.

The oxidation state of every atom in any species can be worked out from a short list of rules, applied in order: (1) an atom in a free, uncombined element is 0; (2) in a simple (monatomic) ion, the oxidation state equals the ion’s charge; (3) the oxidation states in a neutral compound sum to zero; (4) the oxidation states in a polyatomic ion sum to its overall charge; (5) group 1 metals are always +1 and group 2 metals always +2 in compounds; (6) fluorine is always −1; (7) oxygen is usually −2, except −1 in peroxides (H2O2) and positive when bonded to fluorine (e.g. +2 in OF2); (8) hydrogen is usually +1, except −1 in metal hydrides such as NaH and MgH2.

Worked exampleAssign the oxidation state of sulfur in (a) H2SO4 and (b) SO32−.
(a) Neutral molecule: 2(+1) + S + 4(−2) = 0, so S = +6.
(b) Polyatomic ion, charge −2: S + 3(−2) = −2, so S = +4.
Sulfur exists in more than one oxidation state depending on the compound: +6 in sulfuric(VI) acid, +4 in sulfuric(IV) acid.
DisproportionationA disproportionation reaction is a redox reaction in which atoms of the same element, starting in a single oxidation state, end up simultaneously oxidized and reduced into two different oxidation states in the products. In H2O(l) + 2NO2(g) → HNO2(aq) + HNO3(aq), nitrogen starts at +4 in NO2 and ends up at both +3 (reduced, in HNO2) and +5 (oxidized, in HNO3).

Because many elements (particularly transition metals and non-metals such as nitrogen, sulfur and chlorine) form more than one oxidation state, IUPAC nomenclature inserts the oxidation number, written as a Roman numeral in brackets directly after the element’s name (no space), to remove any ambiguity: H2SO4 is sulfuric(VI) acid, SO42− is the sulfate(VI) ion; H2SO3 is sulfuric(IV) acid, SO32− is the sulfate(IV) ion.

HL: Transition Elements and Variable Oxidation States

Transition elementA transition element is a d-block element whose atoms have an incomplete d sublevel, or which can give rise to a cation with an incomplete d sublevel.

The first-row d-block elements (Sc to Zn) fill the 3d sublevel after 4s, with two well-known anomalies: chromium is [Ar]3d54s1 and copper is [Ar]3d104s1 (a half-filled or completely filled 3d sublevel plus a single 4s electron is energetically favourable compared with 3d44s2 or 3d94s2). Because an inner d sublevel is being filled while the outer 4s electrons stay roughly constant, the increase in nuclear charge along the row is largely offset by the extra shielding from each new 3d electron, so effective nuclear charge rises only slightly across the row. This explains the relatively small variation in atomic radius, first ionization energy and density across the first-row transition elements, compared with the much larger changes across an s- or p-block period — and the similarity in atomic radii is also exactly what allows d-block metals to form alloys with one another so readily, since one metal’s atoms can replace another’s in the lattice without much disruption.

Zinc is not a transition elementZinc, [Ar]3d104s2, and its only ion, Zn2+ ([Ar]3d10), both have a complete d sublevel in every compound zinc forms. Because zinc never has an incomplete d sublevel, it does not show the characteristic transition-element properties: it exists in only the +2 oxidation state, forms no coloured compounds, and is not useful as a catalyst in the way the true transition metals are. Scandium is a borderline case debated for the same reason in the opposite direction: its only common ion, Sc3+ ([Ar]), has an empty d sublevel, so it too forms no coloured compounds, although the much less common Sc2+(aq) does retain a single d electron.

In contrast to the s-block metals, which show only the oxidation state matching their group number, transition metals characteristically show a wide range of oxidation states in their compounds. This difference is explained by patterns in successive ionization energies.

Worked example: successive ionization energies of Ca and VCalcium, [Ar]4s2, shows only the +2 oxidation state. Removing a third electron would mean breaking into the full, lower-energy 3p sublevel of the noble-gas core, which requires a huge jump in energy — so Ca3+ is never formed.
Vanadium, [Ar]3d34s2, shows the +2, +3, +4 and +5 states. Because the 3d and 4s sublevels are close in energy, each successive ionization (losing the two 4s electrons, then one, two, then three 3d electrons) requires only a gradually increasing amount of energy, with no sharp jump until the sixth electron has to be removed from the inner, full 3p sublevel — so vanadium shows oxidation states up to +5, but never +6.

More generally across the first transition series: all the elements show both the +2 and +3 states (the M2+ ion is more stable for the earlier elements, scandium to chromium, while M3+ becomes more common for the later elements, as the increasing nuclear charge makes a third electron progressively harder to remove). The maximum oxidation state increases in steps of +1 across the row, reaching a maximum of +7 at manganese (corresponding to using both the 4s and all five 3d electrons in bonding, as in the permanganate ion, MnO4−), and then decreases in steps of −1 for the elements after manganese, as nuclear charge increases and successive 3d electrons become harder to remove. Oxidation states above +3 generally show more covalent character, because a small, highly charged ion polarizes neighbouring negative ions strongly; compounds with higher oxidation states, such as dichromate(VI) and permanganate(VII), also tend to be powerful oxidizing agents.

HL: Physical and Magnetic Properties of Transition Elements

The transition metals share a set of characteristic physical properties: high electrical and thermal conductivity, high melting points, hardness, high tensile strength, malleability and ductility. These properties follow from unusually strong metallic bonding: because the 3d and 4s electrons are so close in energy, both sets become part of the delocalized sea of electrons that holds the metal lattice together, giving more delocalized electrons per atom than a typical s-block metal. The smaller atomic radii of the transition metals, compared with their s-block neighbours in the same period, also contribute to their characteristically high densities.

ParamagnetismThe magnetic properties of the transition elements and their compounds arise from unpaired electrons in the d orbitals. Every electron behaves as a tiny magnet due to its spin; when electrons are paired, their opposite spins cancel and there is no net magnetic effect, but an atom or ion with one or more unpaired d electrons is attracted into an external magnetic field, a property called paramagnetism.

Iron, cobalt and nickel show an unusually strong form of this behaviour, called ferromagnetism, in which the unpaired electron spins of neighbouring atoms align cooperatively throughout the solid metal. Sc3+ (3d0) and Zn2+ (3d10) have no unpaired d electrons and so show no paramagnetism, while an ion such as Mn2+ (3d5, five unpaired electrons, one in each d orbital) is strongly paramagnetic.

HL: Catalytic Behaviour of Transition Elements

Transition metals and their ions are widely used as catalysts, providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, because their variable oxidation states and the availability of d orbitals allow them to form temporary, weak bonds with reactant molecules.

Heterogeneous vs homogeneous catalysisA heterogeneous catalyst exists in a different phase from the reactants (usually a solid catalysing a reaction between gases or a solution). A homogeneous catalyst exists in the same phase as the reactants, typically as a dissolved ion that is regenerated by the end of the reaction mechanism.

Heterogeneous examples include: iron in the Haber process, N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g), where solid iron provides a surface on which the strong N≡N triple bond can be weakened; nickel in the catalytic hydrogenation of the carbon–carbon double bonds in unsaturated vegetable oils, converting them to the saturated fats used in margarine; platinum, rhodium and palladium in catalytic converters, which convert toxic carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxide exhaust gases into carbon dioxide and nitrogen, 2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO2(g) + N2(g); and solid manganese(IV) oxide, MnO2, which catalyses the decomposition of aqueous hydrogen peroxide, 2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g), without itself being consumed.

Homogeneous catalysis and autocatalysisWhen acidified potassium manganate(VII) (permanganate, MnO4−) oxidizes a solution containing oxalate ions, C2O42−, the reaction starts off slowly, since both reactants are negatively charged and repel each other as they approach. However, the Mn2+ ions formed as a product are themselves able to catalyse the same reaction (by providing an alternative pathway via the Mn3+/Mn2+ couple), so as the reaction proceeds and more Mn2+ accumulates, the rate speeds up noticeably, even though the concentration of reactants is simultaneously falling. A reaction catalysed by one of its own products, such as this one, is called autocatalysis. Because the catalytic Mn2+/Mn3+ species is dissolved in the same aqueous phase as the reactants, this is an example of homogeneous catalysis.

HL: Complex Ions — Ligands and Coordination Number

Transition metal ions in solution have a high charge density and readily attract molecules or ions that possess a lone pair of electrons, forming a complex ion.

Ligand, coordination bond, coordination numberA ligand is a molecule or ion that donates a lone pair of electrons to a central metal ion, forming a coordination (dative covalent) bond. The coordination number is the total number of coordination bonds formed between the ligands and the central ion. Common ligands include H2O, NH3, Cl−, CN− and OH−.

In [Fe(H2O)6]2+, for example, six water molecules each donate a lone pair on oxygen to the central Fe2+ ion, giving a coordination number of 6 and an octahedral arrangement of ligands around the metal. The square brackets show that the metal ion and its ligands form one distinct unit, with the overall charge shared between the central ion and the whole complex. Smaller ligands such as H2O, NH3 and CN− typically give an octahedral complex with coordination number 6, while larger or more sterically demanding ligands such as Cl− often give a tetrahedral complex with coordination number 4, as in [CuCl4]2−.

Simple complex ions are named systematically by stating the number of each ligand (using di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa- — mono- is normally omitted), the ligand name (aqua for H2O, ammine for NH3, chloro for Cl−, cyano for CN−, hydroxo for OH−), then the metal with its oxidation number in Roman numerals: [Fe(H2O)6]2+ is the hexaaquairon(II) ion; [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]2+ is the tetraamminediaquacopper(II) ion. For an overall negatively charged complex, the metal name takes the suffix “-ate”: [CuCl4]2− is the tetrachlorocuprate(II) ion. The oxidation state of the metal in any complex ion can be worked out just as for any other species: the sum of the ligand charges and the metal’s oxidation state must equal the overall charge on the complex (e.g. in [Fe(CN)6]4−, Fe + 6(−1) = −4, so Fe = +2).

HL: Coloured Complex Ions — d-Orbital Splitting

In an isolated gaseous transition metal ion, all five d orbitals have exactly the same energy (they are described as degenerate). When ligands approach and bond to the metal ion, however, the electric field created by their lone pairs repels the electrons in the d orbitals unevenly, because some d orbitals point directly at the incoming ligands while others point between them.

Octahedral d-orbital splittingPicture six ligands approaching a central metal ion symmetrically along the +x, −x, +y, −y, +z and −z directions (an octahedral arrangement). Two of the five d orbitals point directly along these axes, so electrons in them experience strong repulsion from the approaching ligand lone pairs and are raised to a higher energy. The other three d orbitals point diagonally, into the gaps between the axes, so electrons in them experience weaker repulsion and sit at a lower energy. The five originally equal-energy d orbitals therefore split into two separate energy levels, with an energy gap, ΔE, between the lower set of three and the upper set of two.

Because most first-row transition metal ions have an incomplete d sublevel, at least one d electron can be excited (promoted) from the lower energy level to the higher one. This only happens if a photon is absorbed whose energy, hf, exactly matches the gap ΔE between the two split levels; for most transition metal complexes, this energy gap corresponds to a photon somewhere in the visible part of the spectrum (about 400–700 nm). The particular colour(s) of visible light absorbed are removed from white light, and the colour transmitted or reflected — the one actually observed — is the complementary colour to the colour absorbed, as shown on the colour wheel.

Worked example: the colour of [Ti(H2O)6]3+A solution of [Ti(H2O)6]3+ absorbs light strongly in the green region of the visible spectrum, exciting its single 3d electron from the lower to the higher split level.
Green and purple are complementary colours (opposite each other on the colour wheel), so once green light is removed from white light, the light transmitted appears purple — the characteristic colour of this complex.
In general: identify the colour of light absorbed from the known energy gap or spectrum, then read off the complementary colour on the data-booklet colour wheel to predict the colour actually observed (and vice versa).
Why Sc3+ and Zn2+ form colourless (white) compoundsA d–d electron transition can only absorb visible light if there is at least one d electron to promote and at least one vacant d orbital at the higher split level for it to be promoted into. Sc3+ (3d0) has no d electrons at all to promote, while Zn2+ (3d10) has every d orbital already full, leaving no vacancy for an electron to move into. In both cases no d–d transition is possible, so neither ion absorbs in the visible region, and both form white solids and colourless aqueous solutions.

HL: Factors Affecting the Colour of a Complex

The size of the energy gap ΔE between the split d levels — and therefore the exact colour observed — depends on several factors, each of which can be illustrated with a specific pair of complexes.

Worked example: ligand substitution reactions of copper(II)Describe what is observed as (a) excess aqueous ammonia, and separately (b) excess concentrated hydrochloric acid, are added to a pale blue solution of [Cu(H2O)6]2+.
(a) Four of the six water ligands are substituted by the stronger-field ammonia ligand: [Cu(H2O)6]2+(aq) + 4NH3(aq) → [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]2+(aq) + 4H2O(l). The solution turns a deep/royal blue, since the larger energy gap produced by the stronger-field NH3 ligand shifts the absorbed light to shorter wavelengths.
(b) The chloride ions substitute all six water ligands and also change the coordination number and geometry: [Cu(H2O)6]2+(aq) + 4Cl−(aq) → [CuCl4]2−(aq) + 6H2O(l). The solution turns yellow-green (often appearing as an intermediate green while both complexes are present), since the change from an octahedral to a tetrahedral arrangement of ligands produces a different d-orbital splitting pattern and a different colour entirely.
ColorimetryBecause the absorbance of a coloured solution at a fixed wavelength is directly proportional to its concentration, measuring the absorbance of a transition metal complex at the wavelength it absorbs most strongly (using a colorimeter or spectrophotometer) allows an unknown concentration to be read from a calibration curve constructed using several standard solutions of known concentration.

Guiding Question Revisited

  • Elements are arranged by increasing atomic number into periods (giving the number of occupied shells) and groups (giving the number of valence electrons), and divided into s, p, d and f blocks according to which sublevel is being filled; metals, non-metals and the metalloid staircase occupy predictable regions of the table.
  • Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group; ionic radius follows the same pattern once account is taken of whether the ion is a cation or an anion; first ionization energy, electron affinity and electronegativity all increase across a period and decrease down a group, tracking the strength of attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons.
  • At HL, small discontinuities in the otherwise increasing trend of first ionization energy across a period (after group 2 and after group 15) provide direct evidence for the existence of s and p sublevels, and for the extra stability of a half-filled sublevel.
  • Reactivity increases down group 1 but decreases down group 17; period 3 oxides shift from ionic and basic, through amphoteric, to covalent and acidic, tracking the increase in electronegativity across the period; oxidation states are assigned by a consistent set of rules and named with IUPAC Roman numerals.
  • At HL, transition elements (d-block elements with an incomplete d sublevel) show variable oxidation states, explained by the closeness in energy of the 3d and 4s sublevels and revealed by the absence of a sharp jump in successive ionization energies until an inner, complete sublevel is reached.
  • Transition elements and their compounds act as heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysts, show paramagnetism from unpaired d electrons, and form coloured complex ions when ligands split the d sublevel into two energy levels, allowing a d–d electron transition to absorb a specific colour of visible light and transmit its complementary colour.
  • The exact colour of a complex depends on the identity and oxidation state of the metal, the identity (field strength) of the ligand, and the coordination number and geometry of the complex; Sc3+ and Zn2+ are colourless because their d sublevels are, respectively, completely empty and completely full, so no d–d transition is possible.
