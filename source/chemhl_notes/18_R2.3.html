Guiding Question

How can the extent of a reversible reaction be influenced?

Many chemical reactions are reversible, proceeding in both the forward and backward directions at once. Industrial processes such as the Haber process (N2(g) + 3H2(g) ↔ 2NH3(g)) rely on understanding exactly how far such a reaction will proceed under given conditions, since this directly determines the yield of a valuable product. At Standard Level, Le Châtelier’s principle gives a qualitative answer to this question. At Higher Level, the equilibrium law lets us quantify it precisely: every homogeneous equilibrium reaction has an equilibrium constant, Kc, whose value can be calculated from experimental data, used to predict equilibrium concentrations, compared with the reaction quotient Q to predict the direction of change, and linked directly to the standard Gibbs energy change, ΔG°, through the equation ΔG° = −RT ln K.

Dynamic Equilibrium (Recap)

Dynamic equilibriumA state of dynamic equilibrium is reached in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction becomes equal to the rate of the backward reaction. Both reactions continue, but because they occur at equal rates there is no further net (overall) change in the amounts of reactants and products.

Consider the dissociation of hydrogen iodide, 2HI(g) ↔ H2(g) + I2(g). Starting from pure HI(g) in a sealed flask, the rate of the forward (dissociation) reaction is fastest at first, when [HI] is greatest, and falls as HI is consumed; the rate of the reverse (association) reaction starts at zero and rises as H2 and I2 accumulate. Once the two rates become equal, the concentrations of HI, H2 and I2 all stop changing – equilibrium has been reached. The same equilibrium mixture, with the same value of Kc, is reached whether the reaction starts from pure HI(g), from a mixture of H2(g) and I2(g), or from any other combination of the three species, provided the overall atom ratios and the temperature are the same.

Characteristic of a system at equilibriumExplanation
Equilibrium is dynamicBoth the forward and backward reactions are still occurring, at equal rates.
Equilibrium requires a closed systemNo matter is exchanged with the surroundings, so reactants and products remain available to react and recombine.
Concentrations are constant, not necessarily equalReactants and products are produced and destroyed at equal rates; one side is usually present in much higher concentration than the other.
No change in macroscopic propertiesColour, density and similar observable properties depend on concentration, which is constant at equilibrium.
Equilibrium can be reached from either directionThe same equilibrium mixture, and the same Kc, results under the same conditions regardless of the starting composition.

A reaction whose equilibrium mixture contains predominantly products is said to lie to the right; one whose mixture contains predominantly reactants is said to lie to the left. This qualitative description of equilibrium position is exactly what the equilibrium constant, introduced later in this chapter, allows us to quantify.

Le Châtelier’s Principle (Recap)

Le Châtelier’s principleWhen a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change, the system responds in such a way as to minimize the effect of that change. A new equilibrium is established, generally with a different composition from the original mixture.

Concentration: increasing the concentration of a reactant, or decreasing the concentration of a product, shifts the equilibrium to the right (towards products); the reverse changes shift it to the left. The value of Kc is unaffected, provided the temperature is constant – a new equilibrium position is reached in which the same Kc expression, evaluated with the new equilibrium concentrations, still gives the same numerical value.

Pressure (for gaseous equilibria): a pressure change only affects the equilibrium position of a gas-phase reaction if the number of gas molecules differs between the two sides of the equation. Increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with fewer gas molecules; decreasing the pressure shifts it towards the side with more gas molecules. For example, CO(g) + 2H2(g) ↔ CH3OH(g) has 3 mol of gas on the left and 1 mol on the right, so high pressure shifts this equilibrium to the right. Kc is unaffected by pressure changes at constant temperature.

Temperature: increasing the temperature shifts an equilibrium in the endothermic direction; decreasing the temperature shifts it in the exothermic direction. For 2NO2(g) ↔ N2O4(g), ΔH° = −57 kJ mol−1 (forward reaction exothermic), decreasing the temperature shifts the equilibrium right, towards N2O4. Unlike concentration and pressure, a temperature change does change the value of Kc – this point is developed quantitatively later in this chapter.

Catalyst: a catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy for both the forward and backward reactions, since they pass through the same transition state. It therefore speeds up both reactions by the same factor, reaching equilibrium faster but causing no shift in the equilibrium position and no change in Kc.

HL: The Equilibrium Law and Writing Kc Expressions

The equilibrium lawFor any homogeneous reaction at a specified temperature, substituting the equilibrium concentrations of all reactants and products into the equilibrium constant expression always gives the same numerical value, Kc, no matter what initial concentrations were used to reach that equilibrium.
For aA + bB ↔ cC + dD:    Kc = ([C]c[D]d) / ([A]a[B]b)

Products appear in the numerator and reactants in the denominator; each equilibrium concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation (a power of 1 is not written); where more than one species appears on a side, the terms are multiplied together. Square brackets [ ] denote equilibrium concentration in mol dm−3. Only equilibrium concentrations may be substituted – using initial or any other non-equilibrium concentrations gives the reaction quotient, Q, discussed later in this chapter, not Kc. This expression applies only to homogeneous equilibria, where every reactant and product is in the same phase (all gases, or all in solution); state symbols should always be included to confirm this.

Worked example

Write the Kc expression for: (i) N2(g) + 3H2(g) ↔ 2NH3(g); (ii) Cu2+(aq) + 4NH3(aq) ↔ [Cu(NH3)4]2+(aq).

(i) Kc = [NH3]2 / ([N2][H2]3)

(ii) Kc = [[Cu(NH3)4]2+] / ([Cu2+][NH3]4)

Kc versus KBecause Kc is calculated from concentrations, it is often written simply as K in older texts; the subscript ‘c’ stands for ‘concentration’ and distinguishes it from equilibrium constants defined using other quantities, such as partial pressure (Kp). Both symbols refer to the same equilibrium law at this level.

HL: The Units of Kc

Because the numerator and denominator of a Kc expression do not always have matching total powers of concentration, the units of Kc depend on the stoichiometry of the reaction and must be derived individually for each equation. To do this, substitute the unit mol dm−3 for every concentration term, keep the same powers as in the Kc expression, and simplify algebraically.

Worked example: deriving units of Kc for four different reactions
ReactionKc expressionUnit substitutionUnits of Kc
H2(g) + I2(g) ↔ 2HI(g)[HI]2 / ([H2][I2])(mol dm−3)2 / (mol dm−3)2no units (the powers cancel exactly)
N2O4(g) ↔ 2NO2(g)[NO2]2 / [N2O4](mol dm−3)2 / (mol dm−3)1mol dm−3
2SO2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2SO3(g)[SO3]2 / ([SO2]2[O2])(mol dm−3)2 / (mol dm−3)3mol−1 dm3
N2(g) + 3H2(g) ↔ 2NH3(g)[NH3]2 / ([N2][H2]3)(mol dm−3)2 / (mol dm−3)4mol−2 dm6

In each case, the overall power of concentration in Kc is (sum of product coefficients) − (sum of reactant coefficients). When this difference is zero, Kc has no units; when it is positive, the units are a positive power of mol dm−3; when it is negative (as for the Haber process, where the difference is 2 − 4 = −2), the units become a corresponding power of mol dm3 (equivalently, mol−2 dm6).

A historical note on unitsStrictly, the terms in the equilibrium constant expression represent a dimensionless thermodynamic quantity called activity, so some sources treat Kc as having no units at all under any circumstances, and many IB examination answers are accepted without units. Even so, you should be able to derive the units shown above whenever they are requested, since they confirm that the Kc expression has been set up correctly.

HL: The Magnitude of Kc and the Extent of Reaction

Because products sit in the numerator and reactants in the denominator, a large value of Kc means the equilibrium mixture is dominated by products (the reaction goes almost to completion), while a small value of Kc means the mixture is dominated by reactants (the reaction hardly proceeds). The magnitude of Kc says nothing about how fast equilibrium is reached – only about the composition of the mixture once it is.

Value of KcExtent of reaction
Kc ≪ 1Reaction hardly proceeds; equilibrium lies far towards reactants
Kc ≈ 1Significant, comparable amounts of both reactants and products at equilibrium
Kc ≫ 1Reaction goes almost to completion; equilibrium lies far towards products

For example, at 550 K: H2(g) + I2(g) ↔ 2HI(g) has Kc = 2 (both reactants and products present in appreciable amounts); H2(g) + Br2(g) ↔ 2HBr(g) has Kc = 1010 (strongly product-favoured); H2(g) + Cl2(g) ↔ 2HCl(g) has Kc = 1018 (reaction essentially complete). The huge range in these values, for reactions that look superficially similar, shows how sensitively Kc depends on the particular bonds being broken and formed.

Rule of thumbKc ≫ 1 – equilibrium lies to the right, favouring products. Kc ≪ 1 – equilibrium lies to the left, favouring reactants. Kc ≈ 1 – an equilibrium mixture with comparable amounts of both.

HL: Kc for the Reverse Reaction, and for Multiples of a Reaction

For aA + bB ↔ cC + dD with Kc = [C]c[D]d / ([A]a[B]b), the reverse reaction cC + dD ↔ aA + bB simply inverts the fraction, giving an equilibrium constant Kc′ = 1 / Kc: the equilibrium constant of a reaction is the reciprocal of the equilibrium constant of its reverse, at the same temperature.

Worked example

Kc = 0.040 for 2HI(g) ↔ H2(g) + I2(g) at a given temperature. Find Kc′ for H2(g) + I2(g) ↔ 2HI(g) at the same temperature.

Kc′ = 1 / Kc = 1 / 0.040 = 25

If every coefficient in an equation is multiplied by a factor n, the new equilibrium constant is the original value raised to the power n (doubling the coefficients squares Kc; halving them takes the square root).

HL: Only Temperature Changes the Value of Kc

The single most important HL distinction in this topicChanging concentration, pressure, or adding a catalyst can all shift the position of an equilibrium – but none of them changes the value of Kc, provided the temperature stays the same. The system simply re-establishes a new equilibrium mixture for which the same Kc expression evaluates to the same number as before. Temperature is the only one of the four factors in Le Châtelier’s principle that changes Kc itself.

This is because a change in temperature alters the rate constants of the forward and backward reactions by different amounts (they generally have different activation energies), so the ratio of rates at the new temperature – and hence the new equilibrium composition – corresponds to a genuinely different Kc.

ReactionKc at lower TKc at higher TForward reaction
2NO2(g) ↔ N2O4(g), ΔH° = −57 kJ mol−11300 (at 273 K)170 (at 298 K)Exothermic – Kc decreases as T increases
N2(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2NO(g), ΔH° = +181 kJ mol−14.5 × 10−31 (at 298 K)1.7 × 10−3 (at 2300 K)Endothermic – Kc increases as T increases

In general: an increase in temperature increases Kc for an endothermic forward reaction, and decreases Kc for an exothermic forward reaction. This is exactly consistent with Le Châtelier’s principle – the equilibrium shifts in the endothermic direction as temperature rises – but now expressed quantitatively through Kc rather than only as a qualitative shift.

HL: Calculating Kc from Equilibrium Concentrations

If the equilibrium concentrations of every reactant and product are known, Kc is found simply by substituting them into the equilibrium expression. When only some concentrations and the initial amounts are known, an ICE table (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) is used to work out the missing equilibrium concentrations first. Changes in a row must respect the stoichiometric ratios of the balanced equation, and when amounts in moles are given for a container that is not 1 dm3, every amount must first be divided by the volume to give concentration in mol dm−3.

Worked example: direct substitution

At equilibrium at 500°C, the reaction mixture for CO(g) + H2O(g) ↔ H2(g) + CO2(g) contains [CO] = 0.150, [H2O] = 0.0145, [H2] = 0.200 and [CO2] = 0.0200 (all mol dm−3). Calculate Kc.

Kc = ([H2][CO2]) / ([CO][H2O]) = (0.200 × 0.0200) / (0.150 × 0.0145) = 0.00400 / 0.002175 = 1.84 (3 s.f., no units since the powers cancel)

Worked example: ICE table from initial amount and one equilibrium amount

0.20 mol of PCl3(g) and 0.10 mol of Cl2(g) are placed in a 1 dm3 flask at 350°C. At equilibrium, PCl3(g) + Cl2(g) ↔ PCl5(g) has produced 0.12 mol of PCl5. Calculate Kc.

PCl3Cl2PCl5
initial / mol dm−30.200.100.00
change / mol dm−3−0.08−0.08+0.08
equilibrium / mol dm−30.120.020.08

The change in PCl3 must equal the change in PCl5 (1:1 stoichiometry), so change = 0.20 − 0.12 = 0.08 for both PCl3 and PCl5, and also for Cl2. Kc = [PCl5] / ([PCl3][Cl2]) = 0.08 / (0.12 × 0.02) = 33.3 mol−1 dm3.

Worked example: ICE table with a reaction volume other than 1 dm3

0.60 mol of NO(g) and 0.60 mol of O2(g) react in a 2 dm3 container at 500°C: 2NO(g) + O2(g) ↔ 2NO2(g). At equilibrium, 0.20 mol of NO2(g) is present. Calculate Kc.

Dividing by the 2 dm3 volume: initial [NO] = [O2] = 0.30 mol dm−3, and [NO2] = 0.10 mol dm−3 at equilibrium. The 2:1:2 stoichiometry gives change in [NO2] = +0.10, change in [NO] = −0.10, change in [O2] = −0.05, so at equilibrium [NO] = 0.20, [O2] = 0.25, [NO2] = 0.10 (all mol dm−3).

Kc = [NO2]2 / ([NO]2[O2]) = (0.10)2 / ((0.20)2 × 0.25) = 0.0100 / 0.0100 = 1.0 mol−1 dm3.

HL: Calculating Equilibrium Concentrations from Kc

The same ICE table method also runs in reverse: given Kc and the initial concentrations, algebra is used to find the unknown change, usually called x, and hence every equilibrium concentration.

Worked example: solving directly for one unknown concentration

CO(g) + 2H2(g) ↔ CH3OH(g) has Kc = 0.500 at 350 K. At equilibrium, [CO] = 0.200 mol dm−3 and [H2] = 0.155 mol dm−3. Find [CH3OH] at equilibrium.

Kc = [CH3OH] / ([CO][H2]2), so [CH3OH] = Kc × [CO] × [H2]2 = 0.500 × 0.200 × (0.155)2 = 0.00240 mol dm−3 (2.40 × 10−3 mol dm−3).

Worked example: a symmetric ICE table solved by taking a square root

Kc = 6.78 for SO2(g) + NO(g) ↔ NO2(g) + SO3(g). Both NO and SO2 start at 0.0300 mol dm−3, with no products initially. Find all four equilibrium concentrations.

Let the change in [SO2] and [NO] each be −x, and the change in [NO2] and [SO3] each be +x (1:1:1:1 stoichiometry). At equilibrium, [SO2] = [NO] = 0.0300 − x, and [NO2] = [SO3] = x.

Kc = x2 / (0.0300 − x)2 = 6.78

Because both sides of the Kc expression are perfect squares, the square root of both sides can be taken directly: x / (0.0300 − x) = √6.78 = 2.60. Rearranging, x = 2.60(0.0300 − x), so x(1 + 2.60) = 0.0780, giving x = 0.0780 / 3.60 = 0.0217 mol dm−3.

Equilibrium concentrations: [SO2] = [NO] = 0.0300 − 0.0217 = 0.00830 mol dm−3; [NO2] = [SO3] = 0.0217 mol dm−3.

Worked example: a true quadratic, where initial concentrations are unequal

H2(g) + CO2(g) ↔ H2O(g) + CO(g) has Kc = 0.640 at a given temperature. 0.300 mol dm−3 of H2(g) is mixed with 0.400 mol dm−3 of CO2(g), with no products initially. Find the equilibrium concentration of every species.

H2CO2H2OCO
initial / mol dm−30.3000.40000
change / mol dm−3−x−x+x+x
equilibrium / mol dm−30.300 − x0.400 − xxx

Because the two reactants have different initial concentrations, this time the two bracketed terms on the bottom are not identical, so the square-root shortcut cannot be used:

Kc = x2 / [(0.300 − x)(0.400 − x)] = 0.640

Expanding the denominator, (0.300 − x)(0.400 − x) = 0.120 − 0.700x + x2, so:

x2 = 0.640(0.120 − 0.700x + x2) = 0.0768 − 0.448x + 0.640x2

Collecting terms into the standard quadratic form ax2 + bx + c = 0:

0.360x2 + 0.448x − 0.0768 = 0

Applying the quadratic formula, x = [−b ± √(b2 − 4ac)] / 2a, with a = 0.360, b = 0.448, c = −0.0768:

x = [−0.448 ± √(0.4482 − 4(0.360)(−0.0768))] / (2 × 0.360) = [−0.448 ± √0.3113] / 0.720 = [−0.448 ± 0.558] / 0.720

This gives x = 0.153 or x = −1.40. The negative root is physically impossible (a concentration change cannot exceed the initial concentration available, and x must be positive here), so x = 0.153 mol dm−3 is the only valid root.

Equilibrium concentrations: [H2] = 0.300 − 0.153 = 0.147 mol dm−3; [CO2] = 0.400 − 0.153 = 0.247 mol dm−3; [H2O] = [CO] = 0.153 mol dm−3. (Check: 0.1532 / (0.147 × 0.247) = 0.0234/0.0363 = 0.640, confirming Kc.)

Choosing the physically sensible rootA quadratic always has two mathematical roots, but only one can correspond to a real chemical system: the valid root for a change in concentration must keep every equilibrium concentration between zero and its initial value. Always check both roots and discard the one that is negative or that would make an equilibrium concentration negative.
Worked example: approximation when Kc is very small

At 1000°C, Kc = 7.3 × 10−18 for 2H2O(g) ↔ 2H2(g) + O2(g). The initial [H2O] = 0.10 mol dm−3. Find the equilibrium [H2].

Let the change in [H2] = +2x and in [O2] = +x (so the change in [H2O] = −2x, matching the 2:2:1 stoichiometry). Since Kc ≪ 10−5, the forward reaction proceeds hardly at all, so the approximation [H2O]initial ≈ [H2O]equilibrium = 0.10 mol dm−3 is justified (2x is negligible compared with 0.10).

Kc = (2x)2(x) / (0.10)2 = 7.3 × 10−18

4x3 = 7.3 × 10−18 × (0.10)2 = 7.3 × 10−20, so x3 = 1.8 × 10−20 and x = 2.6 × 10−7 mol dm−3.

[H2]equilibrium = 2x = 5.3 × 10−7 mol dm−3. The approximation can be checked: subtracting 2x = 5.3 × 10−7 from 0.10 still gives 0.10 to the precision used, confirming the approximation was valid.

When the approximation is validAs a rule of thumb, if Kc < 10−5, the change in reactant concentration is negligible compared with its initial value, so [reactant]initial ≈ [reactant]equilibrium can be used to avoid solving a quadratic or higher-order equation. This situation is especially common for the dissociation of weak acids and bases.

HL: The Reaction Quotient, Q

The reaction quotient, QQ is calculated using exactly the same expression as Kc, but substituting concentrations measured at any point in time – not necessarily at equilibrium. Comparing Q with Kc at that temperature predicts the direction in which the reaction will proceed to reach equilibrium.
ComparisonDirection of net reaction
Q < KcProceeds to the right (forward), forming more products, until Q rises to equal Kc
Q = KcAlready at equilibrium; no net reaction
Q > KcProceeds to the left (backward), forming more reactants, until Q falls to equal Kc
Worked example

Kc = 1.7 × 102 at 500 K for N2(g) + 3H2(g) ↔ 2NH3(g). At a particular moment, [N2] = 1.50, [H2] = 1.00 and [NH3] = 8.00 (all mol dm−3). Is the mixture at equilibrium, and if not, which way will it shift?

Q = [NH3]2 / ([N2][H2]3) = (8.00)2 / (1.50 × (1.00)3) = 64.0 / 1.50 = 42.7

Since Q (42.7) < Kc (170), the mixture is not at equilibrium; the reaction will proceed to the right, forming more NH3, until Q rises to 170.

Worked example

Kc = 1.00 for CO(g) + H2O(g) ↔ CO2(g) + H2(g) at 298 K. At a given moment, [CO] = 2.00, [H2O] = 2.00, [CO2] = 4.00 and [H2] = 4.00 (all mol dm−3). Which way will the reaction proceed?

Q = ([CO2][H2]) / ([CO][H2O]) = (4.00 × 4.00) / (2.00 × 2.00) = 16.0 / 4.00 = 4.00

Since Q (4.00) > Kc (1.00), the reaction will proceed to the left, converting some CO2 and H2 back into CO and H2O, until Q falls to 1.00.

Q changes constantly; Kc does notUnlike Kc, which has one fixed value for a reaction at a given temperature, Q can be calculated at any moment during a reaction and generally has a different value each time, since the concentrations it uses are still changing. Q simply tracks how far the current mixture is from its equilibrium composition.

HL: Kc and Gibbs Energy – ΔG° = −RT ln K

Recall from Reactivity 1.4 that the standard Gibbs energy change, ΔG°, predicts the spontaneity of a reaction at a particular composition: ΔG° negative favours the forward reaction, ΔG° positive favours the backward reaction, and ΔG° = 0 corresponds to equilibrium. As a reaction proceeds, the total Gibbs energy of the mixture falls – whether starting from pure reactants or pure products – until it reaches a minimum value. This minimum defines the equilibrium composition: the relative amounts of reactants and products present at equilibrium depend on exactly how much lower G is for the equilibrium mixture than for the pure reactants or pure products.

Gibbs energy against the extent of reactionA curve of total Gibbs energy (vertical axis) against the extent of reaction, from pure reactants on the left to pure products on the right (horizontal axis). The curve dips down from both ends towards a single minimum somewhere in between. To the left of the minimum, the curve has a negative slope, corresponding to ΔG° negative and the forward reaction being spontaneous; to the right of the minimum, the slope is positive, corresponding to ΔG° positive and the backward reaction being spontaneous. At the minimum itself, the slope is zero (ΔG° = 0): this point is the equilibrium mixture. If the minimum sits far to the right (close to pure products), K is large and the equilibrium mixture is mostly products; if the minimum sits far to the left, K is small and the mixture is mostly reactants.

Because K and ΔG° both describe the same equilibrium position, they must be mathematically related. Thermodynamics gives this relationship directly:

ΔG° = −RT ln K

where R is the gas constant (8.31 J K−1 mol−1), T is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and ln K is the natural logarithm of the equilibrium constant. Because ΔG° is usually quoted in kJ mol−1 while R has units of J K−1 mol−1, one of the two values must be converted so that the units of energy match before using this equation.

ΔG°ln KKEquilibrium mixture
Large and negativeLarge and positiveK ≫ 1Mainly products
ZeroZeroK = 1Appreciable amounts of both reactants and products
Large and positiveLarge and negativeK ≪ 1Mainly reactants
Worked example: calculating K from ΔG°

The esterification reaction CH3COOH(aq) + C2H5OH(aq) ↔ CH3COOC2H5(aq) + H2O(aq) has ΔG° = −4.38 kJ mol−1 at 298 K. Calculate Kc for this reaction.

Converting ΔG° to J mol−1: ΔG° = −4380 J mol−1.

ln K = −ΔG° / (RT) = 4380 / (8.31 × 298) = 4380 / 2476 = 1.77

K = e1.77 = 5.86. Since K is only a little above 1, the equilibrium mixture contains appreciable, comparable amounts of both reactants and products – consistent with ΔG° being only modestly negative.

Worked example: calculating ΔG° from K

The ionic product of water, Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1.00 × 10−14 at 298 K, for H2O(l) ↔ H+(aq) + OH−(aq). Calculate ΔG° for this equilibrium at 298 K.

ΔG° = −RT ln K = −(8.31)(298) ln(1.00 × 10−14) = −(2476)(−32.2) = +79 800 J mol−1 = +79.8 kJ mol−1

The large positive ΔG° is consistent with the very small value of Kw: the autoionization of water barely proceeds, so the equilibrium mixture is overwhelmingly H2O(l) molecules, with only a tiny concentration of H+(aq) and OH−(aq) ions. This same equation, applied to the dissociation of a weak acid or base, is developed further in Reactivity 3.1.

Why ΔG° (not instantaneous ΔG) appears in this equationΔG° = −RT ln K uses the standard Gibbs energy change (reactants and products all at standard states, 100 kPa and a stated temperature) and relates it to the single, fixed value of K at that temperature. This is different from the instantaneous ΔG of a reaction mixture at some arbitrary, non-equilibrium composition, which depends on Q rather than K and becomes zero only once the mixture actually reaches equilibrium.

Guiding Question Revisited

How can the extent of a reversible reaction be influenced?
  • A dynamic equilibrium is reached in a closed system when the forward and backward rates become equal; the equilibrium position describes the relative proportion of reactants and products once this happens.
  • Le Châtelier’s principle predicts qualitatively how concentration, pressure and temperature shift the equilibrium position; a catalyst speeds up both directions equally and shifts nothing.
  • HL: the equilibrium law gives a quantitative Kc expression – products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient – for any homogeneous equilibrium; its units depend on the reaction stoichiometry and must be derived individually.
  • HL: the magnitude of Kc indicates how far a reaction proceeds (Kc ≫ 1 mostly products, Kc ≪ 1 mostly reactants), while ICE tables allow Kc to be calculated from equilibrium data, or equilibrium concentrations to be calculated from Kc and initial conditions, sometimes requiring a quadratic equation or a small-K approximation.
  • HL: only a change in temperature changes the actual value of Kc; concentration, pressure and catalysts change only the equilibrium position, re-establishing the same Kc.
  • HL: the reaction quotient, Q, uses the same expression as Kc but with concentrations at any moment in time; comparing Q to Kc predicts the direction a reaction will shift to reach equilibrium.
  • HL: ΔG° = −RT ln K links the equilibrium constant directly to thermodynamics – a large negative ΔG° corresponds to a large K and an equilibrium mixture dominated by products, while a large positive ΔG° corresponds to a small K and a mixture dominated by reactants.
