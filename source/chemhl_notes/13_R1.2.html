Guiding Question

How does application of the law of conservation of energy help us to predict energy changes during reactions?

Every chemical reaction involves breaking existing bonds in the reactants and forming new bonds in the products. Separating atoms held together by a bond requires energy, while bringing atoms together to form a new bond releases energy; the overall enthalpy change of a reaction is simply the balance between these two opposing contributions. The law of conservation of energy states that the total energy of the universe is constant: energy can be transformed from one form into another, but never created or destroyed. This chapter shows how that single law – applied to a closed loop of reactions – gives rise to Hess's law, and how Hess's law in turn lets chemists calculate enthalpy changes that could never be measured directly in the laboratory: from average bond enthalpies, from enthalpy of formation or combustion data, and, for ionic compounds, from a full Born–Haber cycle.

Energy Cycles and the Law of Conservation of Energy

A general energy cycleThree labelled boxes, A, B and C, arranged in a triangle. An arrow labelled ΔH1 points from A to B, an arrow labelled ΔH2 points from B to C, and a third arrow labelled ΔH3 points directly from A to C. The cycle shows that substance A can be converted into substance C either directly (via ΔH3) or indirectly by first forming B (via ΔH1 and then ΔH2).

Imagine travelling all the way around this cycle – from A to B, from B to C, and then back from C to A. Having returned exactly to the starting point, the net enthalpy change for the complete loop must be zero. Travelling backward from C to A is the reverse of ΔH3, so it has enthalpy change −ΔH3. This gives:

ΔH1 + ΔH2 − ΔH3 = 0    so    ΔH3 = ΔH1 + ΔH2

If the enthalpy change around a closed cycle were not zero, it would be possible to generate energy continuously simply by going around the cycle repeatedly – a perpetual motion machine, which would violate the law of conservation of energy. Because this cannot happen, the direct enthalpy change, ΔH3, can always be calculated from the enthalpy changes of an indirect, measurable route, even when the direct reaction is difficult, dangerous, or simply impossible to carry out. Energy cycles built on this idea are one of the most powerful tools in chemistry for calculating enthalpy changes indirectly, and the rest of this chapter develops four practical versions of them: bond enthalpy cycles, formation cycles, combustion cycles, and Born–Haber cycles.

Bond Enthalpy

A covalent bond exists because of the electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the positively charged nuclei of the bonded atoms. Separating two bonded atoms therefore always requires an input of energy.

Bond enthalpyThe bond enthalpy is the energy needed to break one mole of a given covalent bond in gaseous molecules, under standard conditions.
Cl2(g) → 2Cl(g)    ΔH° = +242 kJ mol−1

The enthalpy change is positive because breaking a bond is always an endothermic process; energy must be supplied to pull the two attracted atoms apart.

Why bond enthalpies are average values

In molecules containing more than two atoms, breaking apparently identical bonds one at a time can require different amounts of energy, because the local chemical environment of a bond changes as neighbouring bonds are broken. Breaking the two O−H bonds in a single water molecule, one after the other, requires different amounts of energy for each step:

H2O(g) → H(g) + OH(g)    ΔH° = +502 kJ mol−1
OH(g) → H(g) + O(g)    ΔH° = +427 kJ mol−1

To obtain a single, useful value for the strength of an O−H bond, chemists take the mean of these two values, since there are two equivalent O−H bonds overall in the original molecule: E(O−H) = (502 + 427) ÷ 2 = 464.5 kJ mol−1. This averaging is repeated over many different molecules containing the same type of bond, producing the tabulated average bond enthalpy values found in the data booklet; because they are averaged over a range of chemical environments, they generally differ somewhat from the bond enthalpy measured experimentally in any one specific molecule.

Bond enthalpies always refer to the gaseous stateAll tabulated bond enthalpies apply strictly to bonds within gaseous molecules. Any additional enthalpy changes caused by forming or breaking intermolecular forces (for example, when a substance condenses from gas to liquid) are never included in a bond enthalpy value or in a calculation based on bond enthalpies.

Bond enthalpy, bond length and multiple bonds

Multiple bonds, which involve more shared electron pairs, are generally shorter and have substantially higher bond enthalpies than single bonds between the same two elements. For example, the C=C double bond (bond enthalpy +614 kJ mol−1, bond length 134 pm) is both shorter and much stronger than the C−C single bond (+346 kJ mol−1, 154 pm). More generally, a shorter bond length between two given elements corresponds to a stronger, higher-enthalpy bond, because the shared electrons are held closer to both nuclei.

Bond enthalpy and polarity

Comparing the bond enthalpy of a polar bond, X−Y, with the average of the bond enthalpies of the corresponding non-polar bonds, X−X and Y−Y, reveals a consistent pattern.

Halogen, XAverage of E(H−H) and E(X−X) / kJ mol−1E(H−X) / kJ mol−1Difference / kJ mol−1
Cl(436 + 242) ÷ 2 = 33943192
Br(436 + 193) ÷ 2 = 314.536651.5
I(436 + 151) ÷ 2 = 293.52984.5

In every case the polar H−X bond is stronger than the simple average of the two non-polar bonds, H−H and X−X, and this extra strength is greatest for the most polar bond, H−Cl, and smallest for the least polar bond, H−I. This pattern is general: a polar bond between two different elements, X and Y, is stronger than the average of the non-polar X−X and Y−Y bonds, and the size of the extra strength increases with the polarity of the X−Y bond.

Bond-Making Is Exothermic, and Calculating ΔH from Bond Enthalpies

Exactly as much energy is released when a bond is formed as is absorbed when the same bond is broken. Bond-breaking is endothermic because it separates particles held together by an attractive force; bond-making is exothermic because it brings together particles that are mutually attracted.

H(g) + H(g) → H2(g)    ΔH° = −436 kJ mol−1
Potential energy changes during bond-breaking and bond-makingA potential energy diagram with two separated atoms, X(g) and Y(g), drawn on the left at a higher potential energy level, and the bonded molecule XY(g) drawn on the right at a lower potential energy level. An upward arrow from XY(g) to the separated atoms represents bond-breaking, labelled as requiring an input of energy; a downward arrow from the separated atoms to XY(g) represents bond-making, labelled as releasing energy. Both arrows have the same length, showing that the energy absorbed on breaking a bond exactly equals the energy released on forming it.

In a chemical reaction, the bonds in the reactants must first be broken (endothermic) before the bonds in the products can form (exothermic). The overall enthalpy change of the reaction is exothermic if the bonds formed are collectively stronger than the bonds broken, and endothermic if the bonds broken are collectively stronger than the bonds formed.

ΔH° = ΣE(bonds broken) − ΣE(bonds formed)

To apply this equation, first draw out the full structural (or Lewis) formula of every reactant and product so that every individual bond can be identified and counted; only bonds that actually change between reactants and products need to be included.

Worked example: combustion of methane

Use bond enthalpies to calculate the enthalpy of combustion of methane, CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g), given E(C−H) = 414, E(O=O) = 498, E(C=O) = 804 and E(O−H) = 463 (all kJ mol−1).

Bonds broken: 4 C−H (4 × 414 = 1656) and 2 O=O (2 × 498 = 996); total = 1656 + 996 = 2652 kJ mol−1.

Bonds formed: 2 C=O (2 × 804 = 1608) and 4 O−H (4 × 463 = 1852); total = 1608 + 1852 = 3460 kJ mol−1.

ΔH° = 2652 − 3460 = −808 kJ mol−1. This should be compared with the experimental value of −891 kJ mol−1 (measured with liquid water as the product): the difference arises because the standard enthalpy of combustion is measured with liquid water as the product, while the bond enthalpy calculation assumes the reaction occurs entirely in the gaseous state; using average, rather than exact, bond enthalpies adds a further small error.

Worked example: ethene and chlorine

Use bond enthalpies to calculate ΔH° for C2H4(g) + Cl2(g) → CH2ClCH2Cl(g), given E(C=C) = 614, E(Cl−Cl) = 242, E(C−C) = 346 and E(C−Cl) = 324 (all kJ mol−1).

Bonds broken: 1 C=C (614) and 1 Cl−Cl (242); total = 856 kJ mol−1.

Bonds formed: 1 C−C (346) and 2 C−Cl (2 × 324 = 648); total = 994 kJ mol−1.

ΔH° = 856 − 994 = −138 kJ mol−1. The four C−H bonds present in ethene are left out of the calculation entirely, since they are unaffected by the reaction and appear unchanged on both sides.

A limitation of the bond enthalpy methodBecause bond enthalpies apply only to the gaseous state, this method cannot give an accurate enthalpy change for any reaction in which a reactant or product is a liquid or solid, since it completely ignores the intermolecular forces (or ionic/metallic bonding) that would need to be broken or formed. It also relies on average bond enthalpy data, so answers calculated this way are always only close estimates of the true, experimentally measured value – the least reliable method of the three covered in this chapter, but often the only one available for a reaction that has never been carried out.

Hess's Law

Hess's lawThe enthalpy change for a chemical reaction is independent of the pathway taken between the initial and final states, provided the initial reactants and final products (and their physical states) are the same.

Hess's law is a direct consequence of the law of conservation of energy, applied to a cycle of chemical reactions in exactly the same way as the general A/B/C cycle above. Consider the formation of water from hydrogen and oxygen, which can be imagined as happening either directly, or in two steps via hydrogen peroxide:

H2(g) + O2(g) → H2O2(l)    ΔH1 = −188 kJ mol−1
H2O2(l) → H2O(l) + ½O2(g)    ΔH2 = −98 kJ mol−1
H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l)    ΔH3 = −286 kJ mol−1

Adding the first two equations and cancelling the H2O2(l) and the extra ½O2(g) that appear on both sides reproduces the third equation exactly, and ΔH1 + ΔH2 = −188 + (−98) = −286 kJ mol−1, which is indeed equal to ΔH3.

Reversing or scaling a reaction in a cycleIf a thermochemical equation must be flipped (written in the opposite direction) to fit into a cycle, the sign of its enthalpy change must also be reversed: if X → Y has ΔH = +50 kJ mol−1, then Y → X has ΔH = −50 kJ mol−1. If an equation must also be multiplied by some factor to balance the cycle, its enthalpy change must be multiplied by the same factor.

The real power of Hess's law is that it allows the enthalpy change of a reaction to be calculated even when that reaction cannot be carried out directly in the laboratory – because it is too slow, too dangerous, or because the elements involved do not react together directly to give a clean product. The only requirement is that a cycle of other, measurable reactions can be constructed connecting the same starting reactants to the same final products; the individual steps may even be entirely hypothetical, as long as each equation balances correctly.

Worked example: formation of sulfur dioxide

Calculate ΔH° for S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g), given: (1) S(s) + 1½O2(g) → SO3(g), ΔH° = −395 kJ mol−1; (2) SO2(g) + ½O2(g) → SO3(g), ΔH° = −98 kJ mol−1.

Equation (1) already starts from the correct reactant, S(s). Equation (2) must be reversed so that SO2(g) appears as a product, which also reverses the sign of its enthalpy change: SO3(g) → SO2(g) + ½O2(g), ΔH° = +98 kJ mol−1. Adding this to equation (1) and cancelling SO3(g) and ½O2(g), which appear on both sides, leaves S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g), with ΔH° = −395 + 98 = −297 kJ mol−1.

Worked example: formation of carbon monoxide

Calculate ΔH° for C(graphite) + ½O2(g) → CO(g), given: C(graphite) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −394 kJ mol−1, and CO(g) + ½O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −283 kJ mol−1.

Both given equations produce CO2(g), which does not appear in the target equation, so the second equation must be reversed: CO2(g) → CO(g) + ½O2(g), ΔH° = +283 kJ mol−1. Adding this to the first equation and cancelling CO2(g) and ½O2(g) from both sides gives C(graphite) + ½O2(g) → CO(g), ΔH° = −394 + 283 = −111 kJ mol−1. The direct combustion of carbon to carbon monoxide, rather than carbon dioxide, is very difficult to control and measure experimentally – a good example of Hess's law in action.

Standard Enthalpy Changes of Formation and Combustion

Standard enthalpy change of formation, ΔHf°The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is formed from its elements in their standard states, under standard conditions. The standard enthalpy of formation of any element in its standard (most stable) state is zero, since no chemical change occurs when an element is formed from itself.
Standard enthalpy change of combustion, ΔHc°The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance burns completely in excess oxygen, under standard conditions. Combustion reactions are generally exothermic, so ΔHc° values are generally negative.
Worked example

Which of the following has a standard enthalpy change of formation of zero at 298 K: H(g), Hg(s), C(diamond), or Si(s)?

ΔHf° is zero only for an element in its standard (most stable) state. Hydrogen's standard state is H2(g), not H(g); mercury's standard state is Hg(l), not Hg(s); carbon's standard state is C(graphite), and diamond is a less stable allotrope. Silicon's standard state at 298 K and 100 kPa is indeed Si(s), so Si(s) is the correct answer.

Note that, because allotropes of the same element can have different stabilities, they can also have different (non-zero) enthalpies of formation relative to the true standard state – C(diamond) has ΔHf° = +1.9 kJ mol−1, reflecting that it is very slightly less stable than C(graphite).

Calculating ΔH Using Enthalpy of Formation Data

A formation cycleA triangular cycle with reactants at the top left and products at the top right, connected by a horizontal arrow labelled ΔH°. A third species, the elements in their standard states, is positioned at the bottom of the triangle. A diagonal arrow leads down from the reactants to the elements, labelled ΣΔHf°(reactants); a second diagonal arrow leads down from the products to the elements, labelled ΣΔHf°(products). Because both reactants and products can be traced back to the same set of elements, the top (direct) route can be found by combining the two diagonal (formation) routes.

Since ΣΔHf°(reactants) + ΔH° = ΣΔHf°(products), rearranging gives the general expression:

ΔH° = ΣΔHf°(products) − ΣΔHf°(reactants)
Worked example

Calculate the standard enthalpy of combustion of propane, C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l), given ΔHf°(C3H8) = −105 kJ mol−1, ΔHf°(CO2) = −394 kJ mol−1, ΔHf°(H2O(l)) = −286 kJ mol−1.

ΣΔHf°(products) = 3(−394) + 4(−286) = −1182 + (−1144) = −2326 kJ mol−1

ΣΔHf°(reactants) = (−105) + 5(0) = −105 kJ mol−1 (oxygen, an element in its standard state, contributes zero)

ΔH° = (−2326) − (−105) = −2221 kJ mol−1, in good agreement with the literature value of about −2220 kJ mol−1.

Calculating ΔH Using Enthalpy of Combustion Data

A combustion cycleA triangular cycle with reactants at the top left and products at the top right, connected by a horizontal arrow labelled ΔH°. A third species, the common combustion products of both the reactants and the products (usually CO2(g) and H2O(l)), is positioned at the bottom of the triangle. A diagonal arrow leads down from the reactants to this bottom species, labelled ΣΔHc°(reactants); a second diagonal arrow leads down from the products to the same bottom species, labelled ΣΔHc°(products). Both reactants and products are combusted to reach the same final state at the bottom, which is why the diagonal arrows point away from reactants and products this time, rather than towards them as in a formation cycle.

Since ΔH° + ΣΔHc°(products) = ΣΔHc°(reactants), rearranging gives the general expression:

ΔH° = ΣΔHc°(reactants) − ΣΔHc°(products)
The single most common error in this topic: do not mix up the two cyclesThe formation-cycle expression is ΔH° = ΣΔHf°(products) − ΣΔHf°(reactants) – products minus reactants. The combustion-cycle expression is the other way round: ΔH° = ΣΔHc°(reactants) − ΣΔHc°(products) – reactants minus products. The sign flip exists because a formation cycle’s diagonal arrows point from the elements up to the reactants and products, while a combustion cycle’s diagonal arrows point from the reactants and products down to the combustion products; reversing the direction of an arrow in a Hess cycle always reverses the sign of the term it contributes. Using the wrong expression with the wrong type of data is the most frequent error made in this entire topic.
Worked example: enthalpy of formation of propane from combustion data

Calculate ΔHf° for 3C(graphite) + 4H2(g) → C3H8(g), given ΔHc°[C(graphite)] = −394 kJ mol−1, ΔHc°[H2(g)] = −286 kJ mol−1, ΔHc°[C3H8(g)] = −2220 kJ mol−1.

Here the ‘reactants’ of the target equation are 3C(graphite) + 4H2(g), and the ‘product’ is C3H8(g); all three combust to give the same products, CO2(g) and H2O(l), so a combustion cycle applies.

ΣΔHc°(reactants) = 3(−394) + 4(−286) = −1182 + (−1144) = −2326 kJ mol−1

ΣΔHc°(products) = −2220 kJ mol−1

ΔHf°(C3H8) = (−2326) − (−2220) = −106 kJ mol−1, in good agreement with the literature value of −105 kJ mol−1, even though propane does not form directly and measurably from carbon and hydrogen.

Worked example: enthalpy of formation of benzene from combustion data

Calculate ΔHf° for 6C(graphite) + 3H2(g) → C6H6(l), given ΔHc°[C(graphite)] = −394 kJ mol−1, ΔHc°[H2(g)] = −286 kJ mol−1, ΔHc°[C6H6(l)] = −3268 kJ mol−1.

ΣΔHc°(reactants) = 6(−394) + 3(−286) = −2364 + (−858) = −3222 kJ mol−1

ΔHf°(C6H6) = ΣΔHc°(reactants) − ΣΔHc°(products) = (−3222) − (−3268) = +46 kJ mol−1, close to the literature value of +49 kJ mol−1. Note that ΔHf° of benzene is positive: benzene is less stable than its elements, a direct consequence of its unusual, highly delocalized ring structure.

Born–Haber Cycles: Lattice Enthalpy and the Formation of Ionic Compounds

An ionic compound can, in principle, be formed from its elements in a series of separate, well-defined steps, each with its own measurable enthalpy change. A Born–Haber cycle is a Hess's law cycle built from exactly these steps, and its main use is to calculate the one step that cannot be measured directly: the lattice enthalpy.

The enthalpy terms in a Born–Haber cycle
  • Enthalpy of atomization, ΔHatom°: the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state, e.g. Na(s) → Na(g), or ½Cl2(g) → Cl(g). For a metal this is closely related to its enthalpy of sublimation; for a diatomic gas it is half the relevant bond enthalpy. Always endothermic.
  • Ionization energy, ΔHIE°: the enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is removed from one mole of gaseous atoms (or ions): M(g) → M+(g) + e−. Always endothermic.
  • Electron affinity, ΔHEA°: the enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is added to one mole of gaseous atoms (or ions): X(g) + e− → X−(g). A first electron affinity is usually exothermic (the electron is attracted to the positive nucleus); a second electron affinity, adding an electron to an already negative ion, is always endothermic, since the incoming electron is repelled by the ion’s existing negative charge.
  • Lattice enthalpy, ΔHlatt°: the enthalpy change when one mole of a solid ionic compound is separated into its gaseous ions: MX(s) → M+(g) + X−(g). Always endothermic, since it involves pulling apart oppositely charged ions held together by strong electrostatic attraction. (The reverse process, gaseous ions coming together to form the solid lattice, is correspondingly strongly exothermic.)
  • Enthalpy of formation, ΔHf°: the enthalpy change when one mole of the compound is formed directly from its elements in their standard states.
Worked example: the Born–Haber cycle for sodium chloride

The formation of sodium chloride, Na(s) + ½Cl2(g) → NaCl(s), ΔHf° = −411 kJ mol−1, can be broken down into five measurable steps.

(1) Na(s) → Na(g)   ΔHatom°(Na) = +107 kJ mol−1

(2) Na(g) → Na+(g) + e−   IE1(Na) = +496 kJ mol−1

(3) ½Cl2(g) → Cl(g)   ΔHatom°(Cl) = +121 kJ mol−1 (half the Cl−Cl bond enthalpy, 242 ÷ 2)

(4) Cl(g) + e− → Cl−(g)   EA1(Cl) = −349 kJ mol−1

(5) Na+(g) + Cl−(g) → NaCl(s)   −ΔHlatt°(NaCl) = ?

Energy level diagram for the Born–Haber cycle of NaClA potential energy diagram with several horizontal levels, from lowest to highest reading up the left-hand side. The lowest level, at the bottom, is labelled NaCl(s). Immediately above it, a short rise leads up through the sequence Na(s) + ½Cl2(g), then Na(g) + ½Cl2(g) (after atomization of sodium), then Na(g) + Cl(g) (after atomization of chlorine), then Na+(g) + e− + Cl(g) (after ionization of sodium), and finally Na+(g) + Cl−(g) at the very top (after the exothermic electron affinity step drops it slightly, it is still the highest level overall). A single long arrow connects this highest level straight back down to NaCl(s) at the bottom, representing the large, exothermic lattice enthalpy of formation that closes the cycle. A second arrow connects Na(s) + ½Cl2(g) directly to NaCl(s), labelled ΔHf° = −411 kJ mol−1, representing the same overall change by the direct route.

By Hess's law, the sum of the five steps must equal the overall enthalpy of formation:

ΔHf°(NaCl) = ΔHatom°(Na) + IE1(Na) + ΔHatom°(Cl) + EA1(Cl) + ΔHlatt°(formation)

−411 = (+107) + (+496) + (+121) + (−349) + ΔHlatt°(formation)

−411 = 375 + ΔHlatt°(formation), so ΔHlatt°(formation) = −411 − 375 = −786 kJ mol−1

This is the enthalpy change of step (5), Na+(g) + Cl−(g) → NaCl(s): strongly exothermic, as expected for oppositely charged ions coming together. Lattice enthalpy is usually quoted for the reverse, endothermic direction (NaCl(s) → Na+(g) + Cl−(g)), so the lattice enthalpy of sodium chloride is reported as ΔHlatt°(NaCl) = +786 kJ mol−1, close to the accepted value of about +787 kJ mol−1.

HL: A Second Born–Haber Cycle – Magnesium Oxide, and the Factors Affecting Lattice Enthalpy

Compounds containing ions of charge greater than 1+ or 1− need extra steps in their Born–Haber cycle: a second ionization energy for a 2+ cation, and a second (always endothermic) electron affinity for a 2− anion.

Worked example: the Born–Haber cycle for magnesium oxide

The formation of magnesium oxide, Mg(s) + ½O2(g) → MgO(s), ΔHf° = −602 kJ mol−1, requires seven steps.

Mg(s) → Mg(g), ΔHatom°(Mg) = +148 kJ mol−1

Mg(g) → Mg+(g) + e−, IE1(Mg) = +738 kJ mol−1

Mg+(g) → Mg2+(g) + e−, IE2(Mg) = +1451 kJ mol−1

½O2(g) → O(g), ΔHatom°(O) = +249 kJ mol−1 (half the O=O bond enthalpy, 498 ÷ 2)

O(g) + e− → O−(g), EA1(O) = −141 kJ mol−1 (exothermic: an electron is attracted into a neutral atom)

O−(g) + e− → O2−(g), EA2(O) = +753 kJ mol−1 (strongly endothermic: a second electron must be forced onto an ion that is already negatively charged)

Mg2+(g) + O2−(g) → MgO(s), ΔHlatt°(formation) = ?

ΔHf°(MgO) = ΔHatom°(Mg) + IE1(Mg) + IE2(Mg) + ΔHatom°(O) + EA1(O) + EA2(O) + ΔHlatt°(formation)

−602 = (+148) + (+738) + (+1451) + (+249) + (−141) + (+753) + ΔHlatt°(formation)

−602 = 3198 + ΔHlatt°(formation), so ΔHlatt°(formation) = −602 − 3198 = −3800 kJ mol−1

The lattice enthalpy of magnesium oxide (reported in the usual, endothermic dissociation direction) is therefore about +3800 kJ mol−1 – nearly five times larger than that of sodium chloride (+786 kJ mol−1).

Factors affecting lattice enthalpyLattice enthalpy is proportional to the product of the ionic charges and inversely proportional to the sum of the ionic radii of the ions involved (following directly from Coulomb's law for the electrostatic attraction between point charges). Magnesium oxide has a far larger lattice enthalpy than sodium chloride because: (i) Mg2+ and O2− each carry twice the charge of Na+ and Cl−, so the product of the charges is four times larger, and (ii) Mg2+ and O2− are both smaller ions than Na+ and Cl−, placing the opposite charges closer together. Both effects increase the electrostatic attraction, and hence the lattice enthalpy, substantially.
Experimental versus theoretical lattice enthalpiesThe value obtained from a Born–Haber cycle is an experimental lattice enthalpy, built entirely from other measured quantities. A separate, theoretical lattice enthalpy can be calculated directly from the ionic model (treating the ions as point charges with no covalent character at all). For a compound such as NaCl, where the ions are a good match for the purely ionic model, the two values agree closely; for a compound where the bonding has significant covalent character (for example, where a small, highly charged cation polarizes a large anion), the experimental value is noticeably larger in magnitude than the theoretical value, since the extra covalent interaction provides additional stabilization that the purely ionic model does not account for.

Guiding Question Revisited

How does application of the law of conservation of energy help us to predict energy changes during reactions?
  • Every reaction involves breaking bonds in the reactants (endothermic) and forming new bonds in the products (exothermic); the overall enthalpy change is the balance between these two contributions, ΔH° = ΣE(bonds broken) − ΣE(bonds formed).
  • Average bond enthalpies are averaged over many similar molecules and always refer to bonds in the gaseous state, so calculations based on them are estimates rather than exact experimental values, and cannot account for intermolecular forces in liquids or solids.
  • Hess's law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken between the same initial and final states; it is a direct consequence of the law of conservation of energy, since any closed energy cycle must have a total enthalpy change of zero. Reversing a reaction in a cycle reverses the sign of its ΔH; scaling a reaction scales its ΔH by the same factor.
  • ΔH° can be calculated from enthalpy of formation data using ΔH° = ΣΔHf°(products) − ΣΔHf°(reactants), or from enthalpy of combustion data using the reversed expression ΔH° = ΣΔHc°(reactants) − ΣΔHc°(products); confusing the two is the most common error in this topic.
  • A Born–Haber cycle is a specialized Hess's law cycle for an ionic compound, built from the enthalpy of atomization, ionization energy, electron affinity and enthalpy of formation, used to calculate the one enthalpy change that cannot be measured directly: the lattice enthalpy.
  • Lattice enthalpy increases with the product of the ionic charges and decreases as the ionic radii increase, which is why magnesium oxide (2+/2− ions, small radii) has a much larger lattice enthalpy than sodium chloride (1+/1− ions, larger radii); of the three methods for predicting ΔH in this chapter, data based on ΔHf° or ΔHc° is more accurate than the bond enthalpy method, since the latter applies only to gaseous species and uses averaged values.
