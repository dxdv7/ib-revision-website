Introduction to Viruses

All living organisms (eukaryotes and prokaryotes) have nucleic acids that make up their genomes. Viruses also contain nucleic acids, but they require living cells to reproduce — they do not have ribosomes or the metabolic enzymes needed for protein synthesis. This makes viruses obligate parasites: they can only reproduce inside a host cell.

Key definitionsVirus: a non-cellular parasite of animals, plants and bacteria that consists of nucleic acid surrounded by a protein coat (a capsid). Parasite: an organism that lives on or in another organism (its host) for most of its life cycle, deriving nutrients from its host. Micro-organism: a living organism that cannot be seen by the naked eye, such as bacteria, protists and single-celled fungi (note: viruses are not classed as living organisms).

Viruses infect all cellular organisms and are highly diverse in shape and structure. Unlike LUCA (the last universal common ancestor) for cellular organisms, there is no presumed single common ancestor for viruses. One method of classifying viruses is based on the genome they contain: the nucleic acid present is either DNA or RNA, which may be single- or double-stranded, and may have positive or negative polarity.

Common mistakeA virus is not a cell. A virus is considered ‘non-cellular’, meaning that no part of it is cellular.

Top tip! Viruses are not part of the Linnaean system of classification or the three-domain system, since they are not considered to be cellular living organisms.

Nature of science — observations and science as a shared endeavour: viruses were first observed directly in 1931, when electron microscopes were developed, but their existence had already been inferred from careful observation. Fine filters could remove bacteria from infected tissue, yet the filtered tissue remained infective, showing that an infectious agent smaller than bacteria was responsible. In 1915 Frederick Twort discovered bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) after observing clear spots within bacterial colonies. Edward Jenner (smallpox vaccine, 1798) and Louis Pasteur (rabies vaccine) developed vaccines before viruses were even known to exist. Since 1966 the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) has maintained a universal taxonomic scheme for viruses, using a modified version of the Linnaean system that starts at order and goes down to species — for example HIV is placed in the family Retroviridae because it is a retrovirus containing RNA and reverse transcriptase.

Structural Features Common to Viruses

Concept: UnityAll viruses share the following common structural features: a small, fixed size range of 20–400 nm in diameter; nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) as genetic material; a capsid made of protein that surrounds the nucleic acid; no cytoplasm; and few or even no enzymes.
Common mistakeViruses do not have a nucleus or a nucleoid — these terms apply only to eukaryotes and prokaryotes respectively. A virus simply has a viral genome, consisting of RNA or DNA.

Diversity of Structure in Viruses

Concept: DiversityViruses are highly diverse in their shape and structure. Genetic material may be RNA or DNA, which can be either single- or double-stranded. Some viruses are enveloped in host cell membrane and others are not enveloped.

Viruses differ from each other in host range (the number of species and cell types they can infect), type of genome, structure, and method of replication.

The Viral Genome

DNA viruses include adenoviruses (a cause of eye disease) and most bacteriophages (viruses that parasitize bacteria). RNA viruses include HIV and coronaviruses. Because the genetic code is shared between viruses and living organisms, host cells can transcribe viral DNA into mRNA and translate it into protein.

Positive-sense vs negative-sense RNAPositive-sense RNA is viral RNA with the same base sequence as mRNA, so it can be translated immediately by host ribosomes (e.g. SARS-CoV-2). Negative-sense RNA is complementary to viral mRNA, so it cannot be translated directly — it must first be converted into positive-sense RNA by RNA polymerase before translation can occur (e.g. influenza viruses).

Nucleic acid replication location also varies: in SARS-CoV-2 it occurs entirely in the cytoplasm, whereas influenza viruses replicate their RNA in the nucleus. In some viruses, such as HIV, RNA acts as a template for synthesizing viral DNA in the cytoplasm using the viral enzyme reverse transcriptase.

The viral genome may be linear (e.g. coronaviruses) or circular (e.g. hepatitis B); single-stranded (e.g. hepatitis E virus and coronaviruses) or double-stranded (e.g. HIV and SV-40). Some viruses have more than one copy of their genome (e.g. HIV and influenza viruses).

The genomes of simple viruses, such as bacteriophage Qbeta, may be only a few thousand nucleotides long and contain just four genes. More complex viruses contain many more genes: bacteriophage T4 has 289 genes, largely involved in building its complex capsid. Rapid replication is favoured by a small genome size — the smaller the genome, the faster it can replicate. Viruses with prokaryotic hosts tend to replicate especially rapidly, reflected in compact genomes with overlapping genes.

Figure A2.3.3: Coloured TEM of T4 bacteriophageThe micrograph shows the T4 bacteriophage, which has a large, complex icosahedral (polyhedral) head containing its DNA, connected to a tail structure used to attach to and inject DNA into a host bacterium. T4’s comparatively large genome (289 genes) is reflected in the structural complexity of its capsid.

Top tip! There is a roughly inverse relationship between genome size and dependence on the host for essential functions in DNA replication — the smaller the viral genome, the more the virus depends on host-cell machinery.

Capsid

Capsid and capsomereCapsid: the protein coat of a virus, which encloses the viral genome. Capsomere: one of the individual protein subunits that make up a viral capsid.

Capsids come in a variety of shapes: helical (e.g. tobacco mosaic virus, TMV), conical (e.g. HIV) and polyhedral (e.g. adenovirus). Some viruses carry viral enzyme molecules within their capsids.

Figure A2.3.4: The tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), which infects the tobacco plantThe figure shows the helical capsid of TMV: a protein coat made of polypeptide building blocks arranged in a spiral around a central canal that contains a single strand of RNA (running from its 3’ to 5’ end). A transmission electron micrograph (negatively stained, ×40 000) shows the hollow tube construction of the virus in side view and end view, alongside an enlarged drawing of part of the virus. The figure also contrasts a healthy tobacco leaf with an infected leaf showing the characteristic mosaic pattern of disease.

Some of the most complex capsids are found in bacteriophages. Phage capsids have elongated icosahedral heads enclosing their genome, built from equilateral triangles fused together into a 20-sided (icosahedral) spherical shape — the optimal way to form a closed shell from identical protein subunits. Joined to the head is a tail shaft with fibres used to attach to the bacterial cell wall.

Figure A2.3.5: Unfolded two-dimensional (left) and folded three-dimensional (right) icosahedronThe figure shows a flat net of 20 identical equilateral triangular faces on the left, and the same net folded into a closed, roughly spherical 20-sided solid (icosahedron) on the right — illustrating how a capsid can be built as a closed shell from many identical protein subunits.

Envelope

Envelope, nucleocapsid, virionEnvelope: a membrane, typically derived from the host cell plasma membrane, studded with viral glycoproteins. Nucleocapsid: the capsid of a virus that has an envelope. Virion: an isolated but infectious virus particle found outside the host cell.

Viruses can be classified as enveloped (e.g. HIV, influenza viruses and coronaviruses) or non-enveloped (e.g. bacteriophages and poliovirus). Enveloped viruses typically contain host cell phospholipids embedded with virally encoded spike glycoproteins — usually taken from the host plasma membrane, although coronaviruses take their envelope from internal host cell membranes instead. The envelope protects the virion from enzymes and other chemicals, giving enveloped viruses an advantage over non-enveloped virions. Glycoproteins on the envelope help the virus bind to receptor molecules on the host cell surface, enabling recognition and possible uptake of the virion into the cell.

Nature of science — patterns and trends: for many years, viruses could only be studied by observing the effects of virus-infected extracts on living animals. An approximate measure of infectivity could be estimated by diluting an extract and testing how far it could be diluted while still causing infection.

Viral Reproductive Life Cycles

Virus particles cannot carry out life processes independently, so they rely on the mechanisms of the host cell for energy supply, nutrition, protein synthesis and other functions. The general stages of viral reproduction are:

  • attachment (or adsorption) to host cells
  • entry (or penetration)
  • replication of the viral genome
  • transcription of viral mRNA and protein synthesis using host cell ribosomes
  • maturation and assembly of new viruses, followed by release from the host cell
Common mistakeA virus cannot move independently. Viruses move passively, carried through phloem, blood, other body fluids or the air. When a virus reaches a host cell, it binds to receptors on the cell surface, which causes the virus particle to be drawn into the cell.

Bacteriophage Lambda

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that infect bacteria. They played a central role in the development of molecular biology, particularly our understanding of gene structure and expression, and are used in the laboratory as vectors for cloned DNA. Bacteriophages are highly specific to the bacteria they infect: bacteriophage lambda only infects E. coli. The wild type has a temperate life cycle, meaning it can either persist in the bacterial genome via lysogeny, or enter a lytic cycle that kills the host cell.

Figure A2.3.6: Structure of bacteriophage lambdaThe diagram shows an icosahedral capsid (head) containing double-stranded linear DNA, connected via a collar to a tail structure consisting of a sheath and core, ending in a conical part with a tail fibre used for attachment to the host bacterium.

Lytic Cycle

Key termsLytic: a phage life cycle in which a phage attaches to a host bacterium, injects its DNA which undergoes replication to form new virions, which then lyse the cell. Lysis: breakdown, typically of cells. Virulent: the ability of a virus (or bacterium) to cause rapid and severe disease.
Stages of the lytic cycle1. The tail fibre of bacteriophage lambda binds to specific receptors on the surface of E. coli (attachment). 2. The phage injects its double-stranded DNA genome into the host cell (penetration); the protein capsid is left outside. 3. The viral DNA enters the bacterium and remains as a separate molecule, replicating independently of the host bacterial DNA. 4. The bacterial DNA is broken down (DNA hydrolysis) while phage DNA is replicated (biosynthesis). 5. Viral genes are expressed using host enzymes to produce viral proteins, which self-assemble into mature bacteriophage lambda virions (maturation). 6. Lysis of the bacterial cell wall releases the new virus particles, killing the host cell.
Figure A2.3.7: An overview of the lytic cycle of a virusA six-step diagram: (1) the phage attaches to the surface of the bacterium; (2) the phage is adsorbed onto the bacterium and injects its DNA; (3) the viral DNA enters the bacterium; (4) phage DNA is replicated while bacterial DNA is hydrolysed; (5) phage proteins are made and assembled into many new phage particles; (6) the cell lyses (bursts) and the newly made phage particles are released.

The lytic cycle results in the destruction of the infected cell and its membrane. Bacteriophages that only use the lytic cycle are called virulent phages.

Lysogenic Cycle

Key termsLysogenic: a phage life cycle in which a phage attaches to a bacterium and injects its genome, but does not undergo a full replication cycle; instead it becomes resident within the bacterial host, maintained in a dormant state. Prophage: a bacteriophage in an inactive state in which the genome is typically integrated into the chromosome of the bacterial host.
Stages of the lysogenic cycle1. The phage infects the cell by injecting its DNA. 2. The phage DNA becomes incorporated into the host genome, forming a prophage and altering the genotype of the bacterium (a bacterial host carrying a prophage is called a lysogen). 3. When the bacterial chromosome is copied as the cell divides, the phage’s DNA is also replicated and passed to daughter cells. 4. Under stressful conditions, the phage DNA is excised from the bacterial chromosome and enters the lytic cycle. 5. Phage DNA replicates and phage proteins are made; new phage particles are assembled. 6. The cell lyses, releasing the new phages.
Figure A2.3.8: An overview of the lysogenic cycle of a virusThe diagram follows the same six numbered stages described above, noting that the final two stages (5 and 6) are shared with, and identical to, the lytic cycle — i.e. once induced out of dormancy, a lysogenic infection proceeds through the lytic pathway to release new phage.

In the lysogenic cycle, unlike the lytic cycle, the viral DNA does not remain separate but is incorporated into the host cell’s DNA. The bacteriophage’s DNA then replicates together with the E. coli host DNA whenever the host replicates, and because the prophage carries genes, it can confer novel properties on the host. Bacteria grown in rich medium contain high levels of proteases (protein-degrading enzymes essential for completing viral infection) and are more likely to support lytic replication when infected; poorly growing bacteria have lower protease levels, which favours establishment of lysogeny instead.

Top tip! Bacteriophages that can adopt either a lytic or a lysogenic replication cycle are termed temperate phages.

Lytic vs lysogenic cycleIn the lytic cycle, viral DNA remains separate from host DNA, host DNA is degraded, and the host cell is always destroyed by lysis. In the lysogenic cycle, viral DNA integrates into the host chromosome as a prophage, host DNA is not degraded, and the host cell survives and reproduces normally (passing the prophage to daughter cells) until induction triggers entry into the lytic cycle.

Nature of science — experiments: if a bacteriophage infects bacteria spread on an agar plate, phages released from an individual infected cell can only infect neighbouring bacteria, producing localized clearings of the bacterial lawn called plaques. Counting plaques allows a biologist to estimate the concentration of bacteriophage particles in a suspension (the phage titre), assuming the number of plaques corresponds to the number of phage particles present.

MOI (multiplicity of infection) = number of infectious virus particles ÷ number of target cells present

The multiplicity of infection (MOI) is one factor that determines whether a virus enters the lytic cycle or the lysogenic (dormant) phase. For example, to achieve an MOI of 0.5 using virus particles at a concentration of 2 × 109 cm−3 and bacteria at a concentration of 8 × 108 cm−3, in 0.25 cm3 of bacteria, a scientist would first calculate the number of target bacterial cells present (8 × 108 × 0.25 = 2 × 108 cells), then the number of virus particles needed for an MOI of 0.5 (0.5 × 2 × 108 = 1 × 108 particles), and finally the volume of virus suspension required to supply that many particles at the given concentration.

Evidence for Several Origins of Viruses from Other Organisms

There are many different types of virus depending on the cells they infect, and it seems unlikely that all known viruses share a single common ancestor. Viruses are therefore considered polyphyletic in origin, meaning they have arisen from a few independent origins, almost certainly at different times from cellular organisms. Three main hypotheses have been proposed to explain the evolutionary origin of viruses.

The virus-first hypothesisViruses evolved before, or co-evolved with, their current host cells. Bits of RNA with self-complementary sequences may have folded like a protein, gradually becoming more complex until they could self-replicate. They then began infecting other cells, becoming viruses.
Figure A2.3.9: The escaped genes (progressive) hypothesis for the origin of virusesThe diagram shows a single-celled organism containing RNA and a protein-building structure, enclosed by a protein coat. Unequal cell division produces two mini-cells: one lacks the protein-building structure and cannot survive independently, but the other survives because of its protein coat. This surviving entity — now a virus — is able to replicate itself and infect new organisms.

The escaped genes (progressive) hypothesis suggests that viruses arose from genetic elements (such as transposons, repetitive DNA sequences that can move between chromosomes in eukaryotes) that gained the ability to move between cells.

Figure A2.3.10: The regressive (reduction) hypothesis for the origin of virusesThe diagram shows a single-celled organism (with RNA and a protein-building structure) forming a mutualistic relationship with another organism. Over time, the smaller organism loses its protein-building structures, becoming dependent on its partner, but retains the ability to replicate itself and infect new organisms — it has become a virus.

The regressive (or reduction) hypothesis suggests that viruses are remnants of cellular organisms that have lost non-essential structures through their dependence on a host.

Both the escaped genes and regressive hypotheses assume that cells existed before viruses, but some researchers propose that viruses may have been among the first replicating entities, appearing before LUCA. There is huge diversity among viruses, with no single gene shared by all of them. It has been argued that precursors of a group called nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDV) — which have large genomes containing genes for DNA repair, DNA replication, transcription and translation, and which replicate in both the host nucleus and cytoplasm — may have been involved in the emergence of eukaryotic cells; it is hypothesised that the eukaryotic nucleus arose through endosymbiosis of a complex, enveloped DNA virus into an ancestral eukaryotic cell. Some researchers hypothesise that NCLDV viruses themselves evolved through a regressive process, in which their ancestral cells lost key genes and adopted an obligate parasitic replication strategy; this irreversible loss of genes may have been a dominant force in viral evolution.

No single hypothesis may explain the origin of all viruses. Retroviruses probably arose through a progressive process (mobile genetic elements gaining the ability to travel between cells), while large DNA viruses probably arose through a regressive process (once-independent entities losing essential genes and adopting parasitism). Since all viruses share an extreme form of obligate parasitism on host cells, the structural features they have in common could be regarded as a form of convergent evolution, in which species occupying similar ecological niches adapt in similar ways to similar selective pressures, producing ‘analogous structures’.

Rapid Evolution in Viruses

The evolution rate of a virus can be defined as the number of nucleotide substitutions per nucleotide site, per year. This rate is affected by mutation, recombination, large population size and a rapid life cycle.

Mutation in Viruses

Mutations are a natural by-product of viral replication, occurring at a frequency determined by the error rate of the replication enzymes (adjusted by any proofreading that occurs). Whether a mutant succeeds depends on whether it confers a selective advantage: mutations that improve replication, transmission, or the ability to evade the host immune system increase in frequency, while those that reduce viral fitness are removed from the population. RNA viruses tend to have a much higher mutation rate than DNA viruses because errors in copying their RNA genomes are not corrected by proofreading. Coronaviruses are an exception among RNA viruses, having a lower mutation rate because they produce an RNA polymerase that corrects some replication errors. Even so, viruses generally evolve faster than their host cells, giving them an advantage in overcoming the host immune response.

Case Study: Influenza

There are three types of influenza (flu) virus: A, B and C. Influenza is an enveloped virus with a genome of negative-sense, single-stranded, segmented RNA. It infects the epithelial cells of the respiratory tract (and, in birds, the intestinal tract). Virions recognize target cells through complementary binding between haemagglutinin (a spike protein in the viral envelope) and specific sialic acid receptors on the host cell membrane.

Antigenic drift vs antigenic shiftAntigenic drift consists of small mutations in influenza genes that gradually change the virus’s surface proteins, haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), over time as the virus replicates — leading to surface antigens no longer recognized by the immune system, and so to disease. Antigenic shift is a sudden, major change in an influenza A virus, producing new HA and/or NA proteins and potentially a new influenza A subtype; it results from recombination, in which genetic material from two different virus strains that have co-infected the same host cell is combined to produce new combinations.
Figure A2.3.11: Conceptual illustration of antigenic drift and antigenic shiftThe diagram contrasts antigenic drift, shown as gradual random mutation producing different strains within the same subtype of influenza A, with antigenic shift, shown as genetic shuffling (reassortment) between different subtypes of influenza A co-infecting the same cell, giving rise to an entirely new influenza A subtype.

Antigenic shift can occur if an influenza virus from an animal population gains the ability to infect humans; such animal-origin viruses may carry HA/NA combinations different enough from existing human viruses that most people have no immunity to the novel virus.

Case Study: HIV

Retrovirus, reverse transcriptase, provirusRetrovirus: a virus that has RNA as its nucleic acid and uses the enzyme reverse transcriptase to copy its genome into the DNA of the host cell’s chromosomes. Reverse transcriptase: an enzyme found in HIV and other retroviruses that converts viral RNA into viral DNA (reverse transcription). Provirus: a virus that has integrated into a host’s genome and is replicated whenever the cell replicates its DNA.

Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) is caused by HIV, a retrovirus. After entering the cytoplasm of a host cell, HIV’s RNA is converted into DNA by reverse transcriptase; the resulting viral DNA is then integrated into the host cell nucleus’s DNA to form a provirus.

Figure A2.3.12: The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)The diagram shows an enveloped, roughly spherical virion. The outer lipid membrane (envelope) is studded with glycoprotein spikes. Inside, a conical protein capsid encloses the RNA genome together with viral enzymes, including reverse transcriptase and protease.

HIV has a high mutation rate because reverse transcriptase lacks a proofreading mechanism; AIDS patients often carry many genetically distinct variants of HIV, different from the original infecting virus. This is a major problem for treatment: antiviral drugs that block a single essential viral enzyme work only temporarily, as drug-resistant strains arise rapidly by mutation. However, combinations of drugs (typically three) that target different viral proteins are highly effective at inhibiting the virus and preventing replication. The population size of HIV within a host is the total number of infectious proviruses integrated into cellular DNA at a given time; high mutation rates combined with short generation times and large population sizes allow HIV to evolve rapidly and adapt to its host.

Nature of science — falsificationThe central dogma of molecular biology proposed that genetic information flows only from DNA to RNA to protein. In 1970, David Baltimore discovered the enzyme reverse transcriptase in retroviruses, falsifying this strict one-directional flow by showing that RNA can be copied back into DNA. This discovery underpinned the discovery of HIV in 1984 and the development of effective HIV therapies. The transfer of genetic information from RNA to DNA is now recognized as a general evolutionary principle operating throughout biology, not just in viruses.

Top tip! A variant describes a subtype or strain of a virus (e.g. the delta and omicron variants of SARS-CoV-2) that is genetically distinct from the main strain but not different enough to be classed as a separate strain (as influenza A, B, C and D are).

Consequences of Rapid Viral Evolution

The rapid evolution of viruses has real consequences for treating the diseases they cause. Yearly flu vaccines must be redeveloped to maintain their efficacy (the percentage reduction in disease among a vaccinated group in a clinical trial). During the COVID-19 pandemic, many new variants evolved, sometimes with different symptoms, reducing the efficacy of vaccines developed against earlier strains. Top tip! Emerging diseases such as HIV and COVID-19 are often particularly virulent because they are not yet adapted to their new host species.

Epidemiological Modelling: The SIR Model

Compartmental models are a general modelling technique used in the mathematical modelling of infectious diseases, including COVID-19. The population is divided into labelled compartments — for example S (susceptible), I (infectious) and R (recovered) — and people move between compartments over time.

Figure A2.3.13: SIR model showing the transition between susceptible, infectious and recovered statesThe diagram shows three boxes labelled Susceptible, Infectious and Recovered, connected by arrows showing the one-way progression of individuals from susceptible to infectious to recovered as an epidemic proceeds. This model is accurate for diseases transmitted only human-to-human, where recovery confers long-lasting resistance.
Figure A2.3.14: SIR model showing numbers of susceptible, infectious or recovered in a populationThe graph plots the number of people (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis) for an outbreak. The S curve (susceptible, those who can still catch the infection) starts high and falls over time; the I curve (infectious, those who have the infection and can spread it) rises to a peak and then falls; the R curve (recovered, those who have had the disease but are now better) starts at zero and rises steadily as the epidemic progresses.

Such models try to predict how a disease spreads, the total number infected, and the duration of an epidemic, and to estimate epidemiological parameters such as the reproductive number, R0 — the expected number of secondary cases produced by a single infection in a population that is completely susceptible. Models can also show how interventions such as lockdowns or vaccination might affect the outcome of an epidemic.
