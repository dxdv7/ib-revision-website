Production of Unspecialized Cells Following Fertilization

Key termsZygote: cell produced by the fusion of gametes. Cell cycle: repeating process of an orderly sequence of events where cells arise by the division of existing cells, grow, and then divide. Embryo: an organism in the earliest stages of growth, when its basic structures are being formed.

Multicellular organisms begin life as a single cell, the zygote, which grows and divides, at first producing unspecialized cells and then forming many specialized cells that eventually form the adult organism. Cells arise by the division of existing cells, and the time between one cell division and the next is the cell cycle.

Figure B2.3.1: The life history of a cell and the role of stem cellsA flow diagram showing a young cell undergoing cell division and cell growth; some cells continue to grow and divide repeatedly rather than specializing (stem cells, e.g. the stem cells of bone marrow, which divide to form the cells that develop into red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets, as well as more stem cells). Other cells undergo cell specialization to become a mature cell that is unlikely or unable to divide again – for example, a red blood cell, which loses its nucleus and fills its cytoplasm with the oxygen-carrying pigment haemoglobin and the enzyme carbonic anhydrase.

In healthy organisms, cells eventually die by programmed cell death (PCD), a process controlled by specific genes. In PCD, all parts of a cell are packaged in membrane and engulfed by surrounding cells. PCD normally removes superfluous, infected and damaged cells and is a key part of tissue and organ development.

Morphogens and differentiation

MorphogenA molecule present in a concentration gradient that specifies the fate of each cell along the gradient.

During early-stage embryo development, complex mechanisms of gene expression determine how cells differentiate and take on specific roles. A small number of genes determine body patterns during development. The signalling molecules involved are called morphogens. Morphogens are extracellular (they exist outside the cells of a tissue) and occur across a gradient of concentrations. This gradient drives the differentiation of unspecialized stem cells into different cell types: where morphogen concentration is high, cells change differently to cells where the concentration is lower. The initiation or inhibition of gene expression results from these different morphogen concentrations, controlling how cells differentiate and develop into specific tissues.

The concentration of morphogen in each cell determines a series of subsequent signals (cascades). Responses to these signals determine the direction and extent of cell growth and development, ultimately forming all the tissues and organs of the body. Expression of such gradients also controls the length of body structures such as toes and fingers, and the location of the nose and other body patterns.

Properties of Stem Cells

Key termsBlastocyst: embryo as a fluid-filled ball of cells, at the stage of implantation. Embryonic stem cell: undifferentiated cell in the early-stage embryo, capable of continual cell division and of developing into all the cell types of an adult organism. Stem cell: undifferentiated cell in the embryo or adult that can undergo unlimited division and can give rise to one of many different cell types.

The first step after formation of the zygote is continual cell division to produce a tiny ball of cells called the blastocyst. All these cells are capable of further division. The cells of the inner cell mass of the blastocyst are known as embryonic stem cells.

Stem cell propertiesA stem cell has the ability for endless repeated cell division. Stem cells maintain an undifferentiated state (called self-renewal), while also having the capacity to differentiate into mature cell types (potency) along different pathways. Stem cells are the “building blocks” of life, dividing to form cells that develop into the range of mature cell types of the organism, and are found in all multicellular organisms.

At the next stage of embryological development, most cells lose the ability to divide and differentiate as they develop into the tissues and organs of the organism, such as blood, nerves, liver and brain. However, a very few cells within these tissues keep many of the properties of embryonic stem cells: these are called adult stem cells.

Although all cells in a multicellular organism contain the same genetic code (because they formed from one cell that divided repeatedly by mitosis), cells can differentiate into different functions. Location and Function of Stem Cell Niches in Adult Humans

Stem cell nicheA specific microenvironment in the body that either maintains stem cells in an undifferentiated and self-renewable state or promotes their proliferation and differentiation. Stimuli in the niche include cell-to-cell and molecular signals that activate or repress genes and the subsequent transcription of proteins. As a result, stem cells are maintained in a dormant state, caused to self-renew, or committed to a more differentiated state. Stem cell niches have defined locations in the body; two examples are explored below.

Bone marrow

Bone marrow within the skeleton is the site of blood cell formation (haematopoiesis) for all vertebrates except fish and some amphibians. In human adults, osteoblasts (cells that regulate the creation of new bone) and haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs, which produce the cellular components of blood and blood plasma) are closely connected in the bone marrow. HSCs produce red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.

A subgroup of osteoblasts functions as a key component of the HSC niche (the osteoblastic niche), controlling HSC numbers – suggesting these cells rely on each other to determine their functions, with cells that give rise to mature skeletal cells being critical in regulating HSCs. The vascular niche (the site of blood vessels) in adult bone marrow is a place for stem cell mobilization, or for proliferation and differentiation.

Hair follicles

Hair follicles are structures of mammalian skin that can regenerate to continuously produce new hair. The stem cell niche of hair follicles is located between the opening of the sebaceous gland and the attachment site of the hair erector muscle: these are known as bulge hair follicle stem cells. These stem cells are multipotent and have the potential to increase rapidly. During the growth phase, hair follicle stem cells become activated to regenerate the hair follicle and hair, and hairs grow longer each day. During the resting phase, the stem cells are dormant and hairs can shed more easily.

Complex physiological changes take place in the hair follicle with age. Hair follicles have pigment cells that make melanin; certain stem cells act as pigment-producing cells, and when hair regenerates, some of the stem cells convert into pigment-producing cells that colour the hair. With age, fewer of these cells are active and the hair loses its ability to produce melanin, so hair greys or turns white. An imbalance in stem cell differentiation and altered stem cell activity can also lead to hair loss with age.

Totipotent, Pluripotent and Multipotent Stem Cells

Key termsTotipotent: capable of giving rise to any cell type in an organism, including making more totipotent stem cells and making the cells that become the placenta. Pluripotent: able to develop into many different types of cells or tissues in the body, except for becoming placental cells or totipotent stem cells. Multipotent: can create, maintain and repair the cells of one particular organ or tissue.

The first-formed stem cells of a zygote are totipotent and are the most versatile type of stem cell: they can become any type of body cell, including making more totipotent stem cells and the cells that become the placenta. Only the cells within the first couple of cell divisions after fertilization are totipotent.

Cell division continues, and the cells organize into the fluid-filled blastocyst. Stem cells in these subsequent divisions of the embryo are pluripotent – a little more limited in potential, as they can give rise to all of the cell types that make up the body except placental cells or totipotent stem cells.

By the time differentiation has occurred, some stem cells are able only to develop and form the nervous system: this restricted potency is called multipotent. Multipotent stem cells can develop into more than one cell type but are more limited than pluripotent cells, having a limited capacity for self-renewal; they can create, maintain and repair the cells of one particular organ or tissue. Stem cells in adult tissue, such as bone marrow, are multipotent.

The blastocyst’s inner cell mass gives rise to embryonic stem cells (pluripotent). Common mistakeMake sure you know the difference between totipotent, pluripotent and multipotent. Simply referring to “stem cells” is ambiguous, so the specific type of stem cell should be named, especially when discussing a particular stage of development. As stem cells mature, their function becomes more limited: early stem cells can differentiate into any cell type in the body, including placental cells, while later stem cells can only become a limited range of cells in a particular tissue or organ.

Cell Size as an Aspect of Specialization

Cells exist at a wide variety of different scales, with each type of cell adapted to its function in part by its size.

Figure B2.3.4: Different sizes of cells (not to scale)A comparative diagram of cell sizes: a neuron in the brain (300–400 µm long), a plant cell (100–150 µm), a muscle fibre (300 mm in length), a Paramecium (250–300 µm), a human cheek cell (30–50 µm), a white blood cell (12–17 µm), a red blood cell (8 µm), a human egg (100 µm) and a human sperm cell (55 µm).

Male and female gametes

Human male and female gametes are very different in size. The egg cell is around 100 µm, whereas the main body of the sperm is 5 µm, with a mid-piece (containing mitochondria) and flagellum of 50 µm. The larger cell body of the egg allows it to store nutrients for early development of the fertilized egg, whereas the cell body of the sperm only needs to hold the nucleus for delivery to the egg, so it can be much smaller. The long flagellum of the sperm provides propulsion to reach the egg.

Figure B2.3.5: Scanning electron micrograph of a sperm cell fertilizing an egg cell (×3600)An SEM image showing the marked difference in size between a single sperm cell attaching to the much larger surface of an egg cell during fertilization.

Blood cells

Red blood cells (erythrocytes) are around 8 µm, much smaller than white blood cells (around 12–17 µm). Red blood cells need to be small so they can fit through the narrow lumen of capillaries; being small also gives the red blood cell a larger surface area relative to its size, allowing oxygen to diffuse in and out at a faster rate. White blood cells attack and destroy foreign matter such as bacteria, viruses or other organisms entering the body. They are bigger than red blood cells but fewer in number (though they increase on infection); their larger size allows them either to engulf and digest pathogens or to produce antibodies that bind to and destroy the body’s foreign invaders.

Figure B2.3.6: A light micrograph of a blood smear showing erythrocytes and white blood cellsA stained blood smear in which red blood cells are clearly discernible by their colour and lack of nucleus, while white blood cells show a purple-stained nucleus that is either large and rounded (lymphocytes) or “lobed”, with several sections joined together (phagocytes).

Neurons

Neurons (nerve cells) transmit electrochemical impulses through the body, allowing coordination and response to stimuli. They differ in length – the longest neuron in the human body reaches from the base of the spine to the foot, up to one metre. This long length enables the electrical impulse to be sent without interruption over a long distance; fast transmission, achieved by the long, myelinated neuron, is especially important for rapid responses such as those to pain.

Striated muscle fibres

Striated muscle fibres are multinucleated cells that attach to muscles to allow movement. Instead of being made from many individual cells, muscle fibres are extended cellular structures that can be several centimetres long. This extended length allows them to coordinate contraction and has a significant effect on muscle force generation.

Surface Area-to-Volume Ratios and Constraints on Cell Size

The materials required for growth and maintenance of a cell enter through the plasma membrane, and metabolic waste products must leave through it too. The rate at which materials can enter and leave a cell depends on the cell’s surface area, but the rate at which materials are used and waste is produced depends on the amount of cytoplasm present (the volume). Heat transfer between the cytoplasm and the cell’s environment is likewise determined by surface area.

As cells grow and increase in size, an important difference develops between the surface area available for exchange and the volume of cytoplasm in which the chemical reactions of life occur. Volume increases faster than surface area, so the surface area-to-volume ratio (SA:V) falls as cell size increases. With increasing cell size, therefore, less and less of the cytoplasm has access to the cell surface for exchange of gases, supply of nutrients and loss of metabolic waste products.

The exchange of materials across a cell surface depends on its surface area, whereas the need for exchange depends on cell volume. The smaller a cell is, the more quickly and easily materials can be exchanged between its cytoplasm and environment. One consequence is that cells cannot grow larger indefinitely: when a maximum size is reached, cell growth stops and the cell may then divide.

The square–cube lawThe relative differences in the changes of surface area and volume as objects get bigger or smaller is known as the square–cube law: the volume of an object grows faster than its surface area, because surface area changes as a factor of the squared value of length, whereas volume changes as a factor of the cubed value. For example, if the edge length of a cube is doubled, its surface area becomes four times its original value, while its volume becomes eight times its original value. This limits cell size, because the smaller relative surface area of larger cells means oxygen and food cannot be transported in, and wastes cannot be removed, at a sufficient rate to maintain metabolic activities.

Higher Level Adaptations to Increase Surface Area-to-Volume Ratios of Cells

Red blood cells (erythrocytes)

Red blood cells transport respiratory gases (primarily oxygen) in the bloodstream. They are approximately 8 µm in diameter and have the shape of a flat, round disk with an indentation in the centre – described as biconcave. The flattening of the cell increases its surface area relative to its size, increasing the diffusion of oxygen across its surface, and ensures a short diffusion distance so oxygen can quickly move throughout the cell. The biconcave shape, achievable because the erythrocyte lacks a nucleus, further increases surface area and also increases the cell’s flexibility, helping it move through the narrow lumen of capillaries.

Figure B2.3.9: An erythrocyteA three-dimensional depiction of a red blood cell showing its flattened, biconcave (round disk with a central indentation on each face) shape.

Proximal convoluted tubule cells in the nephron

Key termsMicrovillus (plural, microvilli): one of many tiny infoldings of the plasma membrane, making up a brush border. Brush border: tiny, finger-like projections (microvilli) on the surface of epithelial cells, for example in the small intestine. Invagination: the intucking of a surface or membrane.

The kidney maintains homeostatic balance of water in the body and filters out metabolic wastes (e.g. urea); its functional units are the nephrons. The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the longest section of the nephron, and here a large part of the filtrate is reabsorbed into the capillary network. Epithelial cells in the PCT reabsorb components of the filtrate with nutritional significance (such as glucose, amino acids and ions). The walls of the tubule are one cell thick, and these cells are packed with mitochondria.

The surface facing the lumen (the apical surface) is folded into microvilli, forming a brush border, which greatly increases the surface area for absorption. On the opposite side – the membrane facing the blood at the base of the cell (the basolateral region) – invaginations (infoldings) of the plasma membrane similarly increase surface area. Invaginations of the basolateral membrane are commonly found in cells engaged in active transport of molecules and ions, where the infoldings increase the surface area available for transport.

Higher Level Adaptations of Type I and Type II Pneumocytes in Alveoli

Key termsAlveolus (plural, alveoli): air sac in the lung. Pneumocyte: specialized cell occurring in the alveoli of the lungs. Epithelium: sheet of cells, bound strongly together, covering internal or external surfaces of multicellular organisms. Surfactant: a detergent secreted by cells in the wall of alveoli which breaks surface tension and stops alveolar walls from sticking together, keeping the alveoli open.

There are some 700 million alveoli in our lungs, providing a total surface area of about 70 m² – an area 30–40 times greater than that of the body’s external skin. The alveoli are the major sites of gas exchange in the lungs, and their enormous surface area helps maximize gas exchange. Two types of epithelial cell, called pneumocytes, line the alveoli, with different adaptations for their different roles – the alveolar epithelium is an example of a tissue in which more than one cell type is present, because different adaptations are required for the tissue’s overall function.

Figure B2.3.12: The position of the alveoli in the human lungsA diagram of the human thorax showing the nasal cavity leading via the trachea to the two lungs, each supplied by a bronchus that branches into bronchioles ending in clusters of alveoli; also shown are the ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm and pleural membranes.

Type I pneumocytes

The wall of an alveolus is one cell thick, and a capillary lies very close to the alveolar wall, so the combined thickness of the alveolar and capillary walls separating air and blood is typically only 2–4 µm. The capillary is extremely narrow, just wide enough for red blood cells to squeeze through, so red blood cells are close to, or in contact with, the capillary walls. Type I pneumocytes carry out gas exchange between the alveoli and capillaries: they have a flattened shape, extremely thin to minimize diffusion distance, and are tightly connected to prevent leakage of tissue fluid into the alveolar air space.

Type II pneumocytes

Type II pneumocytes produce a detergent-like mixture of lipoproteins and phospholipid-rich secretion called surfactant, which lines the inner surface of the alveoli. Type II cells are cuboidal in shape and bigger than type I cells so they can store surfactant components, and they contain many secretory vesicles (lamellar bodies) in their cytoplasm that discharge surfactant into the alveolar lumen. Surface tension is a force acting to minimize the surface area of a liquid; because of the tiny diameter of the alveoli (about 0.25 mm), they would tend to collapse under surface tension during expiration, with the walls sticking together. The surfactant secreted by type II pneumocytes lowers surface tension, permitting the alveoli to flex easily as pressure within the thorax falls and rises.

Figure B2.3.13: Cells of the alveolar epitheliumA diagram of an alveolus opening off an airway, showing a flattened type I pneumocyte forming most of the alveolar wall in close contact with a capillary carrying a red blood cell, a cuboidal type II pneumocyte (surfactant cell) containing secretory vesicles, and an alveolar macrophage patrolling the inner surface.

Macrophages

The delicate structure of the alveoli is protected by macrophages (dust cells), abundant in the surface film of moisture – the main detritus-collecting cells of the body. They originate from bone marrow stem cells and are dispersed around the body in the blood circulation. These phagocytic white cells migrate into the alveoli from the capillaries, where they ingest debris, fine dust particles, bacteria and fungal spores, and also line the surfaces of the airways leading to the alveoli.

Higher Level Adaptations of Cardiac Muscle Cells and Striated Muscle Fibres

Key termsMyogenic: originating in heart muscle cells themselves, as in the generation of the heartbeat. Myofibril: contractile protein filament from which muscle is composed. Striated: muscle tissue marked by alternating dark and light bands. Sarcolemma: membranous sheath around a muscle fibre. Intercalated discs: link cardiac cells together and define their borders, and facilitate cell-to-cell communication needed for coordinated muscle contraction.

Cardiac muscle cells

Cardiac muscle is unique to the heart. Heart muscle fibres contract rhythmically from formation until they die. Muscles typically contract when stimulated by an external nerve supply; in the heart, however, the impulse to contract is generated within the heart muscle itself – it is said to have a myogenic origin, meaning the central nervous system is not needed to stimulate cardiac muscle to contract. This myogenic control plays a role in autoregulation: a constant flow of blood must be balanced with blood pressure, so the myogenic origin of the impulse allows the heart, and therefore blood circulation, to adapt quickly to changes in the body, such as those following exercise.

Individual cardiac muscle cells have a tubular structure composed of chains of fibres. Each fibre is composed of a mass of myofibrils, made up of repeating sections called sarcomeres, the fundamental contractile units of muscle cells. Cardiac muscle consists of cylindrical, branching columns of fibres forming a unique, three-dimensional network, allowing contraction in three dimensions and faster spread of the contraction signal. Each fibre has a single nucleus, and the repeating sarcomeres give it a striped or striated appearance under the microscope. Fibres are surrounded by a special plasma membrane, the sarcolemma, and are very well supplied by mitochondria and capillaries.

Figure B2.3.14: Cardiac muscleA photomicrograph-style diagram of cardiac muscle fibres, labelled with a branch point where one fibre splits into two, the striations (alternating dark and light bands), an intercalated disc at a junction between two cells, the sarcolemma (plasma membrane of the muscle fibre), and a single nucleus within a fibre.

Propagation of stimuli throughout the heart wall

The structure of cardiac muscle cells allows propagation of stimuli through the heart wall. Intercalated discs, present at the junctions between cardiac muscle cells, hold cells together so they cannot separate and consist of a double membrane with gap junctions, through which cytoplasmic connections form between adjacent cells. Gap junctions form channels allowing continuous flow of cytoplasm between cells; this direct electrochemical coupling allows waves of depolarization to pass through the entire network, synchronizing contraction of the muscle as if it were a single cell. The branching structure of cardiac muscle allows connection to multiple cells, providing a larger surface area of contact between cells and allowing groups of cells to work together and synchronize their activity. Although cardiac muscle fibres form an interconnected network, the network of the atrial walls is entirely separate from that of the ventricles, ensuring a transmission delay of the electrical signal between atria and ventricles.

Striated muscle fibres

TendonFibrous connective tissue connecting a muscle to bone.

Skeletal muscles are attached to the moveable parts of skeletons by tendons and work in antagonistic pairs; their contraction brings about movement. Skeletal muscle consists of bundles of muscle fibres. A remarkable feature of a muscle fibre is its ability to shorten to half, or even a third, of its relaxed or resting length. Fibres appear striped under the light microscope, so skeletal muscle is also known as striated muscle (like cardiac muscle). Each muscle fibre is composed of a mass of myofibrils.

Figure B2.3.15: The structure of skeletal muscleA diagram showing skeletal muscle cut to reveal a bundle of thousands of individual muscle fibres, connected to a tendon and surrounded by connective tissue; each individual fibre is shown containing several nuclei (a syncytium) and consisting of a mass of myofibrils, with a photomicrograph inset (×1500) showing the alternating light and dark bands (striations) of the fibres.

The ultrastructure of skeletal muscle

Using electron microscopy, each muscle fibre is seen to consist of many parallel myofibrils within a sarcolemma, together with cytoplasm (sarcoplasm) containing mitochondria packed between the myofibrils. Skeletal muscle fibres are multinucleate (containing multiple nuclei per cell) and contain specialized endoplasmic reticulum: the sarcolemma is folded to form a system of transverse tubular endoplasmic reticulum known as the sarcoplasmic reticulum, arranged as a network around individual myofibrils.

Figure B2.3.16: The ultrastructure of a muscle fibreA stereogram of part of a single muscle fibre alongside an electron micrograph of a transverse section (×36,000), labelled with the sarcoplasmic membrane (sarcolemma), myofibrils, sarcoplasm (cytoplasm of the muscle cell), mitochondria packed between myofibrils, and the sarcoplasmic reticulum with its transverse tubules forming a network around each myofibril.

Is a striated muscle fibre a cell?

The striated muscle fibres of mammalian skeletal muscle have an atypical cell structure: the fusion of cells to create one elongated, multinucleated fibre is very different from typical cell structure. Myofibrils give the impression of a structure beyond that of a simple cell, and muscle fibres are long and can measure 300 mm or more – much larger than regular cells. However, the fibres contain the usual organelles of a cell (such as mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum) and are surrounded by one cell membrane, indicating they are indeed cells – though striated muscle fibres challenge the view that cells act as autonomous (self-sufficient) units.

Striated muscle shares many similarities with cardiac muscle: both are surrounded and enclosed by a sarcolemma, from which transverse tubules (T tubules) tunnel in and around the sarcomeres; both possess a sarcoplasmic reticulum (a modified form of endoplasmic reticulum); both are striated in appearance with a similar arrangement of actin and myosin filaments; and both consist of fibres that can shorten by a half to a third of their length and contain many mitochondria to supply ATP for contraction.

Higher Level Adaptations of Sperm and Egg Cells

Key termsEgg/ovum (plural, ova): a female gamete. Sperm: motile male gamete. Oocyte: a female sex cell in the process of a meiotic division to become an ovum.

Sexual reproduction is the production of offspring from two parents using gametes – egg and sperm cells in animals – and involves two stages: meiosis, the special cell division that makes gametes with half the number of chromosomes (haploid); and fertilization, the fusion of two gametes to form a zygote (restoring two sets of chromosomes). Human gametes have specific adaptations that allow them to carry out their function.

Sperm cell

Figure B2.3.17: A human sperm cellA labelled diagram of a sperm cell showing the acrosome (containing hydrolytic enzymes) capping the haploid nucleus at the head, a centriole, the middle piece packed with mitochondria, and a flagellum containing microtubules in a 9 + 2 arrangement, all enclosed within a plasma membrane.

Structures and functions of a mature sperm cell: the flagellum provides motility; the middle piece contains many mitochondria to provide the energy required for movement of the flagellum; the head is surrounded by a plasma membrane and contains a haploid nucleus, smooth and oval in shape to make the cell streamlined for fast movement; and the acrosome (at the top of the head) contains enzymes for digesting the zona pellucida (jelly coat, containing glycoproteins) surrounding the oocyte (egg).

The role of a sperm is to deliver a nucleus from a male into the egg of a female, so fertilization can take place and a new zygote can form. Only the nucleus enters the egg cell – the rest of the cell (the neck and flagellum) remains outside the egg – which means all mitochondria in the resulting zygote come from the mother, via the egg cell.

Egg cell

Egg cells, or ova, go through several stages of development. Primary oocytes (immature egg cells) form after the first stage of meiosis. During the menstrual cycle, once a month, one primary oocyte begins the second stage of meiosis to produce a secondary oocyte and a polar body (containing a second haploid nucleus, but with unequal division of cytoplasm, so it fails to develop further). Meiosis is only completed at fertilization. By delaying the two divisions of meiosis (meiosis I and meiosis II), the oocyte is provided with more genetic material for longer, for mRNA production and subsequent protein synthesis – important as the cell grows and matures. Eggs are much larger than sperm as they contain all the nutrients needed for growth and development of the zygote, and only become the final egg cell on fertilization.

Structures and functions of a mature secondary oocyte: follicle cells nourish and protect the oocyte; the zona pellucida (jelly coat) allows binding of sperm cells, prevents polyspermy (several sperm haploid nuclei entering) and premature implantation of the embryo (ectopic pregnancy), and is also where the polar body is found; the plasma membrane surrounds the haploid nucleus and contains microvilli to absorb nutrients from follicle cells; cortical granules prevent polyspermy during fertilization; the cytoplasm is the site of metabolic reactions and where all organelles are located; and the nucleus is a haploid nucleus (half the chromosome number).
