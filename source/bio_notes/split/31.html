Gene Mutations as Structural Changes to Genes

A gene mutation involves a change in the sequence of bases of a particular gene. Mutations are more likely to occur at certain times in the cell cycle than others, especially when the DNA molecule is replicating. DNA polymerase, which builds a complementary DNA strand during replication, also “proofreads” and corrects most errors, but this proofreading, although highly efficient, does not correct all errors, so gene mutations can and do occur spontaneously during replication. Environmental factors or chemicals, including ionising radiation, UV light and various chemicals, may also cause changes to the DNA base sequence.

Key term Gene mutation: a change in the sequence of bases of a particular gene.

A point mutation is a change in one base in a gene sequence, and can result from:

  • the change of one base to a different base, often caused by the DNA being copied incorrectly – called a substitution mutation;
  • the insertion of an additional base or bases into the DNA;
  • the deletion of a base or bases from the DNA.

A point mutation may change the amino acid encoded by the gene. Because bases work in triplets to determine which amino acid is needed, an addition or deletion of bases can have a profound effect: if the number of bases inserted or deleted is not a multiple of three, every codon beyond that point is read incorrectly during translation. This effect is called a frameshift. Some changes result in a “nonsense” mutation, where the changed genetic code cannot be read and has no meaning; if the change results in a different amino acid being coded for, the mutation is called a “missense” mutation.

Substitution, insertion and deletion A substitution replaces one nucleotide with a different nucleotide, without changing the total number of bases, so it does not cause a frameshift (though it may still change the amino acid encoded, or produce a nonsense or missense mutation). An insertion adds one or more nucleotides, and a deletion removes one or more nucleotides; if the number of bases added or removed is not a multiple of three, a frameshift results, altering every codon that follows.
Key term Frameshift: a mutation in a DNA chain that occurs when the number of nucleotides inserted or deleted is not a multiple of three, so that every codon beyond the point of insertion or deletion is read incorrectly during translation.

Consequences of Base Substitutions

Base substitutions can have an adverse effect – for example, sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a base change in the codon for glutamic acid, resulting in valine appearing instead. However, because of the degeneracy of the genetic code, base substitutions may not always change the amino acid in a polypeptide: each amino acid has several possible codons, and if the substituted base still matches an alternative codon for the same amino acid, there is no effect on the cell’s proteome.

If a substitution occurs in a non-coding region of DNA, it may have a neutral effect on the proteome. However, if the mutation is in a regulatory region that directs the expression of a gene, it may prevent expression of that gene, significantly affecting the proteins found in the cell. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are also the result of base substitution mutations.

Common mistake Mutations are not automatically “bad”. Because of the degeneracy of the genetic code and the presence of non-coding DNA, many mutations have a neutral effect. Others can be beneficial, leading to an adaptive advantage, while some are harmful, leading to deleterious effects on the organism.

Consequences of Insertions and Deletions

Mutations involving major insertions, deletions, or the resulting frameshifts, increase the likelihood that a polypeptide will fail to function correctly.

Worked example: insertion – the HTT gene and Huntington’s disease

Insertions can expand trinucleotide repeat sequences, producing trinucleotide repeat disorders, caused by an abnormal number of triplet repeats in coding or non-coding regions of the genome. Huntington’s disease is caused by an abnormality in the HTT gene, involving a DNA segment called a CAG trinucleotide repeat – a series of three bases (cytosine, adenine and guanine) repeated multiple times in a row. Normally the CAG segment is repeated 10 to 35 times; more frequent repeats within this normal range are even associated with higher cognitive function. However, mutation leading to expansion of the repeat beyond 39 copies leads to Huntington’s disease, a neurodegenerative disorder involving progressive mental deterioration accompanied by involuntary muscle movements.

Figure D1.3.1: Huntington’s disease The figure illustrates Huntington’s disease as a neurodegenerative disorder caused by multiple insertions of a CAG trinucleotide repeat sequence within the HTT gene, contrasting the normal range of repeats (10–35) with the expanded, disease-causing range (beyond 39 repeats).

Huntington’s disease is extremely rare (about 1 case per 20 000 live births) and is caused by a mutated dominant allele. Symptoms usually do not appear until the affected person is 40–50 years old, by which time they may already have passed the dominant allele to their children. In a pedigree chart of an affected family, the condition tends to appear in one or more members of every generation, characteristic of inheritance by a dominant allele.

Worked example: deletion – the delta 32 mutation of the CCR5 gene

The delta 32 mutation of the CCR5 gene is an example of a deletion mutation. People with two copies of this mutation (one inherited from each parent) are less susceptible to HIV-1 infection. The deletion prevents functional expression of the CCR5 protein co-receptor normally used by HIV-1 to enter CD4+ T-cells (white blood cells processed by the thymus, responsible for cell-mediated immunity, and the specific type infected by HIV). Without a functional CCR5 receptor, HIV can no longer efficiently bind to and enter these cells. The mutation does not confer complete resistance, since the virus can use alternative, less efficient co-receptors, but it significantly lowers the likelihood of infection.

Figure D1.3.2: The delta 32 mutation of the CCR5 gene The figure contrasts two scenarios on the surface of a CD4+ T-cell. With a normal CCR5 receptor protruding from the cell surface, most HIV strains can bind to CCR5 and use it to enter the cell. Scientists believe the CCR5 delta 32 mutation arose as long as 2500 years ago but was originally rare (about 1 in 20 000 Europeans). Today it is much more common, found in around 1 in 10 people, suggesting that a past viral disease provided a selection pressure that increased the frequency of the mutation over time.

Causes of Gene Mutation

A factor capable of causing a mutation is called a mutagen, and its effects are described as mutagenic.

As well as by mutagens, mutation can be caused by errors in DNA replication or repair. DNA polymerase “proofreads” during replication to ensure the base code is copied correctly, removing most mistakes. It is estimated that, in eukaryotes, DNA polymerases make errors approximately once every 104–105 nucleotides copied, meaning that each time a diploid mammalian cell replicates, between 100 000 and 1 000 000 polymerase errors occur. Most are corrected, but when the correction mechanism fails, a mutation occurs; if the error is in a coding sequence, a gene mutation may result, potentially changing the protein synthesized.

Randomness in Mutation

Mutation is a random process, though mutations are more likely to occur at certain times in the cell cycle (e.g. during DNA replication) than at others. There is no known natural mechanism for deliberately changing a particular base to change a trait; evolution by natural selection instead selects favourable alleles that have already been generated by random mutation.

Transitions and transversions

There are two types of DNA substitution mutation. Transitions are swaps between bases of similar shape: between adenine and guanine (the two-ring purines), or between cytosine and thymine (the one-ring pyrimidines). Transversions are swaps between a purine and a pyrimidine base. Transition mutations are more common because they preserve the overall ring-shape structure when bases form complementary pairs, and are also less likely to result in an amino acid substitution (owing to the degeneracy of the genetic code). Transitions are therefore more likely to persist as “silent substitutions” with no phenotypic effect, appearing in populations as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) – although not all SNPs are transitions.

Transitions vs transversions A transition is a substitution between two bases of the same chemical class: purine↔purine (A↔G) or pyrimidine↔pyrimidine (C↔T). A transversion is a substitution between bases of different classes: a purine and a pyrimidine. Transitions are more common and more often “silent” (no phenotypic effect).
Figure D1.3.3: Types of substitution mutation The figure shows the chemical ring structures of the two purine bases (adenine and guanine, each with a double-ring structure) and the two pyrimidine bases (cytosine and thymine, each with a single-ring structure). Arrows labelled “transitions” connect adenine to guanine and cytosine to thymine (swaps within the same base type), while arrows labelled “transversions” connect purine bases to pyrimidine bases (swaps between the two different types).

Mutation hotspots

The most common mutations occur at the sequence CG (often written CpG, indicating the phosphate linking the two bases). Proofreading is “blind” to the location of the bases it corrects, yet CpG sequences are mutated particularly often: both C and G form three hydrogen bonds with their partner base, making this region of DNA more strongly held together than a region of two purines or a purine and a pyrimidine, and CpG-rich regions are often highly methylated, which makes proofreading somewhat more difficult. Environmentally induced mutations are also biased, because exposure to different mutagenic stimuli is not equally likely. Mutations are therefore not randomly distributed across genomes; there are mutational “hot spots”, possibly due to variations in the large-scale organizational (super)structure of DNA within chromosomes, or to regions where accessory proteins are less common, increasing the local error rate beyond the correction rate.

Consequences of Mutation in Germ Cells and Somatic Cells

Mutations occurring in the ovaries or testes (or anthers or embryo sacs of flowering plants) are called germ line mutations. Because these occur in germ cells that give rise to gametes, they may be passed on to offspring and can result in inherited diseases, such as Huntington’s disease. Mutations occurring in the body (somatic) cells of a multicellular organism, by contrast, are only passed on to the immediate descendants of those cells and disappear when the organism dies; new mutations acquired during an individual’s lifetime in somatic cells are not heritable, though they can still lead to cancers.

Key term Germ cell: a reproductive cell of the body.
Germ line mutations vs somatic mutations A germ line mutation occurs in a cell that gives rise to gametes, so it can be inherited by offspring and appear in every cell of the resulting organism. A somatic mutation occurs in a body cell, is passed only to that cell’s descendants within the same organism, disappears when the organism dies, and is never inherited by offspring – though it may still contribute to cancer within that individual.

Mutation as a Source of Genetic Variation

Variation between organisms is a defining feature of life, with no two individuals identical in all their traits, and the original source of all genetic variation is gene mutation. Alleles, different versions of the same gene, are created by random mutations. Many mutations affecting non-coding regions of a chromosome are neutral, with no phenotypic effect; others may be harmful (e.g. sickle-cell anaemia). However, mutations are essential in the long term for evolution by natural selection: because all individuals in a population differ, environmental change can give some individuals a selective advantage and an increased chance of survival, ultimately changing the gene pool of the species. If reproductive isolation then occurs, speciation can take place.

Higher Level

Gene Knockout

Gene knockout (KO) produces an organism with one non-functional gene, allowing researchers to investigate the function of that gene, typically using a model organism. A model organism is a non-human organism used to understand simple and complex biological functions. Researchers knock out, or destroy the function of, a particular gene (usually by introducing a deletion) and observe the effect on the phenotype.

Key terms Gene knockout (KO): a technique that produces a genetically engineered organism with one non-functional gene, allowing researchers to investigate the function of that gene.
Mutant: an organism with altered genetic material (abruptly altered by a mutation).

The mouse (Mus musculus) is a commonly used model organism for gene knockout. The p53 KO mouse carries a mutated version of the p53 gene, which normally directs synthesis of a protein that regulates the cell cycle; without this protein, tumour cells are created, increasing the risk of bone and other cancers. The Methuselah KO mouse shows increased longevity, allowing researchers to study the complexity of ageing. The obese KO mouse gives insights into the metabolic disorders involved in human obesity. The 2007 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Mario Capecchi, Martin Evans and Oliver Smithies for developing the gene knockout technique, and there is now a library of knockout organisms available for research in some species.

The knockout mouse shows a clear increase in body fat, having developed metabolic-related disorders leading to obesity, illustrating the use of gene knockout to study the genetics of obesity.

Model organisms are useful for studying gene function

Studying how a gene controls a function in a model organism allows researchers to apply the findings to other organisms, including humans. Model organisms range from bacteria (e.g. Escherichia coli) to mammals (e.g. the mouse), with some plant models (e.g. Arabidopsis thaliana) widely used to investigate crops and other plants. It is the universality of the genetic code, and the commonality of basal biological processes (such as oxidative phosphorylation during aerobic respiration) performed by almost all living organisms, that makes the use of model organisms possible: studies performed in vivo using model organisms, such as gene knockout, can predict similar effects in closely related organisms, including humans.

Higher Level

Use of CRISPR and the Enzyme Cas9 in Gene Editing

Prokaryotic cells have a defence mechanism against infection, comparable to but very different from the eukaryotic immune response, based on a region of DNA sequences called CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats). CRISPR sequences allow an immune response that protects prokaryotes from bacteriophages (viruses that attack bacteria). Together with the CRISPR-associated Cas9 enzyme, bacteria use these sequences to recognize and deactivate future invading viruses.

Key terms CRISPR sequences: sequences in the genomes of some prokaryotes that act as a genomic record of previous viral attack.
Cas9 enzyme: a bacterial endonuclease that forms a double-strand break in DNA at a specific target site within a larger recognition sequence.
Mechanism: bacterial CRISPR–Cas9 immune response
  1. When infected by a virus, bacteria use their CRISPR system to cut up the invading viral DNA and insert pieces of it (spacers) into their own genome as a genomic “memory” of the infection.
  2. Bacteria transcribe these spacers into RNA, which forms a complex with the Cas9 enzyme; this complex monitors the cell for any DNA sequence complementary to the spacer RNA.
  3. If matching (viral) DNA is encountered, the spacer RNA–Cas9 complex binds to it, and the Cas9 enzyme is activated and cuts the viral DNA at specific locations, preventing it from replicating and halting the infection.

Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna were awarded the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their collaborative work developing the CRISPR-Cas9 “genetic scissors” as a precise genome-editing technology – the first time this Nobel Prize had been won by two women alone. Scientists can now use this technology to edit genomes, including the human genome.

Key term Genome editing: the manipulation of DNA by deleting, replacing or inserting a DNA sequence, with the aim of, for example, correcting a genetic disorder.
Mechanism: CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing in the laboratory Cas9 recognizes a target DNA sequence with the help of a guide RNA that is complementary to it. The Cas9–guide RNA complex binds to the recognized target sequence and cuts the DNA at that specific site, removing the unwanted target sequence. A desired DNA sequence, supplied via a DNA template, can then be added at the cut site, producing a customized (edited) DNA sequence. Multiple types of Cas enzyme exist in nature, but Cas9 is the one most commonly used in the laboratory; researchers manufacture guide RNAs specific to particular genetic diseases, enabling sequences to be added, replaced or removed from genes.
Figure D1.3.6: CRISPR and its application in gene editing The figure shows the Cas9 enzyme bound to a guide RNA, which recognizes and binds a complementary target sequence within a DNA molecule at the site to be cut. Cas9 cuts the DNA, and the unwanted target sequence is removed. A DNA template carrying the desired sequence is used to supply new genetic material, which is added in place of the sequence that was cut out, producing a final, customized DNA molecule.

Worked example: treating sickle-cell anaemia with CRISPR

In an experimental treatment for sickle-cell disease, scientists used CRISPR to edit a gene in a patient’s bone marrow cells so that they would produce foetal haemoglobin – a form of haemoglobin normally made only before birth – to compensate for the sickle-shaped haemoglobin. In one patient, blood tests showed about 46% of haemoglobin was foetal haemoglobin, present in 99.7% of red blood cells, and further bone marrow samples found more than 81% of cells contained the genetic change, showing the edited cells continued to survive and function in the patient’s body over a sustained period.

The precise specificity of the CRISPR system offers opportunities to modify a gene of interest with significantly reduced risk of accidentally affecting other genes. Researchers are investigating CRISPR for editing out genetic defects that cause cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell disease, haemophilia and muscular dystrophy, as well as for more targeted cancer treatments; for example, adult rats genetically engineered to have a form of genetic blindness have been successfully treated using CRISPR gene therapy.

Nature of science: ethical and global impact Despite its potential to treat many genetic diseases, using CRISPR-Cas9 to change genetic traits in humans raises serious concerns, including possible off-target (unintended) mutations that may be deleterious, and the very high cost of germ line-editing technology, which could make it accessible only to people from rich countries. Ethical issues that must be addressed before wider use include genome editing in human embryos, which could have unpredictable effects on future generations, and the use of the technology for non-therapeutic modifications, which could lead to loss of human diversity and concerns about eugenics. Because scientists around the world are subject to different regulatory systems, there is an international effort to harmonize regulation of genome-editing technologies such as CRISPR.
Higher Level

Conserved and Highly Conserved Sequences in Genes

Conserved sequences are identical or similar sequences in nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) shared across species or a group of species; highly conserved genetic sequences are identical or similar over long periods of evolution. Examples include proteins associated with ribosomes, organelles present in all domains of life.

Key term Conserved sequences: identical or similar sequences in nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), across species or a group of species, that have remained essentially unchanged throughout evolution.

Two hypotheses explain the mechanisms behind conserved sequences:

  • Functional requirements for the gene products: highly conserved sequences are usually required for basic cellular stability, function and reproduction, and so must be maintained from generation to generation throughout evolutionary history.
  • Slower rates of mutation than the background mutation rate for these sequences.

Research has shown that mutation rate is linked to the level of gene expression: highly transcribed genes generally show lower mutation rates than less-expressed genes, and the transcribed strand of a gene shows a lower mutation rate than the non-transcribed strand. This link between gene expression and mutation rate may be due to enhanced proofreading and repair mechanisms operating on these actively transcribed sections of DNA.

In addition to protein-coding sequences, the genome contains a significant amount of regulatory DNA, and these non-coding regions also contain highly conserved sequences. In vertebrates, such sequences occur in and around genes that act as developmental regulators; highly conserved regions for transcription factors related to embryonic and subsequent development provide further evidence for a common origin of all vertebrates.
