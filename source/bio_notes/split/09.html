Biodiversity: variety of life in all its forms, levels and combinations

DefinitionBiodiversity is the amount of biological or living diversity per unit area. It is a contraction of “biological” and “diversity” and refers to the total number of different species living in a defined area or ecosystem, as well as the incredible abundance of different types of species on Earth. Biodiversity includes three linked concepts: ecosystem diversity, species diversity and genetic diversity.

The three levels of biodiversity

  • Ecosystem diversity is the range of different habitats or number of ecological niches per unit area in an ecosystem. A woodland contains many different habitats (river, soil, trees) and so has high ecosystem diversity; a desert has few (sand, occasional vegetation) and so has low diversity. Conserving ecosystem diversity usually leads to conservation of species and genetic diversity too.
  • Species diversity is the variety of species per unit area, including both the number of species present and their relative abundance.
  • Genetic diversity is the range of genetic material present in a gene pool or population of a species. A large gene pool gives high genetic diversity; a small gene pool gives low genetic diversity. The term usually refers to diversity within one species but can also describe the diversity of genes across all species in an area.
Common mistakeBiodiversity is not only about animals — it applies to all living organisms, including plants, fungi and microorganisms.

Species richness and species evenness

Richness and evenness are the two components of species diversity. Richness is the number of species in an area. Evenness is the relative abundance of each species. A community with high evenness has a similar abundance of all species, implying a complex ecosystem with many niches supporting a wide range of species. Low evenness describes a community where one or a handful of species dominate, suggesting lower complexity and fewer available niches.

Figure A4.2.1: Species richness versus species diversityTwo ecosystems, A and B, each contain the same three species of beetle, so their species richness is identical. In ecosystem A, one species dominates numerically while the other two are rare (low evenness), indicating a less complex ecosystem with fewer effective niches. In ecosystem B the three species are present in similar numbers (high evenness), so overall species diversity is higher in B than in A even though richness is the same in both.
Richness vs diversitySpecies richness is simply the number of species present in an area. Diversity is a combined measure of the number of species AND their relative abundance (evenness). Two habitats can have identical richness but very different diversity if one has an even spread of species and the other is dominated by a single species.

Simpson’s reciprocal index

Diversity indices, such as Simpson’s reciprocal index, are used to describe and compare communities, and to assess whether human development is having a sustainable impact on ecosystems. Low diversity can indicate pollution, eutrophication or recent colonization of a site. The number of species present is often indicative of general biodiversity patterns, although disturbed sites can show artificially increased richness because habitats that are normally spatially separate become mixed together.

D = N(N − 1) ÷ Σn(n − 1)
where D = diversity index, N = total number of organisms of all species found, n = number of individuals of a particular species

Index values are relative, not absolute (unlike a fixed scale such as temperature) — two areas can be compared using the index, but a single value on its own is not informative. Comparisons must be made between areas containing the same type of organism in the same kind of ecosystem. A high value of D suggests a stable, ancient site where all species have similar abundance (evenness). A low value of D suggests disturbance, for example through logging, pollution, recent colonization or agricultural management, where one species may dominate.

Common mistakeDo not confuse Simpson’s reciprocal index (used in this course) with the plain Simpson’s index — they use different equations and are interpreted differently.

Comparisons between current numbers of species and past levels of biodiversity

How many species exist?

Scientists do not know exactly how many types of organism exist. Millions of species have been discovered, named and described, but many more remain to be found. Estimates of the total number of species on Earth range from 5 to 100 million, with a scientific consensus of around 9 million species, broken down approximately as: animals 7.77 million (12% described), fungi 0.61 million (7% described), plants 0.30 million (70% described), and other species 0.07 million. New species continue to be discovered in all countries, even those that pioneered the systematic study of plants and animals.

Figure A4.2.2: The relative number of animal and plant groupsA diagram showing the relative numbers of known species across the major divisions of life: higher plants (mainly flowering plants), insects (a class of arthropods), other arthropods (segmented animals with an external skeleton and jointed limbs), other non-vertebrate groups, fungi, protoctista, and vertebrates (fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals). Of the roughly 1.8 million described species, more than 50% are insects, with flowering plants the next largest group. In contrast, only around 4000 species of mammals are known, about 0.25% of all known species.

Evidence from fossils

Fossils provide evidence about the history of life, but fossilization is an extremely rare, chance event: predators, scavengers and bacterial action normally break down dead remains before fossilization can occur, and of the relatively few fossils formed, most stay buried or are overlooked or destroyed when exposed. Nonetheless, huge numbers of fossils have been found. Where a fossil or its surrounding rock can be accurately dated using radiometric techniques (measuring naturally occurring radioactive substances such as carbon-14 relative to carbon-12, or the ratio of potassium-40 to argon-40), we obtain good evidence of the history of life. Fossils in an accurately dated rock stratum give clues about the community of organisms living at that time, though the picture is necessarily incomplete. The fossil record can suggest the sequence in which groups of species evolved and the timing of appearance of major phyla. Evidence from fossils suggests there are currently more species alive today than at any time in the past, although this remains debated because the fossil record is patchy (especially from deep time), soft-bodied organisms such as jellyfish fossilize poorly, and not all existing fossils will ever be discovered.

Figure A4.2.3: The process of fossilizationShows the different fossil forms and the steps of fossilization by petrification. Fossil forms: petrification (organic matter of the dead organism is replaced by mineral ions); mould (organic matter decays but the space left becomes a mould filled with mineral matter); trace (an impression of a form, such as a leaf or footprint, made in layers that later harden); preservation (of the intact whole organism, for example in amber, which is fossilized conifer resin, or in anaerobic acidic peat). Steps of petrification: (1) dead remains fall into a lake or sea and are buried in silt or sand under anaerobic, low-temperature conditions; (2) hard parts of the skeleton or lignified plant tissue become impregnated by silica or carbonate ions and harden; (3) the hardened remains become compressed within layers of sedimentary rock; (4) after millions of years, upthrust brings rocks to the surface and erosion begins; (5) land movements expose some fossils and a few are discovered by chance, though only a tiny fraction of fossilized organisms are ever found by humans.
NOS: what taxonomy cannot verifyAs classification tools have evolved, what counts as a species (or genus, or kingdom) has changed. In bacterial taxonomy there has long been tension between “lumpers”, who group similar organisms into the same species, and “splitters”, who split subgroups into new species — for example, over 200 named species of Salmonella were eventually reclassified as one species, S. enterica, with subspecies, after DNA–DNA hybridization showed they were highly related. This illustrates that some knowledge (such as whether species diversity was ever greater in the deep past) may remain permanently beyond the ability of science to verify, given gaps in the fossil record.

Causes of anthropogenic species extinction

AnthropogenicAnthropogenic means relating to human activity. There have been five natural mass extinctions in Earth’s history, in each of which more than 75% of species went extinct, mostly linked ultimately to climate change often caused by volcanic activity; the last, 66 million years ago, was caused by an asteroid impact and led to the extinction of the dinosaurs. Scientists believe we are now in a sixth mass extinction, this time caused by human actions rather than natural causes.

Case study: North Island giant moa

Figure A4.2.4: The North Island giant moa (Dinornis novaezealandiae)Illustrates this large flightless bird, one of three extinct moa species in the genus Dinornis endemic to New Zealand.

The North Island giant moa was one of the nine moa species, the second tallest, reaching up to 2 metres to the back and up to 3 metres including the neck, and showed sexual dimorphism with females much larger than males. It lived in North Island lowlands, shrublands, grasslands, dunes and forests — an example of terrestrial megafauna (large or giant animals of an area, habitat or geological period). Its population was stable for 40 000 years until the arrival of the Māori around 1280. The moa was hunted for food, but the most important contributing factor to its extinction (by 1500) was probably farming: forests were cut and burned and land converted to arable use. Ancient DNA, radiocarbon dating and stable dietary isotope analysis show that before humans arrived, moa adapted to climate change by tracking their preferred habitat as it shifted, meaning climate and geological change were not the major cause of their decline. As large herbivores, moa played a major role in shaping vegetation structure and composition, so their extinction likely altered vegetation composition, regeneration patterns and fire frequency across New Zealand.

Case study: Caribbean monk seal

Figure A4.2.5: The Caribbean monk seal (Neomonachus tropicalis)Illustrates this pinniped, declared extinct in 2008.

The Caribbean monk seal, a member of the Pinnipedia (which also includes sea lions and walruses), was once widespread across the Caribbean Sea, Gulf of Mexico and western Atlantic. It is the only pinniped species to have become extinct, and was the first seal species confirmed extinct due to human causes (per NOAA Fisheries). It was hunted for fur, meat and blubber oil, captured for museums and zoos, and suffered from overfishing that disturbed its habitat and reduced its prey (mainly fish); its tame, non-aggressive behaviour made it easy for hunters to approach. At peak abundance an estimated 233 000–338 000 seals lived across 13 colonies, each adult eating around 245 kilograms of fish a year. As a top predator, its loss allowed some fish species to expand at the expense of others, significantly altering the biodiversity of Caribbean coral reef food webs.

Case study: Falkland Islands wolf

Figure A4.2.6: The Falkland Islands wolf (Dusicyon australis)Illustrates the only native land mammal of the Falkland Islands.

First sighted by Europeans in 1692, the Falkland Islands wolf was described by Charles Darwin in 1833 as “common and tame”. It lived in burrows and, since there were no native rodents on the islands, probably fed on ground-nesting birds such as geese and penguins, grubs, insects and shoreline scavenging. Settlers (mainly Scottish, with some French and English) saw it as a threat to their sheep and mounted a large-scale poisoning and shooting campaign. Extinction happened rapidly, helped by the lack of forest cover and the animal’s tameness (it trusted humans, who lured it with meat before killing it). Although not a significant predator, removal of this top predator would have affected the rest of the food chain, for example by increasing the population size of its prey.

Causes of ecosystem loss

DefinitionsAn ecosystem is a community of organisms and the physical environment with which it interacts. A community is a group of different species living in an area. Abiotic factors are the non-living components of an ecosystem. Disturbance and loss of ecosystems can be natural (e.g. typhoons, volcanic activity) but is currently predominantly anthropogenic.

Case study: loss of mixed dipterocarp forests in South East Asia

Tropical rainforests cover only 6% of Earth’s land surface but may contain up to 50% of all species. They occur near the equator in South America, Africa, India, South East Asia and Australia, with a warm, stable climate (20–35 degrees Celsius) and high rainfall (up to 2500 millimetres per year). Constant warmth, high sunlight and high rainfall drive high photosynthesis and productivity, which leads to ecosystem complexity, abundant resources and high niche diversity, and therefore high species richness and biodiversity. However, rainforest soils are nutrient-poor, thin and easily eroded once cleared, making forest re-establishment difficult after clearance.

Figure A4.2.7: Logging activities in Sabah, Malaysian BorneoShows commercial logging operations removing dipterocarp hardwood trees from the rainforest.
Figure A4.2.8: Oil palm trees replacing tropical rainforest in BorneoShows large areas of former rainforest converted to monoculture oil palm (Elaeis guineensis) plantation.

Dipterocarp trees are the main hardwood species of South East Asian rainforests; more than 270 species have been identified in Borneo, 155 of them endemic. Large areas of dipterocarp forest have been cleared for oil palm; in Malaysia, oil palm plantations now cover 20% of land area, up from 1% in 1974. Palm oil is used in food (margarine, cooking oil, ice cream, ready meals, biscuits, cakes), domestic products (detergents, cosmetics) and biofuel, with global production exceeding 35 million tonnes per year. Because the soils are nutrient-poor, oil palm requires heavy fertilizer input, which can raise ground-level ozone, harming humans, wildlife and plant productivity.

Description
Borneo is the third-largest island in the world, historically covered by tropical rainforest around 130 million years old — one of the oldest rainforests on Earth.
The rainforest is dominated by dipterocarps: long-lived, tall, hardwood trees that are valuable timber species.
The island has high species diversity: 15 000 plant species, 220 mammal species and 420 bird species recorded; around 20% of mammal species are endemic.
Up to 300 tree species can be found in a single hectare of forest.
Many species are on the IUCN Red List, e.g. orang-utan, sun bear, Asian elephant.
Human threats
Commercial logging for export since the 1970s, accelerating in the 1980s and 1990s.
Forest cover fell from 6.4 million hectares (88% of land area) in 1974 to 4.5 million hectares in 1985 — a 30% reduction in 11 years; deforestation rate 2000–5 was 3.9% per year.
At peak logging, up to 100 cubic metres per hectare of timber were removed; conventional (non-selective) logging damages the remaining forest.
More recent reduced-impact logging (RIL) causes less damage and allows faster regeneration.
Since the 1990s, cleared land has increasingly been planted with oil palm, yielding up to 5000 kilograms of crude palm oil per hectare, mostly on state-owned or transnational-corporation land.
Consequences of disturbance
Damage to remaining forest is proportional to timber removed; conventionally logged forest can lose 94% more timber than RIL sites.
Conventional logging damages forest structure, favouring light-loving riverine species such as Macaranga, and reduces biodiversity.
Oil palm plantations are low-diversity monocultures that fragment rainforest, block migration routes, remove habitats and use insecticides/herbicides that further reduce biodiversity.
Animals straying into plantations (e.g. elephants, orang-utans) can be illegally killed.
Without forest cover, soil erodes and original vegetation struggles to regrow; reduced transpiration alters local weather, causing drier, more fire-prone conditions.
The rainforest’s value for biodiversity, climate regulation and ecotourism is reduced.

Case study: loss of the Great Barrier Reef

Figure A4.2.9: Satellite image of the Great Barrier Reef, 2004Shows the Queensland coastline on the left with the extensive reef systems clearly visible offshore to the right.

The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park covers 345 000 square kilometres in the Coral Sea off northeast Australia. It is important to Indigenous Australian culture and spirituality, a major tourist destination (especially around Cairns), and supports fishing worth AU$1 billion per year. Coral reefs, like rainforests, are highly diverse for similar reasons: location, complexity and high productivity. The reef stretches 2300 kilometres and comprises 3000 individual reef systems, home to 1500 fish species, 359 types of hard coral, a third of the world’s soft corals, 6 of the world’s 7 threatened marine turtle species, more than 30 marine mammal species (including vulnerable dugongs), 5000–8000 mollusc species, thousands of sponges, worms and crustaceans, 800 echinoderm species, and 215 bird species (29 of them seabirds).

Description
The world’s largest coral reef ecosystem and one of the richest, most complex natural ecosystems on the planet; began growing around 18 million years ago on older reef platforms; made a World Heritage Site in 1981.
Ecological, sociopolitical and economic pressures are degrading the reef and threatening its biodiversity.
Human threats
Physical damage from divers’ fins, boat anchors, and tourists illegally removing coral as souvenirs.
Over-fishing disrupts the food chain; seafloor trawling for prawns (still allowed in over half the marine park) destroys the seafloor and causes bycatch.
Shift from subsistence to large-scale farming means heavy fertilizer and pesticide use; nutrient run-off has raised inorganic nitrogen pollution by 3000%, contributing (with sewage) to algal blooms.
Sedimentation has increased 800% due to mangrove clearance for tourism, housing and farming.
Climate change raises sea temperatures, causing coral bleaching; aerial surveys in 2002 found almost 60% of reefs bleached to some degree.
Consequences of disturbance
Sea turtle habitats (reefs and seagrass beds) damaged by sedimentation, nutrient run-off and tourist development.
Destructive fishing and climate change reduce population numbers; mass bleaching events occurred in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017 and 2020.
Rising sea level and changing sea temperature may permanently reduce the reef’s biodiversity and ecological value.
Fish moving away to cooler waters increases mortality in seabirds that prey on them.

Evidence for a biodiversity crisis

Evidence for the current biodiversity crisis is drawn from reports of the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), an independent intergovernmental body founded in 2012 by 94 countries to strengthen links between scientific information and policy on conservation, sustainable use of biodiversity, and long-term human well-being. Although not a UN organization, IPBES is supported by the UN Environment Programme (UNEP). Reliable surveys of biodiversity across many habitats are needed to establish current biodiversity levels and estimate extinction rates; such surveys may use Simpson’s reciprocal index to compare disturbed and undisturbed habitats and are repeated over time to detect changes in richness and evenness.

Citizen science

Citizen science is scientific research carried out, in whole or in part, by amateur (non-professional) scientists. It has four common features: anyone can participate; participants use the same protocol so data can be combined and remain high quality; the data help professional scientists reach reliable conclusions; and a wide community of scientists and volunteers works together and shares data with public access. Citizen science allows far larger quantities of data to be collected than professional scientists could gather alone, offering a rapid, global mechanism for gathering evidence to support conservation given the scale and urgency of the sixth mass extinction.

NOS: peer review and citizen scienceVerifiable evidence usually comes from peer-reviewed publication, in which other experts (peers) evaluate the quality, validity and credibility of the work before publication — a form of self-regulation that gives scientific papers objectivity. Review is often “blind”, hiding reviewer and/or author identities to reduce bias, though even peer-reviewed articles can contain inaccuracies, and rejected papers often get published elsewhere. Citizen science brings unique methodological concerns because participants are not experts and data could be recorded inaccurately, but this is mitigated by expert support, training and standardized protocols that allow data from many sources to be combined reliably.

Causes of the current biodiversity crisis

Human population growth

For most of Earth’s history, natural processes (plate tectonics, ocean and atmospheric currents, volcanic activity) shaped life. More recently, Homo sapiens has become the dominant influence on ecosystems. Early humans were hunter-gatherers living in balance with nature with little environmental impact. The Neolithic (“new stone age”) revolution, beginning in the Middle Eastern “fertile crescent” about 10 000 years ago, introduced settled agriculture and allowed populations to grow; the domestication of many plant and animal species followed over the next 10 000 years, occurring independently in different parts of the world. Human population grew from around 2 million in the early Neolithic to over 7 billion today. The population doubling time has shortened repeatedly (doubling between 1804–1922, 1922–1959, and 1959–1974), and in the twentieth century growth became exponential. Contributing factors include better healthcare, more nutritious food, cleaner water and better sanitation. Population growth is greatest in less economically developed countries (LEDCs), driven by high infant death rates pressuring women to have more children, larger families providing farm labour and old-age security, and limited access to contraception. Exponential growth demands enormous extra resources (food, housing, clothing) and is an overarching cause of biodiversity loss, alongside specific causes such as over-exploitation, urbanization, deforestation and agricultural land clearance, pollution, disease spread, and invasive species from global transport.

Population rises slowly at first, then increasingly steeply, reaching 7.8 billion in 2020, with the LEDC component contributing the great majority of the growth and continued rise projected toward 10–12 billion.

Hunting and other forms of over-exploitation

Overharvesting and hunting have significantly reduced population sizes of many species. Animals are hunted for food, medicines, souvenirs, fashion and the exotic pet trade.

Figure A4.2.11: Overfishing of North Atlantic cod in European watersA graph showing North Sea cod spawning stock biomass and the fishing exploitation rate from the 1960s to the late 1990s. As exploitation rate (amount of fishing) rose through the 1960s–1980s, spawning stock biomass declined sharply. Since about 1990, reduced fishing effort has begun to reverse the decline in spawning fish, suggesting that, given time, stocks may recover.

Urbanization

Urbanization is the increase in the proportion of people living in towns and cities. Today around 4.2 billion people (55% of the world’s population) live in cities, projected to reach 68% by 2050. Urban areas are built over land once covered by natural habitats — for example, New York was built on drained and filled wetland. Higher population density in cities can be argued to be a more efficient way of housing a growing population than less-dense alternatives, but continued urbanization drives ongoing loss of natural ecosystems and biodiversity.

Deforestation and clearance of land for agriculture

Habitat loss due to deforestation is a major cause of biodiversity loss, especially where highly biodiverse ecosystems such as tropical rainforests are cleared. Rainforests cover only 6% of Earth’s surface yet may hold 50% of all species; the equatorial climate provides optimal photosynthetic conditions. Rainforests are rich in valuable timber and so vulnerable to exploitation — an average of 1.5 hectares (about the size of a football pitch) was lost every 6 seconds in 2019.

Figure A4.2.13: A range of crops being grown in the UKShows agricultural fields under cultivation, illustrating land converted from natural habitat to arable use.

Rising human population (from around 3 billion in the 1950s to over 7 billion by 2023) has increased demand for food, driving further conversion of land to agriculture. Nearly 40% of Earth’s land surface is now used for agriculture: an area roughly the size of South America is used for crop production, with even more land (3.2–3.6 × 109 hectares) used to raise livestock such as cattle.

Figure A4.2.14: Nearly 40% of the Earth’s surface is used for agricultureA pie/flow diagram breaking down global land area (13.4 billion hectares = 100%) into agricultural area (5 billion hectares = 37%), which splits into pasture (3.5 billion hectares = 70% of agricultural area) and arable land (1.4 billion hectares = 30%); arable land is further divided into food and feed (1.29 billion hectares = 27% of agricultural area), material use (100 million hectares = 2%) and biofuels (55 million hectares = 1%).

Replacing native habitat with monocultures (crops of a single species) represents a large loss of diversity compared with native ecosystems, though growing awareness has led to re-establishment of hedgerows and undisturbed corridors that encourage natural communities to return. Habitats are also lost to mining: mobile phones require the metallic element tantalum, obtained by mining coltan (a niobium- and tantalum-bearing ore) found mainly in the eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, where mining has caused extensive destruction of forest habitat for gorillas and other endangered species. Plantation crops such as sugar cane have replaced mangrove forest in Australia, and oil palm has replaced tropical forest across South East Asia.

Pollution and the spread of disease

Pollution is the addition to an environment of a substance or agent (such as heat) by human activity, at a rate greater than the environment can render harmless, having an appreciable effect on the organisms present. Pollution — including chemicals, litter, nets, plastic bags and oil spills — damages habitats and kills organisms, reducing population numbers. Human effects on the environment can also spread disease: the golden toad (Incilius periglenes), once common in a small area of high-altitude cloud forest above Monteverde, Costa Rica, was discovered in 1966, last seen in 1989, and recorded extinct by the IUCN in 2004, possibly due to airborne pollution or fungal/parasitic infection linked to rising global temperatures. Small populations are especially vulnerable to disease because a reduced gene pool leaves them more prone to disease and inbreeding, as seen in the critically endangered Asiatic cheetah in Iran.

Invasive species

Alien vs invasive speciesAn alien species is a species introduced into an area by human activity, either deliberately (e.g. the rabbit introduced to the UK from Normandy, or buddleia from China) or accidentally as an unwanted colonizer. An invasive species is an alien species that has increased rapidly in number and has a negative effect on the environment and native species, usually by out-competing them. An alien species is not necessarily invasive — invasive means it actively expands into and modifies the ecosystems it has entered. If an alien species lacks the usual limiting factors of its home environment (predation, disease, competitors), it can reproduce rapidly and put pressure on native species.

Case study: lionfish. The red lionfish (Pterois volitans), native to the Indo-Pacific, has become abundant in the Atlantic and Caribbean over the past decade after apparently escaping from Florida aquaria into coastal waters, where it has thrived due to lack of competition and predation and abundant food.

Figure A4.2.16: A lionfish on a coral reefShows the fish’s distinctive red, white and black striped pattern and elaborate pectoral and dorsal fins.

Lionfish are venomous, aggressive members of the family Scorpaenidae; their warning stripes make them inedible to Atlantic and Caribbean predators unfamiliar with them. They overpopulate reef areas and force native species into less favourable conditions, posing a major threat to reef ecosystems along the US east coast and Caribbean.

Case study: water hyacinth. Water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) was introduced to the USA from South America as an ornamental plant in the 1880s; by the early 1960s it covered more than 50 000 hectares of public lakes and rivers.

Figure A4.2.17: Water hyacinth in a Louisiana swampShows the floating plant forming dense mats that spread across the water surface.

The plant grows rapidly, forming dense mats that can cover entire water bodies, reducing native algae and plankton (the food source for native fish and wildlife) and crowding out native species. Invasive species can drive native species to extinction: in Hawaii, many endemic snail species have been wiped out by habitat loss, introduced rats, and the deliberate 1955 introduction of the carnivorous snail Euglandia to control an alien African snail — Euglandia ate the endemic snails as well as its intended target.

Approaches to conservation of biodiversity

Conservation, in ecological terms, means striving to “keep what we have”. Conservation biology is the scientific study of Earth’s biodiversity with the aim of protecting habitats, ecosystems and species from human-made disturbance such as deforestation and pollution. Conservation activities aim to slow extinction caused by unsustainable exploitation of resources and to maintain biotic interactions between species. No single conservation approach is sufficient on its own; several approaches are usually needed together.

In situ conservation

DefinitionIn situ conservation is the conservation of species within their natural habitat, working within conservation areas or nature reserves. This protects not only the target species but also the habitat and ecosystem, preserving many other species too.

In situ conservation may require active management: clearing overgrowth, limiting predators, controlling poaching and access, reintroducing locally extinct species and removing alien species. Successfully protected areas also provide vital habitat (including for migrating species), build community support, receive adequate funding, carry out ecological research and monitoring, play an educational role, are protected by legislation, have policing and guarding, and give the site economic value.

The effect of biogeographic factors

Biogeographic factors affect species diversity and must be considered when planning nature reserves. Island biogeography theory predicts that smaller islands of habitat contain fewer species than larger islands, so protected areas will inevitably have lost some of the diversity of the original, undisturbed ecosystem.

Larger reserves support a greater range of habitats and greater species diversity, higher population numbers of each species, greater productivity at each trophic level (giving longer, more stable food chains), and can maintain top carnivores and large mammals. A low perimeter-to-area ratio reduces edge effects: edge conditions (hotter, less humid, windier) differ greatly from interior conditions, and interior specialist species cannot survive there, so a circular reserve (lowest edge-to-area ratio) is better than an elongated strip or an undulating-edged reserve of the same total area. Where a reserve must be divided, fragments need to be close together or linked by corridors, which maintain gene flow and allow movement of large mammals and top carnivores between fragments.

Rewilding and reclamation of degraded ecosystems

DefinitionRewilding is a form of environmental conservation that seeks to reinstate natural processes and, where appropriate, missing species, allowing the complex interactions that exist in an ecosystem to be restored.

Rewilding reintroduces lost species, especially top predators with major effects on the food web, back into natural environments. For example, the US National Park Service reintroduced the grey wolf (Canis lupus) to Yellowstone National Park in the mid-1990s, which lowered the local elk population and reduced overgrazing of plants. Rewilding has three main principles: (1) establishing core habitat areas that support biodiversity; (2) connectivity, with corridors linking core areas to allow movement of biodiversity across the landscape; (3) carnivores, with top predators (apex predators) reintroduced to maintain ecological balance. Benefits of rewilding include increased carbon storage (restoring UK woodland, peat bog, heath and grassland over 6 million hectares could capture 47 million tonnes of carbon dioxide per year, over a tenth of current UK emissions), helping wildlife adapt to climate change by allowing movement between connected reserves as ecosystems shift, reversing biodiversity loss, supporting local economic opportunities through nature-based enterprise, and improving human health and well-being through access to clean water, healthy soils, flood defences, food and clean air.

Figure A4.2.20: Active land management in Scotland, UKShows exotic pine tree species being removed and replaced with native broadleaf trees as part of ecosystem restoration.

Ecosystem restoration means preventing, halting and reversing damage to degraded ecosystems, sometimes through active management such as replanting native trees. Restoration ecology aims to return an ecosystem as closely as possible to its former state after a major disturbance, though rewilding may also reorganize and regenerate a landscape’s wildness in ways that modify the biodiversity originally present, with long-term sustainability in mind.

Ex situ conservation

DefinitionEx situ conservation is the preservation of species outside their natural habitats. For animals this usually occurs in zoos via captive breeding and reintroduction programmes: a small population is obtained from the wild or other zoos, enclosures mimic the natural habitat as closely as possible, and breeding may be assisted by artificial insemination.

Botanical gardens support ex situ plant conservation through living collections and seed banks that store genetic diversity. Germ plasm (cells capable of, or produced by, sexual reproduction, e.g. sperm, pollen, seeds, carrying genetic material passed to the next generation) storage is the most successful method of conserving the genetic characteristics of endangered and commercially valuable species, and can be regenerated whenever required. Tissue banks store body tissue from which researchers can extract genomic DNA, potentially to help restore endangered or even extinct organisms.

Case study: the golden lion tamarin

Figure A4.2.21: A golden lion tamarin (Leontopithecus rosalia)Shows this small, critically endangered primate with its distinctive golden mane.

Found in Brazilian tropical rainforest, 90% of the golden lion tamarin’s original habitat has been cleared, leaving small, fragmented remaining forest; it is restricted to one small area of Brazil, making it especially vulnerable to extinction. It was hunted to near-extinction partly because it was mistakenly thought to carry human diseases such as yellow fever and malaria. Its loss would have shortened the rainforest’s food chains, since prey species such as insects and small lizards would have become more numerous while its own predators declined. Combining ex situ captive breeding (e.g. at Bristol Zoo, UK) with in situ habitat protection (e.g. at the Reserva Biológica de Poço das Antas near Rio de Janeiro) has raised wild numbers from a low of about 400 in the 1970s to around 1000 today, demonstrating that combining in situ and ex situ methods can be the best solution for effective species conservation.

Case study: the Bengal tiger

Figure A4.2.22: The Bengal tiger (Panthera tigris tigris)Shows this tiger subspecies, found mainly in India with smaller populations in Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan and China.

With more than 2500 individuals left in the wild, the Bengal tiger is one of the most numerous tiger subspecies but remains threatened by habitat loss and poaching. Tiger reserves established across India in the 1970s under “Project Tiger” (launched 1972) have helped stabilize numbers by preserving habitat and supporting tiger populations; the Sundarbans mangrove forests of India and Bangladesh are the only mangroves where tigers occur, though they are threatened by rising sea level from climate change. Tourism centred on the Bengal tiger raises conservation funds. Captive breeding maintains genetic diversity, tracked via a Bengal tiger studbook that records captive individuals and their breeding history, helping avoid inbreeding and cross-breeding with other tiger subspecies. As with the golden lion tamarin, a combination of habitat protection and captive breeding is needed — but the specific threats differ: the tamarin is endangered mainly due to its restricted range in a small area of Brazil, while the Bengal tiger, a large carnivore requiring extensive hunting territory, is threatened chiefly by habitat loss and poaching. Each species has specific ecological requirements that must be addressed for effective conservation.

The EDGE of Existence programme

International conventions on biodiversity

International conventions have shaped attitudes to sustainability. The UN Conference on the Human Environment (Stockholm, 1972) was the first time the international community met to consider global environment and development together, leading to the Stockholm Declaration, which set targets and triggered action at local and international levels. The 1992 UN Rio Earth Summit, attended by 172 governments, produced the Rio Declaration and Agenda 21, and led to two legally binding conventions: the UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Both are governed by a Conference of the Parties (CoP), which meets annually or biennially to review progress (for example, CoP15 of the CBD was held in Kunming, China, in 2022). The CBD’s “Vision of the Strategic Plan for Biodiversity” (2010–2020) aimed that “by 2050, biodiversity is valued, conserved, restored and wisely used, maintaining ecosystem services, sustaining a healthy planet and delivering benefits essential for all people.” A subsequent “post-2020 Framework” seeks to “bring about a transformation in society’s relationship with biodiversity”, emphasizing the need for transformative change to better value biodiversity and avert its loss.

Phylogenetic diversity and the EDGE programme

DefinitionPhylogenetic diversity measures the evolutionary history represented by a set of species. The extinction of a species representing an entire branch of the “tree of life” means a disproportionately large loss of diversity, since an evolutionarily distinct species and its unique evolutionary history are lost entirely.

The Zoological Society of London (ZSL) applies this concept practically through its “EDGE lists”. EDGE species (Evolutionarily Distinct and Globally Endangered) are those that disproportionately represent threatened phylogenetic diversity: they have an above-median Evolutionary Distinct (ED) score AND are threatened with extinction (Critically Endangered, Endangered or Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List). There are currently over 550 EDGE mammal species (around 10% of all mammal species) and over 900 EDGE amphibian species (around 13% of all amphibian species). “Potential EDGE species” are those with a high ED score but unclear conservation status. EDGE species identified so far span mammals, amphibians, birds, corals, reptiles, gymnosperms (conifers and relatives) and elasmobranchs (sharks, rays and skates). Prioritizing EDGE species for conservation represents an opportunity to stop the loss of phylogenetic diversity; in 2012 the IUCN formally adopted a resolution recognizing the importance of conserving threatened evolutionarily distinct lineages.

Complex issues in conservation decision-making

Many different types of argument shape which species and habitats are prioritized for conservation, and because these considerations are complex, they must be debated to identify the most effective strategies.

Type of argumentExamples
EthicalEvery species has a right to survive; responsibility to safeguard resources for future generations (intrinsic or utilitarian value).
CulturalA community’s culture shapes how it interacts with nature, e.g. the Khmer Dauem community in Cambodia protecting the critically endangered Siamese crocodile and endangered Asian elephant through custom and tradition.
EconomicValue of genetic resources (e.g. improved crops); commercial value of natural capital (e.g. new medicines); value of ecotourism, which benefits from high biodiversity.
PoliticalGovernments set local conservation strategy, so decisions depend on which party is in power; publicly appealing strategies are more likely to be proposed; tensions arise when multiple valid management strategies compete.
Ecological/environmentalConserving rare habitats (e.g. specific habitats required by endemic species); more diverse ecosystems tend to be more stable and provide ecological services (e.g. pollination, food production); loss of species diversity has knock-on effects through the food web.
SocialLoss of natural ecosystems can mean loss of homes, livelihoods and culture; high-biodiversity areas can provide tourism income that supports social cohesion and cultural services.
