Ecosystems as Open Systems

Key terms Ecosystem: a community of organisms and their surroundings, the environment in which they live and with which they interact; the basic functional unit of ecology.
Open system: a system where both energy and matter can enter and exit.
Closed system: a system where only energy can pass in and out, not matter.

An ecosystem cannot realistically be considered apart from its physical environment. Examples such as a woodland or a lake illustrate two important features of an ecosystem: it is a largely self-contained unit, since most organisms spend their entire lives there and their essential nutrients are endlessly recycled around and through it; and it is an interactive system, in that the kinds of organism living there are largely decided by the physical environment, while the physical environment is constantly altered by the organisms.

The organisms of an ecosystem are the biotic component; the physical environment is the abiotic component. Within any ecosystem, organisms are normally found in a particular part, or habitat.

Ecosystems are open systems because both energy and matter can enter and exit. It is this constant input of energy that sustains the ecosystem, and although matter is recycled, organisms can bring matter into and take it out from these natural systems. A closed system, by contrast, exchanges only energy across its boundary. Closed systems only exist experimentally, although global geochemical cycles (such as the carbon cycle) approximate to closed systems.

Figure C4.2.1: The exchange of matter and energy across the boundaries of different systems Two systems are compared against their surroundings. (a) An open system exchanges both energy and matter with its surroundings — energy and matter both enter and exit across the boundary. (b) A closed system exchanges only energy with its surroundings; no matter crosses the boundary.

Sunlight as the Principal Energy Source

Key term Producer: an autotrophic organism that can synthesize glucose; a photosynthetic green plant or chemosynthetic bacterium, forming the first trophic level in a food chain.

To carry out their activities, organisms need energy. The immediate source of energy in cells is ATP, produced by respiration. The energy of ATP has been transferred from sugar and other organic molecules (respiratory substrates), which are obtained from nutrients according to the organism’s mode of nutrition.

The ultimate source of energy for most food chains is sunlight — this is the principal source of energy that sustains most ecosystems. Energy from sunlight is trapped by chlorophyll or other photosynthetic pigments and used to synthesize glucose, transforming light energy into a store of chemical energy. Organisms that can trap energy in glucose in this way are called producers; this chemical energy is then used to synthesize ATP, used in all life processes.

Not all food chains begin with sunlight energy. Exceptions include ecosystems in caves and those in the ocean below the level light can reach, such as hydrothermal vents. Producers in these ecosystems synthesize glucose using energy from chemical reactions instead (chemoautotrophy, covered below).

Nature of science: laws as generalizations

Laws in science are generalized principles created to describe patterns observed in living organisms; unlike theories, they describe phenomena rather than offering explanations, but like theories they can be used to make predictions. It is a useful generalization to say “sunlight is the principal source of energy that sustains ecosystems”, because most food chains do obtain energy this way — although it is more accurate to say “sunlight is the principal source of energy that sustains most ecosystems”, since some food chains (e.g. around hydrothermal vents) use other sources of energy.

Flow of Chemical Energy Through Food Chains

Flow of energy vs cycling of matter Unlike matter, which cycles through ecosystems, energy enters an ecosystem (usually as sunlight) and leaves it as heat. Energy does not cycle but is conserved; once lost from ecosystems as heat it is not available to be reused by living organisms. Nutrients, by contrast, are continually recycled between organisms and the abiotic environment.

Chemical energy flows up through the feeding levels (shown by one arrow style), while energy as light or heat flows in and ultimately out of the system to space (shown by a different arrow style). A separate cycle shows inorganic matter being taken up by producers, passed through the food chain, and returned to inorganic matter in the environment via detritivores and saprotrophs acting on dead organic matter — illustrating that materials are recycled while energy passes through only once.

Food Chains and Food Webs

Key terms Food chain: a sequence of organisms within a community in which each is the food (and hence energy source) of the next, starting with a producer.
Consumer: an organism that is unable to synthesize glucose and so eats other organisms or organic matter to obtain it and other nutrients.
Food web: interconnected food chains in an ecological community.
Trophic level: a feeding level within a food chain.

A feeding relationship in which a carnivore eats a herbivore, which itself has eaten plant matter, is called a food chain. Food chains are simple models that represent the flow of energy through ecosystems. Chemical energy passes to a consumer as it feeds on the previous organism in the chain.

Figure C4.2.3: A food chain Shows a linear food chain with light as the initial energy source: oak (Quercus robur) → oak beauty caterpillar (Biston strataria) → caterpillar-hunting beetle (Carabus nemoralis) → common shrew (Sorex araneus) → red fox (Vulpes vulpes). Both common and scientific (Latin) names are given for each organism, and arrows point towards each consumer, showing the direction of energy flow.
Common mistake Arrows in food chains indicate the direction of energy flow, not which organism is eating which. Arrows go from producer → primary consumer → secondary consumer, and so on.

In an ecosystem, food chains are not isolated; they interconnect with other chains because most prey species must escape more than one predator, and predators exploit alternative food sources when one becomes limited, or take advantage of gluts of food. Consequently, individual food chains may be temporary and are interconnected, forming a food web. As with food chains, arrows in a food web indicate the direction of transfer of energy and biomass. Food chains and food webs tell us about the qualitative feeding relationships of organisms (which are prey and which are predators) but not quantitative data about the numbers of organisms at each level.

Figure C4.2.4: Example of a marine food web Shows an interconnected marine food web with phytoplankton and seaweeds (e.g. sea lettuce) as producers; zooplankton, limpet, common mussel and sea urchin as primary consumers grazing on phytoplankton or seaweed; grey mullet as a further primary/lower consumer; pollack, herring gull, dog whelk, lobster and starfish as consumers feeding at various points in the web; and seal as a top predator that feeds on grey mullet, pollack and lobster. Multiple arrows converge on and diverge from most species, showing that individual species can occupy more than one trophic level depending on which chain within the web is followed — for example, the seal is a secondary, tertiary or quaternary consumer depending on which prey species it has eaten.

Food chains show a sequence of organisms in which each is the food of the next, so each organism represents a feeding, or trophic, level. Chains typically start with a producer and end with a consumer — perhaps a secondary or tertiary consumer. There is no fixed number of trophic levels in a food chain, but typically there are only three, four or five levels (the reason for this is covered later in this chapter).

Supply of Energy to Decomposers

Key term Decomposer: organisms (typically micro-organisms) that feed on dead plant and animal material, causing matter to be recycled by other living things.

Organisms that feed on dead plants and animals, and on the waste matter of animals, are decomposers. Producers support all ecosystems through the constant input of energy and new biomass; decomposers feed at each trophic level of a food chain and are essential for recycling matter, including elements such as carbon and nitrogen. Feeding by decomposers releases inorganic nutrients from dead organic matter, including carbon dioxide, water and ammonia, and ions such as nitrates and phosphates. Sooner or later these inorganic nutrients are absorbed by producers and reused.

Figure C4.2.5: The role of producers and decomposers in ecosystems Shows a cycle in which elements (e.g. carbon, nitrogen) exist either as inorganic molecules in the environment or as organic molecules in biomass. Producers convert environmental inorganic elements into organic molecules in biomass; decomposers break down that biomass (and biomass passed on to consumers) back down into inorganic elements in the environment, completing the cycle.
Common mistake: “decomposer” vs “detritivore” These terms are often used interchangeably, but strictly: decomposer can refer to all organisms that feed on dead biomass and waste, but is also used more specifically for micro-organisms carrying out saprotrophic nutrition (saprotrophs), such as bacteria and fungi. Detritivores eat detritus (dead or waste plant and animal material) and process it in their intestines — examples include earthworms and woodlice. Matter consumed by both groups includes faeces, dead parts of organisms, and dead whole organisms.

Cycling of nutrients

Recycling of nutrients is essential for survival because the available resources of many elements are limited. When organisms die, their bodies are broken down into simpler substances (e.g. carbon dioxide, water, ammonia and various ions), and nutrients are released.

Figure C4.2.6: The sequence of organisms involved in decay Shows two parallel decay sequences. For a dead animal: (1) the body is broken up by scavengers and detritivores (e.g. carrion crow, magpie, fox); (2) a succession of micro-organisms, mainly bacteria, feed — first on simple nutrients (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids), then on polysaccharides, proteins and lipids, then on resistant molecules such as keratin and collagen. For a dead plant: (1) the body is broken up by detritivores (e.g. slugs and snails, earthworms, wood-boring insects); (2) a succession of micro-organisms, mainly fungi, feed in the same three stages, with the final resistant molecules being cellulose and lignin instead. Both sequences converge on stage (3): release of simple inorganic molecules such as CO2, H2O, NH3, and ions such as Na+, K+, Ca2+, NO3− and PO4−, all available to be reabsorbed by plant roots for reuse.

Detritivores begin the process of breakdown and decay, but saprotrophic bacteria and fungi (decomposers) always complete it. Released elements may become part of the soil solution, or react with soil or rock chemicals, before becoming part of living things again through reabsorption and assimilation by plants. Both plants and animals ultimately depend on the activities of saprotrophic micro-organisms to release matter from dead organisms for reuse.

The complete range of recycling processes by which essential elements are released and reused involves both the biota (living things) and the abiotic environment (the atmosphere, hydrosphere, and lithosphere). All essential elements take part in such cycles, one example being the carbon cycle. The supply of inorganic nutrients in an ecosystem is finite and limited, as there are usually no new inputs from outside, but recycling ensures their continuous availability — in contrast to the continuous, but variable, supply of energy as sunlight.

Autotrophs

Energy flow through ecosystems depends on the interrelationship between organisms that can trap and store energy as glucose, and those that release energy through respiration. Energy is required for carbon fixation (photosynthesis) and for the anabolic (condensation) reactions that synthesize macromolecules such as polysaccharides, polypeptides and nucleic acids. Green plants make their own organic nutrients from an external supply of inorganic molecules, generally using energy from sunlight in photosynthesis; this nutrition is described as autotrophic, meaning “self-feeding”.

There are a few exceptions, such as carnivorous plants and root parasites. Broomrape (Orobanche sp.) is a “root parasite”, attaching to the root systems of host plants below ground; its shoots above ground are virtually colourless (chlorophyll-free) with leaves reduced to small bracts, making it heterotrophic rather than autotrophic.

Figure C4.2.7: Not quite all green plants are autotrophic Shows the parasitic plant broomrape (Orobanche sp.), a chlorophyll-free plant with reduced, scale-like leaves that attaches to the roots of a host plant underground and obtains its organic nutrients from the host rather than photosynthesizing.

Photoautotrophs and chemoautotrophs

Producers make their own glucose, converting (“fixing”) inorganic molecules into organic molecules. Plants, algae and some bacteria are producers, of which there are two types:

  • Photoautotrophs (e.g. nearly all plants) transfer solar energy into chemical energy.
  • Chemoautotrophs (e.g. nitrifying bacteria) use chemical energy from oxidation reactions to create glucose.
Photoautotrophs vs chemoautotrophs Redox (oxidation–reduction) reactions release energy and are used by both types of producer. Light is the external energy source in photoautotrophs, while oxidation reactions are the energy source in chemoautotrophs. Iron-oxidizing bacteria (e.g. Gallionella, Sphaerotilus, Crenothrix and Leptothrix species) are chemoautotrophs: they oxidize iron(ii) ions (Fe2+) to iron(iii) ions (Fe3+), taking iron atoms out of water and removing electrons to release energy for metabolic processes, including glucose synthesis.

Heterotrophs

Key term Heterotroph: an organism that obtains organic molecules from other organisms.

Animals and most other organisms (excluding producers) are consumers, using only existing organic matter; they are also known as heterotrophs. Consumers do not contain photosynthetic pigments and so cannot make their own food — they must obtain energy, minerals and nutrients by eating other organisms. Complex carbon compounds such as proteins and starch are digested to monomers (e.g. amino acids and glucose), absorbed into heterotrophs’ cells, and assimilated into the organism’s own polymers. Digestion can be internal or external, but absorption is always into cells. Energy and biomass are passed through a food chain from producers through to top carnivores, so animal nutrition is dependent, directly or indirectly, on plant nutrition.

Herbivores feed directly and exclusively on plants. Carnivores (and omnivores) feed on other consumers.

Top tip! Heterotroph vs consumer vs saprotroph A heterotroph is an organism that obtains organic molecules from other organisms. A consumer is an organism that ingests other organic matter that is living or recently killed. Saprotrophs are heterotrophs that do not ingest organic matter but instead secrete enzymes for external digestion.

Release of Energy in Autotrophs and Heterotrophs

Autotrophs store chemical energy as glucose, whereas heterotrophs must consume other organisms to acquire stored chemical energy. What both have in common is the ability to release this stored energy to produce ATP for life processes: the release of energy in both autotrophs and heterotrophs comes from the oxidation of carbon compounds in cell respiration. Autotrophs are the base of all food chains, providing the energy that maintains ecosystems; heterotrophs pass this energy from one trophic level to the next. All energy is ultimately released from food chains as heat, through the breakdown of organic molecules via respiration.

Common mistake It is often assumed that only animals respire. In fact, all organisms respire, including plants, bacteria, fungi and protists; respiration provides all organisms with energy for growth, synthesis of biological molecules, and other living processes.

Classification of Organisms into Trophic Levels

Key terms Primary consumer: the second trophic level of a food chain; organisms that consume/feed on producers.
Secondary consumer: the third trophic level of a food chain; a carnivore that feeds on herbivores.
Tertiary consumer: the fourth trophic level of a food chain; an animal that feeds on secondary consumers.

Producers are at the bottom of food chains, the first trophic level. Herbivores, which consume producers, are primary consumers. Animals that feed exclusively on other animals are carnivores: those that feed on primary consumers are secondary consumers; those that feed on secondary consumers are tertiary consumers; and so on.

Construction of Energy Pyramids

Top tip! An energy pyramid is a graphical representation showing the flow of energy at each trophic level in an ecosystem.

Feeding relationships in a food chain may be structured like a pyramid. At the start of the chain is a very large amount of energy in green plants; this supports a smaller quantity of energy in primary consumers, which in turn supports an even smaller quantity of energy in secondary consumers.

Figure C4.2.8: A generalized pyramid of energy Shows a pyramid with four horizontal bars of decreasing width, representing producers (100%), primary consumers (10%), secondary consumers (1%) and tertiary consumers (0.1%). Only energy taken in at one trophic level and built into chemical energy in cells and tissues is available to the next trophic level — about 10% of the energy. The reasons given are: much energy is used in cell respiration for growth, movement, feeding and other life processes; not all food eaten can be digested (indigestible matter such as bones, hair, feathers and lignified plant fibres passes out in the faeces); and not all organisms at a trophic level are eaten (some escape predation).
Roughly 10% of the energy available at one trophic level is transferred to the next (the “10% rule”)

Nature of science: pyramids as models

Pyramids are graphical models showing quantitative differences between trophic levels. Pyramids of energy model the flow of energy through ecosystems and are always pyramid shaped, since energy is lost at each trophic level, unlike pyramids of numbers or pyramids of biomass (which can be inverted, as a “snapshot” at a single point in time may show a lower biomass of producers than of the consumers feeding on them, even though production of new producer biomass over time is faster). Pyramids of energy represent productivity — how much new biomass (and stored energy) is made in a period of time in a specific area — and are measured in units of flow (e.g. kg m−2 yr−1 or kJ m−2 yr−1). Because productivity, recorded over time, is always higher in producers than in subsequent trophic levels, pyramids of energy are never inverted.

Figure C4.2.9: Pyramids of energy for different biomes Shows pyramids of energy, drawn to the same scale (100 g m−2 yr−1), for tundra, temperate grassland, temperate forest and tropical rainforest. Each pyramid is built by plotting horizontal bars of equal height but different lengths for each trophic level, using the 10% rule of energy conversion; the pyramids show that tropical rainforest has by far the largest producer level and can support more trophic levels, while tundra has the smallest.
Common mistake Diagrams of pyramids of energy often show inaccurate proportions between trophic levels. The width of each level should be about 10% of the level below it.

Reductions in Energy Availability Between Trophic Levels

At the base of the food chain, green plants transfer light energy into the chemical energy of sugars by photosynthesis, but much of the light energy reaching a green leaf is not retained: some is reflected, some transmitted, and some lost as heat. Sugars are converted into lipids, amino acids and other metabolites; some are used in growth and development (energy stored in organic molecules of the plant body), while the reactions of respiration and other metabolism produce heat energy that is lost. Chemical energy is transferred every time plant tissues are eaten by herbivores, and finally, on death of the plant, remaining energy passes to detritivores and saprotrophs.

Figure C4.2.10: Energy flow through producers and consumers Shows energy flow through a producer: (1) light energy input; (2) energy used to drive life processes; (3) energy stored in organic molecules of new cells and tissues; (4) heat energy lost as a waste product of cell respiration and metabolism; (5) energy transferred to primary consumers when plant tissues are eaten; (6) final energy lost to detritivores and saprotrophs on death and decay of the plant. It also shows energy flow through a consumer, in parallel stages: (1) energy input as chemical energy in the tissues of prey, eaten by the consumer; (2) energy used to drive life processes; (3) energy stored in organic molecules of tissues; (4) energy lost as heat (a waste product of respiration and metabolism) and as undigested food and excreted matter; (5) energy transferred to a consumer at a higher trophic level if caught and eaten; (6) final energy lost to detritivores and saprotrophs on death and decay of the animal.

Around 90% of energy is lost between trophic levels due to: biomass not being ingested (eaten); food not being digested (and absorbed for assimilation); excretion; loss as heat from respiration; and loss as inedible parts such as bones, teeth and fur.

Figure C4.2.11: Loss of energy between trophic levels Illustrates, using a caterpillar (primary consumer) eaten by a shrew (secondary consumer), that of 100 units of energy eaten by the shrew, only 10 units are built into its tissues, while 30 units are lost in respiration and 60 units are lost as gas, faeces and other waste. The example shows that only 10 units out of 100 (10%) become available as tissue to be passed on to the next trophic level.

The percentage of ingested energy converted to biomass depends on respiration rate: animals with greater respiration rates pass on less energy, on average, than those with a lower metabolic rate, and herbivores are generally less efficient at digesting plant material (producing more faecal matter) than carnivores are at digesting meat. Ectothermic (cold-blooded) animals such as snakes and reptiles lose less heat from respiration than endothermic animals such as mammals, which maintain a constant body temperature. Decomposers and detritus feeders are not usually considered part of food chains, but they do play a role in energy transformations: biomass not eaten by consumers is recycled by decomposers and detritivores, which ultimately return the energy to the environment as heat via their own respiration.

Heat loss in ecosystems

Heat loss to the environment in both autotrophs and heterotrophs is due to the conversion of chemical energy to heat in cell respiration; cell respiration and metabolism always produce heat as a waste product.

Figure C4.2.12: Heat is lost from food chains Shows a linear food chain (light energy → green plant/producer → primary consumer → secondary consumer → detritivores and saprotrophs), with photosynthesis and feeding transferring chemical energy along the chain, while respiration at every stage, plus death, waste matter and faeces feeding into detritivores/saprotrophs, continuously loses heat energy to space. The diagram notes that living organisms cannot convert heat back into other forms of energy, so heat is permanently lost from ecosystems.

The transfer of energy in food chains is inefficient. The first law of thermodynamics states that energy cannot be created or destroyed — the total energy in an isolated system does not change, although it is gradually dissipated from useable forms (such as ATP) to less useful, simpler forms (such as heat) as work is done; this dissipation is described by the second law of thermodynamics.

Top tip! Energy transfers are not 100% efficient, so heat is produced both when ATP is produced in cell respiration and when it is used in cells.

Restrictions on the number of trophic levels

Only about 10% of what is eaten by a consumer is built into that organism’s body, and so is potentially available to be transferred on through predation or grazing. Two consequences follow: at each successive stage in a food chain there are fewer organisms or smaller organisms (there is less biomass, but the energy content per unit mass is not reduced); and the number of trophic levels in ecosystems is restricted due to these energy losses.

Energy is lost as heat during respiration, through incomplete digestion, and through excretion of metabolic waste. With roughly 10% of energy passed on at each stage, it is very uncommon for food chains to have more than four or five links between producer and top carnivore. Food chains containing species that expend a lot of energy (such as a warm-blooded predator that loses energy maintaining body temperature and hunting prey) will be shorter than those containing organisms that expend less energy (such as a cold-blooded spider that catches prey using a web, losing less energy through movement and body heat).

Trophic efficiency and world food supply

The human population places heavy demands on food supply and production resources, and world hunger remains a major global problem, raising an ethical question for well-nourished people living alongside others who may be starving. Some people choose a vegetarian diet on the basis that eating meat rather than plant-derived food extends food chains by at least one trophic level: because energy transfer between trophic levels is so inefficient, eating animal products wastes far more of the original energy captured by producers than eating plant matter directly, leaving less food energy available overall.

Primary Production

Key terms Primary production: the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass by autotrophs; occurs when autotrophs grow and reproduce.
Gross primary productivity (GPP): the total gain in biomass per unit area per unit time fixed by producers.
Net primary productivity (NPP): the gain by producers in biomass per unit area per unit time remaining after allowing for respiratory losses (R); this is potentially available to consumers.

Primary production is measured in units of mass (of carbon) per unit area per unit time, usually g m−2 yr−1. The amount of glucose produced by a plant in a specific area over a specific time is gross primary productivity (GPP), equivalent to the rate of photosynthesis; much of this energy is used in respiration and lost as heat. The energy remaining, after allowing for respiratory losses, is stored as new biomass: net primary productivity (NPP).

NPP = GPP − R
(where R = respiratory loss)
Figure C4.2.13: Primary production Shows sunlight driving photosynthesis, which produces glucose (gross primary production, GPP). Some of this glucose supplies energy to drive cellular processes via respiration; the remaining glucose is available to be laid down as new material, i.e. biomass (net primary production, NPP).

NPP is the rate at which plants accumulate new biomass, representing the actual store of energy available as potential food for consumers; it is easier to calculate than GPP because biomass is simpler to measure than the amount of energy fixed as glucose. NPP varies greatly between biomes, being affected by factors that influence the rate of photosynthesis: insolation (sunlight), rainfall (precipitation), and temperature (warmer temperatures speed up the enzyme-catalysed reactions of photosynthesis, though excessive heat causes denaturation).

Rainforests have the highest NPP because of year-round insolation, warm temperatures and high precipitation, giving them a complex layered structure from ground level to canopy, and enough stored energy to support several trophic levels, even up to tertiary or quaternary consumers. Temperate ecosystems (deciduous/evergreen forests, grasslands) have seasonal rainfall, temperature and sunlight, reducing overall NPP compared to rainforests, meaning less stored energy and biomass at each trophic level and potentially shorter food chains. Colder ecosystems such as tundra have slower nutrient cycles, a lack of soil fauna, acidic leaf litter, and stunted tree/shrub growth; evergreen species there maximize productivity by photosynthesizing as soon as spring sunlight is available. Low tundra productivity means large areas are needed to support grazing animals such as reindeer, which must migrate long distances; in the most stressful environments (very low temperature or rainfall) only mosses and lichens may survive, and food chains are usually short, sometimes supporting only the primary consumer level.

Secondary Production

Key terms Secondary production: the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass by heterotrophs; occurs when heterotrophs grow and reproduce.
Gross secondary productivity (GSP): the total gain by consumers in energy or biomass per unit area per unit time through absorption.
Net secondary productivity (NSP): the gain by consumers in energy or biomass per unit area per unit time remaining after allowing for respiratory losses (R).

Much of the biomass eaten by consumers is absorbed (e.g. through the gut) and converted into new biomass within cells: this is gross secondary productivity (GSP). Consumers do not use all the biomass they eat — some energy passes out in faeces and excretion — so only the biomass remaining can be used by the consumer.

GSP = food eaten − faecal loss

Some of the biomass absorbed is used in respiration to support life processes; the remaining energy is available to form new biomass: net secondary productivity (NSP).

NSP = GSP − R
(where R = respiratory loss)

Constructing Carbon Cycle Diagrams

Key term Carbon cycle: the series of processes by which carbon compounds are cycled between the abiotic and biotic environments, involving photosynthesis, feeding, decomposition and respiration.

All the chemical elements of nutrients circulate between living things and the environment through a continuous process of exchange, or “flux”. These movements take more or less circular paths and have both a biological and a geochemical component, so they are known as biogeochemical cycles.

Photosynthesis converts atmospheric carbon (as carbon dioxide) into carbon stored in biomass, first as glucose and then as other organic compounds. Feeding — first by herbivores and omnivores on producers, then by carnivores on other consumers — passes carbon along food chains. Carbon returns to the atmosphere via respiration, in which glucose and other organic compounds are broken down to release ATP, producing carbon dioxide as a waste product; respiration is carried out by producers, consumers and decomposers alike.

Figure C4.2.16: The carbon cycle Shows carbon dioxide in the atmosphere at the centre, linked by arrows to: photosynthesis by green plants, aquatic plants and phytoplankton (removing CO2); respiration by plants, animals and aquatic organisms (releasing CO2); feeding, which passes carbon from aquatic plants/phytoplankton to aquatic animals, and from green plants to animals; death of plants and animals, producing dead organic matter that becomes humus in soil via decay by detritivores and decomposers (saprotrophic micro-organisms), itself releasing CO2. In water, CO2 interconverts with hydrogencarbonate (HCO3−) and carbonic acid (H2CO3). Long-term stores are also shown: sedimentation of skeletons and shells forms chalk and limestone (terrestrial chalk and limestone); under exceptional geological and anaerobic conditions, dead organic matter instead forms fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas). These stores return carbon to the atmosphere via combustion (industry and transport, and combustion of biomass) and via industrial lime production and chalk quarrying. On a geological timescale, sedimentary rocks can be subducted into the mantle, with carbon dioxide returned to the atmosphere through volcanic eruptions.
Common mistake The carbon cycle should be drawn using labelled boxes (stores/reservoirs, such as “CO2 in atmosphere”) and arrows (fluxes) labelled with the process that causes them (e.g. “photosynthesis”, “respiration”, “combustion”) — not pictures of organisms or a factory smokestack. Carbon dioxide, the ultimate source of all carbon in living things, must always be included.

Top tip! If photosynthesis exceeds respiration, there is a net uptake of carbon dioxide; if respiration exceeds photosynthesis, there is a net release of carbon dioxide.

Carbon Sinks, Carbon Sources and Carbon Fluxes

Carbon sink vs carbon source A carbon sink is anything that absorbs more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases — for example, plants, the ocean and soil; these are reservoirs that retain carbon and keep it from entering the atmosphere. Forests and oceans are typically carbon sinks; when plants produce new biomass via photosynthesis, carbon dioxide is removed from the atmosphere (forest regrowth is a form of carbon sequestration). A carbon source is anything that releases more carbon into the atmosphere than it absorbs — for example, the burning of fossil fuels. Deforestation is a source of carbon emissions.
Figure C4.2.17: Carbon fluxes due to processes in the carbon cycle Shows the global carbon cycle as a set of reservoirs (boxes, in gigatonnes of carbon, GT) connected by arrows labelled with the approximate annual flux (GT/year). The atmosphere (about 750 GT) exchanges with land biota (about 1 000 000 GT of standing carbon, with photosynthesis removing about 110 GT/year and respiration returning about 50 GT/year) and with soil (about 1580 GT). The atmosphere also exchanges with the surface oceans (about 970 GT) via ocean–atmosphere diffusion (roughly 90 GT/year in each direction), which in turn exchange with ocean biota (about 3 GT) via photosynthesis and respiration, and with the deep oceans (about 38 000 GT) via upwelling and downwelling (roughly 90–100 GT/year). Farming and litter fall contribute smaller fluxes (of the order of 60 GT/year for litter fall) between land biota and soil. Fossil fuel burning releases about 6 GT/year into the atmosphere (a flow related to human activities, highlighted in the figure). Very slow fluxes connect soil/ocean sediments to sedimentary rocks (sedimentation, about 0.6 GT/year) and sedimentary rocks to the mantle (subduction, about 0.6 GT/year), with volcanic eruptions returning a similarly small flux (about 0.6 GT/year) from the mantle to the atmosphere. Flows sensitive to temperature are marked with a “T”. One gigatonne of carbon is one billion metric tonnes, i.e. 1015 g.

The atmosphere–land biota and atmosphere–ocean fluxes (tens to over a hundred GT/year) are vastly faster than the fluxes between the deep ocean/soil and sedimentary rocks, or between sedimentary rocks and the mantle (a fraction of a GT/year) — the latter operate on a geological timescale, while the former operate on an annual, biological timescale.

Combustion, Fossil Fuels and Carbon Dioxide Release

Biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas are all carbon sinks that vary in when they were formed; all can undergo combustion to release carbon dioxide. Combustion sometimes happens naturally (e.g. following lightning strikes), but human activities have greatly increased combustion rates.

Peat: formed over millions of years from plant matter that is not fully decomposed. Draining wetlands (e.g. the East Anglian fens in the UK) for farming degrades peat: no longer waterlogged, it is eroded by wind, increasing the flux of carbon dioxide to the atmosphere; drier conditions also make peat fires more likely, and climate-related drying further increases carbon emissions from peat.

Fossil fuels: most carbon dioxide emissions come from burning fossil fuels. The discovery of coal and other fossil fuels enabled widespread use of machinery in the nineteenth-century Industrial Revolution, releasing energy and carbon trapped by plants millions of years ago (mostly during the Carboniferous period, roughly 350 million years ago) — carbon that had been locked away is now added to the atmosphere, an entirely new development in geological history. Fossil fuel combustion has altered how energy from the Sun interacts with the atmosphere and Earth’s surface; rising atmospheric carbon and associated positive feedback mechanisms drive global warming.

Fires in the Amazon: parts of Amazonia experience a dry season (July–September). Land-use activities (fragmenting the forest, thinning it through logging, clearing land by burning) increase susceptibility to fire during droughts, and fires often spread beyond their intended boundaries. During the severe 1998 drought, about 39 000 km2 of standing forest caught fire — twice the area clear-cut that year. Forest fires create a positive feedback loop: they kill trees, opening the canopy and allowing more solar radiation and drying at the forest floor, increasing susceptibility to further fires. Projections for 2030 suggest substantial further deforestation and drought/logging damage to the Amazon, potentially releasing a significant additional proportion of the forest’s stored carbon to the atmosphere.

The Keeling Curve

Key term Keeling curve: a daily record of global atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration kept since the 1950s.

Nature of science: long-term, reliable measurement

Accurate measuring devices were established at the Mauna Loa Observatory, Hawaii, in 1957, to monitor the global environment; measurements begun by Dr Charles David Keeling gave the resulting graph its name. Keeling measured carbon dioxide concentrations representative of the “free atmosphere” over a large part of the Northern Hemisphere. Measurement is now the responsibility of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, with monthly data posted publicly.

In early work at Mauna Loa, Keeling noticed carbon dioxide concentration rising in April 1958 to a maximum in May, then declining to a minimum in October 1958, before rising again and repeating the same seasonal pattern in 1959 — nature “withdrawing carbon dioxide from the air for plant growth during summer and returning it each succeeding winter”.

The line shows a clear long-term upward trend, rising from roughly 320 ppm in 1960 to over 400 ppm by the 2010s–2020s, with a fine annual zig-zag superimposed on the overall rising trend.
Figure C4.2.19: Carbon dioxide daily and weekly means at Mauna Loa, 2021–22 A more detailed graph plotted over more than one year, showing the annual rhythm in atmospheric CO2 concentration — lower in Northern Hemisphere summer months and higher in winter months — superimposed on the long-term rising trend; in June 2022 CO2 varied between about 413 and 422 ppm.

The annual rhythm (lower in summer, higher in winter) is due to photosynthesis on land in the Northern Hemisphere, which has more land area than the Southern Hemisphere. We might expect atmospheric carbon dioxide to be maintained by a balance between fixation during photosynthesis and release via respiration, combustion and decay; in fact photosynthesis withdraws almost as much carbon dioxide during daylight hours as is released by all other processes day and night, but not quite as much. Overall, outputs from respiration and combustion exceed inputs into producers via photosynthesis, so atmospheric carbon dioxide continues to rise.

Top tip! The Keeling curve shows both annual fluctuations and a long-term trend. Short-term (yearly) fluctuations are due to seasonal changes in photosynthesis in the Northern Hemisphere. The overall upward trend is due to increasing combustion of fossil fuels, deforestation and other factors that make outputs of carbon dioxide exceed inputs.

Interdependence of Respiration and Photosynthesis

Concept: interdependence There is dependence of aerobic respiration on atmospheric oxygen produced by photosynthesis, and of photosynthesis on atmospheric carbon dioxide produced by respiration. The oxygen produced by autotrophs as a product of photosynthesis is used by organisms (including the autotrophs themselves) in aerobic respiration; autotrophs use the carbon dioxide released by aerobic respiration in photosynthesis. The fluxes between the release of carbon dioxide by aerobic respiration and its intake by photosynthesis are very large, representing a major interaction between autotrophs and heterotrophs.

Recycling of Chemical Elements

Ecosystems recycle chemical elements — not just carbon, but all elements used by living organisms. Nutrients provide the chemical elements that make up the molecules of cells and organisms: all organisms are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, together with mineral elements such as nitrogen, calcium, phosphorus, sulfur and potassium (and several others in smaller amounts). Plants obtain essential nutrients as carbon dioxide and water, from which they manufacture glucose; with the addition of mineral ions absorbed from the soil solution, they build the complex organic molecules they require. Animals obtain nutrients as complex organic molecules of food, which they digest, absorb and assimilate into their own cells and tissues.

Figure C4.2.20: Nutrient cycles Shows a cyclical diagram: inorganic molecules or ions are absorbed by producers, becoming organic molecules in producers; feeding and digestion transfers these into organic molecules in consumers; death of producers and consumers, and decomposition, transfers organic matter into organic molecules in decomposers; decomposition then releases the elements back to inorganic molecules or ions in the environment, ready for absorption again — completing the cycle. Decomposers play a key role by breaking down detritus and waste products into simple inorganic chemicals released into the environment, which are absorbed by organisms and used to synthesize complex biological molecules such as proteins, fats, polysaccharides and DNA, before the cycle repeats.
Top tip! You need to know the carbon cycle in detail, but are not required to know the details of other nutrient cycles, such as the nitrogen cycle.
