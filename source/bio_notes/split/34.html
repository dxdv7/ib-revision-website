Solvation with water as the solvent

Solvation is the interaction of a solvent with the molecules or ions (the solute) that dissolve in it. Water is an ideal solvent because of its polarity: the hydrogen ends of the V-shaped molecule are partially positive, and the oxygen end is partially negative. Water molecules can therefore interact with the positive and negative charges of a solute, forming hydrogen bonds or ion-dipole forces (with ions). Solvent polarity is the most important factor in determining how well a solvent solvates a given solute; polar water molecules orient the appropriately charged part of the molecule towards the solute, stabilizing the system and creating a hydration shell around each solute particle.

DefinitionSolvation: the interaction of a solvent with dissolved molecules or ions.

Water movement from less to more concentrated solutions

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion involving only water molecules. Water is small enough to move through the phospholipid bilayer directly, though it also moves through specific pore proteins called aquaporins. Osmosis is the net (overall) passive movement of water molecules from a region of lower solute concentration (more dilute) to a region of higher solute concentration (more concentrated), across a selectively (partially) permeable membrane.

Figure D2.3.2: Water moves by osmosis from a less concentrated to a more concentrated solutionShows a membrane separating a dilute solution (low concentration of solute molecules) from a concentrated solution (high concentration of solute molecules); water molecules are shown moving across the membrane in both directions, but with a net movement of water from the dilute side to the concentrated side.

Depending on the relative solute concentrations either side of a membrane, a solution can be described as hypotonic, hypertonic, or isotonic relative to a cell.

DefinitionsHypotonic: the external solution is less concentrated than the cell cytoplasm, so there is a net inflow of water into the cell by osmosis. Hypertonic: the external solution is more concentrated than the cell cytoplasm, so there is a net outflow of water from the cell by osmosis. Isotonic: the external solution has the same concentration as the cell cytoplasm, so there is no net entry or exit of water by osmosis.
Figure D2.3.3: Flow of water into or out from a cell depends on the osmotic concentrationThree panels, each showing a cell membrane with aquaporins, cytoplasm above the membrane and an external solution below: (a) Hypotonicity – the external solution has fewer solute particles than the cytoplasm, so water moves net into the cell. (b) Isotonicity – solute concentration is equal on both sides, so there is no net water movement. (c) Hypertonicity – the external solution has more solute particles than the cytoplasm, so water moves net out of the cell.
Terminology checkThere is no verb “to osmosis”. Always say that water “moves by osmosis”, never that water “osmosises”. To remember hypotonic vs hypertonic: hypo = low solute concentration (relative to the cell), hyper = high solute concentration (relative to the cell).

Water movement by osmosis into or out of cells

The direction of net water movement can be predicted from whether the solution surrounding a cell is hypotonic or hypertonic. In an isotonic solution, there is no net movement of water because solute concentration is equal on both sides of the membrane; this is described as dynamic equilibrium — water continues to move across the membrane in both directions (the situation is dynamic), but there is no overall (net) change in water concentration on either side (the situation is at equilibrium).

Isotonic solutions still involve water movementIt is incorrect to say there is no movement of water in an isotonic solution — water does move across the membrane, but movement out of the cell is exactly balanced by movement into the cell, so there is no net osmosis.

Changes in plant tissue bathed in hypotonic and hypertonic solutions

When plant tissue is bathed in an isotonic solution, there is no net entry or exit of water and the tissue retains the same dimensions and mass. In a hypertonic solution, the tissue decreases in dimensions and mass (net water loss); in a hypotonic solution, it increases in dimensions and mass (net water gain). This principle underlies a classic experiment to determine the solute (osmotic) concentration of potato tuber tissue: strips or cylinders of potato are bathed in a range of sucrose solutions of known concentration, and changes in length or mass are measured after a fixed time. The concentration of sucrose solution that causes no change in length or mass is isotonic with the potato cell cytoplasm, and thus reveals its osmotic concentration.

Standard error (SE) = standard deviation (SD) ÷ √n

The standard error represents how well a sample mean approximates the population mean; the larger the sample size (n), the smaller the SE, and the closer the sample mean is expected to be to the true population mean. Error bars (±1 SE) are plotted above and below each mean value on a graph to show this variability. Non-overlapping error bars indicate a significant difference between mean values; overlapping error bars suggest the difference is not significant.

Effects of water movement on cells that lack a cell wall

In an animal cell, the absence of a protective cellulose wall creates a serious problem for water relations. A red blood cell placed in pure water or a hypotonic solution will swell and burst (lyse) from the pressure of excess water entering by osmosis. The same cells placed in a hypertonic solution shrink due to net water loss; such shrunken cells are described as crenated, and are less able to move smoothly through capillaries, increasing the risk of blood clots. To avoid these problems, the osmotic concentration of body fluids (blood plasma and tissue fluid) in mammals is very carefully regulated to remain isotonic with the cells (an aspect of osmoregulation).

Figure D2.3.7: Osmosis in animal cellsShows three outcomes for an animal cell (such as a red blood cell) in solutions of different tonicity: in a hypotonic external solution, there is net inflow of water and the cell swells and bursts; in an isotonic external solution, there is no net water movement and the cell keeps its normal shape; in a hypertonic external solution, there is net outflow of water, the cell solution becomes more concentrated, and the cell outline becomes crinkled (crenated).

Osmosis in freshwater aquatic unicellular animals

Many unicellular animals live in freshwater, where the external medium is normally hypotonic to their cell contents, causing a continuous net inflow of water and a danger of the plasma membrane rupturing under internal pressure. The protozoan Amoeba deals with this using a contractile vacuole, a tiny water pump that continuously expels excess water from the cell. If the cytoplasm is temporarily anaesthetized (stopping the contractile vacuole from working), the organism quickly bursts, demonstrating the vacuole’s importance.

Figure D2.3.8: Amoeba – the role of the contractile vacuoleShows an Amoeba cell surrounded by pond water, which is less concentrated than the cytosol of the Amoeba, causing a net inflow of water over the whole cell surface membrane. Inside the cytoplasm, a contractile vacuole collects this excess water and periodically expels it from the cell by a pumping action, preventing the cell from bursting.
DefinitionContractile vacuole: a small vesicle in the cytoplasm of many freshwater protists that expels excess water.

Effects of water movement on cells with a cell wall

Osmosis in a plant cell, protected by a cellulose cell wall, produces different outcomes from those in animal cells. When the external solution is hypotonic to the cell solution, there is net inflow of water, diluting the cell solution; the permanent (central) vacuole swells, pushing its membrane (the tonoplast) against the cytoplasm, which in turn presses against the cell wall, increasing turgor pressure. A cell in this state is described as turgid. The developing pressure (from the stretched but inextensible cell wall) eventually prevents further water uptake; the cell wall protects the delicate cell contents from bursting, though the resulting tissue may be quite rigid.

When the external solution is hypertonic to the cell solution, there is net outflow of water, concentrating the cell solution. As the volume of the cytoplasm and vacuole decreases, the cytoplasm pulls away from parts of the cell wall (though contact may be maintained at points of cytoplasmic connection between cells). The cell becomes soft and less rigid — described as flaccid — and is said to be plasmolysed (plasmo = cytoplasm, lysis = splitting).

Figure D2.3.9: Osmosis in plant cellsShows three panels of a plant cell (with a fully permeable cellulose cell wall, a partially permeable plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and a permanent vacuole bounded by the tonoplast membrane): in a hypotonic external solution, there is net inflow of water, diluting the cell solution, and the cell wall is stretched (this stretching prevents rupture of the plasma membrane); in an isotonic external solution, there is no net water movement; in a hypertonic external solution, there is net outflow of water, the cell solution becomes more concentrated, and the cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall — the cell is plasmolysed.
DefinitionsTurgor pressure: hydrostatic pressure in a cell that pushes the plasma membrane against the cell wall. Turgid: condition where a plant cell’s vacuole is full of water, pushing the cell membrane against the cell wall. Flaccid: a plant cell that has become soft and less rigid because its cytoplasm has shrunk and contracted away from the cell wall through water loss. Plasmolysis: condition in hypertonic solutions where the plant cell membrane pulls away from the cell wall due to reduced cell volume and pressure.

Turgor pressure, made possible by the cell wall, provides support to plant tissues in the absence of a skeleton or shell. Plasmolysed plant tissue can survive for a time under hypertonic conditions, but not indefinitely: as the plasma membrane moves away from the cell wall, solute can enter the space between wall and membrane, and over time this can damage the membrane.

Medical applications of isotonic solutions

An intravenous (IV) drip delivers fluids directly into a patient’s circulatory system, used to treat dehydration (replenishing lost salts and water) and to deliver medicine. A sterile saline solution of 0.9% NaCl is safe to use because it is isotonic with body tissues and blood cells. If a hypotonic solution were introduced instead, it would lower the solute concentration of blood plasma, causing red blood cells to swell and burst (reducing oxygen delivery to cells); a hypertonic (high-salt) solution would cause water loss from red blood cells, making them move less freely through capillaries and increasing the risk of blood clots.

Similarly, donated human organs intended for transplant must be kept in a saline solution that is isotonic with the cells of the tissues and organs, to prevent cell damage from water uptake or loss during transit to the recipient.

Higher Level

Water potential as the potential energy of water per unit volume

It is useful to quantify the tendency of water to move from one place to another, to predict the direction and degree of water movement. Water potential is used to quantify the tendency of water to move from dilute to concentrated solutions by osmosis; it is the potential energy of water per unit volume. Because water movement generates pressure against a membrane, water potential is measured in kilopascals (kPa), and represented by the Greek letter ψw (psi). Because the absolute quantity of potential energy of water cannot be measured directly, water potential values are standardized relative to pure water at standard atmospheric pressure and 20 °C (which is given a water potential of zero).

DefinitionWater potential: the potential energy of water per unit volume, relative to pure water, denoted ψw (psi) and expressed in units of pressure (kPa).
Higher Level

Movement of water from higher to lower water potential

Water with a low solute concentration has a high water potential and therefore high potential energy for movement, whereas water with a high solute concentration has a low water potential and lower potential energy for movement. In a solution, water molecules form hydrogen bonds with solute particles, restricting the water molecules’ freedom of movement and lowering their potential energy — so water containing solute always has a lower water potential than pure water. Water therefore always moves from a region of higher water potential (lower solute concentration, higher potential energy) to a region of lower water potential (higher solute concentration, lower potential energy).

Higher Level

Solute potential and pressure potential

The water potential of a cell has two components: the concentration of solute (solute potential) and the pressure within the cell (pressure potential). Dissolving any solute in pure water lowers the water potential (makes it more negative); the highest possible water potential (zero) is found in pure water.

Figure D2.3.10: Water potential becomes more negative as solute is added to a solutionShows pure water with the highest water potential (0 kPa); a dilute solution of solute with a lower (negative) water potential, e.g. −50 kPa; and a concentrated solution of solute with an even lower (more negative) water potential, e.g. −100 kPa — illustrating that increasing solute concentration makes water potential progressively more negative.
ψw = ψs + ψp

where ψw = water potential, ψs = solute potential, and ψp = pressure potential. Both solute and pressure potentials are measured in kPa (their effect on water potential). Because adding solute always lowers water potential, solute potential values range from zero downwards (always zero or negative). Pressure potentials, resulting from water molecules pushing against the cell membrane and cell wall in plant, fungal and prokaryote cells, are generally positive inside cells; negative pressure potentials can occur inside xylem vessels, where water is drawn up the plant under tension.

Higher Level

Water potential and water movements in plant tissue

As established by the potato tissue experiments, plant cells gain water in a hypotonic solution and lose water in a hypertonic solution by osmosis. The changes in solute and pressure potential differ between these two situations.

In a hypotonic solution

As water moves into the cell, pressure potential increases (more water molecules exert greater pressure against the plasma membrane). The solute concentration inside the cell decreases (relative to the number of water molecules), so solute potential becomes less negative. Overall, the water potential of the cell increases, due to the combination of a decreasingly negative solute potential and an increasingly positive pressure potential.

In a hypertonic solution

As water moves out of the cell, pressure potential decreases (fewer water molecules exert less pressure against the plasma membrane). The solute concentration inside the cell increases (relative to the number of water molecules), so solute potential becomes more negative. Overall, the water potential of the cell decreases, due to the combination of an increasingly negative solute potential and a decreasing pressure potential.
