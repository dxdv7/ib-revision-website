Introducing the Nervous System

The nervous system is composed of the central nervous system (CNS), consisting of the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS), the nerves running to and from the CNS. The PNS enables communication between the CNS and all parts of the body.

Nerve cells are called neurons. A neuron has a cell body containing the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm. Multiple short, elongated cytoplasmic fibres called dendrites project from the cell body, forming connections to other neurons.

Neurons are specialized for transmitting information as impulses. An impulse is a momentary reversal in the electrical potential difference across a neuron’s membrane. Transmission occurs at 30–120 metres per second in mammals, so nervous coordination is extremely fast, and because impulses travel to particular points connected by fibres, their effects are localized rather than diffuse.

Key terms Central nervous system (CNS): in vertebrates, the brain and spinal cord.
Peripheral nervous system (PNS): in vertebrates, nerves that convey sensory information to the CNS, and nerves that convey impulses to muscles and glands (effector organs).
Nerve: a bundle of many nerve fibres connecting the CNS with parts of the body.
Neuron: a cell within the nervous system that carries electrical impulses.
Dendrite: a fine fibrous structure on a neuron that receives impulses from other neurons.

Neuron Structure and Types

Three types of neuron make up the nervous system:

  • Motor neurons have many fine dendrites bringing impulses towards the cell body, and a single long axon carrying impulses away from the cell body to an effector (a muscle or gland).
  • Sensory neurons have a cytoplasmic fibre called a dendron running to the cell body (starting at the receptor). The cell body sits part-way along the neuron, with the dendron running to it and an axon running away from it.
  • Relay neurons (also called intermediate neurons or interneurons) have numerous short fibres and occur in the CNS, connecting sensory neurons to motor neurons.
Common mistake Do not confuse effectors and receptors. Receptors detect stimuli; effectors respond to them. Sensory neurons pass impulses from receptors to the CNS. Motor neurons stimulate effectors (muscles or glands) to bring about movement.

A neuron is surrounded by supporting cells. Schwann cells wrap around the axons of neurons, forming an insulating structure called the myelin sheath. Myelin consists largely of lipid (giving it high electrical resistance) and also contains protein (15–25% of dry mass). Frequent gaps occur along the sheath between individual Schwann cells; these gaps are called nodes of Ranvier.

Key terms Schwann cells: cells of the peripheral nervous system that wrap around the axons of motor and sensory neurons to form the myelin sheath.
Myelin sheath: an insulating sheath around the axons of nerve fibres, formed by Schwann cells.
Nodes of Ranvier: gaps in the myelin sheath around a myelinated nerve fibre.
Motor neuron: nerve cell that carries impulses away from the CNS to an effector.
Axon: fibre carrying impulses away from the cell body of a neuron.
Sensory neuron: nerve cell carrying impulses from a sense organ or receptor to the CNS.
Receptor: a cell specialized to respond to stimulation by producing an action potential.
Dendron: fibre carrying impulses towards the cell body of a neuron.
Relay neuron: a specialized CNS cell that relays an electrical impulse from a sensory neuron to a motor neuron.

The Resting Potential

An impulse is not an electrical current flowing along a wire – it is a momentary reversal in electrical potential difference across the neuron membrane, caused by a change in the position of charged ions between the inside and outside of the membrane. This reversal flows from one end of the neuron to the other in a fraction of a second.

Between impulses, a neuron is often said to be “resting”, but this is misleading: the membrane is actively setting up an electrical potential difference known as the resting potential – the potential difference across a nerve cell membrane when it is not being stimulated, normally about −70 millivolts (mV). After an impulse has passed, this difference is re-established and the fibre is said to be repolarized.

The resting potential results from the active transport of potassium ions (K+) into the cell and sodium ions (Na+) out of the cell, via a Na+/K+ pump using energy from ATP. Three Na+ are pumped out for every two K+ pumped in across the plasma membrane. This leaves the tissue fluid outside the neuron with many more positive ions than the cytoplasm inside, developing a negative charge inside relative to outside – the resting neuron is said to be polarized.

The membrane potential is the difference in charge between the inside and outside of a neuron created by this unequal ion distribution. The next event is the passage of an impulse, known as an action potential.

Top tip! Energy from ATP (derived from aerobic respiration) drives the pumping of sodium and potassium ions in opposite directions across the plasma membrane. Even when “doing nothing”, the nervous system continually uses ATP to maintain the resting potential.

Key terms Resting potential: the potential difference across a nerve cell membrane when it is not being stimulated; normally about −70 mV.
Membrane polarization: a state in which a lipid membrane has a positive electrical charge on one side and a negative charge on the other.
Membrane potential: the difference in charge between the inside and outside of a neuron, created by the unequal distribution of ions across the cell membrane.

Nerve Impulses as Action Potentials

A stimulus causes positively charged sodium ions to flow into the axon cytoplasm, reversing the polarity of the axon. The action potential is the potential difference produced across the plasma membrane of a stimulated nerve cell, reversing the resting potential from about −70 mV to about +40 mV. Because the nerve impulse involves the movement of positively charged ions, producing a potential difference (voltage) across the membrane, it is electrical in nature. Nerve impulses are action potentials that are propagated (spread) along nerve fibres.

At any one point on the fibre, the action potential exists for only about two-thousandths of a second (2 milliseconds) before the resting potential begins to be re-established, so transmission is exceedingly quick.

In myelinated axons, the nodes of Ranvier (occurring at 1–2 µm intervals) are the only points where the axon membrane is exposed, so the action potential “jumps” from node to node. In unmyelinated dendrons and axons (common in non-vertebrate animals), the action potential must instead travel step-by-step along the entire surface of the fibre.

Key term Action potential: the potential difference produced across the plasma membrane of a nerve cell when stimulated, reversing the resting potential from about −70 mV to about +40 mV.

Variation in the Speed of Nerve Impulses

An unmyelinated fibre with a large diameter transmits an action potential much faster than a narrow fibre, because transmission speed depends on the resistance offered by the axoplasm (the cytoplasm inside the axon). The narrower the fibre, the greater its resistance and the lower its conduction speed; a larger diameter also allows more ion channels per length of axon.

Nervous systems have evolved two contrasting mechanisms for increasing conduction speed. One is the development of giant axons, several times larger in diameter than typical large axons – found, for example, in squid (Loligo sp.). The other is the myelin sheath, formed by lipid-rich Schwann cells wrapping the axon. Either mechanism, alone or combined, is used across many animal groups, vertebrate and invertebrate.

Myelination speeds transmission because the axon membrane is only exposed at the nodes of Ranvier, so the action potential jumps from node to node; the electrical resistance of the myelin sheath prevents polarity reversal elsewhere. In unmyelinated fibres, the action potential must instead travel step-by-step along the whole surface – a comparatively slow process.

Action potential propagation velocity is directly correlated with axon diameter: the larger the diameter, the faster the propagation, because there is less resistance to ion flow. Myelination further increases velocity by allowing the action potential to jump between nodes.

Top tip! Some invertebrates, such as the squid and the earthworm, have giant fibres allowing fast transmission of action potentials, although not as fast as in myelinated fibres.

Correlation and the Coefficient of Determination

The strength of a correlation, such as that between axon diameter and conduction speed, can be tested using the coefficient of determination (R2), which measures how closely data fit a regression line – the line of best fit with the smallest overall distance to the data points.

Regression line: y = mx + b, where m is the slope and b is the y-intercept; y is the dependent variable and x is the independent variable.

R2 ranges from 0 to 1 (0% to 100%). If R2 = 0, the dependent variable cannot be predicted from the independent variable; if R2 = 1, it can be predicted without error. A coefficient of determination of about 70% is generally considered a good correlation. R2 is calculated by finding the Pearson correlation coefficient (R) and squaring it, then expressing the result as a percentage. R2 indicates the proportion of variance in the dependent variable predicted by the independent variable – for example, an R2 of 0.50 means 50% of the variance in y is predicted from x.

Synapses

A synapse is the link point between neurons, or between a neuron and an effector cell. It consists of the swollen tip (synaptic knob) of the axon of one neuron (the presynaptic neuron) and the dendrite or cell body of another neuron (the postsynaptic neuron). The two neurons are extremely close but have no direct contact – they are separated by a tiny gap, the synaptic cleft, about 20 nm wide.

An action potential can only cross this cleft via chemicals called neurotransmitters – small molecules that diffuse quickly. They are produced in the Golgi apparatus of the synaptic knob and held in vesicles before release. Because neurotransmitters only cross from the presynaptic to the postsynaptic membrane, a signal can pass in only one direction across a typical synapse.

Acetylcholine (ACh) is a common neurotransmitter (neurons releasing it are cholinergic neurons); norepinephrine is another (found in adrenergic neurons). In the brain, glutamic acid and dopamine are commonly occurring transmitters.

Top tip! Acetylcholine exists in many types of synapse, including neuromuscular junctions.

A neuromuscular junction is a specialized synapse between a motor neuron ending and a muscle fibre, converting the motor neuron’s electrical impulse into electrical activity in the muscle fibre. When an action potential arrives, vesicles of acetylcholine are released and bind receptors on the sarcolemma (the muscle fibre’s plasma membrane), triggering the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which leads to muscle contraction.

Synaptic Transmission: Release of Neurotransmitter and the Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential

How synaptic transmission works 1. The arrival of an action potential at the synaptic knob changes the polarity of the presynaptic membrane, opening calcium ion channels; calcium ions flow in from the synaptic cleft.
2. The calcium ions cause vesicles of transmitter substance to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing transmitter into the synaptic cleft (calcium acts here as an intracellular signalling chemical).
3. The transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds to a specific receptor protein in the postsynaptic membrane. Each receptor also acts as an ion channel, opening instantly on binding. When ACh binds its receptor, an Na+ channel opens; as Na+ rushes in, the postsynaptic membrane’s polarity reverses. As more ACh molecules bind, this reversal becomes increasingly likely to reach the threshold level (a process called facilitation); once threshold is reached, an action potential is generated in the postsynaptic neuron – the generation of an excitatory postsynaptic potential.
4. The transmitter on the receptors is immediately inactivated by enzyme action – for example, the enzyme cholinesterase hydrolyses ACh to choline and ethanoic acid, which are inactive. This closes the receptor’s ion channel, allowing the resting potential to be re-established.
5. The inactivated products re-enter the presynaptic knob, are resynthesized into transmitter substance, and are repackaged for reuse.
Common mistakes It is not enough to say the neurotransmitter “moves” across the synaptic cleft – state that it reaches the postsynaptic membrane by diffusion, from high to low concentration. Do not forget to mention the removal of neurotransmitter by an enzyme such as cholinesterase when describing how a synapse functions.

Higher Level Depolarization and Repolarization During Action Potentials

An action potential is triggered by a stimulus received at a receptor cell or sensory nerve ending. The stimulus energy causes a temporary, local reversal of the resting potential: the membrane is briefly depolarized. Afterwards, polarity returns towards the resting potential – repolarization. When the resting potential is restored, the axon is said to be repolarized.

This change occurs because of ion channels – globular transmembrane proteins with a central pore and a gate that can open and close. One type is permeable to sodium ions, another to potassium ions; during the resting potential, both types are closed. The stimulus energy first opens the gates of sodium channels, and sodium ions diffuse in down their electrochemical gradient. Because these channels open in response to changes in membrane potential difference (voltage), they are called voltage-gated channels.

Top tip! Sodium ions are predominantly outside the membrane; when they enter, the inside becomes more positively charged. Potassium ions are predominantly inside; when they flood out, the inside becomes more negatively charged.

The Electrochemical Gradient

An ion’s electrochemical gradient depends on both its electrical properties (charge – an ion is attracted to an opposite charge) and its chemical properties (concentration – an ion tends to move from high to low concentration).

With sodium channels open, the cytoplasm becomes progressively more positive relative to the outside. When the charge has reversed from −70 mV to +40 mV, an action potential has been created. This voltage change causes further sodium channels to open, since channels open or close depending on whether a threshold membrane potential has been reached. Almost immediately after the peak, sodium channels close and voltage-gated potassium channels open; potassium ions exit down their electrochemical gradient, and the interior becomes less positive again – repolarization. The potassium channels then also close, and the resting potential is finally re-established by the sodium–potassium pump together with facilitated diffusion.

Key terms Depolarization: a temporary, local reversal of the resting potential difference of the membrane that occurs when an impulse is transmitted along the axon.
Repolarization: the return of polarity towards the resting potential following depolarization.
Voltage-gated channels: transmembrane proteins forming ion channels that are activated by changes in the electrical membrane potential of a neuron.

The Refractory Period

Following depolarization (Na+ influx) and repolarization (K+ efflux), the ionic distribution across the membrane is largely reversed from normal. Before the neuron can fire again, the resting potential must be restored via the sodium–potassium pump. For a brief period – the refractory period, lasting only 5–10 milliseconds – the fibre is not excitable, because a large excess of sodium ions inside prevents further influx. As the resting potential is progressively restored, generating another action potential becomes increasingly possible. Because of the refractory period, the maximum frequency of impulses is between 500 and 1000 per second. Since recently depolarized membrane will not depolarize again during its own refractory period, the action potential only travels in one direction.

Common mistake It is incorrect to say that the sodium–potassium pump causes the falling phase of the action potential by pumping Na+ back out. It is the opening of potassium channels, allowing K+ to leave the axon, that returns the membrane to a negative potential difference.

The All-or-Nothing Principle

A stimulus must reach or exceed a minimum intensity, the threshold potential, to initiate an action potential. Either depolarization is sufficient to fully reverse the potential difference (from −70 mV to +40 mV), or it is not: with sub-threshold stimuli, the sodium influx is quickly reversed and the full resting potential is re-established, so no action potential arises.

As stimulus intensity increases, the frequency at which action potentials pass along the fibre increases (individual action potentials are always of the same, standard strength), up to the maximum permitted by the refractory period. This allows an effector or the brain to recognize stimulus intensity from the frequency of action potentials, not their size.

Key terms Refractory period: the period immediately following stimulation when a nerve or muscle is unresponsive to further stimulation, because the voltage-gated sodium channels are closed and will not respond to voltage changes.
Threshold potential: the critical level to which a membrane potential must be depolarized to start an action potential; if not reached, the neuron remains at rest and no action potential is triggered.

Higher Level Propagation by Local Currents and Oscilloscope Traces

Propagation of an Action Potential Along a Nerve Fibre

The area of an axon depolarized during an action potential sets up local currents: positive charges flow towards adjacent negative areas, depolarizing the adjacent membrane, so the action potential is conducted along the axon. As sodium ions enter and cause depolarization, they flow to adjacent areas, making those areas less negative; this leads to further depolarization and the opening of voltage-gated sodium channels there, moving the action potential along the axon. Because recently depolarized membrane cannot depolarize again during the refractory period, the action potential travels in one direction only. Action potentials are therefore propagated along axons via local currents, which induce depolarization of adjacent membrane and, where threshold is reached, generate further action potentials.

Figure C2.2.11: Diffusion of sodium ions inside and from outside an axon can cause the threshold potential to be reached A cut-open section of axon shows Na+ ions entering at the depolarized region and local currents of positive charge spreading to the neighbouring, still-resting region both inside the axoplasm and in the fluid immediately outside the membrane. This local flow of charge brings the neighbouring membrane to its threshold potential, opening its voltage-gated sodium channels and propagating the action potential onward.

Oscilloscope Traces of Resting Potentials and Action Potentials

Oscilloscopes measure the membrane potential across an axon membrane, displaying data as a graph with time (milliseconds) on the x-axis and membrane potential (millivolts) on the y-axis. Such traces allow both the shape of an action potential and the number of impulses per second to be measured and interpreted.

Higher Level Saltatory Conduction

The myelin sheath affects the speed of action potential transmission. Only at the nodes of Ranvier is the axon membrane exposed, and ion pumps and channels are grouped there; elsewhere, the electrical resistance of the myelin sheath prevents depolarization of the membrane. The action potential therefore “jumps” from node to node – a process called saltatory conduction (meaning “to leap”), which greatly speeds up transmission.

By contrast, unmyelinated dendrons and axons (common in invertebrates) undergo step-by-step depolarization along the entire fibre surface – a relatively slow process compared with saltatory conduction.

Key term Saltatory conduction: how an electrical impulse jumps from node to node along a myelinated axon, speeding the arrival of the impulse compared with the slower continuous progression of depolarization spreading down an unmyelinated axon.
Figure C2.2.13: Saltatory conduction between small gaps in the myelin sheath The diagram shows a myelinated axon with two exposed nodes of Ranvier. At each node, arrows show Na+ ions diffusing in and K+ ions diffusing out, while a localized circuit of current flows through the myelin-covered region between the nodes, effectively carrying the depolarization from one node directly to the next in the direction of impulse travel, bypassing the insulated stretch of membrane in between.

Higher Level Effects of Exogenous Chemicals on Synaptic Transmission

Drugs that interfere with neurotransmitter action are exogenous chemicals – they originate outside the body. Some drugs amplify postsynaptic transmission (for example, nicotine); others inhibit synaptic transmission, decreasing it (for example, beta blockers, used to treat high blood pressure); others are stimulants that speed up transmission of electrical impulses (for example, amphetamines). Understanding neurotransmitters and synapses has led to the development of numerous pharmaceuticals for treating mental and neurological disorders.

Neonicotinoids

Neonicotinoids are pesticides, structurally similar to nicotine, that completely block synaptic transmission at the cholinergic synapses of insects. They bind acetylcholine receptors in the CNS synapses of insects, blocking ACh binding and inhibiting synaptic transmission. Because they cannot be broken down by acetylcholinesterase, their effects are irreversible. There are concerns about their impact on the wider insect community, including effects on honeybees.

Cocaine

Cocaine blocks the reuptake of neurotransmitters, primarily dopamine (important in the brain’s “reward and reinforcement” systems), and also affects serotonin and norepinephrine. It binds the transporters that normally remove excess neurotransmitter from the synaptic gap, preventing reabsorption into the presynaptic neuron and so increasing neurotransmitter concentration in the synapse. This amplifies the natural effect of the neurotransmitter on the postsynaptic neuron, producing dependency (via dopamine), feelings of confidence (via serotonin) and energy (via norepinephrine).

In chronic users, the brain comes to rely on this exogenous drug to maintain artificially elevated neurotransmitter levels. The brain responds to increased dopamine by synthesizing new dopamine receptors; if cocaine use stops and dopamine levels return to normal, this increased receptor sensitivity produces cravings and depression.

Higher Level Inhibitory Neurotransmitters, Summation and Postsynaptic Potentials

At an excitatory synapse, the incoming action potential excites the postsynaptic membrane and generates an action potential transmitted along the postsynaptic neuron. Some synapses have the opposite effect: at these inhibitory synapses, the released neurotransmitter opens ion channels through which chloride ions enter or potassium ions leave, making the postsynaptic neuron’s interior more negative – it is hyperpolarized. This makes it more difficult for the postsynaptic cell to generate a nerve impulse in response to excitation from other excitatory synapses on the same cell.

Concept: Interaction Some neurotransmitters excite nerve impulses in postsynaptic neurons; others inhibit them. There are complex interactions between excitatory and inhibitory presynaptic neurons at synapses, operating across a very great number of connections between the very large numbers of neurons present.

Summation

An action potential is only generated in the postsynaptic neuron if the combined effects of excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic potentials exceed the threshold level – this additive process is called summation. Several impulses arriving at the synapse in quick succession from a single axon can trigger an action potential through temporal summation; alternatively, impulses from several different axons can contribute to the total through spatial summation. Summation contributes to decision-making processes in the brain. There are complex interactions between the activities of excitatory and inhibitory presynaptic neurons operating across the vast numbers of connections between the enormous numbers of neurons present.

Key terms Inhibitory synapse: a synapse at which arrival of an impulse blocks forward transmission of impulses in the postsynaptic membrane.
Hyperpolarization: a change in a neuron’s membrane potential to a more negative value, making it less likely to generate an action potential.
Summation: the process that determines whether an action potential is generated by the combined effects of excitatory and inhibitory signals, both from multiple simultaneous inputs (spatial summation) and from repeated inputs (temporal summation).

Higher Level Perception of Pain by Free Nerve Endings

Different receptors respond to different stimuli: mechanoreceptors detect physical change, thermoreceptors detect temperature, and nociceptors detect pain. Many neurons end in encapsulated receptors, but free nerve endings are unencapsulated dendrites of a sensory neuron that detect stimuli that may damage tissue. They are commonly found in skin, the cornea of the eye, tooth pulp and mucous membranes, and are the most common nerve ending in skin, extending into the middle of the epidermis.

Stimuli cause channel proteins called transient receptor potential (TRP) channels to open, allowing positively charged ions to diffuse into the axon. There are many types of TRP channel, each a unique protein, acting as sensors in the epidermis: in response to a specific stimulus, they open an ion channel that lets positive ions flow into the neuron, raising it to threshold and generating an action potential that passes along the sensory neuron to the CNS, where it is perceived as pain. TRPA1 is a sensor for a wide variety of noxious external stimuli, including intense cold, pungent compounds, reactive chemicals and endogenous signals associated with cell damage.

TRPV1 is a single protein that responds to both heat and capsaicin (the chemical compound found in chilli peppers). One explanation is that TRPV1 evolved in some animals as a temperature sensor, and that certain plants then evolved compounds able to activate these sensors to deter predators from consuming them; plants producing capsaicin gained a survival and reproductive advantage and became more widespread – plant evolution driving the dual function of the sensor.

TRP function can also affect pain perception in other ways: sunburnt skin undergoes inflammatory processes that produce compounds such as prostanoids and bradykinin, which reduce the temperature threshold of TRPV1 activation from 43 °C to 29 °C – explaining why a normally comfortable bath or shower temperature can then feel painfully hot.

Higher Level Consciousness

The cerebral hemispheres make up a larger proportion of the brain and are more highly developed in humans than in other animals. Each hemisphere is divided into four lobes, each named after the cranial bone covering it. The human cerebral cortex has become enlarged principally through an increase in total area, with extensive folding to fit within the skull, giving each hemisphere a vastly extended surface via deep grooves. The cerebral hemispheres are responsible for higher-order functions.

The surface of the cerebral hemispheres, the cerebral cortex, is covered by grey matter about 3 mm deep, densely packed with unmyelinated neurons called pyramidal cells, each with a mass of dendrites and a very large number of synaptic connections. Beneath this, the bulk of the hemispheres consists of white matter – myelinated neurons connecting the cortex with the midbrain, hindbrain and spinal cord. The right and left hemispheres are connected beneath by tracts of fibres.

Consciousness is often described as the mind’s subjective experience. Unlike a robot that can unconsciously detect conditions such as colour, temperature or sound, consciousness describes the qualitative feeling associated with those perceptions, together with deeper processes of reflection, communication and thought. Brain-scanning technologies – electroencephalography (EEG), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and functional MRI (fMRI) – have enabled scientists to research the brain mechanisms associated with conscious processing of information.

Concept: Interaction Research has shown that consciousness is a property that emerges from the interaction of individual neurons in the brain – an example of an emergent property arising from interaction.

In EEG, electrodes (small metal discs with thin wires) attached to the scalp detect tiny electrical charges resulting from brain cell activity, which are amplified and displayed as a graph or printed recording. MRI uses a powerful magnetic field, aligning the hydrogen nuclei (protons) of water molecules in the body like compass needles; a pulse of radio waves changes their orientation, and when it is switched off the nuclei revert and emit a radio-frequency signal, from which the scanner computes the three-dimensional location of each nucleus. fMRI is an advanced form of MRI that detects which parts of the brain are active during particular tasks, since active neurons demand more energy and oxygen locally; this is detected as an increase in red blood cell oxygenation using blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) contrast, which distinguishes signals from hydrogen nuclei near oxyhaemoglobin from those near deoxyhaemoglobin – as oxyhaemoglobin concentration increases, the fMRI signal rises.

fMRI data are transformed into three-dimensional images recording the most active brain regions, mapping the spatial organization of human brain function down to sub-millimetre level. The cerebral cortex has been known to be important for consciousness since the nineteenth century, but consciousness is not confined to one brain region; different cells and pathways are engaged depending on the stimulus. fMRI data show that healthy individuals’ brains display more complex, constantly changing patterns of coordinated signalling compared with people in minimally conscious states or under anaesthesia.

Key term Consciousness: the qualitative feeling associated with perceptions such as colour, temperature or sound, together with the deeper processes of reflection, communication and thought.
