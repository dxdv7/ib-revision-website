Amino acids: building blocks of proteins

Proteins make up about two-thirds of the total dry mass of a cell. Unlike carbohydrates and lipids, proteins contain atoms of nitrogen and usually sulfur, in addition to carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Proteins are crucial biomolecules for basic life processes: they are responsible for transport throughout a cell or organism, for maintaining cellular structures and for basic metabolism, among other roles. Haemoglobin and myoglobin transport and store oxygen; proteins form channels and pumps that move ions, water and other molecules through the cell membrane; proteins carry molecules between the cell membrane and intracellular compartments; and many proteins act as enzymes.

Amino acids are the molecules from which peptides and proteins are built — typically several hundred or even thousands of amino acid molecules combine to form a protein. The terms “polypeptide” and “protein” are often used interchangeably, but once a polypeptide is about 20 amino acid residues long, it is generally agreed to have become a protein.

Key termAn amino acid residue refers to an amino acid once it is bonded into a polypeptide chain.

Amino acids carry two functional groups attached to the same alpha (central) carbon atom: an amino group (−NH2, basic) and an organic acid group (carboxyl group, −COOH, acidic). A side-chain part of the molecule, called an R-group, is also attached to this alpha carbon, together with a hydrogen atom. The 20 different amino acids that make up proteins in cells and organisms differ only in their side chains (R-groups); the R-group determines the chemical character of each amino acid, and therefore of the proteins that contain it.

Three specific examples are illustrated using the same generalized skeleton: glycine (R = a single hydrogen atom, the simplest amino acid), alanine (R = −CH3) and leucine (R = a branched −CH2−CH(CH3)−CH3 group). Amino acid abbreviationsAmino acids can be represented by three-letter codes or single-letter abbreviations. For example, glycine is represented by “gly” or “G”, and alanine is represented by “ala” or “A”.

Forming dipeptides and longer chains of amino acids

Two amino acids combine with the loss of a water molecule to form a dipeptide — a catalysed condensation reaction. The reaction, which occurs in ribosomes, takes place between the amino group (−NH2) of one amino acid and the carboxyl group (−COOH) of another amino acid, forming a dipeptide plus a molecule of water. The two amino acid residues are held together by a covalent bond called a peptide bond or peptide linkage.

Key termsPeptide linkage: a covalent bond formed between the amino group of one amino acid and the carboxyl group of another, with the loss of a molecule of water. A tripeptide is a peptide made of three amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds.
Figure B1.2.3: Peptide linkage formationAmino acid 1 (with its own R-group) and amino acid 2 (with a different R-group) are shown approaching each other. The carboxyl group (−COOH) of amino acid 1 reacts with the amino group (−NH2) of amino acid 2. A hydroxyl (−OH) from the carboxyl group and a hydrogen from the amino group combine to leave as a molecule of water (H2O), while the remaining carbon and nitrogen atoms bond together to form the peptide linkage (−CO−NH−), joining the two amino acids into a dipeptide.

When a further amino acid residue is attached by another condensation reaction, a tripeptide is formed; repeating this process builds long strings of amino acid residues into polypeptides and proteins. The start of an amino acid chain is called the N-terminus (the end with a free amino group) and the end is called the C-terminus (the end with a free carboxyl group). Peptide or protein chains are assembled one amino acid at a time in the presence of a specific enzyme, peptidyl transferase, located in the ribosome.

Proteins differ in the variety, number and order of their constituent amino acids. The order of amino acids in the polypeptide chain is controlled by the sequence of bases in DNA. Changing just one amino acid in the sequence of a protein can alter its properties completely. Each protein folds into a particular three-dimensional conformation depending on its amino acid sequence, and this shape determines its function. Some proteins, such as haemoglobin, consist of more than one polypeptide chain linked together.

Dietary requirements for amino acids

A nutrient is a chemical substance found in foods that is used in the body. Some nutrients are essential components of the diet; others are non-essential. An essential nutrient is one that cannot be synthesized by the body and so must be obtained from food. Non-essential nutrients are those that are made in the body, or that have a replacement nutrient able to fulfil the same dietary purpose.

Proteins in the diet are first digested — broken down — by proteases into their constituent amino acids. These amino acids are absorbed into the body and contribute to a pool of amino acids from which new proteins are built, whether the original protein came from animal or plant sources. All 20 amino acids must be present in this pool for protein synthesis to proceed. Any amino acids present in excess cannot be stored: instead they are deaminated, and the resulting −NH2 group is combined with carbon dioxide to form urea, which is excreted. The ten amino acids that humans can synthesize themselves, when necessary, are produced by a process called transamination, in which an amino acid present in excess reacts with another organic acid to form a new amino acid plus a different organic acid.

Figure B1.2.4: The supply of amino acids in human nutritionProteins from animal and plant dietary sources are digested into their 20 constituent amino acids, which are then absorbed into the body. All 20 amino acids must be present for protein synthesis. Any amino acids present in excess cannot be stored — they are deaminated, and the −NH2 group released combines with CO2 to form urea, which is excreted. Ten of the amino acids required by humans can, if necessary, be synthesized in metabolism by transamination; the other ten are essential amino acids that cannot be synthesized and must be present in the diet in sufficient amounts if malnutrition is to be avoided.

About 20 amino acids are needed for the range of proteins made in the human body. Half of these cannot be synthesized in the body, at least at some stage of life, and are therefore essential amino acids. When one or more essential amino acids is in short supply in the diet, the body cannot make sufficient of the proteins it requires — a condition known as protein deficiency malnutrition.

Non-essential amino acids are made from other amino acids: for example, tyrosine is synthesized from the essential amino acid phenylalanine, and cysteine from the essential amino acid methionine. The liver contains enzymes called transaminases and is responsible for non-essential amino acid synthesis by transamination; many of the pathways involved connect to glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. Vegan diets require attention to ensure all essential amino acids are consumed, although a few plant sources — including quinoa, buckwheat, hemp seeds and soy — contain all of the essential amino acids.

The infinite variety of possible peptide chains

Each type of protein has a unique sequence of amino acids, and there are many thousands of different proteins in cells. The proteome is the entire set of proteins found in an individual organism. The 20 amino acids used to build proteins are coded for in the genetic code; a gene is a sequence of DNA that encodes a polypeptide or protein, and typically one gene codes for one protein. Peptide chains can be any length, from a few amino acids to many thousands, and the amino acids can occur in any order.

Examples of polypeptides include enzymes (such as amylase, which digests starch), hormones (such as insulin) and proteins found in the cell membrane. Histone proteins are involved in DNA packing in eukaryotic chromosomes.

P = An, where P = the number of different possible polypeptides, A = the number of different types of amino acid available, and n = the number of amino acid residues in the polypeptide.

Using the naturally occurring pool of 20 different amino acids, this formula shows just how enormous the potential diversity of proteins is: a short polypeptide of only 5 residues could already be assembled in 205 (3,200,000) different ways, while a polypeptide of 25 residues could take on around 2025 different sequences, and one of 50 residues around 2050 different sequences — numbers so large they vastly exceed the number of atoms in the observable universe. This calculation illustrates why the amino acid sequence of a protein provides an almost limitless source of biological diversity in form and function.

Effects of pH and temperature on protein structure

Once a polypeptide chain is constructed, a protein takes up a specific shape, and this shape is closely related to its function — especially for proteins that are enzymes. The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is sensitive to environmental conditions. In extreme cases, proteins, including enzymes, may become denatured.

DenaturationA structural change in a protein that results in a loss — usually permanent — of its biological properties, altering its three-dimensional shape. Denaturation occurs when the ionic interactions, hydrogen bonds and other weak intermolecular forces within a globular protein, formed between different amino acid residues, break down, changing the protein’s shape, including the active site of an enzyme.
Figure B1.2.5: Folding of a protein from its denatured stateA correctly folded protein is shown being exposed to high temperature or incorrect pH, causing it to unfold into a denatured protein with a disrupted three-dimensional shape. If the denaturant is then removed, the diagram shows the protein refolding back into its original conformation — illustrating that, for small proteins, the amino acid sequence alone can determine the final three-dimensional structure.
How denaturation happensRises in temperature, or even a small deviation in pH away from the optimum, can denature proteins. Exposure to heat causes atoms to vibrate more violently, disrupting the weak, non-covalent intermolecular forces that hold a protein’s shape together. For many small proteins, once the triggering conditions are removed, the protein reverts to its original shape — suggesting that it is the amino acid sequence of the protein that ultimately decides its three-dimensional structure. However, more extreme denaturation is often irreversible: cooking a hen’s egg denatures the translucent protein albumen in the egg white, which becomes permanently opaque and insoluble. Small changes in pH can be reversed if the optimum pH is restored, but exposure to strong acids or alkalis usually denatures proteins irreversibly.

Higher Level Chemical diversity in the R-groups of amino acids

The reactive groups of an amino acid — the basic amino group (−NH2) and the acidic carboxyl group (−COOH) — are both bonded to the same carbon atom, and it is these groups that take part in the condensation reactions forming peptide bonds. The remainder of the molecule, the R-group, may vary enormously between different amino acids, giving each amino acid a distinct chemical character. The categories of amino acid found in cell proteins are:

  • Acidic amino acids: have an additional carboxyl group in the R-group (e.g. aspartic acid).
  • Basic amino acids: have an additional amino group in the R-group (e.g. lysine).
  • Amino acids with hydrophilic properties (water soluble): have polar or charged R-groups (e.g. serine).
  • Amino acids with hydrophobic properties (insoluble): have non-polar R-groups (e.g. alanine).
Figure B1.2.6: The range of amino acids used in protein synthesisFour generalized amino acid structures are shown, each with the alpha carbon bearing a carboxyl group and an amino group, but with contrasting R-groups: aspartic acid (an acidic amino acid, with an additional carboxyl group in its R-group), lysine (a basic amino acid, with an additional amino group in its R-group), serine (an amino acid with a polar, hydrophilic R-group), and alanine (an amino acid with a small, non-polar, hydrophobic R-group).
Polar vs chargedA polar molecule (such as water) has an uneven distribution of electrons, so it has regions of positive and negative charge, but its overall charge is neutral. In contrast, a group is described as charged when the number of positive and negative charges are not equal — protons and electrons are not balanced.

Water molecules force hydrophobic R-groups together to minimize disruption to the hydrogen-bonded network of water molecules. Hydrophobic amino acids have little or no polarity in their side chains, so they cannot interact with highly polar water molecules; this is sometimes described as a “hydrophobic force”, but the effect is really caused by repulsion from water rather than any attraction between the hydrophobic groups themselves. This repulsion is also important for the assembly of lipid vesicles and membranes, and for protein folding: polar amino acid side chains tend to be displayed on the outside of a folded protein where they can interact with water, while non-polar side chains are buried on the inside.

Higher Level Levels of protein structure: primary and secondary

The sequence of amino acids determines the precise position of each residue within a structure, and therefore the overall three-dimensional shape of the protein. There are four levels of protein structure, each significant in biology: primary, secondary, tertiary and, in some proteins, quaternary.

Figure B1.2.7: The different levels in the hierarchy of protein structureThe diagram shows, in sequence: the primary structure (a simple linear chain showing the sequence of amino acids joined by peptide linkages); the secondary structure (parts of the same chain coiled into a helical shape); the tertiary structure (the coiled chain further folded into a compact three-dimensional shape that creates an active site); and the quaternary structure (two or more separately folded polypeptide chains associating together to form one larger functional protein).

Primary structure

The primary structure of a protein is the linear sequence of amino acid residues joined by peptide linkages, and it determines the overall shape and properties of the protein. The sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain is controlled by the coded instructions stored in DNA, mediated via mRNA. Changing just one amino acid in the sequence, which can arise through mutation, often drastically alters the protein’s properties.

Common mistakePrimary structure is not simply “a string of amino acids” — the idea of a specific sequence or order is essential to the definition.

Secondary structure

The secondary structure of a protein develops when parts of the polypeptide chain take up a particular shape immediately after formation at the ribosome, becoming folded or twisted (or both). The most common shapes are formed by coiling into an alpha-helix, or folding into beta-sheets. These shapes are permanent and held in place by hydrogen bonds.

Figure B1.2.8: The secondary structure of proteinsTwo structures are shown side by side. The alpha-helix is drawn as a rod-like, right-handed coil of the polypeptide backbone, with regularly spaced hydrogen bonds running roughly parallel to the helix axis, linking the C=O group of one peptide linkage to the N−H group of a peptide linkage further along the chain, holding the coil in shape; R-groups project outward from the helix. Beta-sheets are shown as sections of polypeptide chain lying alongside one another in a pleated, zig-zag arrangement, with hydrogen bonds forming between the backbone atoms of adjacent, parallel sections of chain, and R-groups projecting alternately above and below the sheet.
Figure B1.2.9: BipD, an invasion protein from the bacterium Burkholderia pseudomalleiA ribbon model of the protein BipD (which causes the disease melioidosis) shows extensive regions of alpha-helix (coloured red) and beta-sheet (coloured pale green), connected by flexible loop regions (coloured cream) — illustrating how a single protein can contain substantial stretches of both types of secondary structure.

Hydrogen bonding in regular positions helps to stabilize both alpha-helices and beta-pleated sheets.

Super-secondary structure

Super-secondary structure describes recurring patterns that alpha-helices or beta-sheets commonly adopt within proteins. Four alpha-helices, for example, often form a coiled-coil arrangement called a four-helix bundle, with pairs of adjacent helices further stabilized by salt bridges (ionic bonds) between charged amino acids. Two beta-sheets may stack on top of each other in a “beta sandwich”, as seen in the immunoglobulin fold.

Figure B1.2.10: Super-secondary structure in a proteinPart (a) shows a four-helix bundle: four separate alpha-helices arranged side by side and twisted around each other in a coiled-coil. Part (b) shows a “beta sandwich”: two beta-sheets stacked flat on top of one another.

Super-secondary structure often corresponds to a particular domain — a compact, independently folded structure within a polypeptide chain that usually has a specific function, such as DNA-binding capability or the ability to induce dimerisation between two proteins that share the domain. A protein dimer is a macromolecular complex formed from two protein monomers.

Higher Level Tertiary structure of proteins

The tertiary structure of a protein is the precise, compact structure unique to that protein, which arises as the molecule folds further and is held in a particular complex shape. This shape is stabilized by four different types of bonding, established through interactions between R-groups and other parts of the chain: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide covalent bonds, and hydrophobic interactions (including weak London/dispersion forces).

Figure B1.2.11: Cross-linking within a polypeptideA folded polypeptide chain made of amino acid residues is shown with four types of cross-link labelled. A hydrogen bond is shown as a hydrogen atom shared between an electropositive and an electronegative atom on nearby parts of the chain. A disulfide bond is shown as a strong covalent bond formed by the oxidation of the −SH groups of two cysteine side chains. An ionic bond is shown as an electrostatic interaction between oppositely charged R-groups (for example a positively charged amine group and a negatively charged carboxyl group), which may be broken by changing the pH. London (dispersion) forces are shown coming into play where two or more non-polar atoms lie very close together (0.3–0.4 nm apart).
Top tipAmine (−NH2) and carboxyl (−COOH) groups in R-groups can become positively or negatively charged by gaining or losing hydrogen ions, allowing them to take part in ionic bonding. Two of the 20 amino acids — cysteine and methionine — contain sulfur, so disulfide bridges can form between their side chains in a protein’s tertiary structure. Apart from this example, you are not required to name specific amino acids involved in the bonding that maintains tertiary structure.
Figure B1.2.12: The protein lysozyme — primary, secondary and tertiary structureThe full amino acid sequence of lysozyme is shown written out from the N-terminus to the C-terminus, representing its primary structure. Below this, the same chain is shown with sections drawn as coiled alpha-helices and pleated beta-sheets, representing its secondary structure. Finally, the whole chain is shown folded into its compact, globular three-dimensional shape, representing the tertiary structure — illustrating how the same protein can be represented at each of the three structural levels.
Common mistakeAlthough hydrogen bonds are important in the tertiary structure of proteins, it is the interactions between the side groups (R-groups) of amino acids that determine tertiary structure. Hydrogen bonds along the backbone mainly stabilize the alpha-helix of secondary structure.

Effect of polar and non-polar amino acids on tertiary structure

Amino acids with polar R-groups have hydrophilic properties; when built into a protein in prominent positions, they can influence its properties and function. Amino acids with non-polar R-groups have hydrophobic properties. Hydrophobic amino acids are clustered in the core of globular proteins that are soluble in water. Integral membrane proteins have regions rich in hydrophobic amino acids that help them embed permanently in the membrane; the portions of these proteins found inside the membrane are hydrophobic, while portions exposed to the cytoplasm or extracellular fluid tend to be hydrophilic, matching the non-polar fatty acid tails that form the interior of the membrane.

Figure B1.2.13: Polar and non-polar amino acids in membrane proteinsA diagrammatic cross-section of the plasma membrane (fluid mosaic model) shows a channel protein with a pore, a protein sitting on one side of the membrane only, and a protein that traverses the full width of the membrane. Non-polar amino acid residues are positioned to make the bulk of each protein hydrophobic, compatible with the hydrocarbon tails of the phospholipid bilayer, while polar amino acid residues are positioned on the protein surface exposed to the cytosol, making that part of the protein hydrophilic.
Figure B1.2.14: Polar and non-polar amino acids in the enzyme superoxide dismutaseSuperoxide dismutase, an enzyme common to all cells that breaks down superoxide ions as soon as they form as metabolic by-products, is shown as an approximately saucer-shaped molecule with its active site centrally placed. Non-polar amino acid residues occupy two regions away from the active site. Amino acid residues with positively charged R-groups are positioned near the active site to attract the negatively charged superoxide ions towards it, while residues with negatively charged R-groups are positioned elsewhere to repel superoxide ions away from those regions, helping to direct the ions specifically to the active site.
Integral proteinsProteins that traverse from one side of a membrane to the other side.

Higher Level Quaternary structure: conjugated and non-conjugated proteins

The quaternary structure of a protein arises when two or more polypeptide chains, or proteins, are held together to form a single complex, biologically active molecule.

Conjugated proteins — haemoglobin

Conjugated proteinA combination of a protein and a non-protein prosthetic group.

Haemoglobin is a conjugated protein: it consists of four polypeptide chains (two alpha-chains and two beta-chains), and each chain is held around a non-protein haem group (the prosthetic group), which contains an atom of iron. Conjugated proteins in general may have carbohydrate groups attached (such as in the glycocalyx of cell membranes), lipids, bound metal ions, or other organic groups.

Figure B1.2.15: Haemoglobin — a quaternary protein of red blood cellsEach of haemoglobin’s four subunits is shown as a conjugated protein: a globin protein chain (about 150 amino acid residues, folded as a helix that turns 5–7 times) wrapped around a haem group, a flat prosthetic “helper” molecule made of four pyrrole groups held together by =C− linkages, with an iron(II) atom at its centre. In haemoglobin, four such subunits interlock to form one compact molecule; each red blood cell contains about 280 million molecules of haemoglobin, and each iron atom can combine loosely and reversibly with one molecule of oxygen.
Figure B1.2.16: Structure of haemoglobinFour separate globin subunits (two alpha-chains and two beta-chains) are shown packed together into one globular quaternary structure, each cradling its own haem group with its central iron atom.
Figure B1.2.17: The haem–iron–oxygen complexPart (a) shows the complex ion in haemoglobin: an iron(II) ion (Fe2+) held at the centre of the haem group, coordinated by four surrounding nitrogen atoms of the pyrrole rings and attached to the surrounding protein chain. Part (b) shows that this iron(II) ion can bond reversibly with a single oxygen molecule (O2), which is how haemoglobin picks up and releases oxygen.
Top tipHaemoglobin is not an enzyme. Unlike enzymes, it does not catalyse a chemical transformation of the molecules to which it binds — it simply binds and releases oxygen reversibly.

Non-conjugated proteins — insulin and collagen

Non-conjugated proteinA protein that is not associated with a non-protein prosthetic group. Insulin and collagen are examples.

In 1951, the English biochemist Frederick Sanger determined the first amino acid sequence of a protein, insulin — a major discovery that established that every protein has a precisely defined amino acid sequence. Insulin is a hormone involved in glucose regulation and is an example of a globular protein. It is composed of an A chain and a B chain, linked together by two disulfide bonds, with an additional disulfide bond formed within the A chain itself.

Cysteine residues occur at positions 6, 7, 11 and 20 of the chain; the disulfide bridge formed between the cysteines at positions 6 and 11 is shown explicitly, illustrating how such bridges stabilize the three-dimensional structure of the insulin molecule.
Figure B1.2.19: The formation of a disulfide bridgeTwo cysteine side chains, each ending in a sulfhydryl (−SH) group, are shown reacting by oxidation to form a covalent sulfur–sulfur (S−S) disulfide bond, releasing hydrogen atoms; the reverse reaction, reduction, breaks the disulfide bond back into two separate −SH groups.
Figure B1.2.20: The quaternary structure of human insulinThe insulin molecule is shown as two separate polypeptide chains: chain A (21 amino acids) and chain B (30 amino acids), each running from an N-terminus to a C-terminus. Two disulfide (S−S) bonds link chain A to chain B (inter-chain bridges), while a third disulfide bond joins two cysteine residues within chain A itself (an intra-chain bridge), together holding the two chains in a single stable quaternary structure.

Collagen is the most abundant protein in animals. It gives structure and strength to the body, and is found in bones, muscles, skin and tendons; it is an example of a fibrous protein. The quaternary structure of collagen consists of three left-handed helices twisted together into one right-handed triple helix.

Figure B1.2.21: Collagen — an example of a non-conjugated proteinThree long polypeptide chains are shown coiled together to form a triple helix. Every third amino acid along each chain is glycine, the smallest amino acid, which is small enough to fit into the crowded centre of the helix; the other two amino acids in the repeating triplet are frequently proline and hydroxyproline. Covalent bonds, together with numerous hydrogen bonds, are formed between the three polypeptide chains, holding the triple helix together.

Each polypeptide chain has a repeated triplet sequence, Gly-X-Y, where X and Y can be any amino acid but are frequently proline (X position) and hydroxyproline (Y position). This regular pattern of glycine allows each helical chain to turn every three amino acids and intertwine around the other two chains to form a compact triple helix, held together by interchain hydrogen bonds to form a unit called tropocollagen. Many triple helices lie parallel to one another in a staggered pattern to form fibrils, cross-linked by covalent bonds between neighbouring triple helices; the fibrils then unite to form fibres.

Figure B1.2.22: Observing protein structure — X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopyPart (a) illustrates single-crystal X-ray crystallography: an X-ray beam is directed at a highly ordered protein crystal, and the atoms in the crystal scatter (diffract) the X-rays to produce a pattern of spots on a detector. Part (b) shows an electron density contour map of the small molecule urea, derived from X-ray diffraction data, alongside urea’s structural formula, illustrating how a diffraction pattern is computer-processed into a three-dimensional structure.

X-ray crystallography has traditionally been used to obtain high-resolution images of crystalline proteins: diffracted X-rays generate spots on a detector whose intensity and position can be computer-processed into an electron density contour map showing the protein’s structure and shape. An alternative technique, cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), uses the same basic principle as transmission electron microscopy, but cools samples to cryogenic temperatures (using liquid nitrogen) and embeds them in non-crystalline (vitreous) ice. Randomly orientated protein molecules are struck by an electron beam, producing faint images; thousands of these images are computer-averaged to generate a high-resolution three-dimensional density map, allowing different conformations of the same protein to be distinguished and large, intact complexes (such as ribosomes and viruses) to be studied almost to atomic scale. X-ray crystallography, however, remains much quicker and simpler, and the majority of known protein structures still come from X-ray data — a reminder that new technology alone does not always determine how scientific knowledge is produced or which tools scientists prefer to use.

Higher Level Form and function: globular and fibrous proteins

Globular proteins

Globular proteins take up a roughly spherical shape and are mostly highly soluble in water. Examples include enzymes such as lysozyme and catalase, and hormones such as insulin. Insulin is a very small protein, which allows it to move quickly through the blood, and its shape is recognized by specific receptors on target cell surfaces. Because it is difficult to make such a small protein fold into a stable structure directly, the cell instead synthesizes a longer protein chain that folds correctly, after which the extra piece is removed, leaving the two small chains of the mature hormone, further stabilized by three disulfide bridges.

Figure B1.2.24: Insulin, a globular proteinInsulin is first produced as preproinsulin, a single polypeptide of 102 amino acid residues, by ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) of beta cells in the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas. A signal peptide part of the molecule directs it into the lumen of the RER. Post-translational modification converts preproinsulin into proinsulin, which is stabilized by disulfide bonds and takes shape in the lumen of the RER. Proinsulin is transported from the RER to the Golgi apparatus in vesicles, where further enzyme modification removes the central C chain, leaving mature insulin — the A chain and B chain held together by disulfide bonds — which takes up its tertiary structure as a more or less globular protein.

Fibrous proteins

Some proteins take up a tertiary structure that is a long, much-coiled chain with a long, narrow shape; these are called fibrous proteins, and they are often insoluble. Examples include collagen (a component of bone and tendons) and keratin (found in hair, horn and nails). The collagen molecule consists of three polypeptide chains, each shaped as a helix and wound together as a triple helix, forming a stiff cable strengthened by numerous hydrogen bonds. The ends of individual collagen molecules are staggered so there are no weak points along collagen fibres, giving the whole structure high tensile strength — making the protein well suited to providing structural support in skin, tendons and cartilage.

Figure B1.2.23: Photomicrograph of collagenA photomicrograph of the fibrous protein collagen shows numerous triple helices bound together side by side into thick, ropelike fibres.
Globular vs fibrous proteinsGlobular proteins are compact, roughly spherical, and generally highly soluble in water; their functions (such as catalysis in enzymes, or oxygen transport and hormone signalling) usually depend on a precisely shaped active site or binding site formed by their folded tertiary structure. Fibrous proteins are long, narrow and much-coiled, and are often insoluble; their function is typically structural — providing mechanical strength and support, as in collagen (bone, tendon, skin) and keratin (hair, horn, nails) — rather than depending on a single reactive site.
