ATP as the Molecule that Distributes Energy Within Cells

Cell respiration The enzyme-controlled release of energy from organic compounds to produce ATP.

Living organisms require energy to build and repair body structures and to maintain all the activities of life — movement, reproduction, nutrition, excretion and sensitivity — as well as for life processes within cells. Energy is transferred in cells by the breakdown of nutrients, principally carbohydrates such as glucose. This process, by which energy is made available from nutrients in cells, is called cell respiration.

Energy in respiration is transferred in small quantities; much of it is made available to cells and may be temporarily trapped in the energy currency molecule adenosine triphosphate (ATP), though some energy is always lost as heat at each step — which is why we become warm during strenuous physical activity.

Figure C1.2.1: Combustion and respiration compared Two graphs of energy in molecules (y-axis) against time (x-axis), both showing the conversion of glucose + oxygen to CO2 + H2O. In burning (combustion), all the energy is lost as heat in one abrupt step. In cellular respiration, the same overall energy change occurs, but energy is transferred to ATP in many small steps (ATP that can then be used, for example, in muscular contraction), rather than being released all at once.
Common mistake People sometimes talk about “burning up food” in respiration, but comparing respiration to combustion is unhelpful. In combustion, energy is released in a one-step reaction as heat and light; such a rapid change would be disastrous for body tissues. In cellular respiration, many small steps occur, each catalysed by a specific enzyme.

Energy made available in the cytoplasm is transferred from glucose to ATP. ATP is called the energy currency because, like money, it can be used in different contexts and is constantly recycled — respiration changes energy from one “currency” (glucose) into a different one (ATP). Glucose is relatively stable, whereas ATP breaks down, releasing its stored energy, when coupled to another reaction.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) A nucleotide, present in every living cell, formed in photosynthesis and respiration from ADP and Pi, and functioning in metabolism as a common intermediate between energy-requiring and energy-yielding reactions.

ATP is a nucleotide with an unusual feature: it carries three phosphate groups linked in a linear sequence. ATP may lose both of its outer phosphate groups, but usually loses only one at a time. It is a relatively small, soluble organic molecule, occurring in cells at a fairly constant concentration of 0.5–2.5 mg cm−3 (except in fatigued muscle cells).

Figure C1.2.2: ATP, ADP and AMP A diagram of adenosine (adenine bonded to ribose) with one, two or three phosphate groups attached in a linear chain: adenosine monophosphate (AMP, one phosphate), adenosine diphosphate (ADP, two phosphates) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP, three phosphates).

Like many organic molecules of its size, ATP contains a good deal of chemical energy locked in its structure. What makes ATP special is its role as a common intermediate between energy-yielding reactions (such as the individual steps of respiration) and energy-requiring reactions and processes (such as cellulose synthesis from glucose, protein synthesis from amino acids, and muscle fibre contraction). In summary, ATP: moves easily within cells and organisms by facilitated diffusion; is a very reactive molecule, able to take part in many steps of metabolism; and is an immediate energy source, delivering energy in relatively small amounts sufficient to drive individual reactions and physical processes.

Life Processes Within Cells that ATP Supplies with Energy

ATP provides a direct energy source for many life processes within cells, including: active transport of molecules and ions across membranes by membrane pumps; synthesis of macromolecules (anabolism); movement of the whole cell; and movement of cell components, such as chromosomes.

Because ATP is highly reactive, it is never stored in cells. The conversion of ATP to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and phosphate ion (Pi) is thermodynamically favourable, since the products ADP and Pi are thermodynamically more stable (lower in free energy) than the reactant ATP. Glucose and fatty acids serve as short-term energy stores, while glycogen, starch and triglycerides are long-term stores.

Adenosine diphosphate (ADP) A nucleotide, present in every living cell, made of adenosine and two phosphate groups bonded in a linear sequence; important in energy-transfer reactions of metabolism.

Energy Transfers During Interconversions Between ATP and ADP

In cells, ATP is formed from ADP and Pi using energy released during respiration. In the presence of enzymes, ATP then participates in energy-requiring reactions, releasing energy by hydrolysis to ADP and phosphate. The free energy available from ATP hydrolysis is approximately 30–34 kJ mol−1; some of this is lost as heat, but much is made available to do useful work — more than sufficient to drive a typical energy-requiring reaction of metabolism.

Top tip! You are not required to know the exact quantity of energy (in kilojoules) released during interconversions between ATP and ADP, only that it is sufficient for many cellular tasks — chemical reactions or physical processes.

Sometimes ATP reacts directly with water (a hydrolysis reaction), being converted to ADP and Pi — for example, in the direct hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate group during muscle contraction. Mostly, however, ATP reacts with other metabolites to form phosphorylated intermediates, making them more reactive; the phosphate groups are released later, so both ADP and Pi become available for reuse as metabolism continues.

Cell Respiration as a System for Producing ATP Within the Cell

Carbohydrates such as glucose are the preferred respiratory substrate, even though their energy yield is lower than that of lipids, because glucose is easier to oxidize and can directly enter glycolysis. Fats must first be converted to carbohydrate intermediates through gluconeogenesis before the glycolytic pathway can begin; proteins must first be hydrolysed into amino acids, then deaminated (the NH2 group removed), before entering the respiratory pathway — a process that itself requires ATP, reducing the net ATP yield from protein. Because proteins are structural molecules, they are respired only when glucose and fats are limited, as in starvation conditions.

Differences Between Anaerobic and Aerobic Cell Respiration in Humans

Key terms Aerobic respiration: respiration requiring oxygen, involving the oxidation of glucose to carbon dioxide and water.
Anaerobic respiration: respiration in the absence of oxygen, producing either lactic acid (humans) or ethanol and carbon dioxide (plants and yeast).
Lactic acid: an organic acid produced by the body when glucose is broken down to generate ATP in the absence of oxygen; lactic acid is present as lactate in solution.

While no oxygen is required for the early steps of cellular respiration, most animals and plants, and very many micro-organisms, require oxygen for the rest of the process — they respire aerobically. In aerobic respiration, sugar is completely oxidized to carbon dioxide and water, and much energy is made available.

C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + ATP

This equation is a balance sheet of inputs and outputs; it says nothing about the separate steps, each catalysed by a specific enzyme, by which respiration occurs. Aerobic respiration occurs in mitochondria and produces large amounts of ATP.

In the absence of oxygen, many organisms (and sometimes oxygen-deprived tissues) continue to respire glucose by different pathways, known as fermentation or anaerobic respiration, at least for a short time. Anaerobic respiration occurs in the cytoplasm and produces only a small quantity of ATP. Vertebrate muscle tissue respiring anaerobically forms lactic acid rather than ethanol; under cytoplasmic conditions, lactic acid is weakly ionized and exists mainly as the lactate ion. Lactic acid fermentation occurs in muscle fibres only when the energy demand for contraction is very great and cannot be met fully by aerobic respiration; its sole waste product is lactate.

glucose → lactate + ATP
Common mistakes It is not correct to say that “respiration occurs in the mitochondria” — mitochondria are required for aerobic, but not anaerobic, respiration; anaerobic respiration occurs in the cytoplasm, and respiration begins in the cytoplasm and ends in mitochondria. Do not confuse cell respiration with gas exchange: gas exchange is the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide at a surface (e.g. the alveoli), while cell respiration is a chemical process in cells in which glucose is oxidized step by step, yielding ATP.
Top tip! You need to know simple word equations for aerobic and anaerobic respiration, with glucose as the substrate.

Variables Affecting the Rate of Cell Respiration

Several variables affect the rate of cellular respiration:

  • Metabolic rate of the cell — e.g. muscle cells require more energy and so have higher respiration rates.
  • Size of the organism — smaller organisms have a larger surface area relative to their volume, and a correspondingly higher respiratory rate to allow for heat loss.
  • Supply of oxygen — cells need a constant oxygen supply to release the maximum amount of ATP; inadequately supplied cells respire anaerobically.
  • Supply of respiratory substrates, e.g. glucose — other substrates can also be respired, and the rate of respiration and quantities of CO2 and H2O produced depend on which substrate is used.
  • Temperature — because respiration is enzyme-controlled, temperature increases the rate up to an optimum.
  • pH — the release of carbon dioxide during respiration decreases pH (increases acidity) in cells and tissues, affecting the enzymes involved.

The rate of cell respiration can be measured using a respirometer, which detects the uptake of oxygen per unit time via a manometer that responds to changes in the pressure or volume of a gas.

Figure C1.2.3: A respirometer to measure respiration rate A respirometer tube containing respiring organisms (e.g. germinating seeds or fly maggots) and soda lime pellets (which absorb CO2) sits in a water bath, connected via a U-tube manometer to a control tube (a thermobarometer) containing glass beads of the same volume as the sample, also with soda lime, and to a graduated syringe. With clips A and B closed: (1) the respiring organisms give off CO2, which is absorbed by the soda lime, and absorb O2; (2) the resulting volume decrease causes the manometer fluid to move towards the respirometer tube; (3) after a fixed time, the syringe is used to inject air back in to level the fluid in both arms, and the volume of O2 absorbed is read directly from the syringe. Because any change in ambient pressure or temperature affects both the respirometer tube and the thermobarometer equally, such changes are automatically cancelled out and do not distort the oxygen-uptake reading.

With soda lime present, only oxygen uptake causes a volume change, so the coloured liquid moves towards the respirometer tube; the volume of air that must be injected back to re-level the fluid equals the volume of oxygen taken up. To estimate the quantity of carbon dioxide produced, the experiment is repeated with the soda lime removed: the difference between the fluid movement with and without soda lime indicates the volume of CO2 produced. Without soda lime, if equal volumes of O2 are taken in and CO2 given out, there is no net change in gas volume and the water does not move; if less CO2 is produced than O2 taken in, the fall in pressure moves the water slightly towards the organisms.

Top tip! Using invertebrates in respirometer experiments has ethical implications. IB guidelines state that any experimentation should not cause pain or undue stress to any animal (vertebrate or invertebrate), or compromise its health in any way. Animals must be handled with care, never exposed directly to soda lime, used only for brief periods, and returned to a safe environment once the experiment ends.

Calculating the rate of respiration

Method for calculating respiration rate The distance travelled by the fluid in the manometer over a fixed time (e.g. 10 minutes) is measured. The volume of oxygen absorbed is calculated from the radius of the capillary tube, r (mm), and the distance moved by the manometer fluid, d (mm), using the formula for the volume of a cylinder, πr²d. The average rate of oxygen consumption is then found by dividing this volume by the time in minutes. The experiment should be repeated at least five times to obtain reliable data; carrying it out for longer at each repeat improves accuracy.

Higher Level The Role of NAD as a Carrier of Hydrogen

During aerobic cellular respiration, glucose (a relatively large, 6-carbon molecule) undergoes a series of enzyme-catalysed oxidation and decarboxylation reactions, grouped into three major phases and a link reaction: glycolysis, in which glucose is converted to pyruvate; the link reaction, in which pyruvate is converted to acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA), releasing carbon dioxide; the Krebs cycle, in which acetyl-CoA is converted to carbon dioxide; and the electron transport chain, in which hydrogen removed during the earlier oxidation reactions is ultimately converted to water while generating a proton gradient that drives ATP synthesis (the bulk of the cell’s ATP is produced here).

Figure C1.2.4: The stages of aerobic cellular respiration A flow diagram: glucose enters glycolysis, producing some ATP (from ADP + Pi) and releasing hydrogen atoms (H) as it forms pyruvate. Pyruvate passes through the link reaction into the Krebs cycle, releasing carbon dioxide and more hydrogen atoms, and producing some more ATP. The hydrogen atoms removed at each stage pass to the electron transport chain, where they combine with oxygen to form water and generate much ATP.

Respiration as a series of redox reactions

Key terms Oxidation: the loss of electrons in a chemical reaction; this can be by adding oxygen atoms or by removing hydrogen atoms.
Reduction: the gain of electrons in a chemical reaction; this can be by removing oxygen atoms or by adding hydrogen atoms.
Redox reaction: a linked reaction in which one substance loses an electron while another gains an electron — the substance that gains the electron is reduced, while the one that loses it is oxidized.

In cellular respiration, glucose is oxidized to carbon dioxide while, at the same time, oxygen is reduced to water — tissue respiration is in fact a series of oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions, since when one substance is oxidized, another is automatically reduced. In biological oxidation, oxygen atoms may be added to a compound, or, alternatively, hydrogen atoms may be removed (dehydrogenation); in respiration, hydrogen atoms are gradually removed from glucose and added to hydrogen acceptors, which are thereby reduced. Since a hydrogen atom is an electron plus a proton, gaining hydrogen (reduction) involves gaining electrons — so the best definitions are: oxidation is the loss of electrons, and reduction is the gain of electrons.

Top tip! Mnemonic OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
Figure C1.2.5: Respiration as a redox reaction The equation 6O2 + C6H12O6 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy is annotated to show that glucose is oxidized (addition of oxygen, removal of hydrogen, loss of electrons, release of energy) while oxygen is reduced (removal of oxygen, addition of hydrogen, gain of electrons, uptake of energy).

Redox reactions occur in biological systems because of compounds with a strong tendency to take electrons from another compound (oxidizing agents) or to donate electrons (reducing agents). Reduction absorbs energy (an endergonic process), while oxidation releases energy (an exergonic process) — as seen, for example, when a fuel burns in air, releasing heat. The amount of energy in a molecule depends on its degree of oxidation: an oxidized substance has less stored energy than a reduced substance (e.g. methane, CH4, holds more chemical energy than carbon dioxide, CO2).

Coenzymes

Key terms Coenzyme: a substance that works with an enzyme to initiate or aid its function.
Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD): a coenzyme that acts as a hydrogen carrier in cellular respiration; NAD+ is the oxidized form that accepts hydrogen atoms.
Flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD): a coenzyme that acts as a hydrogen carrier in cellular respiration; FAD is the oxidized form that accepts two hydrogen atoms, forming FADH2.

The hydrogen atoms removed from glucose during respiration are added to hydrogen acceptors (electron carriers), which are thereby reduced; these hydrogen acceptors are coenzymes that link oxidation and reduction in cells. The main electron carrier in respiration is NAD; a second is FAD. Electron carriers can receive hydrogen atoms (i.e. become reduced), oxidizing the substance from which they removed them — for example, NAD is reduced to NADH and H+ (reduced NAD, also written NADH2):

NAD+ + 2H+ + 2e− → NADH + H+

Reduced NAD can pass hydrogen ions and electrons on to other acceptor molecules and, in doing so, becomes reoxidized back to NAD.

Higher Level Glycolysis

Key terms Glycolysis: the first stage of cell respiration, in which glucose is converted to pyruvate by stepwise enzyme-controlled reactions, without the use of oxygen; ATP is generated.
Pyruvate: the end product of glycolysis, which is converted into acetyl-CoA that enters the Krebs cycle when sufficient oxygen is available.
Phosphorylation: the addition of a phosphoryl group (PO32−) to an organic compound.

In the first steps of respiration, the 6-carbon glucose molecule is split into two 3-carbon molecules, which are converted to an organic acid called pyruvic acid (existing in the cytoplasm mainly as the pyruvate ion). Two molecules of pyruvate are formed from each glucose molecule, together with a small amount of ATP. No molecular oxygen is required for these first steps, which take place in the cytoplasm and are collectively called glycolysis (“sugar splitting”).

glucose → pyruvate + small amount of ATP
The four stages of glycolysis 1. Phosphorylation: glucose is activated by reaction with ATP, forming glucose phosphate, which is converted to fructose 6-phosphate; a further phosphate is added at the expense of a second ATP molecule, forming fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. Two molecules of ATP are consumed per glucose molecule at this stage.
2. Lysis: the fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (6-carbon backbone) is split into two molecules of a 3-carbon sugar, triose phosphate.
3. Oxidation: each triose phosphate molecule is oxidized by removal of hydrogen, a reaction catalysed by a dehydrogenase enzyme working with the coenzyme NAD.
4. ATP synthesis: as each triose phosphate is converted to pyruvate, ATP is formed twice — this is substrate-level phosphorylation, distinct from the bulk of ATP synthesis that occurs later via the electron transport chain. Since two triose phosphate molecules are converted per glucose, four ATP molecules are synthesized at this stage; overall, glycolysis has a net gain of two ATP per glucose molecule.
Figure C1.2.6: Glycolysis — a summary A glucose molecule is first phosphorylated using 2 × ATP, forming a phosphorylated 6-carbon sugar (fructose 1,6-bisphosphate); this is then split (lysis) into two 3-carbon sugar phosphates (triose phosphate). Each triose phosphate is oxidized, with hydrogens removed and transferred to NAD (forming 2 × NADH + H+), and inorganic phosphate is added; the resulting molecules are converted to 2 × pyruvate, generating 4 × ATP in total. For each glucose molecule respired: 2 ATP are used and 4 ATP are formed, giving a net gain of 2 ATP. A parallel carbon-skeleton diagram tracks the 6-carbon glucose backbone splitting into two 3-carbon fragments, each gaining and losing phosphate groups (linked to ATP/ADP interconversions) as they are converted stepwise into pyruvate.
Top tip! You are not required to know the names of the intermediates in glycolysis, but you do need to know that each step in the pathway is catalysed by a different enzyme.

Once pyruvate has formed in the cytoplasm, if oxygen is available it passes into the mitochondria by facilitated diffusion (since the enzymes needed for further breakdown are located only there), where it is completely oxidized to carbon dioxide, water and a large quantity of ATP:

pyruvate → carbon dioxide + water + large amount of ATP

In this phase, pyruvate is oxidized by the removal of hydrogen atoms (by hydrogen acceptors) and the addition of oxygen to carbon atoms to form carbon dioxide; these reactions occur one at a time, each catalysed by a different enzyme. In the mitochondria, the hydrogen carried by reduced hydrogen-acceptor molecules reacts with oxygen to form water, reoxidizing the acceptor for reuse; the majority of ATP is generated at this stage. Enzymes of respiration occur partly in the cytoplasm (glycolysis) and partly in mitochondria (pyruvate oxidation and most ATP formation); ATP, once formed, passes to all parts of the cell, and both ADP and ATP cross mitochondrial membranes by facilitated diffusion.

Figure C1.2.7: The sites of cellular respiration in cells A diagram of a mitochondrion within the cytoplasm. In the cytoplasm outside the organelle, glucose → pyruvate + ATP occurs (glycolysis). Inside the mitochondrion, pyruvate → CO2 + H2O + much ATP occurs (aerobic cellular respiration). If oxygen is unavailable, pyruvate → lactate or ethanol (anaerobic cellular respiration) occurs instead, back in the cytoplasm.

Higher Level Conversion of Pyruvate to Lactate and Anaerobic Respiration in Yeast

When oxygen is unavailable in human skeletal muscle, pyruvate remains in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactate. In yeast, whether or not oxygen is available, pyruvate is instead converted to ethanol. This solves a potential problem: in pyruvate formation, hydrogen-acceptor molecules (NAD) are reduced (they take up hydrogen), and without oxygen present these must still be reoxidized (lose their hydrogen) for pyruvate formation to continue. The answer is that, in anaerobic respiration, reduced NAD (NADH) donates its hydrogen to convert pyruvate into lactate or ethanol; NAD is thereby reoxidized and available for further pyruvate synthesis, allowing glycolysis to continue.

reduced NAD + pyruvate → lactate + oxidized NAD
reduced NAD + pyruvate → ethanol + CO2 + oxidized NAD

Anaerobic respiration is “wasteful” of respiratory substrate: because no additional energy is transferred in the later steps (pyruvate to lactate or ethanol), the total ATP yield per glucose molecule is limited to the net two molecules of ATP produced during glycolysis, in both alcoholic and lactic-acid fermentation. Both lactate and ethanol are therefore energy-rich molecules whose remaining chemical energy can be used later — in humans, lactate is transported to the liver and later metabolized aerobically; ethanol is sometimes even used as a fuel in cars.

Interdependence The anaerobic and aerobic pathways of cellular respiration are interdependent: pyruvate, produced by the anaerobic reactions of glycolysis, is the substrate that forms the basis of aerobic respiration. Lactic acid formed by anaerobic respiration in humans (when insufficient oxygen is available) must ultimately be converted back to pyruvate in the liver, under aerobic conditions.

Anaerobic cell respiration in yeast, brewing and baking

Many species of yeast (Saccharomyces) respire anaerobically even in the presence of oxygen, producing ethanol and carbon dioxide. Humans have exploited this alcoholic fermentation for thousands of years: in bread making, the carbon dioxide produced causes the bread to rise; in wine and beer production, the ethanol is the desired product.

glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + ATP

The pathways of anaerobic respiration are the same in humans and yeast, except for how NAD is regenerated and, therefore, the final products formed — in effect, oxygen is replaced as the hydrogen acceptor. In alcoholic fermentation, ethanal is the hydrogen acceptor (reduced by alcohol dehydrogenase to ethanol); in lactic acid fermentation, pyruvate itself is the hydrogen acceptor (reduced by lactate dehydrogenase to lactate).

Figure C1.2.8: The respiratory pathways of anaerobic respiration From glucose, glycolysis nets 2 × ATP and produces 2 × pyruvate (via a 3-carbon phosphorylated sugar intermediate) and 2 × reduced NAD. If oxygen is present, pyruvate proceeds down the aerobic respiration pathway to 6CO2 + 6H2O. In lactate fermentation, the 2 × pyruvate is reduced (using the 2 × reduced NAD, regenerating 2 × NAD) by lactate dehydrogenase directly to 2 × lactate. In alcoholic fermentation, the 2 × pyruvate is first decarboxylated to 2 × ethanal (releasing 2 × CO2), and ethanal is then reduced (using the 2 × reduced NAD, regenerating 2 × NAD) by alcohol dehydrogenase to 2 × ethanol.

Higher Level The Link Reaction and the Krebs Cycle

Key terms Decarboxylation: a chemical reaction that releases carbon dioxide (usually with hydrogen replacing it).
Link reaction: the reactions that connect glycolysis to the reactions of the Krebs cycle by producing acetyl coenzyme A from pyruvate; carbon dioxide and NADH are produced.
Krebs cycle: a cyclical metabolic process in aerobic cell respiration, involving the oxidation and decarboxylation of acetyl groups, with a yield of ATP and reduced NAD.

Pyruvate diffuses into the mitochondrial matrix, where the 3-carbon pyruvate is first decarboxylated (loses CO2) and, simultaneously, oxidized (loses hydrogen, forming reduced NAD). The product of this oxidative decarboxylation is a 2-carbon acetyl group, which combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA). This step, the link reaction, connects glycolysis to the Krebs cycle.

pyruvate + NAD+ + CoA → acetyl-CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+

The Krebs cycle (also called the citric acid cycle, after its first intermediate) begins when acetyl-CoA reacts with the 4-carbon acid oxaloacetate (OAA), forming the 6-carbon acid citrate and releasing coenzyme A for reuse in the link reaction. Citrate is then converted back to oxaloacetate (the acceptor molecule) through a series of reactions in which: two molecules of carbon dioxide are released in separate decarboxylation reactions; one molecule of ATP is formed by substrate-level phosphorylation; three molecules of reduced NAD are formed; and one molecule of the coenzyme FAD is reduced (FAD, rather than NAD, is the hydrogen-acceptor coenzyme for this particular step).

Top tip! In the Krebs cycle, you are only required to name the intermediates citrate (6C) and oxaloacetate (4C). Key features of the cycle: citrate is produced by transfer of an acetyl group to oxaloacetate, and oxaloacetate is regenerated by the cycle’s reactions, which include four oxidations (dehydrogenation reactions) and two decarboxylations.
Figure C1.2.9: Link reaction and Krebs cycle — a summary Pyruvate (3-carbon), diffusing in from the cytosol, is converted by the link reaction into acetyl coenzyme A (2-carbon), releasing CO2 and forming NADH + H+ from NAD+. Acetyl-CoA then combines with oxaloacetate (4-carbon acid) to form citrate (6-carbon acid), releasing coenzyme A. Around the cycle, citrate is progressively converted via α-ketoglutarate (5-carbon acid) back to oxaloacetate: each decarboxylation step releases CO2 and reduces NAD+ to NADH + H+ (occurring twice), one further oxidation reduces FAD to FADH + H+, one step generates ATP directly, and a final oxidation regenerates oxaloacetate while reducing another NAD+. Several other organic acid intermediates exist in the cycle that are not named in this summary.

Because glucose yields two pyruvate molecules in glycolysis, the whole Krebs cycle “turns” twice for every glucose molecule respired.

In addition to glucose, fats (lipids) are commonly used as respiratory substrates: they are first broken down to fatty acids and glycerol, and a fatty acid is then “cut up” into 2-carbon fragments and fed into the Krebs cycle via coenzyme A. Vertebrate muscle, including heart muscle, is well adapted to respiring fatty acids in this way, and fats are just as likely as glucose to serve as the respiratory substrate.

Higher Level The Electron Transport Chain and Generation of a Proton Gradient

Electron transport chain A series of proteins that transfer electrons received from reduced coenzymes, generating a gradient of protons that drives the synthesis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

Reduced coenzymes (from glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle) pass their electrons to the electron transport chain, a series of proteins in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Energy is transferred as a pair of electrons is passed to the first carrier in the chain, converting reduced NAD (NADH) back to NAD; removal of electrons from hydrogen atoms creates H+ (protons), which go on to play a role in ATP generation. In this final stage of aerobic respiration, electrons received from reduced NAD (or FAD) are passed along a series of carriers before finally combining with oxygen to form water.

Figure C1.2.10: Transfer of energy by reduced NAD to the electron transport chain in the mitochondrion NADH + H+ is oxidized back to NAD+, releasing 2H+ and 2 electrons; the high-energy electrons are passed from carrier to carrier along the chain, with energy released at each step used to synthesize ATP from ADP + Pi (three ATP molecules are shown being formed along the chain, per molecule of reduced NAD oxidized; when reduced FAD is oxidized instead, only two ATP molecules are formed, since FAD feeds electrons in further along the chain). At the end of the chain, an oxygen atom (½O2) combines with the electrons and 2H+ to form water, described as metabolic water since it originates from the respiration of food molecules.

Normally, for every molecule of reduced NAD oxidized (i.e. for every pair of hydrogens), approximately three molecules of ATP are produced (less when FAD is oxidized instead). The total yield from aerobic respiration is 32 ATP per molecule of glucose respired.

Generation of a proton gradient

The electron-carrier proteins are arranged in a highly ordered way in the inner mitochondrial membrane. As they oxidize the reduced coenzymes, the energy released is used to pump hydrogen ions (protons) from the mitochondrial matrix into the space between the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes, where they accumulate. Because the inner membrane is largely impermeable to ions, a significant hydrogen ion concentration gradient builds up across it, generating a potential difference — a store of potential energy.

Higher Level Chemiosmosis and the Synthesis of ATP in the Mitochondrion

Key terms Chemiosmosis: the process by which the synthesis of ATP is coupled to electron transport via the movement of protons, using ATP synthase.
ATP synthase (ATPase): a complex of integral proteins located in the mitochondrial inner membrane, where it catalyses the synthesis of ATP from ADP and phosphate, driven by a flow of protons.

The protons concentrated in the inter-membrane space flow back into the matrix through channels in the ATP synthase enzyme, embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. As protons flow down their concentration gradient through the enzyme, the energy released drives ATP synthesis — ATP synthase couples the release of energy from the proton gradient with the phosphorylation of ADP. The enzyme has a rotational mechanism: energy generated by its rotation drives ATP production.

Figure C1.2.11: Chemiosmosis A stereogram of a mitochondrion cut open to show the outer membrane, the folded inner membrane forming cristae, and the matrix. Proteins of the electron transport chain, embedded in the inner membrane, act as proton pumps, using energy from electron transport (2e− passing from a dehydrogenase enzyme to an oxidase enzyme, ultimately reducing 2H+ + ½O2 to H2O) to move H+ ions from the matrix into the inter-membrane space, where they accumulate and create a potential difference (electrochemical gradient) across the inner membrane. These protons then flow back down their concentration gradient through a proton channel in ATP synthase (ATPase), also embedded in the inner membrane, coupling this flow to the synthesis of ATP from ADP + Pi in the matrix.
Nature of science: evidence for chemiosmosis Peter Mitchell proposed the chemiosmotic hypothesis in 1961, suggesting that the energy of a proton gradient generated by the electron transport chain could drive ATP synthesis; his ideas were not widely accepted for about a decade, since they were considered highly novel — a paradigm shift in bioenergetics. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery. If the gradient drives ATP synthesis, disrupting the inner mitochondrial membrane, or eliminating the proton gradient across it, should inhibit ATP production — and both predictions were confirmed experimentally. Physical disruption of the inner membrane stops ATP synthesis; loss of the proton gradient by a chemical “uncoupling” agent, such as 2,4-dinitrophenol (which carries H+ across the membrane, bypassing ATP synthase), also inhibits mitochondrial ATP production. This natural uncoupling occurs in brown fat cells of hibernating mammals such as bears, releasing most of the energy from fatty acid oxidation as heat rather than converting it to ATP. Chemiosmotic coupling is of ancient evolutionary origin: modern micro-organisms, including archaea living in environments resembling the early Earth, also use chemiosmotic coupling to produce ATP.
Top tip! You do not need to know the names of the individual protein complexes of the electron transport chain.

Higher Level Role of Oxygen as Terminal Electron Acceptor in Aerobic Cell Respiration

At the end of the electron transport chain, electrons need a final destination (a terminal acceptor) so they can be removed from the system, allowing the continued flow of electrons along the chain; protons used to create the electrochemical gradient must similarly be removed. The terminal acceptor of the electrons is oxygen: when oxygen combines with electrons and protons from the mitochondrial matrix, metabolic water is formed.

Figure C1.2.12: Oxygen is the terminal acceptor for the electron transport chain in mitochondria A diagram of proteins in the electron transport chain, showing the flow of electrons along the chain accompanied by a release of energy. NADH is oxidized to NAD+, and FADH2 is oxidized to FAD, both releasing electrons into the chain (an oxidation step, labelled 1). At the end of the chain, oxygen (O2) is reduced, accepting the electrons together with 2H+, to form H2O (a reduction step, labelled 2) — confirming oxygen’s role as the terminal electron acceptor.

Higher Level Differences Between Lipids and Carbohydrates as Respiratory Substrates

When triglycerides are oxidized in respiration, a great deal of energy is transferred; mass for mass, fats and oils transfer more than twice as much energy as carbohydrates when respired. This is because fats are comparatively low in oxygen atoms (the carbon of lipids is more reduced than that of carbohydrates), so more of the oxygen used in respiring fat comes from the atmosphere rather than from within the substrate molecule itself — making fats and oils a concentrated energy store. Because they are insoluble, fat or oil stored in cells does not cause osmotic water uptake, making a fat store especially suited to animals that must survive long unfavourable periods on stored food reserves. Oils are often a major energy store in plants, seeds and fruits — maize, olives and sunflower seeds, for instance, are used commercially as sources of edible oil.

Complete oxidation of fats and oils also produces far more metabolic water than the same mass of carbohydrate. Desert animals such as the camel and desert rat retain much of this water within the body, aiding survival without access to drinking water; developing bird and reptile embryos likewise benefit from metabolic water released by oxidizing the fat stored in egg yolk.
