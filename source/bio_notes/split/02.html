DNA as the Genetic Material of All Living Organisms

DNA is a nucleic acid. Nucleic acids are the information molecules of cells (and of viruses) found throughout the living world. The genetic code — the information carried within nucleic acids — is universal: it makes sense in all organisms, not just a few species or groups.

Key definitionsNucleic acid: a polynucleotide chain of one of two types, DNA or RNA. Genetic code: the order of bases in DNA (of a chromosome) that determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein. Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA): a form of nucleic acid consisting of two complementary chains of deoxyribonucleotide subunits, containing the bases adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine. Ribonucleic acid (RNA): a form of nucleic acid containing the pentose sugar ribose and the organic bases adenine, guanine, uracil and cytosine.

Living cells contain two types of nucleic acid: DNA and RNA. DNA is the genetic material and occurs in the chromosomes of the nucleus (and also in chloroplasts and mitochondria). While some RNA occurs in the nucleus, most is found in the cytoplasm, particularly in ribosomes.

Some viruses (such as SARS-CoV-2, which causes COVID-19, and the viruses responsible for the common cold, influenza, Dengue fever, hepatitis C, rabies, Ebola, polio, mumps and measles) use RNA rather than DNA as their genetic material. Viruses depend entirely on the cells of living organisms to survive and replicate, so they are not considered living — meaning it remains true to say that DNA is the genetic material of all living organisms.

The presence of genetic material in a structure does not, by itself, indicate life: viruses, usually considered non-living, contain genetic material. In addition, DNA is chemically stable, so it can persist in dead organic matter and in some fossils.

Components of a Nucleotide

A nucleotide consists of three substances joined by covalent chemical bonds: a nitrogenous base (in DNA: cytosine (C), guanine (G), adenine (A) or thymine (T)); a pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA); and a phosphate group. These components combine via an enzyme-controlled condensation reaction — a reaction in which two molecules combine and water is released as a by-product. Since any one of the four bases can be incorporated, there are four different types of nucleotide in DNA.

Key definitionsNucleotide: the phosphate ester of a nucleoside — an organic base combined with a pentose sugar and a phosphate group. Cytosine, guanine, adenine, thymine: the four nitrogenous bases of DNA. Pentose: a 5-carbon monosaccharide sugar. Condensation: formation of larger molecules involving the removal of water from smaller component molecules.

The bases fall into two groups by molecular structure: the purines (adenine and guanine, double-ring structures) and the pyrimidines (cytosine and thymine, single-ring structures).

The Sugar–Phosphate Backbone

Nucleotides combine one at a time, by further condensation reactions, to form large macromolecules called nucleic acids or polynucleotides. Nucleic acids are very long, thread-like (linear) macromolecules, with alternating sugar and phosphate molecules forming the “backbone” of each strand — this part of the molecule is uniform and unvarying. Sugar–phosphate bonding creates a continuous chain of covalently bonded atoms along each strand of DNA (and RNA), forming a strong molecular backbone. Along the strand, each base is attached to a pentose sugar and projects sideways from the backbone; because the bases vary from nucleotide to nucleotide, their sequence carries the coded information held by the nucleic acid.

PolynucleotideA long, unbranched chain of nucleotides, as found in DNA and RNA.

Bases and the Genetic Code

The information in DNA lies in the sequence of its nitrogenous bases (cytosine, guanine, adenine, thymine), which together make up the genetic code. This sequence dictates the order in which amino acids are assembled to synthesize a protein. The code lies in the sequence of one DNA strand, the coding strand (the other strand is complementary to it), and the coding strand is always read in the same direction by enzymes.

4 (bases) × 4 × 4 = 64 possible triplet (codon) combinations
CodonThree consecutive bases in DNA (or RNA) which specify one amino acid. The genetic code is a three-letter or “triplet” code: each sequence of three bases (a codon) stands for one of the 20 amino acids used to build proteins. With a four-letter base “alphabet” (C, G, A, T), there are 64 possible different triplet combinations.

Nucleic acids code for the production of proteins in cells. Proteins make up about two-thirds of the total dry mass of a cell, and differ from carbohydrates and lipids in that they contain nitrogen (and sometimes sulfur) as well as carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

RNA as a Polymer

RNA molecules are relatively short compared with DNA, typically from a hundred to several thousand nucleotides long, depending on their role. RNA is a polymer — a large organic molecule made of repeating subunits (monomers). In messenger RNA (mRNA), it takes the form of a single strand of polynucleotide in which the sugar monomer is ribose. The bases found in RNA are cytosine, guanine, adenine and uracil (which replaces the thymine of DNA).

Key definitionsPolymer: large organic molecules made of repeating monomer subunits. Uracil: a pyrimidine nitrogenous base found in RNA (not DNA); it pairs with adenine. Messenger RNA (mRNA): single-stranded RNA formed by transcription of the genetic code in the nucleus, which then moves to ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Transfer RNA (tRNA): short lengths of RNA that combine with specific amino acids prior to protein synthesis. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): RNA that forms part of the ribosome, the protein-synthesizing organelle.

The two ends of the strand are labelled 3′ and 5′, showing the strand has directionality.
Figure A1.2.4: Numbering the carbon atoms of ribose and deoxyriboseShows the molecular and structural formulas of ribose (C5H10O5) and deoxyribose (C5H10O4) in both their straight-chain (linear) and ring (cyclic) forms. In each case the five carbon atoms of the sugar are numbered 1′ through 5′, running clockwise around the ring starting next to the ring oxygen. Deoxyribose differs from ribose only in lacking an oxygen atom (an –OH group is replaced by –H) at the 2′ carbon. This numbering convention is what allows the bonds between adjacent sugars and phosphates, and the direction of the polynucleotide strand, to be identified.

There are three functional types of RNA: mRNA, tRNA and rRNA. All three are made in the nucleus; mRNA is then transported out through nuclear pores to ribosomes in the cytoplasm, while tRNA and rRNA also occur in the cytoplasm.

Forming an RNA strandRNA is formed by the condensation of many nucleotide monomers. These condensation reactions link the pentose sugar of one nucleotide to the phosphate group of the next, forming the new strand. The covalent bond formed between adjacent nucleotides is called a 3′–5′ phosphodiester bond (a bond between the 3′ carbon of one sugar and the 5′ carbon of the next).

The DNA Double Helix

The DNA molecule consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands, paired together and held by hydrogen bonds, twisted into the shape of a double helix. The strands are termed antiparallel because one strand runs from its 5′ carbon to its 3′ carbon while the other runs from its 3′ carbon to its 5′ carbon — i.e. the two strands run in opposite directions.

Double helixTwo interlocking helical polynucleotide strands joined by hydrogen bonds between pairs of purine–pyrimidine bases (A pairs with T, G pairs with C). The helical structure makes a 360° twist after every 10 nucleotides, i.e. every 3.4 nm.

The whole ladder-like structure is shown twisted into a double helix. An inset notes that, within chromosomes, this helical structure is further stabilized and supported by associated proteins. A label also shows that each phosphate group bridges carbon-3 of one deoxyribose to carbon-5 of the next sugar along a strand.
Common mistake – drawing DNAA frequent error is linking nitrogenous bases directly to phosphate groups rather than to the pentose sugars, or linking phosphate groups to the wrong oxygen on the sugar ring (it should connect via C5, not directly to the ring oxygen). When drawing DNA, two antiparallel strands should be shown (the helical twist itself is not required), with A paired to T and G paired to C; only a short section of the molecule needs to be drawn, and the numbers of hydrogen bonds between each base pair do not need to be included.

Nature of science – models: a model is a simplified description of a biological system, concept or process, used to describe phenomena that cannot be directly experienced or to simplify complex systems. The first model of DNA’s structure was built in 1953 by Francis Crick and James Watson, who combined experimental evidence from other scientists to deduce the likely structure of the molecule. Like theories, models may be revised as new experimental data emerge. A key piece of evidence was an X-ray diffraction image of a DNA fibre, taken by Raymond Gosling in 1952 at King’s College London under the supervision of Rosalind Franklin; calculations Watson and Crick made from this image gave crucial dimensions for their double-helix model. The 1953 publication of DNA’s structure, and a proposed mechanism for its replication, marked a paradigm shift from classical to molecular genetics, later enabling recombinant DNA technology, genetic engineering, monoclonal antibodies, CRISPR gene editing and genetic fingerprinting — developments that raise their own ethical questions (e.g. germline editing for non-therapeutic “enhancement” reasons, or the potential misuse of genetic data by third parties such as insurers).

Genes and chromosomes

Within a DNA molecule, sections that code for proteins are called genes. A gene is a heritable factor influencing a specific characteristic of an organism (e.g. height in a pea plant, or blood group in humans); “heritable” means the factor passes from parent to offspring during reproduction. Genes are located on chromosomes — a chromosome is a length of DNA carrying specific genes in a linear sequence, and each gene occupies a specific position, or locus (plural loci), on a particular chromosome. A gene may exist in two or more alternative forms, called alleles.

Key definitionsGene: a heritable factor consisting of a length of DNA that codes for a protein. Chromosome: a length of DNA carrying specific genes in a linear sequence. Locus: the particular position of a gene on homologous chromosomes. Allele: different versions of the same gene. Homologous chromosomes: pairs of chromosomes, one from each parent, that carry the same sequence of genes (but not necessarily the same alleles of those genes).

In eukaryotic cells, chromosomes occur in pairs called homologous chromosomes — one member of each pair originally came from each parent. Humans, for example, have 46 chromosomes: 23 from each parent. Homologous chromosomes resemble each other in structure and carry the same sequence of genes (though possibly different alleles at each locus).

At each locus an allele is shown on each of the two chromosomes (e.g. A/a, C/C, F/F, j/j, M/m). Where the two alleles at a locus are identical (e.g. C/C, F/F, j/j), that locus is described as homozygous; where they differ (e.g. A/a, M/m), the locus is described as heterozygous. Differences Between DNA and RNA

Both DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides, but they differ in structure. One key difference is the pentose sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose, RNA contains ribose.

Figure A1.2.10: DNA and RNA nucleotides comparedTwo single nucleotides are drawn side by side using the numbered-carbon sugar ring convention. The DNA nucleotide shows a deoxyribose sugar (no –OH at the 2′ carbon) attached to a phosphate group and to one of the bases A, C, G or T. The RNA nucleotide shows a ribose sugar (with an –OH at the 2′ carbon) attached to a phosphate group and to one of the bases A, C, G or U.
DNA vs RNADNA: double-stranded; pentose sugar is deoxyribose; bases are adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine; generally very long, forms a stable double helix, and is used for long-term storage of genetic information. RNA: single-stranded; pentose sugar is ribose; bases are adenine, uracil, guanine, cytosine (uracil replaces thymine); generally much shorter, and includes mRNA, tRNA and rRNA, each with different, more transient functional roles.

Complementary Base Pairing

Bases pair specifically — adenine (A) with thymine (T), and cytosine (C) with guanine (G) — simply because only these combinations fit correctly together along the helix. When A pairs with T the bases are held by two hydrogen bonds; when C pairs with G they are held by three hydrogen bonds. Only these specific pairs can form hydrogen bonds with each other.

Complementary base pairingDescribes how the nitrogenous bases of nucleic acids align with each other in a specific way — adenine with thymine (or uracil in RNA), cytosine with guanine — held together by hydrogen bonds. Because of this specific pairing, the sequence of bases on one strand of the double helix determines the sequence of bases on the other. This is what allows genetic information to be replicated (copied precisely, during DNA replication) and expressed (used as a template for building proteins).

Diversity of Possible DNA Base Sequences

Although the genetic code uses only four bases (A, C, T, G), the order in which they can be combined is effectively limitless — giving DNA a limitless capacity for storing information across any length of molecule and any base sequence.

GenomeThe whole of the genetic information of an organism or cell.

In a human cell, the DNA held in the nucleus measures about 2 m in total length and contains 3.2 Gb of genetic “data”, within which it is estimated humans have between 20 000 and 25 000 protein-coding genes — an indication of how DNA offers enormous data storage capacity with great economy.

Conservation of the Genetic Code

The 64 codons of the genetic code have the same meaning — coding for the same amino acids — in nearly all organisms. This supports the idea of a common origin of life on Earth: that the first DNA has sustained an unbroken chain of life from the first cells to every cell alive today, with only very minor variations in the genetic code arising since life originated some 3.5 billion years ago.

MutationA change in the amount or the chemical structure (base sequence) of the DNA of a chromosome.

Over many generations, mutations can change the base sequence of the genome and therefore of mRNA and the resulting order of amino acids. However, many sequences — both protein-coding and non-coding — persist largely unchanged over many generations; these are called conserved sequences, and highly conserved sequences may have particular functional importance (although the function of many non-coding sequences remains unclear). Even where the base sequence of a coding region does change, the amino acid sequence it codes for may not, because most amino acids are specified by more than one possible codon — such changes that do not alter the resulting protein are called synonymous mutations.

Common mistakeDo not equate “genome” or genome size with the total number of genes in an organism. The correct definition of genome is the total amount of DNA (i.e. all the genetic information) of an organism or cell — not simply a count of its genes.

The most highly conserved genes are those found in all organisms, including genes for proteins involved in transcription, translation and ribosome structure. The existence of such universally shared genes indicates that all life is interlinked, descending from a single universal ancestor. Histone proteins, which help package DNA within the nucleus, are similarly highly conserved in sequence and structure across species, again pointing to universal common ancestry.

Higher Level Directionality of RNA and DNA

The DNA double helix has direction, or polarity: along each strand, phosphate groups bridge carbon-3 of one sugar to carbon-5 of the next, so one strand runs 5′ to 3′ while its partner runs 3′ to 5′ — the two strands of DNA are antiparallel. This directionality matters throughout gene expression: during DNA replication (when DNA is copied), during transcription (when the genetic code is copied into mRNA), and during translation (when the mRNA message is read to build a protein).

The coding strand of DNA is the template read during transcription, and it is always read in the 3′ to 5′ direction. The resulting single strand of mRNA is synthesized in the 5′ to 3′ direction by complementary base pairing, and mRNA is itself later translated (read by a ribosome) in the 5′ to 3′ direction to build a polypeptide chain.

Common mistakeDNA’s role is not limited to coding for proteins. While genes code for polypeptides, other regions of DNA regulate gene expression, and some sections code for RNA molecules (such as tRNA) that play their own direct role in building proteins at ribosomes, rather than coding for a protein themselves.

Higher Level Purine-to-Pyrimidine Bonding and Helix Stability

When Watson and Crick built their first physical model of DNA in 1953, they initially used cardboard cut-outs based on incorrect molecular shapes for thymine and guanine, so the model did not fit together correctly — the lengths of the base pairings were wrong. Following a suggestion from the American scientist Jerry Donohue about the correct shapes for the bases, Watson made new cardboard cut-outs and found that the complementary bases now fitted together perfectly: a purine always paired with a pyrimidine (A with T, C with G), each pair held together by hydrogen bonds, and this structure also matched Chargaff’s rules (below).

Figure A1.2.11: Watson and Crick with their demonstration model of DNAA photograph of James Watson and Francis Crick standing beside their large physical demonstration model of the DNA double helix, built from metal plates and rods representing the sugar–phosphate backbones and the paired bases.

Because a purine (double-ring) always pairs with a pyrimidine (single-ring) across the helix, every base pair (A–T or C–G) has the same width, so the DNA helix has the same three-dimensional structure regardless of the underlying base sequence. This purine-to-pyrimidine pairing, and the hydrogen bonding it allows, confers stability on the molecule, making DNA an ideal molecule for storing information in cells.

Figure A1.2.12: Direction, base pairing and hydrogen bonding in the DNA double helixA detailed section of the DNA double helix showing both antiparallel sugar–phosphate backbones (deoxyribose sugars linked by phosphate groups, each phosphate bridging the C3 of one sugar to the C5 of the next), running in opposite directions, with one strand’s 3′ end (a free –OH group on the sugar) and 5′ end (a phosphate group on the sugar) both labelled. Complementary base pairs project inward and join the two strands: guanine paired with cytosine via 3 hydrogen bonds, and (elsewhere in the figure) adenine paired with thymine via 2 hydrogen bonds, illustrating purine–pyrimidine pairing across the helix.

Nature of science – physical modelling: building physical, scaled models of DNA’s components let Watson and Crick combine known chemical data with X-ray diffraction evidence, trying different arrangements of the model pieces until they found one consistent with all the available data, including Chargaff’s rules. A brief point for reflection: Watson and Crick’s distinctive working method — reinterpreting already-published data and building on the work of others (including Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray diffraction images) — illustrates how scientific progress can rest on a mix of cooperation and competition between different research groups.

Higher Level Nucleosome Structure

In eukaryotic cells, DNA occurs in the chromosomes of the nucleus together with protein — more than 50% of a chromosome’s mass is protein. While some chromosomal proteins are enzymes involved in copying and repairing DNA, most have a support and packaging role.

Packaging is necessary because of the sheer length of DNA relative to the space available: in a human cell, the total DNA of all 46 chromosomes measures over 2 m, yet a typical chromosome only about 5 µm long (1 µm = 1/1000 mm) contains a DNA molecule roughly 5 cm long — meaning about 50 000 µm of DNA is packed into just 5 µm of chromosome.

Key definitionsNucleosome: a sequence of DNA wound around eight histone protein cores — a repeating unit of eukaryotic chromatin. Histone: a protein rich in the amino acids arginine and lysine that forms the scaffolding of chromosomes and is used in chromosome condensation to form nucleosomes.

This phenomenal packaging is achieved by coiling the DNA double helix and looping it around protein “beads” called nucleosomes. Histones, the packaging proteins of the nucleosome, are basic (positively charged) proteins with a high concentration of amino acids bearing extra basic (–NH2) groups, such as lysine and arginine — their positive charge lets them bind strongly to the negatively charged DNA. Eight histone molecules combine to form a single nucleosome “bead”, around which the DNA double helix is wrapped in a double loop.

Figure A1.2.13: The nucleosome and supercoiling of DNAA multi-step diagram of DNA packaging. Step 1: the DNA double helix is shown wound twice around a core of eight histone molecules, forming a single nucleosome “bead”; a linking H1 histone binds the DNA to the bead and connects on to the next nucleosome along the strand, giving a “beads on a string” appearance. Step 2: the beaded thread itself coils up into a thicker 30 nm fibre. Step 3: this 30 nm fibre is further looped and folded (supercoiled) into the highly condensed structure of a metaphase chromosome. An accompanying electron micrograph (at ×40 000 magnification) shows a real metaphase chromosome at this maximum-condensed stage.

At times of cell division, the whole beaded thread coils up to form the chromatin fibre, which is coiled again and looped around a “scaffold” of non-histone chromosomal protein, folding (supercoiling) the structure into the much-condensed chromosome seen at cell division. Nucleosomes are therefore key to the safe storage of these enormous lengths of DNA within the nucleus, while also allowing the cell to open up access to selected lengths of DNA (particular genes) during transcription.

Non-histone chromosomal proteinProteins that remain in the chromatin once histone proteins have been removed; they play a key role in regulating gene expression.

Prokaryotes (organisms without a true nucleus, i.e. bacteria) have much smaller genomes and do not require this histone-based packaging, since protein is absent from their circular chromosomes: their DNA is described as “naked”.

Common mistake“Naked” DNA specifically means DNA that is not associated with histone proteins (as in eubacteria). It should not be used to mean DNA that is simply not enclosed within a nuclear membrane, which is true of all bacteria regardless of whether their DNA is “naked” in the histone sense.

Higher Level The Hershey–Chase Experiment

Because roughly 50% of a chromosome consists of protein, and because there is more chemical “variety” in proteins than in nucleic acids, scientists once speculated that protein — not DNA — might be the information-carrying substance of the cell. This idea proved incorrect.

BacteriophageA virus that parasitizes bacteria (also called a phage). A virus particle consists of a protein coat (capsid) surrounding a nucleic acid core. Once it enters a host cell it can take over the cell’s metabolism to produce new virus particles; eventually the host cell breaks down (lysis), releasing the new viruses to repeat the infection cycle.
Figure A1.2.14: The life cycle of a bacteriophageA diagram of a bacteriophage’s structure (a protein “head” containing nucleic acid, a protein “tail”, and a baseplate), alongside three numbered steps of its replication cycle: (1) the phage attaches to the bacterial cell wall and injects its viral DNA into the bacterium; (2) the injected viral DNA takes over the host cell’s protein- and nucleic-acid-synthesis machinery; (3) new virus particles are assembled inside the host and then escape (by lysis of the host cell) to repeat the infection cycle on new host cells. An accompanying electron micrograph shows a real bacteriophage infecting a bacterium.

In 1952, Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase used a bacteriophage that parasitizes the bacterium Escherichia coli to test whether it is the phage’s protein coat or its DNA that carries genetic information into a host cell.

The Hershey–Chase experimentTwo batches of bacteriophage were prepared: one with the DNA core labelled using radioactive phosphorus (32P), since phosphorus occurs in DNA but not in protein; and one with the protein coat labelled using radioactive sulfur (35S), since sulfur occurs in protein but not in DNA — making each radioactive label specific to one component. Two identical cultures of E. coli were infected, one with each labelled batch of phage. New, radioactively labelled viruses were subsequently obtained only from the bacteria infected with the 32P-labelled (DNA-labelled) phage; the 35S label (protein) did not enter the host cells at all — it remained outside, and could be removed by agitating the mixture (e.g. in a blender) without affecting the infection. This showed that only the DNA of the virus enters the host cell and directs the production of new viruses, so Hershey and Chase concluded that DNA, not protein, carries the genetic message.
Figure A1.2.15: The Hershey–Chase experimentTwo parallel experimental set-ups are shown. In the top branch, phage labelled with radioactive sulfur (35S, in the protein coat) infects bacteria; the 35S label remains outside the bacterial cells, and the new phage particles produced (after bacteriophage reproduction) do not carry the 35S label. In the bottom branch, phage labelled with radioactive phosphorus (32P, in the DNA core) infects bacteria; the 32P label is found inside the cells, and after lysis, the new phage particles produced do carry the 32P label — showing that DNA, and not protein, entered the host cell and directed the formation of new virus particles.

Nature of science – the global impact of science: the Hershey–Chase experiment illustrates how a technological development can open up new kinds of experiment. After the Second World War, the US government publicised peacetime benefits of nuclear technology, including reactor-produced radioisotopes; the resulting availability of these isotopes as a research tool is what made the Hershey–Chase labelling experiment possible, since radioisotopes are especially well suited to tracking the movement of specific molecules through metabolic pathways or life cycles over time.

Higher Level Chargaff’s Data on Purine and Pyrimidine Bases

Watson and Crick’s discovery of base pairing rested partly on their interpretation of data published by Erwin Chargaff, who in 1935 had analysed the base composition of DNA from a range of organisms and found consistent patterns whose significance was not immediately obvious even to Chargaff himself.

Chargaff’s rulesTwo consistent patterns emerged from Chargaff’s analysis of DNA from many different organisms: (1) the number of purine bases (adenine + guanine) always equalled the number of pyrimidine bases (cytosine + thymine); and (2) the number of adenine bases always equalled the number of thymine bases, and the number of guanine bases always equalled the number of cytosine bases. This is explained by the fact that only a purine (double-ring) will fit with a pyrimidine (single-ring) between the two sugar–phosphate backbones during base pairing — so in DNA, adenine must always pair with thymine, and cytosine must always pair with guanine.

Nature of science – falsification: Chargaff’s data illustrate how scientific knowledge advances not just by confirming hypotheses but by falsifying them. The Russian-American biochemist Phoebus Levene, who discovered ribose (1909) and deoxyribose (1929), had earlier proposed — incorrectly, as later shown — that nucleic acid had a repeating “tetranucleotide” structure, with the four bases always present in equal amounts. Levene did not recognize that base composition is organism-specific, partly because the analytical techniques of his time could not reliably measure the relative amounts of each base. Only in the late 1940s did Chargaff establish the organism-specificity of nucleic acid composition and the special numerical relationships between bases described above, which falsified the tetranucleotide hypothesis (since a simple four-unit repeating structure could not carry the variable information needed for heredity).

Inductive vs deductive reasoningBiologists use both forms of reasoning. Inductive reasoning (a “bottom-up” approach) starts from specific observations and measurements, identifies a general pattern, and from this develops a hypothesis and general conclusions — but inductive conclusions can never be fully certain, since a single future counter-example could falsify them (the “problem of induction”). Deductive reasoning works the other way: starting from a general principle and applying it to predict a specific case. The philosopher Karl Popper argued that scientists often work creatively and intuitively to generate a hypothesis first, and that science advances mainly through attempts at falsification: a hypothesis that survives repeated attempts to falsify it (by different research groups) is considered a strong hypothesis, but one counter-example is enough to falsify an inductive conclusion. This is exactly what happened to the tetranucleotide hypothesis once Chargaff’s data became available.
