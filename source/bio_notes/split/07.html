The Need to Classify Organisms

Given the enormous variety of life on Earth, biologists need a system for grouping organisms based on shared similarities. Just as the periodic table organises the elements in chemistry, classification (taxonomy) gives biology an organising framework. Rather than a list of unconnected facts, classification provides a rationale for grouping organisms by shared characteristics, revealing functional and structural similarities and differences, and enabling further comparative study — a broad range of further work becomes possible once classification is complete.

Concept: UnityThe system of classification is universal — everyone uses it. By using the same methodology and language to describe and group species, collaboration between scientists around the world becomes possible.
Concept: DiversityGiven the great diversity of life, classification provides a framework for biological knowledge, revealing functional and structural similarities and differences between organisms.

The quickest way to classify organisms is by obvious similarities, such as grouping together all animals that fly (most birds, many insects, bats, and certain fossil dinosaurs) because they share wings. However, the wings of birds and insects are only superficially similar: both are aerofoils (structures that generate lift when moved through the air), but they are built from different tissues and have entirely different origins in the body. This distinction between superficial and true evolutionary similarity is central to modern classification, and is explored further under cladistics below (and in Chapter A4.1).

The Hierarchical Scheme of Classification

In classification, the aim is to use as many characteristics as possible when placing similar organisms together and separating dissimilar ones. Similar species are grouped into the same genus (plural: genera); similar genera are grouped into families. This approach extends from families to orders, then classes, phyla, and kingdoms — the hierarchical scheme of classification, with each successive group containing more and more different kinds of organism. In a natural classification, a genus and its higher taxa consist of all the species that evolved from one common ancestral species.

Difficulties Classifying Organisms: Domains and Kingdoms

Historically, living things seemed to divide naturally into two kingdoms: plants (autotrophic nutrition) and animals (heterotrophic nutrition), reflecting the original disciplines of botany and zoology. Fungi and micro-organisms were loosely added to botany. This caused a problem: fungi have heterotrophic (“animal-like”) nutrition but a superficially plant-like appearance. Then, the electron microscope revealed two fundamentally different types of cell structure — prokaryotic and eukaryotic. Since bacteria have prokaryotic cells, they could no longer be classified as plants (which have eukaryotic cells). This led to a five-kingdom model of classification.

When RNA sequencing became possible, American microbiologist and biophysicist Carl Woese reasoned that because all living organisms contain ribosomes, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences could be used to determine relatedness between all organisms. His work revealed that the prokaryotes actually comprise two distinct groups — the bacteria and the archaea — that diverged very early in the history of life. This gave rise to a classification level above kingdom: the domain. In the 1970s the five-kingdom model was modified by splitting Prokaryotae into two domains, eubacteria and archaea, with the remaining four eukaryote kingdoms grouped into the domain eukarya.

Key termsKingdom: the second-highest taxonomic rank, below domain.
Domain: the highest taxonomic rank in the hierarchical classification system, above kingdom. There are three domains of life: archaea, eubacteria, and eukarya.

It can be difficult to determine which similarities between species are the most relevant for grouping them:

  • Similarities based on shared ancestry must be distinguished from those that evolved independently under similar selective pressures and so merely appear similar — for example, human and octopus eyes (analogous structures).
  • Species that look alike may not be closely related; their resemblance may be due to analogous adaptations to similar environments (e.g. both bats and dragonflies have wings for flight). In contrast, homologous structures may look very different yet represent common evolutionary origin (e.g. vertebrate forelimbs).
  • Morphology (form and structure) can lead to classification mistakes through misinterpreting analogous structures as homologous ones. Base or amino acid sequences are more accurate for determining clade membership because they represent true homology.
NOS: Theories — a paradigm shift in classificationDarwin observed that a “number of intermediate forms must have existed, linking together all species in each group by gradations as fine as our existing varieties.” A fixed ranking of taxa is therefore somewhat arbitrary, as it does not reflect this gradation, and the traditional hierarchy does not always match the true patterns of evolutionary divergence. Cladistics, using unranked clades, is an alternative classification system based entirely on shared derived characteristics (synapomorphies) that trace to a group’s most recent common ancestor. The adoption of cladistics is an example of a paradigm shift in science, where a new theory replaces an old one.

Cladistics: Key Concepts

Key termsCladistics: a classification system used to construct evolutionary trees. Organisms are categorised based on shared derived characteristics that can be traced to a group’s most recent common ancestor and are not present in more distant ancestors. Characteristics can be anatomical, physiological, behavioural, or based on genetic and protein sequences.
Clade: a group of organisms that have evolved from a common ancestor.
Cladogram: a diagram used in cladistics that shows evolutionary relationships among organisms.

A clade is monophyletic — it includes all the descendants of one, and only one, ancestor. Cladistics developed from a body of taxonomic theory called phylogenetic systematics, organised around three principles: (i) monophyletic taxa are natural groupings; (ii) organisms are related through evolutionary descent; and (iii) evolutionary modifications uniquely shared by organisms are evidence of their unique phylogenetic history.

Advantages of Evolutionary Classification

Ideally, a classification system should reflect the true evolutionary relationships between organisms, not simply physical similarities that may or may not be evolutionarily significant — this is called a phylogenetic classification system. By grouping organisms using biochemical information (DNA and proteins), changes in species over time can be mapped onto a “tree of life”, showing true interconnections between species. The characteristics of organisms within a clade can then be predicted because they are shared within that clade. Classification systems based purely on morphology do not necessarily show evolutionary relationships, because analogous structures can evolve separately in groups that do not share a recent common ancestor.

Figure A3.2.2: Cladistics — two ways of classifying the same three speciesThe left-hand diagram groups a lizard, crocodile, and bird using morphology alone, without reference to common ancestry. The right-hand diagram (a true cladogram) plots the same three species against a time axis and groups them by recency of common ancestor: it shows that the bird and crocodile share a more recent common ancestor than either does with the lizard, even though morphologically the crocodile looks more like the lizard.

Cladograms have two important features: (1) branch points in the tree, representing the time at which a divide between two taxa occurred, and (2) the degree of divergence between branches, representing the differences that have developed since the taxa diverged. The most objective evidence for placing organisms in the same clade comes from base sequences of genes or amino acid sequences of proteins. Morphological traits can also be used, but care must be taken to ensure the characteristics used are homologous, not analogous.

In summary, a cladogram shows patterns of shared characteristics, and classifies organisms according to the order in time at which branches arose along their phylogenetic tree.

Limitations of Cladistics

Cladistics is a powerful tool for testing phylogenetic hypotheses, but it has conceptual and practical limits. It assumes that shared derived characters result from linear (vertical) genetic transfer — from parent to offspring only, as in a pedigree. This is reasonable for most eukaryotes, but lateral (horizontal) genetic transfer — such as transduction or conjugation in bacteria — violates this assumption. Cladistics has no real mechanism for dealing with horizontal gene transfer, so it has been of limited use for the taxonomy of prokaryotes.

How bacterial taxonomy actually worksBacterial taxonomists originally used numerical taxonomy: identifying as many physical and biochemical characteristics as possible (sometimes up to 200) in a group of related bacteria, then building a matrix to group isolates sharing the most characteristics. This morphological (phenotype-based) approach was expensive but gave robust groupings, though no insight into true evolutionary relationships. There is also enormous genetic variation within bacterial species — up to 30% DNA sequence variation between two E. coli genomes, much of it due to presence or absence of entire genes rather than sequence differences in shared genes. Current bacterial taxonomy therefore uses a phylo-morphological species concept: combining ribosomal phylogenetic information with phenotypic characteristics, and applying a somewhat arbitrary threshold of 70% overall DNA similarity to delineate species boundaries.

Sequence Differences as the Basis for Clade Divergence

The similarities and differences in the biochemistry of organisms are extremely important for establishing evolutionary links and dating when common ancestors existed. For example, the beta chain of haemoglobin (146 amino acid residues) shows variation in amino acid sequence between species. Because haemoglobin structure is genetically inherited, the more closely related two species are, the more likely their amino acid sequences are to match. Variations are thought to arise by mutation of an ancestral haemoglobin gene, so the longer ago two species diverged, the more differences are likely to have accumulated.

Biochemical Variation as a Molecular Clock

Molecular clockA measure of evolutionary change over time, based on the mutation rate of DNA sequences or the proteins they encode; used for estimating how long ago two related organisms diverged from a common ancestor. If biochemical changes occur at a roughly constant rate, this rate can be used to date evolutionary divergence.

Top tip: the molecular clock only gives estimates, because mutation rates are affected by generation time, population size, the intensity of selective pressure, and other factors.

Constructing Cladograms from Amino Acid Sequences

DNA and protein comparisons are done by aligning sequences from different species and using computer software to identify the best phylogenetic tree joining them, thereby inferring the species’ phylogeny.

The comparative serology (immunological) methodSerum is the liquid left from a blood sample once blood cells and fibrinogen are removed. Its protein molecules act as antigens if injected into an animal whose immune system does not recognise them. In this method, human serum is injected into a rabbit, which produces antibodies against the human proteins. Serum from the rabbit’s blood (now containing anti-human antibodies) is then mixed with serum from a range of other animal species. The more closely related a test species is to humans, the greater the observable precipitation reaction between the rabbit antibodies and that species’ serum proteins. Precipitation with human serum itself is taken as 100%; for each other species, greater precipitation indicates a more recent common ancestor with humans. This technique is called comparative serology.

NOS: Hypotheses — parsimonyDifferent criteria for judgement can lead to different hypotheses. Parsimony is the concept that the simplest explanation for the data is preferred: in phylogenetic analysis, the hypothesis requiring the smallest number of character changes is considered most likely to be correct. This is the same idea as “Occam’s razor” — the simplest explanation is usually the best one, and one should not make more assumptions than necessary.

Constructing Cladograms from DNA Sequences

Cladograms can also be built using base sequences of genes directly, measuring relatedness by the amount of difference between DNA molecules of different species.

DNA hybridization techniqueDNA is extracted from cells and cut into fragments of about 500 bases. The fragments are heated so they separate into single strands. Single strands from one species are mixed with similarly prepared single strands from another species. Base pairing causes complementary strands to align and bond; the greater the complementarity between the two strands, the more hydrogen bonds link them together, and the higher the temperature required to separate them again. The separation temperature is therefore a direct measure of relatedness — the fewer bonds formed, the lower the temperature required, and the greater the percentage difference in DNA.

The graph shows that percentage DNA difference increases roughly in step with the estimated number of years since each species shared a common ancestor with humans.

Reading a Cladogram: Key Terms

In cladograms, the more derived characteristics two organisms share, the closer their evolutionary relationship (i.e. the more recently their common ancestor lived).

Key termsNode: a branching point from the ancestral population in a cladogram, representing a speciation event.
Terminal branch: a branch leading to a terminal node, representing either an extant (living) or extinct species for which data exist.
Terminal node: represents the hypothetical last common ancestral interbreeding population of the taxon labelled at the tip of a cladogram.
Root: the central trunk of a cladogram, indicating the common ancestor to all groups that branch from it.

Common mistakeDo not look at the end (tip) positions of species on a cladogram to judge relatedness — look at the nodes. The branching points (nodes) represent common ancestors to all the species that descend from that branch point; all branch tips arising from a given node are descendants of the common ancestor at that node.

Using Cladistics to Test Classification: The Figwort Family

Cladistics can be used to test whether an established classification of groups actually corresponds to evolutionary relationships.

The Scrophulariaceae (figwort family) was a long-recognised, large and varied family of flowering plants, typically with irregular (zygomorphic) flowers like the snapdragon and foxglove. Flowering plant families were originally designated before biochemical studies were applied to plant taxonomy. Most modern evidence for plant evolutionary relationships now comes from comparing DNA sequences of one to three conserved genes (each about 1000 nucleotides long) found in plant chloroplasts. These genes have provided more secure evolutionary information than differences in anatomy and morphology, on which traditional classifications were based.

Comparing these genes across many species, including several formerly classified in the Scrophulariaceae, led to major reorganisation: several genera were moved out of Scrophulariaceae into other families (e.g. Orobanchaceae, Plantaginaceae), while other genera previously in different families were reassigned into a newly defined, smaller Scrophulariaceae.

The tree distinguishes the modern, narrower Scrophulariaceae (including genera such as Verbascum and Scrophularia) from genera formerly placed in that family but now reassigned elsewhere, all diverging from a common ancestor.

NOS: FalsificationTheories and other scientific knowledge claims may eventually be falsified. In this example, the morphological similarities that originally grouped these flowering plants together in one family were actually due to convergent evolution rather than shared ancestry, so the traditional classification was shown to be false once cladistic (molecular) evidence became available. Despite this additional biochemical evidence, evolutionary relationships between organisms are still only partly understood, so current taxonomy remains only a partial phylogenetic classification.

Classification of All Organisms into Three Domains

The discovery that prokaryotes comprise two very different groups followed the discovery of the distinctive biochemistry of extremophile bacteria, such as heat-loving bacteria found in hot springs at around 70°C. These micro-organisms have cells that are recognisably prokaryotic, but one group — now identified as the domain archaea — contains rRNA that is unique, with molecular regions distinctly different from the rRNA of other prokaryotes and eukaryotes. This difference in ribosomal RNA is the key evidence that led to the reclassification of life into three domains:

  • Archaea — the extremophile prokaryotes
  • Eubacteria — the true bacteria
  • Eukarya — all eukaryotic cells (protoctista, fungi, plants and animals)

Top tip: archaea have been found in many extreme environments (deep ocean vents, geysers, salt pans, polar environments), but their habitats are actually broader than this — some species occur only in anaerobic conditions such as the guts of termites and cattle, or at the bottom of ponds and swamps among rotting plants.
