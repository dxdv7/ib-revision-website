Water as the Medium for Life

Water covers approximately 71% of the Earth’s surface (97% of this is in oceans, only 3% is fresh water), giving Earth its appearance as a mostly blue planet from space. Evidence from the geological record shows water has existed on Earth for 3.8 billion years — almost as long as the planet itself, which formed an estimated 4.5 billion years ago. The first cells originated in water, where the oceans blocked harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun and allowed early life to evolve. Water remains the medium in which most life processes occur.

Water and living massWater forms a large proportion of living organisms — between 65% and 95% by mass of most multicellular plants and animals (about 80% of a human cell consists of water). Enzymes, the biological molecules that increase the rate of chemical reactions, must be dissolved in water to work: water provides a chemically stable medium for the processes of life.

Despite being taken for granted, water has a number of unusual physical and chemical properties (explored throughout this chapter) that allow life to exist at a huge range of scales, from the smallest bacteria to the tallest tree. Without water, life as we understand it would not exist on Earth.

A brief note on the nature of science: claims that water can retain a “memory” of substances previously dissolved in it (the central idea behind homeopathy) are considered pseudoscientific. Careful replication attempts by independent research groups failed to reproduce the original claims, and the idea of a chemical “memory” contradicts well-established physical chemistry. This illustrates two hallmarks that separate science from pseudoscience: genuine scientific claims must be falsifiable and must be checked by replication, rather than only being supported by evidence that confirms them (a bias known as confirmation bias).

Hydrogen Bonds in Water

A water molecule consists of one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms, sharing pairs of electrons. The molecule is V-shaped (bond angle about 104.5°), not linear. Because the oxygen nucleus draws the shared electrons (negatively charged) away from the hydrogen nuclei (positively charged), the molecule carries an unequal distribution of electrical charge even though it is electrically neutral overall: there is a small negative charge (δ−) on the oxygen atom and small positive charges (δ+) on the hydrogen atoms. This arrangement makes water a polar molecule.

Key definitionsCovalent bond: a bond between atoms in which pairs of electrons are shared. Polar molecule: a molecule with an unequal distribution of electrical charge — one end is slightly positive and the other slightly negative. Hydrogen bond: a weak attractive intermolecular force in which a hydrogen atom in one molecule is attracted to an electronegative atom, such as oxygen, in a different molecule.

Because water molecules are polar, the positively charged hydrogen atoms of one water molecule are attracted to the negatively charged oxygen atoms of nearby water molecules. These attractive intermolecular forces are called hydrogen bonds. They are weak compared with covalent bonds, yet they are strong enough to hold water molecules together and to attract water to charged particles or charged surfaces. Hydrogen bonds account for most of water’s unique properties.

Common mistakeDo not confuse bonding within a water molecule with bonding between water molecules. Covalent bonding (intra-molecular) holds the two hydrogen atoms to the oxygen atom within a single water molecule. Hydrogen bonding (inter-molecular) forms between separate water molecules and is much weaker.

Nature of science – using technology: researchers at Queen’s University Belfast used computer simulations to explore how changes to water’s molecular structure would affect its properties. They found that if the 104.5° bond angle were changed to 90°, or if the hydrogen bonds were made about 15% weaker, the three-dimensional network of hydrogen bonds that gives liquid water its unique properties would be severely disrupted or collapse — illustrating just how finely tuned water’s structure is for sustaining its unusual properties, and how computer modelling lets scientists test such “what if” questions that cannot easily be tested in the laboratory.

Cohesion of Water Molecules

CohesionThe force by which individual molecules of the same type attract and associate (“stick together”). Water molecules continually form and break hydrogen bonds with their neighbours, but at any one moment a large number of molecules are held together by these bonds, giving water unusually strong cohesive properties for a liquid — strong enough to resist “breaking” under tension.
Common mistakeA single hydrogen bond is a weak interaction — hydrogen bonds are not individually strong. It is only because there are enormous numbers of hydrogen bonds in a body of water that they collectively exert large cohesive forces.
Figure A1.1.4: Water drawn up a tree trunk through xylem vesselsA cross-section of a tree trunk showing xylem vessels running as continuous narrow tubes from the roots to the leaves. Labels trace the pathway: water is taken up from the soil by root cells; heat from sunlight causes evaporation of water from the leaves (transpiration); this evaporative loss generates a force that draws the water column up the trunk. Because the water column coheres (does not break) and adheres to the walls of the xylem vessels, and because its viscosity is low, it flows smoothly through the vessels and can be drawn up to great heights without the column breaking or pulling apart.

Related to cohesion is surface tension: a property of the surface of a liquid that allows it to resist an external force, arising from cohesion between water molecules. Water has a very high surface tension — higher than any other liquid except mercury. Molecules on the surface have no water molecules above them, so they experience a net inward force from their neighbours below and to the sides; within the bulk of the liquid, cohesive forces from all directions cancel out and there is no net force. This net inward force causes the surface to contract and resist being stretched or broken, hence “surface tension”. It allows water to form almost perfectly spherical droplets.

Figure A1.1.5: Hydrogen bonding at the surface of waterA depth cross-section of water showing many H2O molecules. Molecules below the surface form hydrogen bonds with neighbours in every direction (up, down and sideways), so the forces cancel and there is no net pull in any one direction. Molecules right at the surface can only form hydrogen bonds with neighbours below and beside them (not above, since there is air rather than water there), so they are pulled more strongly inward and sideways, forming a taut, hexagonally arranged lattice of stronger bonds at the surface. This lattice is what produces water’s unusually high surface tension.
Figure A1.1.6: A pond skater on the water surfacePart (a) shows a pond skater insect standing on top of a body of water, its thin legs resting on the surface without breaking through. Part (b) is a close-up showing that the water surface is visibly depressed (dimpled downward) under each of the insect’s feet, but the surface does not break because the hydrogen bonds between surface water molecules hold it together. The insect’s waxy cuticle stops its body becoming wetted, and its mass is not great enough to overcome the surface tension.

Below the surface, water molecules slide past one another very easily — a property described as low viscosity (viscosity is a measure of a fluid’s resistance to flow). Because of its low viscosity, water flows readily through narrow capillaries, tiny gaps and pores. The rate at which molecules diffuse through a solvent such as water is inversely proportional to the solvent’s viscosity, and viscosity itself falls as temperature rises (the viscosity of water at 25°C is roughly half that at 4°C) — which matters because diffusion rate is critical to many life-sustaining processes.

Adhesion of Water and Capillary Action

AdhesionThe force by which individual molecules cling to surrounding materials and surfaces (as distinct from cohesion, the attraction between water molecules themselves). Materials with an affinity for water are described as hydrophilic (“water-loving”), e.g. hydrogen bonds readily form between such a material and water.
Cohesion vs adhesion – common mistakeThese terms are sometimes wrongly treated as interchangeable. Cohesion (“co” = together) is attraction between water molecules; adhesion (“ad” = toward) is attraction of water to a surface. In xylem transport, cohesion is by far the more significant force — it is what lets the water column resist the tension generated by transpiration. Adhesion becomes significant mainly when air-filled xylem vessels need to refill with sap under positive pressure, which happens rarely (no more than once a year).
Figure A1.1.7: Adhesive and cohesive forces in a xylem vesselA narrow xylem vessel shown in cross-section with water molecules inside. Cohesive forces (hydrogen bonds between neighbouring water molecules, each showing δ+ hydrogens and δ− oxygen) hold the column of water together in the centre of the vessel; adhesive forces attract water molecules at the edge of the column to the polar/charged wall of the xylem vessel, helping to support the water column against gravity.

Soil contains many vertical, thin channels called capillary tubes (channels with a very small internal diameter), in which plant roots are located. Capillary action (or capillarity) is the tendency of a liquid to move up against gravity within such a narrow tube: adhesion between water molecules and the tube wall draws water upward, letting plants draw water up from the water table to their roots when the ground is dry.

Capillary action in plant cell wallsPlant cell walls are made from the fibrous, polar/hydrophilic material cellulose. Fibrous materials can act like wicks, drawing water up into the material by capillary action. Cell walls therefore draw water by capillary action from nearby xylem vessels, keeping water flowing through plant tissue. Cells directly exposed to air — such as the spongy mesophyll cells of a leaf — are kept constantly wetted this way: water evaporates from their moist, blotting-paper-like cell walls and then diffuses out of the leaf through pores (stomata) on the leaf surface, which is what enables water to be continuously transported up through the whole plant.
Figure A1.1.8: Capillary tubes in soils and cell wallsA narrow capillary tube (representing either a channel between soil particles or the space between cellulose fibres in a cell wall) with a column of water rising up inside it against the weight of the water column. Labels show cohesion between water molecules holding the column together (including cohesion between molecules right at the surface, i.e. surface tension at the top of the column) and adhesion between water molecules and the capillary wall pulling the column upward — the narrower the tube, the further up capillary action can draw the water.

Water as a Solvent

Hydrogen bonds pull water molecules very close together, because the potential energy of the hydrogen bonds exceeds the kinetic energy of the water molecules up to 100°C at atmospheric pressure — this is why water is liquid across the temperature and pressure ranges found over most of the Earth’s surface, giving a liquid medium with distinctive thermal and solvent properties.

Key definitionsSolute: a dissolved molecule or ion in a solution. Solvent: a liquid in which another substance can be dissolved. Hydrophobic: repelled by water.

Water is a powerful solvent for polar and ionic substances. When an ionic compound such as sodium chloride (Na+ and Cl−) dissolves, every cation (positive ion) and anion (negative ion) becomes surrounded by a shell of orientated water molecules, with the appropriately charged end of each water molecule facing the ion.

Figure A1.1.9: The hydration of sodium and chloride ionsA central Na+ ion is shown surrounded by several water molecules, each orientated with its negative (oxygen, δ−) end pointing toward the positive ion. A separate Cl− ion is shown surrounded by water molecules orientated the opposite way, with their positive (hydrogen, δ+) ends pointing toward the negative ion. This ordered shell of water molecules around each ion is what allows ionic solids to dissolve in water.

A diverse range of hydrophilic molecules dissolve in water: carbon-containing (organic) molecules with ionized groups (e.g. amino acids, which carry a negatively charged carboxyl group, –COO−, and a positively charged amino group, –NH3+), and soluble organic molecules such as sugars, which dissolve because hydrogen bonds form with their slightly charged hydroxyl (–OH) groups. Once dissolved, solute molecules or ions are free to move by diffusion and become more chemically reactive than in the undissolved solid.

Non-polar substances, by contrast, are repelled by water (e.g. oil on the surface of water) and are described as hydrophobic. Some cell molecules depend on being hydrophobic and insoluble to function — for example, the cell membrane is built from phospholipids whose hydrophobic tails form the internal structure of the membrane.

Of the common atmospheric gases, only carbon dioxide (CO2) is particularly soluble in water; oxygen (O2) and nitrogen (N2) are only slightly soluble. CO2 is moderately soluble because a proportion of it reacts chemically to form carbonic acid (H2CO3), which immediately dissociates into hydrogen ions (H+) and hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO3−). Oxygen and nitrogen, being non-polar, do not form hydrogen bonds with water and do not ionise or dissociate, which is why they dissolve poorly. One consequence of oxygen’s poor solubility in water is the evolution of respiratory pigments such as haemoglobin, which greatly increase the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood compared with plain water.

Most enzymes catalyse reactions in aqueous solution: enzymes require a certain level of water within their structure to maintain their shape and stability, and most naturally occurring enzymes cannot form their active shape without being immersed in water. Hydrogen bonds often act as bridges between an enzyme’s binding site and its substrate. Some enzymes, however, are held in a fixed position (e.g. within a cell membrane), which allows reactions to be localized to particular sites in the cell.

Physical Properties of Water and Aquatic Habitats

The physical properties of water — buoyancy, viscosity, thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity — all depend on the hydrogen bonding between water molecules.

Key definitionsBuoyancy: the ability of any fluid to provide a vertical upward force on an object placed in or on it — objects float when their average density is less than that of water, and sink when denser (density = mass per unit volume). Thermal conductivity (k): a measure of how easily heat flows through a material. Specific heat capacity: the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C.
Common mistakeDo not confuse “heat capacity” with “specific heat capacity”. Heat capacity is the amount of heat needed to change the temperature of a whole body by one degree, and so depends on the mass/volume of that body. Specific heat capacity is the heat energy needed per unit mass, and so is independent of the total mass or volume of the sample.

At sea level, air is 784 times less dense than water — a given volume of air has just 0.13% of the density of the same volume of water.

The importance of these properties for life can be illustrated using two animals that live both in water and in air/on land: the black-throated loon (Gavia arctica), a diving bird that catches fish underwater and breeds around deep freshwater lakes across northern Europe, Alaska and Asia; and the ringed seal (Pusa hispida), which lives in Arctic and sub-arctic regions, spending time both on ice and swimming beneath it.

Specific heat capacity

A relatively large amount of energy is needed to raise water’s temperature, because a great deal of energy is required to break the large number of hydrogen bonds restricting the movement of water molecules. Consequently, aquatic environments (rivers, lakes, seas) change temperature very slowly compared with terrestrial environments, giving them more stable temperatures. Since organisms and their cells are largely composed of water, this same property provides internal “thermal cushioning”, protecting cells and organisms from large temperature fluctuations. Relatively stable sea temperatures let seals feed and live year-round, while air’s lower specific heat capacity means air temperature fluctuates more.

Unusually, water floats when it freezes, because ice is less dense than liquid water (water reaches its maximum density at 4°C) — a consequence of how hydrogen bonds arrange water molecules in ice. The resulting ice platform lets ringed seals live on top of the sea; the seals use their claws to dig breathing holes through the ice so they can live under and on the ice year-round.

Figure A1.1.11: The open lattice structure of iceWater molecules in ice are shown hydrogen-bonded to each other in a rigid, open, tetrahedral lattice arrangement (each molecule bonded to four neighbours in a spaced-out three-dimensional framework), which holds the molecules further apart, on average, than in liquid water — this is why ice is less dense than liquid water and floats.

Thermal conductivity

Water conducts heat about 28 times better than air. By trapping air within its feathers, the black-throated loon creates an insulating layer between its skin and the outside air; the trapped layer also restricts convection currents, helping maintain body temperature. The seal instead relies on a thick layer of blubber (subcutaneous fat) for insulation. Ice also has low thermal conductivity (similar to air), so a surface ice layer traps thermal energy in the water beneath it and insulates it from heat loss to the very cold air above, even in extreme cold.

Buoyancy

Bird anatomy (hollow bones, air trapped in feathers) is adapted to reduce weight for flight, but this makes buoyancy a problem for underwater hunting. The loon compensates with solid bones and by compressing air out of its lungs and feathers before diving, reducing its buoyancy so it can dive successfully. Aquatic diving mammals such as seals store a large amount of subcutaneous fat as blubber, which acts as a buoyancy aid as well as thermal insulation.

Viscosity

Water is far more viscous than air (Table A1.1.1). In the air, the loon’s light feathers move through the low-viscosity air with minimal friction, aiding flight. In water, both animals rely on water’s low viscosity below the surface to move efficiently: the seal uses its flippers (modified forelimbs) to propel itself, while the loon uses large, webbed feet, positioned laterally and toward the rear of its body, to maximize propulsion and reduce turbulent eddies (and so reduce drag) as it swims.

Higher Level Extraplanetary Origin of Water on Earth

The Earth formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago in an environment too hot for water to condense into a liquid, so the Earth’s water must have an extraplanetary origin. Further from the Sun, water vapour can condense directly into ice; it is in these colder regions that water is thought to have first formed. Researchers studying the composition of asteroids, and the meteorites that break off from them, hypothesise that asteroids are the most likely source of Earth’s water. Such asteroids still contain ice and organic material (including amino acids), so could have delivered both water and organic molecules to Earth — both critical for the possible evolution of life.

A group of meteorites called carbonaceous chondrites — among the oldest meteorites in the solar system — can be up to 28% water by composition, similar to ocean water, with the water molecules incorporated into the crystal structures of their minerals. The likely source of water can be assessed by comparing isotopes of hydrogen and their relative proportions.

Figure A1.1.12: Isotopes of hydrogenTwo simple atomic diagrams side by side. Hydrogen-1 (protium): a nucleus with a single proton (P) and an orbiting electron (e), no neutron. Hydrogen-2 (deuterium): a nucleus with one proton (P) and one neutron (N), plus one orbiting electron (e). The two isotopes differ only in the presence of a neutron in the nucleus.

Meteorites with hydrogen isotope ratios (deuterium to protium) that closely match Earth’s seawater could have been the original source of Earth’s oceans. Two 4.5-billion-year-old meteorites containing liquid water, found on Earth, support this hypothesis. When such meteorites heat up (for example on impact with a planet), they release their water as gas, which is then captured by that planet’s gravitational attraction. The Earth’s current deuterium-to-protium ratio also matches ancient eucrite achondrites, a type of meteorite originating from the large asteroid Vesta in the outer asteroid belt (located between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter). Carbonaceous chondrites and eucrite achondrites are therefore both hypothesised to have delivered water to Earth. Once water vapour was present, temperatures on the cooling Earth became low enough for it to condense into liquid water, and gravity was strong enough to retain this water at the surface rather than let it disperse into space.

There are several competing hypotheses for how water first arrived on Earth, including delivery by icy comets and formation beneath the planet’s surface. For this syllabus, only the asteroid-delivery hypothesis needs to be considered in detail.

Higher Level The Search for Extraterrestrial Life and the Presence of Water

Because life as we know it depends on water, any planet where such life could exist must also have liquid water. This requires the planet to orbit at the right distance from its star: too close, and water boils and evaporates; too far, and water freezes solid.

Goldilocks zoneAlso known as the “habitable zone”: the region around a star where it is neither too hot nor too cold for liquid water to exist on the surface of surrounding planets. The name comes from the nineteenth-century British fairy tale Goldilocks and the Three Bears.
Figure A1.1.13: The “Goldilocks zone” for our solar systemA schematic (not to scale) of the Sun with the planets arranged by distance: Mercury and Venus lie in a “too hot” zone closest to the Sun; Earth lies within the habitable (Goldilocks) zone, shown as a shaded band; Mars also lies within this habitable zone but is annotated as too small to retain an atmosphere, which is needed to sustain life; Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune lie beyond the zone, in a “too cold” region. A note indicates the habitable zone is suited to planets roughly 1–2 times the size of Earth.
Transit spectroscopyA technique used to establish whether a distant planet may contain water. As a planet passes in front of (“transits”) its star, starlight passes through the planet’s atmosphere on its way to Earth-based or space-based telescopes. This light is analysed to see which wavelengths have been absorbed or deflected, revealing which elements and molecules — such as water — are present in the atmosphere. A planet showing evidence of water this way is said to have a “water signature”. Scientists search for exoplanets (planets outside our solar system) that have a water signature, orbit at the right distance from their star (within the Goldilocks zone), and are the right size for life to plausibly exist — for example, Kepler-186f, an Earth-sized planet located within a Goldilocks zone, is considered a strong candidate for hosting life in another solar system.
