Evolution as Change in the Heritable Characteristics of a Population

Key termEvolution: cumulative change in the heritable characteristics of a population.

It is generally accepted that present-day flora and fauna arose by gradual change (“descent with modification”) from pre-existing forms of life. Evolution has occurred over geological time, but modern evidence shows it can also happen over much more rapid timescales, such as in bacteria.

Lamarck’s Theory of Evolution

In 1809, French naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck proposed an explanation for change in species over time that influenced evolutionary thought through the first half of the nineteenth century:

  • Change through use and disuse: organs used frequently develop, while those used rarely are lost in succeeding generations. Lamarck proposed that increasing complexity arises from the use or disuse of particular traits.
  • Inheritance of acquired characters: characteristics acquired by an individual during its lifetime are inherited by its offspring. Lamarck used the example of a blacksmith: strong arms developed through work would be inherited by the blacksmith’s children.
  • Effect of environment and new needs: changes in the environment bring about changes in organisms, giving rise to new needs, which in turn produce new structures and change organisms’ habits.

A classic (incorrect) Lamarckian example is the giraffe: ancestral giraffes, similar in appearance to modern horses with short necks, stretched to reach leaves; this stretching supposedly lengthened the neck, and the lengthened neck was passed to offspring, who stretched further still. Similarly, Lamarckism proposed that weightlifters’ well-developed muscles could be passed on to their offspring.

Lamarck vs DarwinDarwin’s theory instead proposed that variation already exists within a population; selection pressures mean that organisms better adapted to the environment survive and reproduce, passing on this advantage to their offspring. For the giraffe: some individuals in ancestral populations already had longer necks than others (chance variation); longer-necked individuals were better adapted to reach food and had more offspring, which inherited genes for longer necks; over many generations, the population evolved longer necks. The key element of Darwinism (not Lamarckism) is that evolution is driven by pre-existing differences between individuals in a population; in Lamarckism, the same change could in principle occur in all individuals simultaneously.
Figure A4.1.1: Comparing Lamarck’s and Darwin’s theories of evolutionTwo parallel sequences of giraffe illustrations compare the two theories. The Lamarck sequence shows: ancestral giraffes stretch their necks to reach higher vegetation; the offspring inherit the stretched necks; over generations, offspring repeatedly inherit longer and longer stretched necks. The Darwin sequence shows: in populations of ancestral giraffes, some individuals already had longer necks than others (variation); giraffes with longer necks were better adapted and had more offspring, also with longer necks; over generations, the population evolved to have longer necks through differential survival and reproduction, not through use and inheritance of a stretched trait.

Later, knowledge of DNA structure and function confirmed Darwin’s theory: variation is caused by random mutations in DNA, producing new alleles that may confer a selective advantage. If a mutation occurs in reproductive cells, it can be passed to the next generation. Modern genetics shows that acquired characters cannot be inherited unless they change an organism’s DNA or how it is expressed — changes to a giraffe’s neck during its lifetime do not alter the DNA that codes for neck length.

Epigenetics — not Lamarckian inheritanceEpigenetics is the study of heritable changes in gene activity that are not caused by changes in DNA base sequences. Environmental factors affecting parents can alter the environment in which genes operate, and these changes can be inherited by offspring, altering gene expression in the next generation. However, this is not Lamarckian inheritance: epigenetic changes typically affect gene expression only, may last only a few generations, and are not a stable, permanent basis for long-term evolutionary change.

Evolution is therefore the development of new types of organism from pre-existing types by the accumulation of genetic differences over many generations, through the natural selection of chance (random) variation. Changes an organism acquires during its own lifetime are not inherited.

NOS: Theories — the status of evolutionary theoryThe theory of evolution by natural selection predicts and explains a broad range of observations and is very unlikely ever to be falsified, though the nature of science makes it impossible to formally prove any theory true by strict logical correspondence. Evolution, like atomic theory in chemistry, is an empirical description of reality rather than a mathematical theory provable by formal logic — it is not necessarily “true” in an absolute sense, but it accurately describes the world based on measurement and observation. It is a mistake to think that some general scientific statements are “theories” while others are “facts”: both categories are empirically supported claims.

Evidence for Evolution from Molecular Sequences

All living things use DNA as their genetic material, with a genetic code that is virtually universal. The processes of transcription, translation, and protein synthesis are very similar in prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Respiration involves the same types of steps, similar or identical intermediates, and similarly catalysed biochemical reactions; ATP is the universal energy currency; and among autotrophs, the biochemistry of photosynthesis is virtually identical across species. This biochemical commonality suggests a common origin for life — some of the earliest events in the evolution of life must have been biochemical, and the results have been inherited widely by all descendant lineages.

Large molecules such as nucleic acids and the proteins they code for do change over time, and measuring the degree of difference between specific molecules (DNA, proteins, enzyme systems) between organisms gives data on their relatedness. This relatedness is a function of the time since the organisms shared a common ancestor — so sequence data provide powerful evidence of common ancestry.

Evidence for Evolution from Selective Breeding

Key termArtificial selection: selection in breeding carried out deliberately by humans to alter populations.

Selective breeding (artificial selection) involves identifying the largest, best, or most useful individuals among the progeny and using them as the next generation of parents, while removing progeny with less desirable features generation after generation. This leads to deliberate, and often rapid, genetic change. Although artificial and natural selection differ in their selecting agent (humans versus nature), the underlying variation on which artificial selection acts is generated in exactly the same way as variation in natural populations — by random, chance mutation.

Examples of Crop Improvement by Selective Breeding

Cereal grains (the fruits of cultivated grass species) form the bulk of the human diet worldwide and are relatively easy to grow and store, containing significant protein as well as starch.

Figure A4.1.5: Crops (cereal grasses) selectively bred from wild grassesThe figure illustrates how major cereal crops such as wheat, barley, oats, rice, and maize have each been selectively bred over many generations from their wild grass ancestors, with cultivated varieties showing traits such as larger grains, non-shattering seed heads, and more uniform ripening compared with the wild forms.

Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) arose by natural crossing of various wild wheats around 8000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent of the Middle East. “Winter” wheat varieties are planted in autumn, produce side shoots before lower winter temperatures suspend growth, and mature in early summer; “spring” wheat varieties are sown after winter, adapted to a shorter growing season but with lower yields. There are two classes of wheat: hard wheats (higher protein, grown in lower-rainfall areas, used for longer-lasting bread) and soft wheats (starchier, used for pasta and French bread, grown in more humid conditions).

Maize, the second most important food crop, was probably first domesticated in the region now called Mexico. Modern maize varieties are hybrids selected from the original wild form, teosinte, which still exists in the wild today. Growers and consumers today seek maize that grows vigorously in the local climate and soil, resists disease, gives high-yielding cobs, and grows to a fairly standard height with cobs ready to harvest at the same time.

Variation between different domesticated breeds and crop varieties, and between them and their original wild species, demonstrates how rapidly evolutionary change can occur under strong selection.

Evidence for Evolution from Homologous Structures

Key termsHomologous structures: similar structures due to common ancestry.
Natural classification: organisms grouped by as many common features as possible, and therefore likely to reflect evolutionary relationships.
Phylogenetic classification: classification based on evolutionary relationships rather than on appearances.

The body structures of some organisms appear fundamentally similar. Vertebrate limbs, for instance, conform to a common plan called the pentadactyl limb (meaning “five-fingered”). Such limbs are described as homologous structures: they occupy similar positions in the organism and share a common underlying basic structure, but may have evolved different functions. This shared, modified plan suggests these vertebrates share a common ancestor, from which the tetrapod vertebrates have diverged over a long period — a process called adaptive radiation. Classification based on homologous structures is a natural, or phylogenetic, classification, because it reflects true evolutionary relationships arising from shared common ancestors.

Figure A4.1.6: Homologous structures show adaptive radiationA central diagram of the “ancestral” pentadactyl limb plan labels its bones: humerus (upper arm)/femur (thigh); radius and ulna (forearm)/tibia and fibula (lower leg); carpals (wrist)/tarsals (ankle); and metacarpals plus phalanges (hand)/metatarsals plus phalanges (foot), each with five digits numbered 1–5. Around this central plan, the same bones are shown modified for different functions in five vertebrates: the bat (forelimb modified into a wing for flight, with elongated digits supporting a membrane), the monkey (forelimb adapted for grasping), the horse (forelimb adapted for running, with reduced digits), the whale (forelimb adapted into a paddle for swimming), and the mole (forelimb adapted for digging, broad and shovel-like). In every case the same basic bone arrangement is retained but reshaped for a different use.

Convergent Evolution and Analogous Structures

Key termsAnalogous structure: a feature with a similar function and superficial structural similarity, but a different fundamental structure and evolutionary origin.
Artificial classification: classifying organisms on the basis of a few, self-evident features.
Convergent evolution: the process by which distantly related organisms independently evolve analogous traits due to similar selection pressures.
Divergent evolution: occurs when an ancestral species splits into two reproductively isolated groups, causing each group to develop different traits due to their respective selective pressures.

Structures with similar functions but fundamentally different origins are described as analogous. The wings of an insect and of a bat, for example, look similar but arose through entirely separate evolutionary pathways; classification based on such analogous structures is an artificial classification.

Convergent evolution occurs when different species evolve similar biological adaptations in response to similar selective pressures, typically because they occupy similar ecological niches; the resulting traits are analogous structures — features with similar functions in different organisms but different evolutionary origins. Examples include the fins of dolphins and sharks, or the wings of birds and insects: these look similar due to shared function, but are not similar in anatomy or origin.

Figure A4.1.7: Convergent and divergent evolutionA branching diagram shows a common ancestor giving rise, through divergent evolution, to two major lineages: invertebrates (leading eventually to the dragonfly) and vertebrates (leading eventually to mammals, including the bat and the dolphin). Convergent evolution is illustrated by showing that both the dragonfly (insect wing) and the bat (modified forelimb) independently evolved structures for flight (locomotion through the air) under a shared selection pressure, despite the dragonfly and bat lineages having diverged from entirely different ancestral body plans; these two flight structures are analogous. Meanwhile, the bat’s forelimb and the dolphin’s forelimb (a paddle used for swimming) are shown as homologous, since both are modified versions of the same ancestral mammalian forelimb despite serving very different functions (flight versus swimming).
Common mistakeDo not confuse “homologous” and “analogous”. Analogous structures appear similar between different, unrelated species due to similar selection pressures; homologous structures may appear different but share a common evolutionary origin from a common ancestor. Classification should be based on homologous, not analogous, structures.

Convergent evolution can be observed at both the phenotypic level and the molecular level (amino acid and DNA sequences); morphology and physiology often converge because of the evolution of similar molecular mechanisms in independent lineages. Most mammals have a placenta supporting offspring until birth; marsupials (found largely in Australia) lack a placenta and give birth to undeveloped offspring that must be supported outside the body. Placental mammals and Australian marsupials show many examples of convergent evolution, with animals of comparable appearance occurring in both groups due to similar selection pressures.

Speciation by Splitting of Pre-existing Species

Key termSpeciation: the process by which new species form, where one species splits into two or more species.

For a new species to form, populations of an ancestral species must become separated so that different environmental factors act on them.

Figure A4.1.9: Beetle speciationA sequence of diagrams shows a single ancestral beetle population inhabiting one continuous forest habitat. Over thousands of years, a river forms across the habitat, splitting the population into two isolated groups on either side of the river — the river acting as a geographical barrier, comparable to how the formation of a mountain range through tectonic plate movement could also separate a population.
Figure A4.1.10: Predation pressures on beetlesThe figure shows how, once separated, the two beetle populations face different selection pressures from bird predation. North of the river, where the habitat is lush and green, green-coloured beetles are camouflaged and survive predation better, so green colouration increases in frequency; south of the river, where the habitat is arid with more exposed earth, brown-coloured beetles are better camouflaged and survive better, so brown colouration increases in frequency. Over time, the two populations diverge in colouration and other adapted traits.

By separating populations, a barrier isolates their gene pools so they can no longer interbreed and exchange genes — they become reproductively isolated. Over time, through natural selection, adaptive genes are selected in each population while maladaptive ones are lost, so the two gene pools change and diverge. Eventually the two gene pools become so different that even if the populations come back into contact (e.g. if the river dries up), individuals from each population can no longer interbreed — they have become separate species. Through speciation, the number of species increases (in contrast to extinction, when the number of species decreases). Speciation is the only way new species arise.

Common mistakesIt is incorrect to describe speciation simply as “evolutionary change over time in a species”: progressive evolutionary change within a single interbreeding population, where individuals share the same gene pool, is not speciation. Speciation specifically means the splitting of one species into two or more separate species. Also, do not confuse “speciation” with species classification and taxonomy: speciation is the process leading to the evolution of new species, whereas classification and taxonomy concern how existing species are grouped based on shared evolutionary origin.

Reproductive Isolation and Differential Selection in Speciation

A first step toward speciation may occur when a local population (particularly a small one) becomes completely cut off, for example by colonising an island. Many generations may pass before the gene pool has changed sufficiently to define a distinct species, but changes in local gene pools are an early sign of speciation. Before separation, individuals share a common gene pool; after isolation, natural selection, mutation, and random genetic drift (random fluctuation in allele frequency within a population) can act independently in each isolated population, causing them to diverge.

Reproductive isolationBy definition, members of different species cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so gene flow between them is prevented. When related populations have diverged to this point, they are recognised as separate species.
Geographical isolationOccurs when a barrier arises that restricts the movement of individuals (and, in plants, their spores and gametes) between two populations, dividing what was once a single gene pool. Barriers may be natural (rivers, mountain ranges) or created by humans.
Figure A4.1.12: A geographical barrierThe diagram shows a single forest habitat, home to one species of plant or animal, being divided when a river breaks its banks and takes a new route through the habitat. With time, the small isolated populations on either side of the new river route may evolve into separate species as they experience different selection pressures and accumulate independent genetic changes.

Case Study: Chimpanzees and Bonobos

Chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes) are found north of the Congo River in west and central Africa (in two distinct groups — central African chimpanzees and a distinct eastern group); the closely related bonobo (Pan paniscus) is found only south of the river, in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The Congo River forms a geographical barrier producing reproductive isolation between the two species. Although chimpanzees and bonobos appear superficially similar, they differ significantly in morphology and behaviour.

Figure A4.1.13: The Congo River as a geographical barrier which led to chimpanzee speciationA map of the Congo River basin in central Africa marks the distribution of three groups relative to the river and its major tributaries and falls: central African chimpanzees and east African chimpanzees, both located north of the main Congo (Lualaba) River, and bonobos, located entirely south of the river, around Kinshasa and Kindu. Chimpanzees and bonobos diverged from a common ancestor between 1 and 2 million years ago. It was long thought that the Congo River formed during the Pleistocene epoch (beginning around 1.5 million years ago) and then separated the common ancestral population, with different environmental conditions on each side driving the split into two species. However, evidence from 2015 suggests the Congo River has actually been a barrier for at least 34 million years — far longer than previously thought. This suggests that when the river first formed, the ancestor of the bonobo did not yet occupy its current range, and that during periods when the river’s level dropped during the Pleistocene, one or more founder populations of ancestral bonobos crossed the river and gave rise to the bonobo populations seen today.

Higher LevelDifferences and Similarities Between Sympatric and Allopatric Speciation

Except for cases of instant speciation by polyploidy, speciation is usually a gradual process taking place over a long period (sometimes several thousand years), and in all cases requires some form of isolation between populations. As well as geographical isolation, reproductive isolation between two potentially compatible populations can arise through:

  • Temporal isolation: organisms produce gametes at different times or seasons (e.g. rainbow trout breed in spring, brown trout in autumn), or populations are active at different times of day or night.
  • Behavioural isolation: organisms acquire distinctive behaviour routines, such as courtship or mating rituals, not matched by other individuals of the species.
Figure A4.1.14: A male Bird of Paradise displays to a potential female mateThe image shows a male Bird of Paradise, with bright, glittering plumage, performing a prominent courtship display posture below a female who observes and evaluates him. Competition between males for female attention has driven the evolution of elaborate and attractive display traits, since these satisfy an innate preference in females. Divergence in male display and female preference between populations, even from small initial differences that become exaggerated over generations, can lead to behavioural (sympatric) reproductive isolation, since the genetic makeup of the next generation is strongly influenced by only the few most sexually successful males of each generation.

Such elaborate courtship displays can prevent hybridization between closely related species living in the same area.

Allopatric vs sympatric speciationAllopatric speciation (literally “different country”): speciation that occurs when two groups of organisms are spatially separated by a physical or geographic barrier, such as a mountain range or a large river — an isolating mechanism involving spatial separation.
Sympatric speciation (literally “same country”): speciation that occurs without geographical separation. Groups from the same ancestral population evolve into separate species due to temporal or behavioural isolation that prevents individuals of one group from mating with the other, even though they live in the same location.

Higher LevelAdaptive Radiation as a Source of Biodiversity

Key termAdaptive radiation: the diversification of an ancestral species into new species, characterized by great ecological and morphological diversity, filling different ecological niches.

Adaptive radiation was touched on earlier in relation to the pentadactyl limb in vertebrates. Another classic example is the beaks of the finches of the Galapagos Islands, now known as “Darwin’s finches” — although the birds mostly escaped Darwin’s attention during his visits, and were studied in detail later by the ornithologist David Lack. The variation in finch beak morphology is genetically controlled and reflects differences in feeding habits.

Figure A4.1.15: Adaptive radiation in Galapagos finchesThe figure shows a set of 14–15 species of Galapagos finch, all descended from a single common ancestor and endemic to the islands, arranged to show how beak shape correlates with diet and feeding action. Examples include the large ground finch (Geospiza magnirostris) and medium ground finch (Geospiza fortis), both with strong crushing beaks for a mainly plant-based diet involving crushing seeds; the small ground finch (Geospiza fuliginosa) and sharp-billed ground finch (Geospiza difficilis) with probing or mixed probe-and-crush beak actions; the cactus finch (Geospiza scandens) with a probing beak; the large and small tree finches (Camarhynchus psittacula and Camarhynchus parvulus) with tip-biting beaks suited to a more mixed or animal-based diet; the woodpecker finch (Camarhynchus pallidus), which uses tools to extract insects; and the warbler finch (Certhidea olivacea), with a thin probing beak for a mainly insect diet.

These numerous, closely related finch species can coexist without competing for the same limited food resources, because differences in beak morphology allow each species to exploit a different ecological niche. The Galapagos Islands presented many different ecological opportunities to the ancestral finch population, and these different niches created a range of selection pressures that, over time, produced the variety of finch species seen today. For example, the warbler finch has a different diet to the cactus finch, so the two species do not compete and can coexist, increasing overall biodiversity.

Higher LevelBarriers to Hybridization and Sterility of Interspecific Hybrids

Courtship displays (as in the Birds of Paradise) can prevent hybridization between animal species, and differences in chromosome number can lead to hybrid sterility (as in the mule). Barriers that prevent interbreeding between closely related species act either before fertilization can be attempted (pre-zygotic isolation) or after fertilization has occurred (post-zygotic isolation).

Higher LevelAbrupt Speciation in Plants by Hybridization and Polyploidy

Unlike the gradual speciation processes discussed above, speciation can also occur abruptly through a sudden change in chromosome number.

Key termPolyploidy: a process that results in more than two sets of chromosomes in the nuclear genome; also known as whole genome duplication (WGD). Normal diploid body cells contain two sets of chromosomes.

Polyploidy is largely restricted to plants, and is thought to be one of the most important evolutionary processes in plants, especially angiosperms (flowering plants). Some asexually reproducing animals are polyploid too, but the sex-determination mechanism of vertebrates prevents polyploidy in these animals.

How polyploids formDiploid gametes can arise when the spindle fails to form correctly during meiosis, leaving an extra set of chromosomes in the gamete. There are two mechanisms by which polyploids then form: (1) a diploid gamete fuses with a haploid gamete, producing triploid offspring with three sets of chromosomes (the “triploid bridge”); or (2) a diploid gamete fuses with another diploid gamete, producing tetraploid offspring with four sets of chromosomes. Top tip: polyploidy causes speciation most frequently because a new tetraploid produces only infertile triploid offspring when it mates with either diploid ancestor — triploids are infertile because their chromosomes cannot pair properly during meiosis. This reproductively isolates the new tetraploid lineage from its diploid ancestors almost immediately.
Autopolyploidy vs allopolyploidyAutopolyploid: results from doubling of a single diploid genome (fusion of two diploid gametes of the same species).
Allopolyploid: results from the combination of two distinct genomes, arising through interspecific hybridization followed by chromosome doubling or gamete fusion, or through interspecific hybridization between two tetraploids.
Figure A4.1.16: Comparing autopolyploidy and allopolyploidyTwo parallel diagrams are shown. In autopolyploidy, gametes from two diploid individuals of the same species fuse to produce a tetraploid offspring containing two doubled copies of the same genome. In allopolyploidy, gametes from two diploid individuals of different species fuse (interspecific hybridization) to produce a tetraploid offspring containing one full diploid genome from each parent species combined together.

Many important crop species are polyploids, including Triticum aestivum (wheat), Arachis hypogaea (peanut), and Fragaria ananassa (strawberry). Invasive species — those found outside their normal range — are also often polyploid: interspecific hybridization (allopolyploidy) creates new genetic combinations on which natural selection can act, allowing rapid evolution. Hybrids often display “hybrid vigour”, being larger, more fertile, and more invasive than either parent species; the proportion of polyploid species increases as invasions become more advanced.
