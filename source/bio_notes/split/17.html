Adaptations for Movement in Living Organisms

Motile vs sessileAll organisms carry out movement – it is one of the key processes of life. Motile (or mobile) organisms can move from place to place in search of food or mates, or to migrate to escape extreme cold. Sessile organisms are fixed to one place once established, though some species (e.g. coral) have both a mobile and a sessile life stage. Sessile organisms are fixed in place but can still show movement of parts of the organism, such as cilia.

The cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus) is the fastest animal on land, running at up to 120 km per hour. Its backbone is very flexible and acts like a spring, absorbing energy as the animal flexes during running and converting it back into movement, while its long tail acts as a balance for smooth locomotion.

Figure B3.3.1: A cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus) in pursuit of preyA photograph of a cheetah running at speed while chasing prey, illustrating its flexible, spring-like backbone and long balancing tail.

Sessile organisms, such as coral, still carry out movements to feed. The coral polyp secretes a calcium carbonate skeleton, rendering it immobile; its tentacles contain stinging cells (nematocysts) that kill small prey, which are then moved into the central mouth. Coral larvae, however, are fully mobile, forming when eggs and sperm released from sessile adults fuse in seawater during fertilization.

Barnacles are another sessile animal, found on rocky shores, feeding by filtering seawater through fine hairs on feather-like structures called cirri; when the cirri are drawn back, food is scraped into the mouth. Sessile organisms can be moved by external forces but are usually attached to something providing stability and security.

Figure B3.3.2: Barnacles are sessileA photograph of barnacles attached to a rocky surface, shown filtering plankton from the seawater using their cirri.

Plants are largely sessile but also carry out movement, such as growing towards light to maximize photosynthesis (phototropism). The Venus fly trap (Dionaea muscipula), a carnivorous plant, closes its trap when an insect touches a trigger hair on its surface a couple of times, then secretes digestive enzymes to digest the prey.

Figure B3.3.3: A Venus fly trap (Dionaea muscipula) with captured flyA photograph of a Venus fly trap with its hinged, hair-triggered trap closed around a captured fly.

Motile organisms have many adaptations that sessile species lack, such as wings, legs, flippers, fins and webbed feet. Motile animals often have a streamlined body to minimize friction with air or water as they move, unlike most sessile species.

Sliding Filament Model of Muscle Contraction

Skeletal muscle fibres are multinucleate and contain specialized endoplasmic reticulum. Their striped appearance is due to an interlocking arrangement of two types of protein filaments. Thick filaments, about 15 nm in diameter, are made of the protein myosin. Longer thin filaments, about 7 nm in diameter, are made of the protein actin and are held together by transverse bands called Z lines. Each repeating unit of the myofibril, attached end to end in a series, is called a sarcomere. In skeletal muscle, fibres appear striped (striated) under the microscope, so this muscle is also known as striated muscle.

Figure B3.3.4: The ultrastructure of a myofibrilA diagram (with an accompanying electron micrograph of an individual sarcomere) labelling one sarcomere between two Z lines: the myosin thick filament (with “heads”) runs through the centre, overlapped at each end by actin thin filaments extending from the Z lines; a dark band marks the region of filament overlap, and light bands mark the regions containing only actin, either side of the Z lines.
The sliding-filament mechanismWhen skeletal muscle contracts in response to nervous stimulation, actin and myosin filaments slide past each other, shortening the sarcomeres, in a ratchet-like series of steps requiring ATP. This shortening is possible because thick filaments are composed of many myosin molecules, each with a bulbous head that protrudes from the filament, fitting into a complementary series of binding sites along the actin filament. At rest, these binding sites are covered by a blocking protein, tropomyosin (which wraps around actin filaments), so binding and contraction cannot occur.
Figure B3.3.5: Muscle contraction of a single sarcomerePaired electron micrographs and interpretive drawings comparing a stretched/relaxed sarcomere (thick myosin and thin actin filaments overlapping only slightly, sarcomere longer) with a contracted sarcomere (filaments overlapping much more extensively, sarcomere shortened), illustrating that filament length itself does not change – only the degree of overlap (sliding) between actin and myosin filaments changes.
The four steps of the contraction cycle
  1. The myofibril is stimulated to contract by the arrival of an action potential, triggering release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to surround the actin molecules. Calcium ions react with the protein troponin, which when activated triggers removal of the blocking molecule tropomyosin, exposing the binding sites.
  2. Each bulbous myosin head, carrying attached ADP and Pi (a “charged” head), reacts with an exposed binding site on the neighbouring actin molecule; the phosphate group (Pi) is dislodged from the head at this point.
  3. The ADP molecule is then released from the bulbous head; this triggers the rowing movement of the head (the power stroke), which tilts by about 45°, pushing the actin filament along and shortening the sarcomere (contraction).
  4. A new molecule of ATP binds to the bulbous head. The head’s ATPase enzyme catalyses hydrolysis of this ATP; the resulting ADP and Pi remain attached, “recharging” the head, which detaches from the binding site and straightens.
Figure B3.3.6: The sliding-filament hypothesis of muscle contractionA cyclical diagram of the cross-bridge cycle around a myosin filament with cross-bridges extending to a neighbouring actin filament, whose binding sites are shown exposed. Step 1: a myosin head (carrying ADP and Pi) binds to an exposed binding site on actin, and Pi is released. Step 2: release of ADP triggers the “rowing action” power stroke of the myosin head, moving the actin filament. Step 3: ATP combines with the myosin head and is hydrolysed to ADP and Pi, releasing the head and allowing the cross-bridge to straighten. Step 4: the cycle repeats at a binding site further along the actin molecule. A separate note explains that when the action potential ceases, Ca2+ ions return to the sarcoplasmic reticulum and binding sites become blocked again by tropomyosin.

Muscles need ATP to release from a contracted state – ATP causes separation of the cross-bridges during relaxation. Rigor mortis (the stiffening of muscles after death) develops because ATP is no longer available to break cross-bridges, so muscles remain contracted. Calcium ions do not directly form cross-bridges between actin and myosin; rather, they act by moving the blocking molecules off the myosin binding sites on actin, allowing cross-bridges to form.

Figure B3.3.7: Analysing states of contraction in striated muscle fibreFour diagrams of a single sarcomere (thin actin and thick myosin filaments, bounded by Z lines) in progressively greater states of contraction: 1) a relaxed sarcomere (little filament overlap, sarcomere at its longest); 2) a slightly contracted sarcomere; 3) a moderately contracted sarcomere; 4) a fully contracted sarcomere, with maximal filament overlap and the shortest Z-line-to-Z-line distance.
Concept: Form and functionStriated muscle tissue takes the form of a repeating series of functional units (sarcomeres). By fusing cells together to form a multinuclear structure, muscle fibres generate force and contract to support breathing, locomotion and posture (skeletal muscle), and to pump blood throughout the body (cardiac muscle).

Titin and Antagonistic Muscles in Relaxation

TitinTitin is an immense mechanical protein found within the sarcomere of striated (and cardiac) muscle – the largest protein in the body, composed of about 27,000 amino acids. Its main function is to act as a molecular spring in the sarcomere. Titin is extended when the sarcomeres of skeletal muscle are passively stretched by contraction of an antagonistic muscle – a muscle that works as one of a pair, so that as one muscle (the agonist) contracts, the other (the antagonist) relaxes/lengthens. For example, titin in the external intercostal muscles is extended and stretched when the internal intercostal muscles contract.
Figure B3.3.9: Diagram showing the position of titin in the sarcomere, before stretchingA diagram of part of a sarcomere showing the titin protein running as a filament from the Z line, anchored along its length, alongside and parallel to the myosin (thick) and actin (thin) filaments, centring and stabilizing the myosin filament between the actin filaments.

Antagonistic muscles are needed because muscle tissue can only exert force when it contracts; after being stretched, titin recoils like a spring, shortening the sarcomere. Titin also stabilizes myosin, centring it between the thin actin filaments, and prevents overstretching of the sarcomere – it binds at several sites along its length to the actin filaments, mainly at the edges of the Z line, serving an anchoring function. Beyond acting as a passive spring, research shows titin can also create “active” contraction by folding itself to generate force, undergoing unfolding–refolding changes as the muscle stretches and recoils.

Common mistakeMuscles only do work when they contract. Muscles can only contract and relax, so they work in antagonistic pairs – they cannot actively extend or lengthen; extension happens only as a result of the contraction of the antagonistic muscle.

Motor Units in Skeletal Muscle

Stimuli received by receptors travel through the nervous system via a series of neurons. Receptors create an electrical impulse (the action potential), which passes along sensory neurons, via relay neurons in the central nervous system, to motor neurons, which connect to effectors (muscle or glands) that carry out the response to the stimulus. Motor neurons have the cell body at one end, with an axon forming the structure through which the action potential passes.

Myelin sheathA neuron is surrounded by many supporting cells; Schwann cells become wrapped around the axons of motor neurons, forming a structure called the myelin sheath. Myelin consists largely of lipid and has high electrical resistance. Frequent gaps occur along the myelin sheath, between individual Schwann cells – these gaps are called nodes of Ranvier.
Figure B3.3.10: A motor neuronA diagram of a motor neuron labelled with dendrites, cell body, nucleus, axon, the myelin sheath of the nerve fibre (formed by a Schwann cell wrapping itself around the axon), a node of Ranvier (the junction between two sheath cells), and, at the far end of the axon, the motor end plates.
The neuromuscular junctionStriated muscle fibres are supplied by a motor neuron nerve ending known as a motor end plate or neuromuscular junction – a specialized synapse between a motor neuron nerve terminal and its muscle fibre, converting electrical impulses in the motor neuron into electrical activity in the muscle fibre. The chemical transmitter substance that diffuses across the synaptic cleft here is acetylcholine (ACh). When an action potential arrives at the neuromuscular junction, calcium ions enter the motor neuron and cause synaptic vesicle exocytosis: vesicles of ACh move to the presynaptic membrane and release ACh into the junction. ACh binds to receptors on the sarcolemma (the plasma membrane of the muscle fibre), triggering release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the cytoplasm around the myofibrils. These calcium ions then remove the blocking molecules on the actin binding sites, starting sarcomere contraction. When action potentials stop arriving, calcium ions return to the sarcoplasmic reticulum and the binding sites are covered again.
Figure B3.3.11: The neuromuscular junctionA diagram of the neuromuscular junction showing the axon (with its myelin sheath) ending at a presynaptic membrane, containing mitochondria and vesicles (of acetylcholine), separated by the synaptic cleft from the postsynaptic membrane of the muscle fibre, which forms part of the sarcolemma; beneath this lies the sarcoplasm containing myofibrils.
Common mistakeActin and myosin are not produced in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), which is smooth endoplasmic reticulum with no associated ribosomes, nor in rough endoplasmic reticulum (those proteins would be exported rather than used within the muscle cell). Actin and myosin are produced by free-floating ribosomes in the cytoplasm of muscle cells. Also, do not confuse the two proteins: myosin is a thick filament with a head, whereas actin is a thinner filament without a head.

Skeletons as Anchorage for Muscles and as Levers

Locomotion – the movement of whole organisms – results from the interaction of nervous, muscular and skeletal systems. Bones support and partially protect body parts, articulate with other bones at moveable joints, and provide anchorage for muscles. In mammals, the skeleton consists of the axial skeleton (skull and vertebral column) and the appendicular skeleton (limb girdles and limbs).

Endoskeleton vs exoskeletonVertebrates have an endoskeleton – an internal support structure (usually bone, though cartilage in some fish such as sharks) to which muscles are attached via tendons (“endo” means inside, as the structure lies within the body). Arthropods (e.g. insects) instead have an exoskeleton – a tough external skeleton covering the body and limbs, secreted external to the epidermis, with a flexible membrane between body segments and at limb joints. The muscles for movement attach to the inside of the exoskeleton; an arthropod’s legs are a series of hollow cylinders made from chitin (a modified, nitrogen-containing structural polysaccharide), held together by joints, across which muscles are attached in antagonistic pairs.
Figure B3.3.12: Limb movement – in insects and mammalsTwo comparative diagrams. The insect (locust) leg is shown in section, with a hard exoskeleton and a pliable hinge at the joint, and antagonistic muscle pairs (muscle a and muscle b) attached to the inside of the exoskeleton, producing movements a′ and b′ respectively when each contracts. The mammal limb diagram shows the humerus and shoulder blade with antagonistic muscle pairs attached to the outside of the bones in a directly comparable arrangement. In both cases, the controlled movement of a limb in any direction depends on the balance of opposing contraction of antagonistic pairs of muscles.
Bones, joints and muscles as leversThe skeleton, together with the muscles attached across joints, functions as a system of levers. Each joint acts as a pivot point or fulcrum. The force applied when a muscle contracts is the effort; the load, or force to be overcome, is the resistance. The further the effort is applied from the fulcrum, the greater the leverage – the smaller the force needed to raise the load.
Figure B3.3.13: A lever system in the bodyA diagram of the arm as a lever system, showing the elbow joint as the fulcrum, the biceps (flexor muscle of the upper arm) providing the effort close to the fulcrum, and a load held in the hand at the far end of the forearm, illustrating how a relatively small muscle effort near the joint can move a load at the far end of the lever.

Movement at a Synovial Joint and Range of Motion

Synovial jointMoveable joints in the body are synovial joints, where a very thin layer of viscous synovial fluid separates and lubricates two cartilage-covered bone surfaces, nourishing the cartilage and removing debris from worn bone and cartilage. Tendons (fibrous connective tissue) attach muscles to bones; cartilage (a flexible connective tissue made of collagen, with a smooth, shiny, friction-reducing surface and high tensile strength) cushions the ends of bones at joints; ligaments (also fibrous connective tissue) connect bones to other bones and help support internal organs.

At the hip is a ball-and-socket joint: the ball-like surface of one bone (the head of the femur) fits into a cup-like depression on another bone (the pelvis). Muscles between the pelvis and femur allow movement of the leg in three planes.

Figure B3.3.14: Hip joint: a) front view, b) muscle attachmentsTwo diagrams of the hip joint. a) A front view showing the pelvis with its cup-like socket, cartilage lining the socket, synovial fluid within the joint cavity, and the ball-shaped head of the femur fitting into the socket, with a ligament and tendons also shown. b) A view showing the muscle and tendon attachments running from the pelvis to the femur across the joint.
Ball-and-socket joints vs hinge jointsThe hip and shoulder are ball-and-socket joints, permitting the widest range of movement, in all three planes – a movement described as circumduction (though, in contrast to the hip, the shoulder’s shallow socket makes it easily dislocated). The knee and elbow are hinge joints, restricting movement to one plane because of the shape of the articulating bone surfaces and the position of the ligaments holding the bones together; movements here are described as flexion and extension.

The ball-and-socket joint (shoulder, and by extension the hip) shows the humerus head, cartilage and synovial fluid within the joint, permitting circumduction (movement in three planes). The hinge joint (elbow, and by extension the knee) shows movement restricted to one plane, illustrating extension and flexion only, with articulating surfaces and ligaments constraining movement to that plane.

Intercostal Muscles: Antagonistic Action for Body Movements

Intercostal muscles are involved in ventilation of the lungs. There are two groups, internal and external, which work antagonistically. The internal intercostal muscles attach to the internal surface of the ribs, and the external intercostal muscles attach to the outer surface.

How the intercostal muscles work
  • External intercostal muscles contract to pull the ribs up and out.
  • This increases the volume of the thorax and reduces the pressure, forcing air into the lungs.
  • Internal intercostal muscles contract to pull the ribs down and in.
  • This decreases the volume of the thorax and increases the pressure, forcing air out of the lungs.
Figure B3.3.17: External and internal intercostal muscles work antagonistically to ventilate the lungsA diagram of the ribcage with a section cut away to reveal two layers of intercostal muscle between adjacent ribs, running from the sternum around to the ribs: the external intercostal muscle (outer layer) and internal intercostal muscle (inner layer), with their muscle fibres shown running in different, crossing orientations, explaining why their contraction moves the ribcage in opposite directions.

The different fibre orientations of the internal and external intercostal layers mean that they move the ribcage in opposite directions. When one layer contracts it stretches the other, storing potential energy in the sarcomere protein titin. Intercostal muscles are generally not used in shallow (quiet) breathing, where the diaphragm alone contracts and relaxes to move air into and out of the lungs; the intercostal muscles are recruited during more vigorous exercise, when greater volumes of air are needed for increased respiration.

Reasons for Locomotion

There are several reasons animals carry out locomotion:

  • Foraging for food – for example, a blackbird (Turdus merula) feeding on an earthworm; insects and other invertebrates dominate its diet in spring and summer, while seeds and berries become more important in colder months when invertebrates are less abundant.
  • Escaping from danger – for example, a snowshoe hare running from a lynx.
  • Searching for a mate – most great apes (e.g. gorillas, chimpanzees) live in groups, but orangutans are solitary as adults because their fruit-based diet is not available in sufficient quantities to support large groups; they meet others of their species only to reproduce, often travelling large distances through the forest to find a mate.
  • Migration – many animals leave an area to escape colder months; for example, many whale species undertake some of the longest migrations on Earth, swimming thousands of miles over several months to breed in warmer tropical waters.

Adaptations for Swimming in Marine Mammals

Marine mammals include seals, sea lions, dugongs, manatees and cetaceans (dolphins, porpoises and whales). Because water is much denser than air, these animals need specific adaptations to move efficiently through it – body forms suited to life on land (such as hands, feet, and flat body parts like the front of the thorax and pelvis) offer too much resistance or obstruction in water. A streamlined, hydrodynamic body shape reduces friction (drag), enabling easier movement. Some marine mammals are fully aquatic (e.g. whales), while others are semi-aquatic, living in both water and on land (e.g. seals and sea lions); this difference influences their specific adaptations, such as differing tail types.

Limb and tail adaptationsFront limbs have modified pentadactyl limbs forming flippers (pectoral fins), with a large surface area but streamlined shape to direct body movement. The pelvic bone, which anchors legs in land mammals, is much reduced in marine mammals, and rear limbs are lacking (as they would hinder movement through water). The tail is modified into a fluke (caudal fin), which moves up and down through muscular movement to propel the animal through the water.
Common mistakeMarine mammals such as whales are sometimes confused with large fish such as sharks because of similar size and shape. One key anatomical difference is tail movement: shark tails move side to side, whereas marine mammal flukes move up and down.

Other features that would hinder movement through water are generally reduced or lacking, such as external ears (though semi-aquatic mammals like sea lions retain external ears to help locate prey on land) and external reproductive organs. The neck is short and relatively immobile, enabling fast, muscular swimming.

Marine mammals have specialized skin, with an inner-most layer of blubber that covers bone that would otherwise project from the body, keeping the skin surface streamlined. The outer skin layer further improves hydrodynamics by continually exuding oil droplets and shedding epithelial cells – this continual shedding prevents dead skin building up and increasing drag, while the oil lubricates the skin so water passes over it more smoothly.

Breathing adaptations in cetaceansCetaceans (whales and dolphins) have nostrils (blowholes) located at the top of the head, allowing them to breathe quickly and easily when they surface. Being mammals, they have lungs and so, unlike fish, cannot ventilate underwater – they must take a breath before diving and close the airway to keep water out. On surfacing, the animal opens its blowhole, expels air from the lungs, then inhales quickly before closing the blowhole again before diving; the blowhole stays closed throughout the dive. Other marine mammals, such as seals and sea lions, lack these adaptations, since they can come up onto land to breathe and so do not need blowholes.
