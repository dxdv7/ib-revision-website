System Integration and Emergent Properties

Organisms are made of many interacting parts, and an increased number or specialization of those parts leads to greater complexity in how they interact. This is true at every level of organization, from molecules to ecosystems. Interactions between systems, or between parts of systems, mean there is integration between them.

Concept: Interaction System interaction and integration is a necessary process in living systems. Coordination is needed for the component parts of a system to collectively perform an overall function.
Figure C3.1.1: System integration exists at every level of biological organization The diagram shows a nested hierarchy running from molecular integration (genes) through cellular integration to organ integration, building up the organism, which in turn sits within an ecosystem where it interacts with abiotic factors (such as climate and soil quality) and biotic factors (such as disease, parasites, competition, predators and decomposers) within a habitat and community.

It can be said that “complex systems are more than the sum of their parts”. A cell’s function, for example, depends on the collective behaviour of many molecular parts acting together. These collective properties, known as emergent properties, are essential properties of biological systems: the overall system’s behaviour cannot be understood or predicted from the individual parts alone – a holistic approach is needed. Emergent properties arise from the interactions of the parts of the larger system.

Concept: Interaction Emergent properties arise from the interactions of the parts of the larger system. A hierarchy of subsystems works together to provide functions that the individual parts do not have. Emergent properties help living organisms better adapt to their environments.
Key terms Integration: the ability of interacting parts of a biological system to perform together and maintain a functioning organism or other biological system.
Emergent property: a property gained by a complex system when the individual parts work together.

The Hierarchy of Subsystems in Multicellular Organisms

Cells, tissues, organs and organ systems each have their own properties, forming a hierarchy of subsystems integrated in a multicellular organism. The cells and tissues of the small intestine, for example, each have their own properties and functions, but working together they allow the whole organ to carry out the emergent properties of peristalsis, digestion, food absorption and transport.

Figure C3.1.2: Tissues of part of the mammalian gut A transverse section of the small intestine (low power) shows the pavement epithelium as a smooth, strong sheet of cells lining finger-like villi that project into the channel of the gut; beneath this, circular and longitudinal smooth muscle layers can contract and relax repeatedly for long periods without fatigue; connective tissue binds together all the tissues; and an outer peritoneum (smooth lining) attaches the gut to the body wall.

Cellular Level

The basic unit of life is the cell, built from macromolecules. Each macromolecule determines the structure and function of the cell. Nucleotides form nucleic acids containing genes that determine an organism’s characteristics, and interactions between genes and amino acids determine which proteins are formed and the ultimate function of the cell.

Tissue Level

Multicellular organisms arrange cells into tissues – groups of similar cells working together to perform a particular function. A single epithelial cell, for instance, cannot form a protective layer, whereas multiple epithelial cells can form layers and structures carrying out protection, secretion, absorption, filtration or sensory reception. The five tissue types found in animals are: epithelial tissue (forms the linings of organs), connective tissue (connects, separates and supports other tissues), blood (a collection of similar specialized cells serving particular functions), nervous tissue (neurons, allowing the body to receive stimuli and coordinate responses) and muscle tissue (cells capable of generating motion, such as pumping blood or moving the body).

Organ Level

Different tissue types combine to form organs performing specialized functions. An organ’s properties depend on those emerging from interactions between its tissues and earlier levels of organization – DNA determines how cells form tissues and how tissues form organs.

Organ System Level

Organs form organ systems. The digestive system, for example, is formed from interactions between the mouth, oesophagus, stomach, liver, pancreas, gall bladder, small intestine and large intestine – the stomach cannot digest food without the enzymes and digestive fluids that the gallbladder, liver and pancreas provide. Interactions between organs in a system are controlled at the molecular level.

Organism Level

Organ systems together create an organism – the combined interaction at every level of organization. The whole body cannot function without closely controlled system integration at every level; ultimately, genes determine the structure and function of an organism’s components and how they interact.

Integration of Organs by Hormonal and Nervous Signalling

Integration of organs in animal bodies is controlled by hormonal and nervous signalling, and by transport of materials and energy. Nervous signalling was examined in C2.2; the hormonal system is outlined here.

The Endocrine System

Hormones are chemical substances produced and secreted by the cells of ductless, or endocrine, glands (“endo” meaning within, “crine” meaning secreting). The network of glands throughout the body is the endocrine system. Hormones are “chemical messengers”: they are transported in the bloodstream but act only at specific target organs. Although present in small quantities, they are extremely effective, helping control and coordinate body activities by changing specific metabolic reactions in target organs. However, they circulate only briefly – they are broken down in the liver and excreted via the kidneys, so long-acting hormones must be secreted continuously to remain effective. Insulin, released from endocrine cells in the pancreas to regulate blood glucose, is one example.

Figure C3.1.3: The human endocrine system The diagram shows the positions of the major endocrine glands: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland at the base of the brain; the pineal gland, which secretes melatonin to help regulate the body’s circadian cycle; the thyroid gland; the thymus gland; the adrenal glands sitting atop the kidneys, secreting epinephrine to control the body’s “fight or flight” response; the pancreas (islets of Langerhans), secreting insulin and glucagon to control blood sugar level; and the ovaries/testes, secreting the sex hormones oestradiol and testosterone.

Transport of Materials in the Blood System

Products of digestion are absorbed from the gut into the blood and then into body cells, a process called assimilation. The blood system transports materials between organs.

  • All products of carbohydrate digestion pass from epithelial cells lining the small intestine into the capillary network by facilitated diffusion. Monosaccharides (mainly glucose) dissolve and are transported in blood plasma to the liver and then the rest of the body; the liver maintains a constant blood sugar level.
  • Amino acids are actively transported into the small intestine’s epithelial cells by membrane protein pumps, then pass into the capillary network and are transported to the liver, contributing to the pool of amino acids used to make new proteins throughout the body.
  • Short-chain fatty acids and glycerol are absorbed by simple diffusion into the epithelial cells and then diffuse out into the capillaries, transported in the bloodstream like glucose and amino acids.
  • Long-chain fatty acids are recombined with glycerol to reform triglycerides, which are hydrophobic and cannot travel in blood plasma unmodified. They are coated with proteins in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum of epithelial cells and enter the lymph system, which transports them into the blood circulation near the heart.
  • Waste products are also transported in blood plasma: urea, produced by the breakdown of amino acids in the liver, is toxic and filtered out by the kidneys; carbon dioxide, a respiration waste product, is carried by both plasma and red blood cells and removed in the lungs.

The hepatic portal vein carries digestion products to the liver, where glucose may be converted to glycogen; excess blood glucose may also be converted to glycogen in muscle cells. Brain cells depend on a continuous glucose supply from the blood, as they cannot store glycogen. Oxygen combines with haemoglobin in red blood cells and is transported to cells for aerobic respiration.

Key term Assimilation: uptake of nutrients into cells and the utilization of this material to provide energy and to synthesize new biological molecules.

The Brain as a Central Information Integration Organ

The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal cord; nerves of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) run to and from it, enabling communication between the CNS and the rest of the body.

Figure C3.1.4: The organization of the central and peripheral nervous systems The diagram shows the CNS (brain and spinal cord) connected via the PNS, which is subdivided into sensory neurons carrying impulses from sense organs to the CNS and neurons carrying impulses from the CNS to muscles and glands. This motor pathway further divides into the somatic nervous system and the autonomic nervous system, and the autonomic nervous system further divides into the sympathetic nervous system and the parasympathetic nervous system.

The human brain controls body functions (apart from those under simple spinal reflexes) by: receiving impulses from sensory receptors; integrating and correlating incoming information in association centres; sending impulses to effector organs (muscles and glands), causing bodily responses; storing information and building an accessible memory bank; and initiating impulses from its own self-contained activity – the brain is also the seat of personality and emotions, enabling imagination, creation, planning, calculation, prediction and abstract reasoning. The cerebral hemispheres make up a large proportion of the brain and are more highly developed in humans than in other animals.

Research into higher brain functions such as memory and learning has relied heavily on cooperation between scientists. The case of patient Henry Molaison (HM), who developed severe amnesia after surgery removed most of his hippocampus, illustrates this: decades of MRI scans and psychological experiments, followed by detailed post-mortem study of his brain tissue, have been shared and built upon by many researchers, greatly advancing understanding of memory. Other research has found that the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, besides controlling circadian rhythm, has a role in learning and memory – in hamsters, a malfunctioning SCN impairs memory, and its surgical removal restores memory abilities; altered circadian rhythms have also been linked to diminished memory in Alzheimer’s disease.

The Spinal Cord and the Pathways of Sensory Input and Motor Output

The Spinal Cord as an Integrating Centre

The spinal cord carries nerve impulses between the brain and the rest of the body, and also controls reflexes without input from the brain: electrical impulses pass from receptors to the CNS along sensory neurons and synapse with interneurons, which send impulses along motor neurons directly to effectors. Reflexes are unconscious, automatic and involuntary responses to stimuli that protect the body from harm (for example, the pain reflex), in contrast with conscious processes, which are deliberate and voluntary. Autonomic functions controlled via the spinal cord include sweating of the lower half of the body, defecation and urination.

Common mistake Do not confuse conscious processes (voluntary muscle movements, thought, speech) with unconscious processes (heart rate, regulation of pupil size, movement of food through the digestive system).

Sensory Input to the Spinal Cord and Cerebral Hemispheres

The cerebral hemispheres, an extension of the forebrain, form the bulk of the human brain, consisting of densely packed neurons (“grey matter”) and myelinated nerve fibres (“white matter”). The ratio of cerebral hemisphere size to whole nervous system size is larger in humans than in any other mammal. The human brain contains about 1011–1012 relay neurons and a similar number of supporting neuroglia cells; most occur in the cerebral hemispheres, where each relay neuron is in synaptic contact with about a thousand others. Many voluntary activities, together with many involuntary ones, are coordinated in the cerebral hemispheres, which act as an integrating centre for memory, learning, emotions and other complex functions.

The pathway of nerve impulses from receptor to CNS can be traced using the example of writing with a pen:

  1. As the pen is touched, a sensory receptor in the skin of the fingers is stimulated.
  2. The receptor converts the touch energy into an electrical impulse (action potential) in the axon of the sensory neuron, which conveys the message from receptor to CNS; the signal travels along the axon to the spinal cord and brain.
  3. The signal causes neurotransmitter release at a synapse between the sensory neuron and an interneuron, stimulating an action potential in the interneuron, which travels along its axon and causes neurotransmitter release at the next synapse with another interneuron.
  4. This process of neurotransmitter release followed by action potential generation repeats as interneurons in higher parts of the brain, such as the cerebral cortex, are activated.
  5. Once interneurons in the cerebral cortex are activated, perception occurs and the surface of the pen is sensed – conscious awareness of a sensation is primarily a function of the cerebral cortex.
Figure C3.1.5: Pathway of nerve impulses from receptor to effector A numbered diagram (1–8) traces the pathway: a sensory receptor in the skin (1) generates an impulse carried by a sensory neuron to the spinal cord (2); this synapses with an interneuron (3), and further interneurons carry the signal up through the spinal cord and thalamus to the cerebral cortex (4), where perception occurs (5); a stimulus in the motor cerebral cortex triggers an upper motor neuron that descends through the brain (6) and synapses in the spinal cord with a lower motor neuron (7), which travels via a neuromuscular junction (8) to the skeletal muscles. The diagram also shows the pathway crossing from one side of the brain to control the opposite side of the body.

Motor Output from the Cerebral Hemispheres

Continuing the example: a stimulus in the brain causes an action potential to form in the dendrites and cell body of an upper motor neuron, which synapses with a lower motor neuron farther down the CNS. The action potential travels down the upper motor neuron’s axon, causing neurotransmitter release at the synapse with the lower motor neuron, generating an action potential there. This action potential causes neurotransmitter release at neuromuscular junctions formed with skeletal muscle fibres, stimulating them to form muscle action potentials, which cause the muscle fibres to contract – allowing writing with the pen.

Top tip! Motor neurons transmit electrical impulses from the central nervous system to muscles and glands (effectors). Motor neuron pathways lead to muscles contracting.
Key term Cerebral hemisphere (cerebrum): the bulk of the human brain, formed during development by outgrowth of part of the forebrain, consisting of densely packed neurons and myelinated nerve fibres.

Nerves as Bundles of Nerve Fibres

A nerve consists of many axons collected into bundles; large nerves, such as the sciatic nerves running from the lower back to the feet, contain tens of thousands of axons. Each bundle has several protective connective-tissue coverings:

  • Endoneurium: tissue surrounding each individual fibre.
  • Perineurium: smooth connective tissue surrounding each bundle of fibres.
  • Epineurium: fibrous tissue surrounding and enclosing a number of bundles of nerve fibres; most large nerves are covered by epineurium.
Common mistake Do not confuse the terms “neuron” and “nerve”. A neuron is a single specialized cell in the nervous system that conducts electrical impulses, whereas a nerve is a bundle of many neurons’ axons.
Figure C3.1.6: Transverse section of a peripheral nerve The diagram shows several nerve fibres (each an axon with its myelin sheath, Schwann cells and nodes of Ranvier visible), each individually wrapped in endoneurium, grouped into a bundle wrapped in perineurium, with the whole nerve enclosed by an outer layer of epineurium; blood vessels run through the connective tissue.

Pain Reflex Arcs

A reflex arc comprises the neurons that mediate a reflex.

Figure C3.1.7: The layout of a reflex arc A stimulus (such as mechanical pressure) acts on a sense organ/receptor, which sends an impulse via a sensory neuron to a relay neuron, which connects to a motor neuron, which carries the impulse to an effector organ such as a muscle.

By means of a specific reflex arc, a particular stimulus produces the same immediate, quick, involuntary response – a reflex action – every time. An example in humans is jerking a hand away from scalding water, or withdrawing a limb from pain. The pain reflex arc has a single relay neuron (interneuron) in the grey matter of the spinal cord and a free sensory nerve ending in a sensory neuron acting as a pain receptor.

Figure C3.1.8: Pain reflex arcs as an example of involuntary responses with skeletal muscle as the effector The diagram shows a cross-section of the spinal cord: a heat stimulus acts on a sensory receptor in the skin, generating an impulse carried along the dendron and cell body of a sensory neuron into the grey matter of the spinal cord, where it synapses with a relay neuron. The relay neuron synapses with a motor neuron whose axon leaves via the ventral root and travels to the biceps (flexor muscle), the effector organ, pulling the hand away from the heat. The relay neuron also has connections to and from the brain, allowing sensory input (awareness, pain, memory) and motor input from the brain (for example, a conscious decision to run the hand under cold water).

Within the nervous system there are a vast number of reflex arcs, connected to the brain – a highly organized mass of relay neurons linked to the rest of the nervous system by motor and sensory neurons – by many neurons. Complex patterns of behaviour occur alongside many reflex actions because impulses that originate in a reflex arc also travel to the brain, and impulses may originate in the brain and be conducted to effector organs. Much activity is therefore initiated by the brain rather than being merely a response to external stimuli, and reflex actions may be overruled and modified by the brain (for instance, choosing not to drop an extremely hot but valuable object). The nervous system thus has roles in both quick, precise communication between sense organs and effectors, and in complex behaviour patterns.

Role of the Cerebellum

The cerebellum, part of the hindbrain, has an external surface layer of grey matter. Here, involuntary muscle movements of posture and balance are controlled, and precise, voluntary manipulations involved in hand movements, speech and writing are coordinated (rather than initiated). The cerebellum is in overall control of body movements, including skeletal muscle contraction and balance: it receives input from receptors and adjusts commands to motor neurons to maintain balance, fine-tuning movement without initiating motor control. While the cerebellum makes up only 10% of the human brain’s volume, it contains 50% of the total number of neurons in the brain, indicating its importance.

Figure C3.1.9: Location of the cerebellum in the brain A sagittal diagram of the brain shows the cerebellum located at the back of the brain, beneath the cerebrum and above the brain stem.
Key term Cerebellum: part of the hindbrain, concerned with muscle tone, posture and movement.

Modulation of Sleep Patterns by Melatonin

Daily rhythms in physiological processes and behaviour are common throughout plants and animals. Animals are active for only part of the 24-hour cycle: some are crepuscular (active at dusk or dawn), some nocturnal (active at night), and many, like humans, diurnal (active during the day). Much of our behaviour – physical activity, sleep, body temperature, hormone secretion and other features – follows regular cycles of about 24 hours, called circadian rhythms. These are controlled by a biological clock in the brain, coordinated with the cycle of light and dark.

Melatonin is a hormone produced in the pineal gland that contributes to setting the biological clock. More melatonin is released in darkness, and more in seasonally longer nights (such as winter). Light decreases melatonin production, preparing the body to be awake; in darkness, melatonin production resumes and sleepiness returns. Seasonal affective disorder (SAD, “winter depression”) is associated with higher-than-normal melatonin levels.

Figure C3.1.10: Circadian rhythm over a 24-hour cycle A circular 24-hour clock diagram shows how physiological variables cycle through the day: melatonin secretion starts and stops at particular points; deepest sleep, lowest heart rate and lowest body temperature occur in the early hours; the sharpest rise in blood pressure, highest alertness and highest risk of myocardial infarction occur around waking; best coordination, fastest reaction time and greatest cardiovascular and muscle strength occur later in the day; and highest blood pressure and body temperature occur in the early evening, before working ability decreases towards night.

Light has an impact on the biological clock, but the effects are not immediate. When light and dark patterns change abruptly – for example, following air travel across time zones – it may take several days for sleep and activity patterns to return to normal (“jet lag”); people working frequently changing shift patterns may face even greater problems.

Key terms Circadian rhythms: physical, mental and behavioural changes that follow a 24-hour cycle.
Melatonin: a hormone produced by the pineal gland in response to darkness; it coordinates the timing of circadian rhythms and sleep.

Epinephrine Secretion by the Adrenal Glands

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is a hormone secreted by the adrenal glands to prepare the body for vigorous activity, released when the body is under stress. Carried in the blood, it causes the heart’s pacemaker to increase heart rate, delivering more oxygen and glucose to tissues (especially muscles) and enabling increased rates of aerobic respiration and ATP production ready for intense muscle contraction. As a peptide hormone circulating in the blood, epinephrine also stimulates liver cells to convert stored glycogen to glucose, which passes into the blood and contributes to elevated blood glucose levels, supporting increased respiration rates in muscle and elsewhere.

Figure C3.1.11: The effects of epinephrine on the body The diagram shows the adrenal glands sitting atop the kidneys; under stress, the adrenal medulla releases epinephrine, triggering the “fight or flight” response, which includes an increase in blood glucose, an increase in heart rate and an increase in breathing rate. The adrenal cortex is shown as a separate part of the adrenal gland structure.
Key term Epinephrine: a hormone secreted by the adrenal medulla (and a neurotransmitter secreted by nerve endings of the sympathetic nervous system); it has many effects, including increasing heart rate and the breakdown of glycogen to glucose in muscle and liver.

Control of the Endocrine System by the Hypothalamus and Pituitary Gland

The hypothalamus is a part of the brain with a major endocrine function. It is exceptionally well supplied with blood vessels and is the site of special neurons. It has a key role in controlling many aspects of body function, acting as a connection between the endocrine and nervous systems – partly by constantly monitoring blood composition as it circulates through its capillary networks, and partly using data from sensory receptors in key organs, delivered via sensory neurons and the spinal cord. This enables the hypothalamus to regulate many activities concerned with maintaining a constant internal environment (homeostasis); for example, it secretes a hormone involved in water reabsorption in the kidneys, and regulates the pineal gland in relation to wake–sleep cycles.

Figure C3.1.12: The formation of the human brain A sagittal section of the brain (formed by enlargement and folding of the fore-, mid- and hindbrain regions) shows the cerebral hemispheres, the pineal gland (site of melatonin secretion), the cerebellum, the medulla, the hypothalamus and the pituitary body, with fluid-filled ventricles inside the brain, the whole structure protected within the cranium.

The pituitary gland lies below and is connected to the hypothalamus. Although part of the endocrine system and often called the “master” hormone gland, its endocrine activity is largely controlled by the hypothalamus, which releases hormones from special neurosecretory cells into a portal vein running between the two glands, as well as via nerve impulses through connecting neurons.

Figure C3.1.13: The hypothalamus and pituitary gland The diagram shows neurosecretory cells in the hypothalamus releasing hormones into a capillary network that feeds a portal blood vessel system carrying them down to a second capillary network in the pituitary gland, where hormone-secreting cells release hormones (via an artery and vein) into the bloodstream to travel to body cells.

Hormones secreted by the pituitary gland control growth, developmental changes in the body’s tissues and organs, reproduction, and homeostasis. One part of the pituitary gland does not synthesize hormones itself, but stores and releases two hormones produced in the hypothalamus: oxytocin and antidiuretic hormone (ADH).

Top tip! Make sure you understand the pituitary gland’s function and purpose – it is controlled by the hypothalamus rather than acting fully independently.
Key terms Hypothalamus: a structure at the base of the brain; a control centre for the autonomic nervous system and source of hormones for the pituitary gland.
Pituitary gland: the “master” endocrine gland, attached to the underside of the brain.

Feedback Control of Heart Rate

Nervous control of the heart is by reflex action. The heart receives impulses from the cardiovascular centre in the medulla of the hindbrain via two nerves: a sympathetic nerve, which speeds up the heart, and a branch of the vagus nerve (part of the parasympathetic nervous system), which slows it down – these nerves are antagonistic.

Nerves supplying the cardiovascular centre bring impulses from baroreceptors (stretch receptors) in the walls of the aorta, the carotid arteries and the right atrium, which detect changes in blood pressure. When blood pressure is high, heart rate is lowered via impulses through the vagus nerve; when it is low, heart rate is increased.

Figure C3.1.14: Control of heart rate The diagram shows baroreceptors in the carotid sinus/arteries and aortic arch sending impulses to a cardiovascular control centre in the medulla, containing an excitatory centre and an inhibitory centre. Impulses via the vagus nerve decrease heart rate; impulses via the sympathetic nerve release norepinephrine, increasing heart rate and the force of contraction; epinephrine in the blood also increases heart rate; and increased blood volume stretches cardiac muscle, triggering an increased volume of blood pumped out. The sinoatrial (SA) node (pacemaker) in the right atrium initiates the spread of electrical activity through the heart muscle via the atrioventricular (AV) node and Purkinje fibres, and the diagram notes that the forebrain (cerebral hemispheres, hypothalamus) may also send sensory impulses, for example in anticipation of physical effort or strong emotion.

Chemoreceptors in the carotid arteries, the aorta and the brain monitor blood carbon dioxide level and pH. Increased carbon dioxide or decreased pH causes chemoreceptors to signal the heart to beat faster; the resulting increase in blood flow moves carbon dioxide more quickly to the lungs, where it diffuses into the alveolar spaces for removal, lowering carbon dioxide levels and restoring blood pH.

Figure C3.1.15: Location of chemoreceptors and baroreceptors controlling heart rate The diagram shows the positions of the carotid sinus baroreceptor, the carotid body chemoreceptor, the aortic baroreceptor and the aortic chemoreceptor in relation to the carotid arteries and the aortic arch, together with the location of the SA node and AV node in the heart.

Using input from these receptors, the medulla coordinates responses, changing stroke volume and heart rate. If blood pressure is low or carbon dioxide concentration high, the cardiovascular control centre increases the rate of SA node firing by activating the sympathetic nervous system; if blood pressure is high or carbon dioxide concentration low, it reduces SA node firing by activating the parasympathetic nervous system. The sympathetic nerve releases norepinephrine at the SA node, increasing heart rate; the parasympathetic (vagus) nerve releases acetylcholine at the SA node, decreasing heart rate.

Key terms Medulla (medulla oblongata): structure at the base of the brain where the brain stem connects the brain to the spinal cord.
Sympathetic nervous system: part of the involuntary nervous system, antagonistic in effect to the parasympathetic nervous system.
Parasympathetic nervous system: part of the involuntary nervous system, antagonistic in effect to the sympathetic nervous system.
Baroreceptor: a sensory receptor responding to stretch in the walls of blood vessels, monitoring blood pressure.
Chemoreceptor: a receptor that monitors blood pH and the concentrations of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the blood.

Feedback Control of Ventilation Rate

The respiratory centre, in the medulla of the hindbrain, controls breathing rate through two adjacent, interacting groups of nerve cells – the inspiratory centre and the expiratory centre – which bring about ventilation movements by reflex action; breathing occurs automatically. The inspiratory centre sends impulses to increase the rate and depth of breathing; the expiratory centre sends impulses to inhibit the inspiratory centre and stimulate expiration; alternating impulses from the two centres cause rhythmic breathing.

Breathing rate is continually adjusted. Normal breathing rate is about 15 breaths per minute; with a typical tidal volume of 400 cm3, the ventilation rate is about 6 litres per minute. Messages from the cerebral hemispheres allow conscious override of breathing rate (for example, before shouting, singing or playing a wind instrument). Breathing rate is also adjusted unconsciously, for example during increased physical activity, when voluntary muscles use more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide. The main stimulus affecting breathing is blood carbon dioxide concentration, detected by chemoreceptors in the carotid arteries and aorta.

Figure C3.1.16: The control of ventilation rate The diagram shows chemoreceptors in the carotid bodies and aorta detecting an increase in blood carbon dioxide concentration and sending impulses via the carotid arteries and aorta to the respiratory centre in the medulla oblongata, which contains an inspiratory centre and an expiratory centre. Impulses travel from these centres via the phrenic nerve to the diaphragm and via nerves to the internal and external intercostal muscles, causing inspiration that inflates and stretches the lungs; the cerebral hemispheres and spinal cord are also shown as part of the pathway connecting the brain to the heart and respiratory muscles.

During strenuous exercise, rising blood carbon dioxide levels are detected by chemoreceptors that act as hydrogen ion detectors (since carbon dioxide is acidic in solution), sending impulses to the inspiratory centre in the brain stem in response to the blood pH change; this centre sends additional impulses to the intercostal muscles and diaphragm, increasing their contraction rate (lowered oxygen concentration is also detected, to a lesser extent). This produces a more rapid breathing rate, exchanging more carbon dioxide at the alveoli for exhalation. After exercise stops, falling carbon dioxide and rising oxygen levels are detected and ventilation rate is adjusted accordingly.

Key term Respiratory centre: region of the medulla of the brain concerned with the involuntary control of breathing.

Control of Peristalsis by the Central and Enteric Nervous Systems

The peripheral nervous system contains the autonomic nervous system (ANS) – “autonomic” meaning self-governing – which controls activities and structures inside the body that are mostly under unconscious control, regulating processes such as heart rate and ventilation rate more or less without our knowledge. The enteric nervous system (ENS), part of the ANS, is a network of sensory neurons, motor neurons and interneurons embedded in the wall of the gastrointestinal system.

Food travels through the alimentary canal (gut) – a long, muscular tube from mouth to anus, specialized for movement, digestion and absorption of food. The ENS extends from the lower third of the oesophagus to the rectum, and digestion is controlled through the integration of multiple signals from the ENS and CNS. Throughout the gut, waves of contraction and relaxation of the circular and longitudinal muscles of the wall propel food along – a process called peristalsis. Gut muscles are involuntary: swallowing food and the removal of faeces are under voluntary CNS control, but peristalsis between these two points is under involuntary control by the ENS, which coordinates the passage of material through the gut by passing neural signals between distinct gut regions.

Common mistake Do not confuse the terms “enteric” and “autonomic”. Both refer to the involuntary nervous system, but the enteric nervous system is specifically the part that serves the gastrointestinal system.
Key terms Autonomic nervous system (ANS): the involuntary nervous system.
Enteric nervous system (ENS): network of sensory neurons, motor neurons and interneurons embedded in the wall of the gastrointestinal system.
Peristalsis: waves of muscular contraction passing down the gut wall.

Higher Level Tropic Responses in Plants

Observations of Tropic Responses in Seedlings

Plant and animal sensitivity to environmental change differs. Most plants are sessile, so their responses are often less evident than those of animals, but plants are still highly sensitive to environmental disturbances. Lacking a nervous system or muscle tissue, fast movements by plants are rare, so most responses are changes in growth – for example, young stems growing towards light. A plant organ’s response to an external stimulus, determined by the direction of the stimulus, is called a tropic movement (tropism) or tropic response.

Qualitative data on tropic responses could be gathered as diagrams recording seedling observations; quantitative data could be gathered by measuring the angle of curvature as seedlings respond to a stimulus.

Accuracy describes how close to the true value a result is; precision describes the reproducibility of repeated measurements – how close they are to each other under the same conditions. Measurements can be precise without being accurate. Replicates (repeat data) improve the reliability of an investigation and help identify anomalies.

Figure C3.1.17: Accuracy versus precision Four target diagrams illustrate the difference: points scattered widely and off-centre (not precise, not accurate); points tightly clustered but off-centre (precise, not accurate); points scattered loosely around the centre (not precise, accurate); and points tightly clustered at the centre (precise, accurate).

Precision in tropism experiments can be improved by repeating exactly the same methodology each time; the range (difference between the lowest and highest measured values) or the standard deviation can be used to analyse precision – a small range or standard deviation indicates high precision. Accuracy is improved by controlling all other variables (for example, water volume, number of seeds, spacing between seeds, and temperature) so that only the independent variable differs between repeats.

Positive Phototropism

Phototropism is a plant’s response to the stimulus of light. Growth towards a stimulus is a “positive” response, so the directional growth response to lateral light in plant shoots is called positive phototropism. Growing towards light orientates leaves towards sunlight, maximizing the opportunity for photosynthesis.

Figure C3.1.18: Phototropism in plants A photograph/diagram of seedlings shows their shoots bending and growing towards a lateral light source, demonstrating positive phototropism.

Classic decapitation experiments reveal the mechanism (Figure C3.1.19): (a) an intact shoot bends towards light; a shoot with its tip covered in opaque black paper shows no bending; a shoot with its tip covered in a transparent cap still bends towards the light – showing that the tip itself must detect the light. (b) A shoot with its tip removed shows no bending; a shoot with the tip removed and replaced on an impermeable block (blocking diffusion) shows no bending; but a shoot with the tip removed and replaced on an agar block (which permits diffusion) does bend towards the light – showing that a diffusible chemical from the tip is responsible. (c) A tip cut off and placed on an agar block, which is then placed asymmetrically on one side of a decapitated shoot kept in even, all-round light, causes the shoot to bend even without any directional light – confirming that an unequal distribution of the chemical itself, not light directly, causes the differential growth.

Figure C3.1.19: Three experiments investigating the response of plants to light The figure shows the three sets of experimental set-ups and results described above, using agar blocks (which allow diffusion of the growth substance) and an impermeable block (which blocks diffusion) to identify both the source of the growth-promoting chemical and the mechanism by which it produces bending.

When lit equally from all directions, a plant grows straight upwards; under unidirectional light, it bends towards the source. This is explained by the tip producing a growth substance that is transported down the shoot: under even lighting, both sides of the shoot are equally stimulated and elongate equally, so the shoot grows straight up; under unilateral light, more of the growth substance is redistributed to the shaded side, so cells there elongate faster than cells on the illuminated side (which receive less growth substance and elongate more slowly), causing the shoot to bend towards the light.

Figure C3.1.20: Possible explanation of phototropism in a shoot The diagram shows the shoot tip producing a growth substance; under even light, both sides of the shoot are equally stimulated by the growth substance and elongate at the same rate, so the shoot grows straight up towards the light; under unilateral light, more growth substance reaches the shaded side, stimulating faster cell elongation there, while cells on the illuminated side receive less growth substance and elongate more slowly, so the shoot bends towards the light.
Top tip! Only phototropism (the response of plants to light) needs to be known for this course; other tropisms do not need to be studied.
Key terms Tropism: a growth response of plants in which the direction of growth is determined by the direction of the stimulus.
Phototropism: the response of plants to the stimulus of light.
Positive phototropism: the directional growth response in plant shoots towards lateral light sources.

Higher Level Phytohormones

The most important internal factors in plant sensitivity are phytohormones (plant growth regulators). There are five major types occurring naturally in plants: auxin, gibberellin (GA), cytokinin, ethylene and abscisic acid (ABA). Their effects differ considerably from those of animal hormones.

Produced in a region of plant structure, e.g. stem or root tips, in unspecialized cellsProduced in specific glands in specialized cells, e.g. islets of Langerhans in the pancreas

Phytohormones occur in very low concentrations, making their precise role difficult to determine. Unlike animal hormones, which are produced in discrete endocrine glands, phytohormones are produced by a variety of plant tissues and can reinforce or oppose one another’s effects. They can diffuse from cell to cell or be carried in the phloem or xylem, although not all are transported away from their source; depending on concentration, they may have very different effects depending on the tissue or developmental stage involved.

Higher Level The Role of Auxin

Auxin was discovered by Darwin while investigating the curvature of the sheath covering emergent plant shoots towards a light source. It is manufactured by cells undergoing repeated division, such as those at stem and root tips, so its concentration is highest there; it is then transported to the region of growth just behind the tip, where it causes cells to elongate. A coleoptile is the sheath covering the primary bud of a seedling, which goes on to form the stem and leaves.

Auxin promotes elongation of cells at the shoot apex and inhibits the growth and development of lateral buds immediately below the terminal growing point, a quality called apical dominance. However, a high concentration of auxin actually inhibits growth in length of the stem; gibberellins interact with auxin to enhance stem elongation, while cytokinins (from the root apex) pass up to the stem and promote lateral bud growth, acting antagonistically to auxin in this respect.

Top tip! Unlike many hormones, auxin is not a protein; it is the chemical indoleacetic acid (IAA) in its naturally occurring form.

Auxin explains the positively phototropic response of stems: the light stimulus causes auxin to be released from the growing tip of the coleoptile; light from the illuminated side causes auxin to accumulate on the darkened side of the stem, increasing its concentration there; and this increased auxin concentration causes cells on the darkened side to elongate more, bending the stem towards the light.

Figure C3.1.21: The role of auxin in phototropism The diagram shows unilateral light striking a coleoptile tip; auxin produced in the tip cells travels down through the stem tissue, but auxin on the illuminated side is laterally transported across to the dark side, so an increased concentration of auxin builds up on the shaded side, enhancing elongation growth there and producing the plant’s positive phototropic bending response.

Auxin Efflux Carriers

Auxin acts directly on the components of growing cells, including the cell wall, and on the gene-expression machinery of the nucleus. Auxin transport across cells is polar: entry into a cell is passive (by diffusion), while efflux out of the cell is active (ATP-driven) – auxin can diffuse freely into plant cells but not out of them. Auxin efflux pumps set up concentration gradients in plant tissues; if the efflux carriers on all the cells in a row are positioned on the same side of the cell membrane, auxin is actively transported from cell to cell through the tissue and becomes concentrated in a particular part of the plant.

Figure C3.1.22: Auxin – mechanisms of movement and control The diagram shows a plant cell (with its cytosol containing ribosomes, rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, a vacuole and nucleus), illustrating polar auxin transport: auxin enters passively through auxin-influx channels at the apical end of the cell (towards the stem or root apex) and is actively pumped out at the basal end by ATP-activated auxin efflux pumps in the plasma membrane, surrounded by a cell wall of cellulose microfibrils bound by other polysaccharides. The diagram also shows two downstream effects of auxin: auxin-mediated regulation of gene expression (promotion or inhibition of transcription via transcription factors) and auxin-triggered cell elongation via the acid growth hypothesis.

Promotion of Cell Growth by Auxin

Auxin promotes the secretion of hydrogen ions into the apoplast (the pathway through the non-living parts of a cell, such as the walls and intercellular spaces), acidifying the cell wall. Proteins called expansins, activated by H+ ions, alter the pattern of hydrogen bonding between wall polysaccharides, allowing these macromolecules to slip past one another, loosening cross-links between cellulose molecules and facilitating stretching of the cell wall and hence cell elongation. Concentration gradients of auxin cause the differences in growth rate needed for phototropism.

In summary: an increase in protons (H+) in the cell wall causes the wall to become acidic; low pH activates expansins, which trigger breakage of cross-links within cellulose; cell wall resistance to stretching decreases; and cell turgor causes stretching/elongation of the cell wall.

Key term Auxin: plant growth substance, indoleacetic acid, which generally stimulates cell elongation.

Higher Level Auxin–Cytokinin Interactions and Positive Feedback in Fruit Ripening

Interactions Between Auxin and Cytokinin

Cytokinin is produced in root tips and transported to shoots; shoot tips produce auxin, which is transported to roots. Interactions between these phytohormones help ensure root and shoot growth are integrated. Both hormones are essential regulators of many plant processes, with complex regulation ranging from molecule synthesis to transport and signalling; their activities are best described as antagonistic, each influencing the other.

Concept: Interdependence The activities of auxin and cytokinin are interdependent. Auxin positively affects cytokinin signalling, and cytokinin positively affects auxin production, thereby positively affecting auxin signalling.

Cytokinins and auxins also help regulate or adjust plant responses to biotic and abiotic environmental stresses, including in crop plants.

Positive Feedback in Fruit Ripening and Ethylene Production

Ethylene (IUPAC name: ethene) is a plant hormone that stimulates the changes occurring in fruit during ripening; ripening in turn stimulates increased ethylene production, which increases fruit ripening further, and so on. This process, in which one change leads to an increased change away from the original condition, is called positive feedback – it tends to amplify change away from equilibrium. This positive feedback mechanism ensures that fruit ripening is rapid and synchronized.

Figure C3.1.23: Positive feedback in fruit ripening A cyclical diagram shows ethylene being produced in one fruit; nearby fruit detect this ethylene gas and begin to ripen; the ripening fruit then release their own ethylene, so overall ethylene production increases; this increased ethylene causes more fruit to ripen, continuing the positive feedback cycle.
Key terms Cytokinin: phytohormone that influences plant growth, development and physiology, including cell division.
Ethylene: a hormone found in plants that causes fruit ripening.
