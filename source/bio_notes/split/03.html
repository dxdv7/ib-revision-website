Conditions on Early Earth and the Pre-biotic Formation of Carbon Compounds

Around 4.3 billion years ago, much of the Earth’s surface was molten rock, during a period known as the Hadean eon (named after Hades, the Greek god of the underworld, reflecting the hellish conditions of the time). As the Earth cooled, gases released by volcanic activity formed its atmosphere, which included ammonia (NH3), nitrogen, methane, water vapour and much higher levels of carbon dioxide than today.

Key definitionsGreenhouse gas: an atmospheric gas that absorbs and emits infrared radiation, causing heating at Earth’s surface. Greenhouse effect: the process by which greenhouse gases trap outgoing long-wave radiation from Earth, making the planet warmer than it would otherwise be.

Methane and carbon dioxide are both greenhouse gases: they absorb and re-emit infrared radiation from the Earth’s surface, raising surface temperature via the greenhouse effect. Today’s atmosphere has relatively high oxygen levels, essential for sustaining life, and a protective ozone (O3) layer in the stratosphere, formed naturally from the interaction of solar UV radiation with molecular oxygen (O2). The early Earth lacked free oxygen (and therefore ozone), so UV radiation reached the surface largely unfiltered: UV radiation 3.7 billion years ago is estimated to have been 100 times more intense than today. Since UV radiation damages and mutates DNA, today’s lower surface UV levels are part of what allows life to exist on the Earth’s surface now.

Conditions on the early Earth may have allowed a variety of carbon compounds to form spontaneously via chemical processes that do not occur today. If energy was added to the gases of Earth’s early atmosphere, building blocks of life — such as amino acids, peptides, ribose, nucleobases, fatty acids, nucleotides and short oligonucleotides — could have formed. This idea, that high-energy or UV radiation led to the formation of the first biological molecules, is called the “primordial soup” (or “pre-biotic soup”) hypothesis, proposed independently by Alexander Oparin (1924) and JBS Haldane (1929).

High-energy UV light is, however, also destructive to early biochemistry: when it destroys a molecule, the resulting small, highly reactive fragments can go on to recombine into new, larger, high-energy molecules (for example, pyruvic acid — central to key metabolic pathways today — forms larger molecules when dissolved in water and illuminated with UV light). Research shows that the bases found in nucleotides are extremely efficient at reducing the harmful effects of UV radiation, protecting the pentose sugar and phosphate parts of nucleic acids; under strong UV light, RNA was shown to be more likely to form chains than other candidate molecules. It is therefore possible that the intense UV levels on the primordial Earth acted as a selection pressure that actually drove the origin of life forward, rather than simply being an obstacle to it.

Cells as the Smallest Units of Self-sustaining Life

Cells are the fundamental, self-sustaining units of life, reproducing by cell division; all present-day cells are believed to have evolved from a single common ancestral cell that existed around 4 billion years ago. All cells are enclosed by a plasma membrane separating their interior from the environment, and all cells contain DNA, which stores genetic information and is used as a template for synthesizing RNA and proteins.

Simply identifying cellular life processes (respiration, nutrition, reproduction, etc.) does not, by itself, define life or explain how life first appeared. Three broader principles are often used to help define life: living organisms must be able to evolve through natural selection (requiring reproduction and a hereditary system showing genetic variation); life forms are contained and separate from, but in communication with, their surrounding environment (as a cell is); and life forms are chemical and physical “machines” that receive and respond to information. A systems-level view is also useful: living organisms can be seen as complex systems that process information about themselves and their environment, not simply as a large set of biochemical reactions.

Figure A2.1.3: Three factors essential for sustaining lifeA simple three-part diagram with “Genetic material”, “Membrane compartment” and “Metabolic processes” shown as three converging elements that together support “LIFE” at the centre — illustrating that all three features must be present together for a self-sustaining living system to exist.

NASA’s working definition of life is: “life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.” This definition would technically include viruses, but a major difficulty in defining life generally is that we only have one confirmed example — life on Earth — where every organism shares the same basic biochemistry, making it hard to know which properties of Earth life are essential to “life” in general versus properties unique to our particular biochemistry. (Mars, which may have sub-surface pockets of water, is considered the most promising location for life elsewhere in our solar system; some scientists have also speculated about “weird life” elsewhere in the universe based on a liquid other than water, such as ammonia.)

Viruses are regarded as non-living: they are non-cellular, lack organelles, and cannot carry out metabolism (e.g. releasing energy as ATP, or protein synthesis) independently — they can only replicate inside a living host cell, using the host’s own cellular machinery. Viruses do not exhibit homeostasis or directly respond to environmental stimuli in the way cells do, although they can respond to external pressures over evolutionary time (e.g. evading host immunity, adapting to antiviral drugs, or changing host range through mutation, especially in RNA viruses). Viruses do carry hereditary material (DNA or RNA) with genes coding for specific structures, such as capsid coat proteins, and (in retroviruses) reverse transcriptase and integrase enzymes. Viral genomes are prone to rapid mutation, which drives their evolution by natural selection; many viruses can also undergo a form of recombination known as genetic shift.

The Challenge of Explaining the Spontaneous Origin of Cells

Cells are highly complex structures that, today, can only be produced by the division of a pre-existing cell — yet the very first cells must have had non-biological origins. Explaining how the first cells arose from non-living matter is one of the most important unanswered questions in biology.

All cells share three common features1. A stable, partially permeable membrane surrounding the cell’s components. 2. Genetic material that can be passed on when new cells form, and which controls cell function and behaviour. 3. Metabolic processes that generate energy, enabling growth, self-maintenance and reproduction.

For living cells to have formed from non-living materials, several steps would have been required: the synthesis of simple organic molecules such as sugars and amino acids; the assembly of these molecules into polymers; the development of self-replicating molecules, such as nucleic acids; and the retention of these molecules within membranous sacs, so that an internal chemistry different from the surroundings could develop. These elements must then have spontaneously self-assembled into the first cells.

Nature of science – competing hypotheses: two broad approaches are used to study how life originated. The “bottom-up” approach (favoured by chemists, e.g. the Miller–Urey experiment) tries to reconstruct the chemical conditions of the primitive Earth in the laboratory. The “top-down” approach (favoured by biologists) studies modern organisms for surviving relics of ancient metabolic pathways and molecular processes. A key limitation of both approaches is that the exact conditions of the pre-biotic Earth cannot be exactly replicated, and the earliest protocells did not fossilize — meaning hypotheses about life’s origin can be tested only indirectly, by seeing whether laboratory experiments produce outcomes consistent with a given theory’s predictions, not by directly observing the historical event.

Three competing theories for the origin of lifeProtocell-first: a cell-like compartment with a basic metabolism, but lacking a full genetic system, arose spontaneously and was able to grow and divide into similar daughter cells; these protocells evolved adaptively until they eventually acquired a genetic system (likely RNA, later DNA). Predicted evidence: cell-like units that can grow and divide without genetic molecules should be able to be engineered, or seen to arise spontaneously, in the laboratory. Gene-first: a genetic molecule (likely RNA), or a small set of them, arose spontaneously with the capacity to replicate; these replicators evolved adaptively by natural selection, eventually forming variants able to assemble a membrane and begin metabolising. Predicted evidence: complex RNA molecules should be able to form spontaneously under the right conditions, and show collective self-replication and open-ended evolution. Metabolism-first: a self-sustaining system of simple chemical reactions, capable of using nutrients and energy (perhaps adsorbed onto a mineral surface), arose spontaneously and evolved adaptively, eventually evolving both cell-like compartments and genetic systems. Predicted evidence: chemical mixtures supplied with a flux of nutrients/energy in the laboratory should sometimes show autocatalysis, adaptive evolution, and the production of lipids and genetic polymers. Many scientists currently favour the metabolism-first theory, since rapid growth, replication and division (all essential to protocell evolution) require significant amounts of energy, which a working metabolism could supply.

The central hypothesis for the origin of the cell is that organic molecules self-assembled within a vesicle to form the first protocell, roughly 4 billion years ago.

Biologists studying the origin of life focus on four critical processes: the formation of organic molecules (such as amino acids and nucleic acids, especially RNA); the polymerization of these molecules; the formation of membranes; and the development of metabolic pathways for energy transfer. Scientists broadly agree that organic molecules formed first via chemical evolution, but disagree about the order in which metabolism, polymerized molecules and membranes then evolved. For example, the “iron–sulfur world” hypothesis proposes that the first steps of polymerization and protocell formation occurred in a hot, high-pressure iron–sulfur hydrothermal environment deep under the sea; this hypothesis gained support after sub-ocean hydrothermal vents were discovered, though testing it experimentally is hampered by the difficulty of simulating such high pressures and temperatures in the laboratory. Because this is a historical problem for which critical evidence may simply no longer exist, we may never know with certainty exactly how life originated on Earth.

Evidence for the Origin of Carbon Compounds

The molecules of living things are built mainly from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with some nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur (plus small amounts of other elements, including biologically important metal ions). Living cells today build these molecules using enzymes, but for life to have originated from non-living material, the very first step must have been the non-biological synthesis of simple organic molecules.

The Miller–Urey experiment (1953)Stanley Miller and Harold Urey investigated how simple organic molecules might have arisen from the chemicals present on the early, lifeless Earth. They passed strong electric sparks (simulating lightning) through mixtures of gases — methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water vapour, circulated with boiling water and a cooling condenser — for an extended period. They found that amino acids (known components of cell proteins), among other compounds, formed naturally within the apparatus, confirming that organic molecules can be synthesized outside cells, in the absence of oxygen. The experiment has since been repeated with different gas mixtures and other energy sources (particularly ultraviolet radiation); products obtained across these repeats have included amino acids, fatty acids, sugars such as glucose, nucleotide bases, and in some cases simple polymers of these molecules — showing that a wide range of the chemical building blocks of cells could plausibly have formed on the pre-biotic Earth.
Figure A2.1.4: Apparatus for simulating early chemical evolutionA sealed glass apparatus: a lower flask of boiling water produces steam that carries a mixture of atmospheric gases (e.g. CH4, NH3, H2) up into a larger reaction chamber, where a spark generated between two electrodes simulates lightning passing through the gas mixture. The gas mixture then passes through a cooling condenser, where it condenses back to liquid; a stopcock at the bottom of the system allows samples of the resulting liquid to be removed for chemical analysis.

An evaluation of the Miller–Urey experiment: at the time (1953), the early atmosphere was believed to be strongly reducing (hydrogen-rich) and rich in methane, matching the experiment’s gas mixture; current evidence instead suggests methane was in low abundance for most of this period, with carbon present mainly as carbon dioxide (i.e. a more oxygen-rich mixture than Miller and Urey assumed). The experiment also used electrical discharges rather than UV light to supply energy, although subsequent work shows organic molecules such as amino acids and bases can also form when carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water are exposed to ionizing radiation or UV light, as well as electrical discharges. Despite this success, efforts to reproduce pre-biotic chemistry did not succeed in generating nucleotides directly until relatively recently, when nucleotides were synthesized via a route using four simple organic molecules (cyanamide, cyanoacetylene, glycolaldehyde and glyceraldehyde) that readily form under plausible pre-biotic conditions.

Assembling polymers without cells or enzymesForming polymers in the absence of cells and enzymes would have required biologically important monomers — monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids — to become concentrated in “pockets” where further reactions between them were possible. Clays have been shown to promote phosphodiester bond formation by binding and concentrating nucleotides, and microscopic clay layers may have played a similar role in forming the first polyribonucleotides — potentially in water near volcanic lava flows, or at submarine volcanic vents, where the environment is hot, under high pressure, and rich in vented sulfur compounds (e.g. H2S) and other chemicals.
PolymerizationThe process by which relatively small molecules, called monomers, chemically combine to produce a larger molecule called a polymer.

Spontaneous Formation of Vesicles

All cells are bounded by membranes that separate genetic material and metabolic reactants from the external environment; the earliest cells (protocells) were likely bounded by basic membranes of this kind.

Key definitionsAmphipathic: having two different affinities within one molecule — a polar end attracted to water, and a non-polar end repelled by it. Microsphere: a microscopic hollow sphere made from a lipid bilayer. Vesicle: a membrane-bound sac. Protocell: a pre-cellular or cell-like entity, e.g. a lipid droplet with a few molecules inside.

Fatty acids are likely to have formed the first protocell membranes because they are amphipathic. If a small number of lipid molecules are placed in water, they form a monolayer on the water’s surface; with more lipid present, bilayers form. Lengths of these bilayers are likely to have curled up into microspheres or very small vesicles — meaning vesicles form spontaneously (without any external cause or stimulus) by the coalescence of fatty acids into spherical bilayers. Simple microspheres enclosing a portion of the surrounding pre-biotic “soup” of polymers and monomers may have been the forerunners of true cells, since they would begin to develop an internal chemical environment distinct from their surroundings.

Figure A2.1.5: Steps in the formation of microspheresA three-step sequence. Step 1: individual lipid molecules are shown scattered near a mineral surface in water. Step 2: the lipid molecules assemble into a bilayer sheet, associated with the mineral surface. Step 3: a length of this bilayer curls up and seals itself into a closed, hollow, spherical microsphere, separating an internal aqueous space from the external water.

Modern cell membranes are made from a modified type of lipid, the phospholipids, which have hydrophobic tails and hydrophilic heads. Fatty acids are chemically simpler than phospholipids, so are thought to have formed the first membranes, with membranes evolving from primitive to more modern via a pathway roughly like this: protocells first formed using stable fatty acids (which may have accumulated to significant levels on the early Earth); condensation of fatty acids with glycerol then produced triglycerides, a more stabilizing membrane component; and phosphorylation (addition of a phosphate group) of a triglyceride then formed the simplest phospholipid.

RNA as a Presumed First Genetic Material

For life to evolve from a mixture of polymers and monomers, two special capabilities needed to emerge: a “self-replication” system, and the ability to catalyse chemical change. In modern cells these roles are filled separately, by DNA (genetic code) and by enzymes (typically large, globular proteins) — but neither DNA nor functioning enzymes have been produced in any experiment repeating the Miller–Urey approach to pre-biotic synthesis.

RNA world hypothesisThe hypothesis that the earliest life forms (protocells) may have used RNA alone — without DNA or protein enzymes — for both storing genetic information and catalysing chemical reactions.

The idea arose partly as a by-product of a genetic engineering experiment investigating enzymes that join short lengths of RNA: it was discovered that RNA itself, not just protein, can function as an enzyme. Because mRNA is single-stranded (simpler than double-stranded DNA), hydrogen bonds can form between nucleotides within the same RNA chain, causing it to fold into a unique three-dimensional shape determined by its base sequence — a fold that can confer enzymatic activity, a property needed by the earliest forms of life.

RibozymeAn RNA molecule capable of acting as an enzyme, using its folded three-dimensional shape as an active site. In 1989, Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discovering catalytic RNA.
Figure A2.1.6: The central dogmaA flow diagram showing DNA at one end, an arrow labelled “Transcription (RNA synthesis)” leading to RNA (built from nucleotides), and a further arrow labelled “Translation (protein synthesis)” leading from RNA to a protein, shown as a chain of amino acids (e.g. Met–Arg–Val–Thr–Leu–Phe–Leu) corresponding to the coded sequence — illustrating the one-way flow of genetic information from DNA to RNA to protein.
Central dogmaThe idea that the transfer of genetic information from the DNA of a chromosome, to mRNA, to protein (amino acid sequence), is irreversible — i.e. information flows DNA → RNA → protein, and not in reverse. (Retroviruses such as HIV are a notable exception: their RNA genome is reverse-transcribed into DNA inside a host cell, which then integrates into the host chromosome.)

Because nucleic acids are needed to make proteins, yet proteins are needed to make nucleic acids in modern cells, it is difficult to see how this interdependent system could have evolved directly by natural selection — the RNA world hypothesis proposes a way out of this “chicken and egg” problem: an RNA-only stage existed in protocells before modern DNA- and protein-based cells evolved, with RNA acting as both the genetic material and the catalyst, and DNA only later taking over as the dedicated genetic material once proteins became the dominant catalysts and structural components of cells.

Relics of a possible RNA world persist in modern cells: RNA primers (short RNA sequences) are used to initiate eukaryotic DNA replication, and ribosomal RNA appears to directly catalyse peptide bond formation within the ribosome — arguably the strongest evidence for an RNA world, since it suggests that protein synthesis itself could first have developed in a pre-protein world, using an RNA-based enzyme (an early ribosome) to build proteins from an RNA template. Further supporting evidence comes from the pentose sugars: ribose (found in RNA) is readily formed from methanal (H2CO), one of the principal products of the Miller–Urey experiment, whereas in modern cells deoxyribose (found in DNA) is produced from ribose only via a reaction catalysed by a protein-based enzyme — implying RNA’s chemistry is the more “primitive” of the two. DNA’s other distinguishing features — its stable double helix, and its use of thymine rather than uracil — further increase its chemical stability and ease of enzymatic repair compared with RNA.

Key labelled stages along the timeline include: formation of Earth (4.5 Bya); pre-biotic chemistry beginning (around 4.2 Bya); a “pre-RNA world” stage (4.2 to 4.0 Bya); an “RNA world” stage (around 4.0 Bya); the appearance of the first DNA/protein-based life (around 3.8 Bya); and ongoing diversification of life from around 3.6 billion years ago to the present. Although the RNA world hypothesis does not fully explain how life originated, it represents an important paradigm shift in the scientific study of life’s origins, and has helped guide subsequent experimental research.

Evidence for a Last Universal Common Ancestor

Fossil remains and other evidence (anatomical and biochemical similarities) reveal how species are related — for instance, ape skeletons show that gibbons, gorillas, chimpanzees, orang-utans and humans share a common ancestor and have since adapted to different environments and lifestyles by natural selection.

Common ancestorThe most recent species from which two or more different species have evolved.

DNA comparisons can be used the same way: species with very similar genes are closely related, while species with very different DNA are only distantly related. All life on Earth is related, and ultimately shares a single common ancestor believed to have existed around 4 billion years ago — the evolutionary link between the abiotic (non-living) and biotic (living) phases of Earth’s history. This organism is known as LUCA, the “Last Universal Common Ancestor”: if all life on Earth is drawn as a tree, LUCA sits at its base, with an unbroken line of descent connecting every living thing back to it. All organisms share the same basic biochemistry, the same DNA bases, and much of the same amino acid “alphabet”; the shared genetic code (the rules for translating RNA into protein) used by nearly all organisms is strong evidence for this common ancestry, since multiple different genetic codes would be expected if separate lineages had independently evolved protein synthesis from scratch.

Figure A2.1.8: The phylogenetic tree of lifeA branching tree diagram with three major domains radiating from a single point at the base, labelled LUCA (the Last Universal Common Ancestor): Bacteria (eubacteria), Archaea (archaeabacteria), and Eukarya (eukaryotes, itself branching further into groups such as animals, fungi, plants, slime moulds, ciliates and flagellates). Several of the shortest branches nearest the base of the tree are marked with asterisks and identified as hyperthermophilic species, suggesting the universal ancestor of all living things was itself a hyperthermophile, possibly having “assembled” at deep ocean hydrothermal vents where volcanic gases are discharged into water under high temperature and pressure. These evolutionary relationships were established by comparing ribosomal RNA (rRNA) base sequences across species from each group.
Common mistakeLUCA should not be described as a “protocell”. A protocell refers to a much simpler, pre-cellular or cell-like entity (e.g. a lipid droplet containing a few molecules) — something far less elaborate than LUCA, which is thought to have resembled an ancestral prokaryote.

Researchers analysing DNA from modern bacteria (eubacteria) and archaea (a separate group of extremophile prokaryotes that live in environments such as deep ocean vents, geysers, salt pans, acidic conditions and polar regions) searched for genes shared between them, as an indicator of genes inherited from a common ancestor. Searching DNA databanks covering roughly six million genes across 2000 sequenced modern microbial genomes, researchers identified 355 gene families that were widespread across bacteria and archaea — likely inherited from LUCA. Care was taken to exclude genes that could have spread between species by lateral (horizontal) transfer, such as bacterial conjugation (direct transfer of genetic material between two bacteria), rather than genuinely by vertical descent from a common ancestor. Genomic analysis of these shared gene families was then used to predict LUCA’s likely structure and function, since DNA codes for proteins and proteins determine an organism’s structure and function. It is likely that other early life forms existed alongside LUCA but went extinct through competition for shared resources, leaving LUCA as the sole ancestor of all life alive today; descendants of LUCA are also likely to have out-competed other species over subsequent evolutionary history, further shaping today’s tree of life.

Estimating the Dates of the First Living Cells and LUCA

LUCA may have existed around 4 billion years ago, roughly 560 million years after Earth itself formed. Several independent approaches let scientists estimate such dates for early life.

Fossils provide direct evidence of the history of life, though fossilization is an extremely rare, chance event: predators, scavengers and bacterial decomposition normally break down dead organisms before fossilization can occur, and of the relatively few fossils that do form, most remain buried, undiscovered, overlooked, or are accidentally destroyed. Nevertheless, many fossils have been found and new ones continue to be discovered. If a fossil, or the surrounding rock, can be accurately dated using radiometric dating (measuring the amounts of naturally occurring radioactive substances, e.g. the ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12, or of potassium-40 to argon-40), it provides good evidence about the history of life; older rocks are expected to contain more ancient groups of organisms, provided conditions allowed fossilization and the fossils were not subsequently destroyed (e.g. by the heat and pressure of metamorphic rock formation, which destroys fossils).

The molecular clockGenomic analysis offers a further technique for dating ancient organisms. DNA accumulates mutations over time; by estimating the average rate at which such mutations occur, and extrapolating this rate backward through time, scientists can estimate when two lineages last shared a common ancestor. The amino acid composition of proteins can be used similarly, since changes to the genetic code alter protein composition and structure. If such biochemical changes occur at a roughly constant rate, they can be used as a “molecular clock”: by comparing specific genes shared between early and present-day life, and applying the estimated rate of biochemical change, scientists can estimate both when those genes last shared a common ancestor and, ultimately, a date for the earliest life on Earth.

Evidence for the Evolution of LUCA Near Hydrothermal Vents

Because LUCA probably evolved in the environment of deep-sea hydrothermal vents, rocks formed from ancient seafloor hydrothermal vent precipitates are the most likely places to find fossils of the earliest life. Scientists have found fossilized evidence of bacteria in ancient seafloor hydrothermal vent precipitates from the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt in Quebec, Canada: thin, hundredth-of-a-millimetre rock slices examined under a light microscope revealed structures resembling those produced by modern hydrothermal-vent bacteria. These fossils have been dated to at least 3.77 billion years old (possibly up to 4.28 billion years old), making them the oldest fossil remains found to date and strong evidence for some of the very first cells to exist on Earth.

Figure A2.1.10: Haematite tubes found in NuvvuagittuqA microscope image showing extremely fine, thread-like tubular structures (roughly half the width of a human hair) made of haematite, the mineral form of iron(iii) oxide, embedded in ancient rock at least 3.77 billion years old — identified as the fossilized remains of ancient filamentous, iron-oxidizing bacteria similar to microbes found at modern hydrothermal vents.

The Nuvvuagittuq rocks also contain carbonate and carbonaceous material, further supporting evidence of past oxidation and biological activity. Iron-oxidizing microbial communities — which extract iron atoms from water and remove electrons from them for metabolic energy transfer — are still found today around widespread ocean-floor hydrothermal vents, and it is likely the ancient Nuvvuagittuq bacteria had a broadly similar biochemistry, even though the deep-sea hydrothermal vent environment as a whole offers many possible routes to energy generation via chemosynthetic pathways.

Key definitionsChemosynthesis: the oxidation of inorganic molecules to release energy, which is then used to synthesize glucose. Extremophile: an organism that lives in conditions of extreme temperature, acidity, alkalinity, salinity, pressure or chemical concentration. Autotrophic: able to synthesize glucose from simple inorganic substances using an external energy source.

Because protein synthesis is the most energy-intensive activity of a cell (using around 75% of a typical cell’s ATP), the fact that LUCA’s conserved genes are dominated by proteins associated with ribosomes shows that LUCA actively released and used energy — though these particular conserved genes alone do not reveal LUCA’s specific metabolism, so other lines of evidence are needed. The 355 shared gene/protein families identified from genomic analysis (see previous section) are not evenly distributed across all organisms, which lets scientists infer LUCA’s likely physiology. For example, LUCA is inferred to have carried a gene for “reverse gyrase”, an enzyme that helps stabilize DNA structure and is today found only in extremophiles living in high-temperature environments, including hydrothermal vents. Taken together, the properties of LUCA’s inferred protein set indicate that LUCA was: anaerobic (survived without oxygen); CO2-fixing (converted carbon dioxide into glucose); H2-dependent (used molecular hydrogen, rather than sunlight, as an energy source); N2-fixing (converted nitrogen gas into ammonia, for subsequent amino acid synthesis); and thermophilic (able to survive at very high temperatures, up to 122°C). Modern microbes with broadly similar physiology include Clostridia (anaerobic bacteria found in soil and in the intestines of humans and other animals) and methanogens (anaerobic bacteria that produce methane as a waste product).

Figure A2.1.11: A hydrothermal ventA photograph of an active deep-sea hydrothermal vent structure on the ocean floor, with dark, mineral-laden, superheated water plumes rising from the vent opening into the surrounding cold, high-pressure seawater — an environment rich in hydrogen, carbon dioxide, iron and dissolved sulfides and metals, of the kind in which LUCA is believed to have evolved.

Overall, the genes inferred to have been passed down from LUCA describe an autotrophic extremophile that likely lived at hydrothermal vents, where seawater and magma meet on the ocean floor, in a geochemically active environment rich in hydrogen, carbon dioxide and iron — among the same toxic plumes of sulfides and metals where similar prokaryotic organisms still live today. Combined with the fossil evidence from Nuvvuagittuq, many researchers now consider deep-sea hydrothermal vents the most likely site for the origin of life on Earth.
