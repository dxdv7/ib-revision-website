D4.3.1 Anthropogenic Causes of Climate Change: The Greenhouse Effect

Key definitions Climate is the long-term average of the weather, taken over 20–30 years — it is not the same as year-to-year variations in weather. Climate change is a long-term change in global or regional climate patterns, caused by natural or human factors, such as increased levels of atmospheric carbon dioxide produced by the use of fossil fuels. Climate change since the mid- to late-twentieth century is attributed primarily to increased atmospheric greenhouse gases from the burning of fossil fuels and land-use change (deforestation and cattle farming together account for almost 40% of historic change).

The greenhouse effect

Radiant energy reaching Earth from the Sun is mostly visible light, with some ultraviolet and short-wavelength infrared radiation, together termed shortwave radiation. This shortwave radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms the sea and land directly, although some is reflected by clouds before reaching the surface. As the surface warms, the Earth radiates infrared radiation at longer wavelengths (longwave radiation) back towards space. Much of this longwave radiation does not escape — it is absorbed by clouds and by certain atmospheric gases, warming the atmosphere. This is the greenhouse effect: the atmosphere behaves somewhat like the glass of a greenhouse, trapping heat.

Greenhouse effect and greenhouse gas The greenhouse effect is the heating caused by the atmosphere on Earth’s surface because certain atmospheric gases absorb and emit infrared radiation. A greenhouse gas is a gas that contributes to the greenhouse effect by absorbing infrared radiation. Without the natural greenhouse effect, surface temperatures on Earth would be far too cold to support life.
Figure D4.3.1: The greenhouse effect The diagram shows incoming solar radiation (ultraviolet, visible and infrared) passing through the atmosphere from the Sun. Some of this shortwave radiation is reflected straight back to space by clouds and the atmosphere; the rest reaches Earth’s surface and warms it. The warmed surface emits infrared radiation. Some of this outgoing infrared radiation passes straight through the atmosphere into space, but some is absorbed by greenhouse gases in the atmosphere and re-emitted in all directions (including back down towards the surface). This re-emitted radiation heats the atmosphere further and raises Earth’s surface temperature, labelled on the diagram as the atmosphere being heated and the surface being warmed twice over — once directly by sunlight and once by the re-emitted infrared radiation from the atmosphere.

Carbon dioxide and water vapour are the most significant greenhouse gases. Methane and nitrogen oxides contribute less overall. The contribution of each gas depends both on its intrinsic properties (how effectively it absorbs infrared radiation) and its abundance. Methane is considerably more powerful, molecule for molecule, than carbon dioxide as a greenhouse gas, but it occurs at much lower concentrations and is short-lived in the atmosphere — in sunlight, methane is steadily oxidised to carbon dioxide and water.

Evidence from ice cores

Long-term records of past greenhouse gas levels and associated climate change come from ice cores drilled in the Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets. The ice formed from layer upon layer of frozen, compacted snow accumulated over thousands of years, trapping bubbles of the surrounding atmosphere as the layers built up. Data from the Vostok (East Antarctic) ice sheet give a record of how carbon dioxide and methane concentrations have varied over 400 000 years of Earth history. Variations in oxygen isotope ratios from the same cores indicate how temperature has changed over the same period.

All three series rise and fall together in repeating cycles. Nature of science — patterns and trends: data from Antarctic ice cores show a positive correlation between global temperature and atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration over hundreds of thousands of years, but correlation alone does not prove that carbon dioxide causes the temperature change. The large temperature swings of glacial cycles are actually initiated by predictable, cyclical changes in Earth’s orbit around the Sun (Croll–Milankovitch cycles); the resulting changes in ice cover and greenhouse gases then amplify these climate changes. However, current CO2 concentrations are well above the natural variability of the last 800 000 years, and other lines of evidence show that today’s changes are driven by human activity.

In June 2022, atmospheric CO2 varied between 413 and 422 ppm. In principle, atmospheric CO2 should be balanced by fixation during photosynthesis against release through respiration, combustion and microbial decay (the carbon cycle). In practice, photosynthesis withdraws almost — but not quite — as much CO2 during daylight as is released by all other processes (including human activity) day and night, so atmospheric CO2 is steadily rising, as tracked by the Keeling curve, a daily measure of global atmospheric CO2 concentration.

The discussion of climate change has become politicised, but this does not place it beyond the scope of science: political claims about the world must still be consistent with how the world actually is, which is best established through established scientific methods. Scientific findings being politically inconvenient does not make them untrue, and political-level disagreement does not imply genuine disagreement within the scientific community.

Enhanced Greenhouse Effect and Global Warming

Global warming Global warming is an increase in the global average temperature of the Earth’s surface and atmosphere. The build-up of greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane due to human activity has intensified the natural greenhouse effect, producing an enhanced greenhouse effect that is causing global warming.
Common mistake: greenhouse effect vs enhanced greenhouse effect The greenhouse effect and global warming are often confused, but they are not the same thing. The greenhouse effect is a natural process that traps some outgoing longwave (infrared) radiation and makes life on Earth possible. The enhanced greenhouse effect is the process by which human activities have increased the concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, increasing the trapping of infrared radiation and leading to a rise in global surface temperatures — i.e. global warming.

Carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide levels have risen from about 315 ppm in 1950 to approximately 418 ppm in 2022, and could reach 600 ppm by 2050 depending on government policy. The rise is due to the burning of fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas), deforestation and agricultural emissions. Since the industrial revolution (roughly the past 200 years), there has been a sharp, accelerating rise in atmospheric CO2, mainly from burning coal and oil laid down as fossil fuels during the Carboniferous period — humans are releasing carbon that had been locked away for about 350 million years, an entirely new development in geological history.

Deforestation of tropical rainforest has a double impact: it directly increases atmospheric CO2 (from burning trees) and removes the trees that would otherwise convert CO2 into glucose while releasing oxygen. Deforestation accounts for about 12–15% of global CO2 emissions.

Methane

Methane (CH4) is the second-largest contributor to global warming and is increasing at a rate of between 0.5 and 2% per year. Cattle release between 65 and 85 million tonnes of methane per year; natural wetlands and paddy fields are another major source, with paddy fields emitting up to 150 million tonnes annually. As global warming continues, permafrost bogs may thaw and release large additional quantities of methane.

D4.3.2 Positive Feedback Cycles in Global Warming

Common mistake: what “positive” means in feedback “Positive” usually implies something good, but in feedback terminology it does not mean the feedback loop benefits the environment. A positive feedback loop increases change in a system, moving it further away from a steady-state equilibrium — in this case, amplifying warming.

Methane feedback (permafrost)

Around one-quarter of Earth’s land surface is affected by continuous or sporadic permafrost, including tundra, polar and mountain regions — permafrost covers about 23 million km2 globally, mostly in the northern hemisphere. It formed during past glacial periods and has persisted through warmer interglacials, including the Holocene (the last 10 000 years). Permafrost is now shrinking rapidly: temperatures in Newtok, Alaska have risen by 4°C since the 1960s (up to 10°C in winter).

Mechanism: the permafrost–methane positive feedback loop
  1. As the atmosphere warms, more permafrost is expected to thaw.
  2. This releases large amounts of methane — the volume of methane stored in permafrost is estimated by some researchers to be more than double the amount of carbon currently in the atmosphere.
  3. Released methane, being a potent greenhouse gas, causes the atmosphere to warm even more quickly.
  4. This further warming causes even more permafrost to melt, releasing yet more methane — the cycle reinforces itself.
Climate-related drying of wetlands also increases methane and CO2 emissions from ancient peatlands and raises the risk of peat fires, which are very difficult to extinguish. A 1°C rise in atmospheric temperature (with stable humidity) can increase decomposition rate by up to 10%; faster peat decomposition releases more methane and CO2, which raises global temperature further — another positive feedback loop.

Terrestrial and marine carbon feedback

Figure D4.3.4: System diagram of combined terrestrial and marine positive feedback loops Starting from “more CO2 from human activity acting as a greenhouse gas”, the diagram traces a global temperature rise that (a) warms the tundra, causing it to emit CO2 and CH4, which feed back into the atmosphere as more greenhouse gases; and (b) warms the oceans, increasing evaporation of water from the ocean surface (more energy available for evaporation), putting more water vapour — itself a greenhouse gas — back into the atmosphere. Warmer oceans are also less able to dissolve CO2, so dissolved CO2 is released from the warmer water back into the atmosphere. Each of these outputs (more CO2, water vapour, clouds and CH4 in the atmosphere) feeds back to reinforce the initial global temperature rise, forming closed positive feedback loops.

Elements of this feedback model include: increasing atmospheric water vapour (itself a greenhouse gas); terrestrial permafrost thawing and releasing methane; and a reduction in seawater’s capacity to absorb surplus CO2, since warmer water is less effective at absorbing CO2 than colder water and may begin releasing rather than absorbing CO2 from deep waters. However, since ocean life also absorbs more CO2 as levels rise, the overall balance is not entirely clear.

CO2 dissolved at the ocean surface can be transferred to the deep ocean where cold, dense surface water sinks (down-welling), carrying carbon to great depths where it may remain for centuries. Carbon is also transported to the ocean floor within the bodies of organisms: phytoplankton absorb CO2 through photosynthesis at the ocean surface, are consumed by other organisms, and organic carbon eventually reaches the deep ocean as dead organisms sink. Increasing deep-ocean temperature releases CO2 back into the water through decreased CO2 solubility and increased decomposition of organic matter on the ocean floor.

Albedo feedback

Albedo Albedo is the fraction of solar radiation reflected by a surface or object, often expressed as a percentage. Ice and snow have high albedo (they reflect most incoming solar radiation).
Figure D4.3.5: Arctic ice melting and decreased planetary albedo A photograph/illustration of melting Arctic sea ice is used to introduce the idea that as polar ice melts, the bright, reflective ice surface is replaced by darker open ocean or land, which absorbs rather than reflects incoming solar radiation, reducing planetary albedo.
Figure D4.3.6: Positive feedback loop in global warming (albedo) A cyclic flow diagram: increased temperature in polar areas → increased melting of polar ice caps → less ice → lower albedo (reflectivity) → increased amount of solar energy absorbed at Earth’s surface → increase in temperature → (loops back to) increased temperature in polar areas, continuing the cycle.

Increased temperature through global warming melts polar ice caps, glaciers and sea ice, decreasing Earth’s albedo. As a result the Earth absorbs more solar energy, raising the temperature further and melting still more ice.

Droughts and forest fires

Increased global temperatures raise the likelihood of drought, which in turn increases the chance of wildfire. Combustion of vegetation releases CO2 into the atmosphere, adding to global warming — another positive feedback loop. High temperatures, drought and strong winds during the summer of 2022 made wildfires in France, Portugal, Spain and Greece especially difficult to fight; these fires released large amounts of CO2 and destroyed important carbon sinks.

D4.3.3 Boreal Forests: From Carbon Sink to Carbon Source as a Tipping Point

Boreal (coniferous) forests, also called taiga, store more carbon overall than tropical rainforests because they cover a much greater area, including much of northern Russia and North America. In boreal forests and wetlands, frozen soils hold large reserves of carbon.

The Siberia Integrated Regional Study (SIRS) investigates environmental change in Siberia arising from global change and its potential impact on Earth system dynamics. Documented and projected consequences of warming in Siberia include: temperatures already rising, particularly in winter in Eastern Siberia (0.5°C per decade); an increase in the number of frost-free days and growing-season length (roughly one day per year); decreased winter snowfall leading to increased drought and reduced primary production in taiga; and a northward shift of the permafrost boundary with dramatic changes in land cover.

SIRS identifies three regional challenges important to the global carbon cycle:

  • Permafrost degradation, especially shifts in its boundary, could become a significant source of carbon and methane to the atmosphere (while also threatening human infrastructure); climate-related drying alters methane emissions from ancient peatlands and increases the risk of unquenchable peat fires.
  • Temperature and precipitation changes that increase forest browning and the frequency/intensity of forest fires, significantly altering the region’s carbon cycle.
  • Northward shifts in ecosystem boundaries: taiga currently covers about two-thirds of Siberia but is projected to shrink to around 40% of its present area.

The southern boundary of Siberian taiga is expected to be most affected by forest fires; conditions favourable to fire have become unusually frequent over the past 20 years there. Fire and permafrost thaw are considered the principal mechanisms that will drive vegetation change across the Siberian landscape and increase the release of carbon (as CO2) into the atmosphere — illustrating a tipping point at which boreal forest switches from being a net carbon sink (accumulating carbon) to a net carbon source (losing carbon).

D4.3.4 Melting of Landfast Ice and Sea Ice as Examples of Polar Habitat Change

Landfast ice Landfast ice is sea ice that is “fastened” (attached) to the coastline, to the sea floor along shoals, or to grounded icebergs.

The Arctic is a highly sensitive region: ice cover varies naturally with the season, but since 1979 the September Arctic sea-ice extent has declined by 12.6% per decade (compared with its 1981–2010 average), and at this rate there may be no summer sea ice by 2060. Greenland ice is similarly in decline. Loss of sea-ice habitat harms animals that rely on it for feeding and breeding: walruses (Odobenus rosmarus) cannot swim continuously, so they use sea ice as a resting place between dives to the sea floor where they feed on clams and mussels; sea-ice floes are especially important for walrus calves.

Figure D4.3.8: Adult female Atlantic walrus and juvenile resting on pack ice, Svalbard Archipelago, Arctic Norway A photograph shows an adult female walrus and a juvenile hauled out and resting together on a floating ice floe, illustrating the dependence of walruses on sea ice as a resting platform between foraging dives.

In Antarctica the picture is more complex: NASA data indicate that Antarctica loses approximately 150 billion tonnes of ice mass per year, and major sections of the Antarctic ice shelf have broken off, yet at times the interior ice has become cooler and thicker as circular winds around the landmass prevent warmer air from reaching the interior. Warmer seas may erode the ice from underneath, but the IPCC predicts Antarctica’s contribution to sea-level rise will be relatively small.

As temperatures rise, areas of landfast ice break away from their attachment points (“breakout”) earlier in the year. Early breakout of landfast ice in the Antarctic threatens animals that live and breed on it, such as the emperor penguin (Aptenodytes forsteri), the largest penguin species, which uses landfast ice such as that in the Weddell Sea to live and breed; earlier melting destroys their breeding grounds and could severely affect the species’ long-term survival.

D4.3.5 Changes in Ocean Currents Altering Nutrient Upwelling

Upwelling Upwelling is the process by which deep, cold water rises toward the ocean surface, occurring for example in the north Pacific Ocean and the Indian Ocean.

The global oceanic conveyor belt transfers energy around, and links, the world’s major oceans. Water flowing from the Equator is generally warm, while water flowing from high latitudes is cold. At the poles, cold, salty water sinks and is replaced by warm surface water from the tropics. As polar temperatures rise, increased ice melt reduces ocean salinity, which slows the great ocean currents. Ocean currents convey heat energy from warmer to colder regions by convection; for example, as the Gulf Stream (which has kept European temperatures relatively warm compared with Canada) slows, more heat is retained in the Gulf of Mexico, and since hurricanes draw their energy from warm water, they are becoming more frequent and more severe.

Figure D4.3.9: The Earth’s ocean currents A world map shows the pattern of major surface and deep ocean currents forming the global “conveyor belt”, including warm surface currents flowing away from the Equator and cold deep currents returning from the poles, with regions of upwelling (for example in the north Pacific and Indian Ocean) marked where deep, nutrient-rich water rises to the surface.

Upwelling brings nutrients from the deep ocean to the surface, boosting phytoplankton abundance and, in turn, zooplankton abundance. Some marine animals, such as whales, migrate to these highly productive areas. If climate change disrupts the oceanic conveyor belt and reduces primary production in upwelling zones, there will be knock-on effects throughout marine food chains. Warmer surface water can prevent nutrient upwelling altogether, decreasing ocean primary production and energy flow through marine food chains.

D4.3.6 Poleward and Upslope Range Shifts of Temperate Species

Upslope range shift Upslope range shift is the process by which montane (mountain-dwelling) species move higher up mountains in response to recent temperature increases.

With rising mean global temperatures, effects are expected on the distribution of habitats, communities and ecosystems. Many terrestrial organisms are shifting their distribution in latitude or elevation in response to changing climate. Species have zones of tolerance, so as the areas they inhabit warm, they must shift to colder conditions — higher in elevation or further poleward — to remain within their niche requirements.

Montane bird species in Papua New Guinea have been recorded moving higher up the mountains they inhabit. Data collected from two mountains — Mt Karimui and Karkar Island — in 2012–13 were compared with historical data from the 1960s.

The distribution of biomes depends on climate, so changes in rainfall patterns together with changes in land and sea surface temperatures will shift biome distributions. A study sampling 92 tree species across 43 000 plots in eastern North America found that latitudinal shifts correlated with twentieth-century temperature change: more than half of tree species showed ranges shrinking at both their northern and southern boundaries, slightly more species showed ranges shifting only northward, and only a few species showed range increases at both boundaries. A separate 30-year study of 16 temperate tree species in Quebec, Canada, also found predominantly northward range shifts, with saplings shifting range faster (0.34 km yr−1) than full-grown trees (0.13 km yr−1).

D4.3.7 Threats to Coral Reefs as an Example of Potential Ecosystem Collapse

Corals are colonies of small animals embedded in a calcium carbonate shell that they secrete, forming coral reefs in warm, shallow, sunlit water. Microscopic photosynthetic algae called zooxanthellae live sheltered within the cells of tropical corals in a mutualistic relationship. Coral reefs are the most diverse ecosystems known and, although they cover less than 0.1% of the ocean surface, they are home to about 25% of all marine species.

Figure D4.3.11: Coral reefs — a crisis situation A paired photograph contrasts a healthy, colourful coral reef (left) with a bleached, white coral reef (right) resulting from climate change, illustrating the visual effect of coral bleaching.
Mechanism: coral bleaching Increased release of CO2, changing ocean currents, and oceans absorbing more heat as greenhouse gases trap more energy all cause ocean temperatures to rise. Corals have a very narrow range of tolerance: under environmental stress such as high water temperature, corals expel the zooxanthellae living in their tissue, causing the coral to turn white — this is bleaching. Repeated bleaching events, or bleaching combined with other damage, kill the coral. Mass bleaching has occurred on the Great Barrier Reef in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017 and 2020.

The coral skeleton is built from calcium carbonate through a process called calcification. Increased atmospheric CO2 causes ocean acidification, which suppresses calcification in corals. Once corals are lost, other species lose their niches and the reef loses its biodiversity — coral reefs dying around the world represents the collapse of entire reef ecosystems, and the effects of thermal stress are likely to worsen under future climate scenarios. Climate change affects not just individual species but also the interactions between them, such as the coral–zooxanthellae mutualism.

D4.3.8 Carbon Sequestration: Afforestation, Forest Regeneration and Peatland Restoration

Carbon sequestration and afforestation Carbon sequestration is the capture and storage of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere by physical or biological processes such as photosynthesis. Afforestation is the establishment of forests in an area where there was no previous tree cover.

Afforestation

Afforestation involves planting trees in deforested areas or areas that have never been forested. New trees act as carbon sinks and so help mitigate climate change. The UN-REDD Programme, launched in 2008 (the United Nations Collaborative Programme on Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation in Developing Countries), gives developing countries financial incentives to conserve rainforests by placing a monetary value on forest conservation — an example of successful global governance. UN-REDD emphasises conservation, sustainable forest management and increasing forest carbon stocks; by 2020 total funding had reached almost US$325 million, contributed by Norway, the EU, Denmark, Spain, Japan, Luxembourg and Switzerland.

China has the largest afforested area in the world, gaining an average of 19 370 km2 of forest per year, and plans to plant 36 000 km2 of new forest per year between 2020 and 2025 — more than the total area of Belgium. As well as storing carbon and helping offset emissions, China’s tree-planting campaign aims to halt desert spread (for example along its northern border adjacent to the Gobi Desert), stabilise water resources, and supply local people with sustainable timber and other forest products.

Figure D4.3.12: Farmers drilling tree holes for afforestation, northern China A photograph shows farmers using machines to drill holes for planting trees during afforestation activities in northern China during the Spring Festival period.

Agroforestry and forest regeneration

Agroforestry combines agriculture with forestry, allowing farmers to continue cropping while also using trees for animal food, fuel and building timber. Trees protect, shade and fertilise the soil, decreasing rates of decomposition and respiration and increasing photosynthesis; such practices improve carbon sequestration in agricultural soils and above-ground biomass, protecting existing soil carbon by slowing organic matter decomposition and reducing erosion.

Forest regeneration is the process by which new trees and forest species become established after forest trees have been harvested or have died from fire, insects or disease. Like afforestation, forest regeneration increases carbon storage in trees and so reduces atmospheric CO2 through photosynthesis.

Figure D4.3.13: Hard tree ferns growing in burnt forest after the 2019–20 Australian bushfires A photograph shows tree ferns (Blechnum sp.) regenerating amid charred, burnt trees, illustrating natural forest regeneration following a major fire disturbance.

Restoration of peat-forming wetlands

In the acidic, anaerobic conditions of waterlogged soils, dead organic matter is not fully decomposed and instead accumulates as peat; peatlands form the largest terrestrial carbon store. From 1640 onwards, large wetland areas such as the East Anglian Fens in the UK were drained for farming, degrading the peat: no longer waterlogged, it shrank, decomposed and eroded by wind, increasing the flux of CO2 to the atmosphere. Restoring degraded peatlands increases the land carbon store and decreases the atmospheric carbon store. Degraded and drained peatlands emit more than 2 gigatonnes of carbon (GtC) annually; rewetting peatlands can help restore carbon levels in soils that have already been degraded.

Nature of science — global impact of science: there is active scientific debate over whether plantations of non-native tree species or rewilding with native species offer the best approach to carbon sequestration. Peat forms naturally in waterlogged soils of temperate and boreal zones, and can also form very rapidly in some tropical ecosystems.

Common mistake: afforestation vs reforestation Do not confuse the two terms. Afforestation is the addition of forest to land that never previously had it. Reforestation is the restoration of forest to land where forest once existed but was removed.

Higher Level D4.3.9 Phenology: Research into the Timing of Biological Events

Phenology and photoperiod Phenology is research into the timing of seasonal or cyclical biological events, such as flowering, budburst and bud set, bird migration and nesting. Photoperiod is the period of time each day during which an organism receives light, i.e. day length.

The timing of biological events that depend on seasonal cycles can be affected by variables such as photoperiod and temperature patterns. Flowering must coincide with the availability of pollinators, so its timing is carefully controlled: day length determines whether flowering occurs, with some species requiring long days (short nights) to flower and others requiring shorter days (longer nights).

Many animals leave their spring/summer range to escape colder winter months. For example, the black-throated loon (Gavia arctica) migrates south to the Black Sea, the Mediterranean, north-east Atlantic coasts and the eastern/western Pacific, returning to its breeding grounds in early April once sea ice has melted there. For breeding to succeed, temperatures must be warm enough to guarantee sufficient food (such as insects) for the young. Research shows that increases in average global temperature are affecting the timing of such biological events.

Higher Level D4.3.10 Disruption to the Synchrony of Phenological Events by Climate Change

Cerastium arcticum (Arctic mouse-ear), a shrub native to Greenland, northern Canada, Svalbard and western Siberia, now shows spring growth advancing (occurring earlier in the year) due to climate change; a warmer climate lengthens the growing season, giving plants more time to establish, reproduce and spread.

Figure D4.3.14: Caribou (Rangifer tarandus) migrating in Alaska, USA A photograph shows a herd of caribou migrating across open tundra terrain in Alaska.

Migratory animals such as the caribou (Rangifer tarandus, also known as reindeer) track the growth of spring vegetation during a synchronised spring migration. A combination of snow cover and temperature strongly influences autumn migratory movements, and earlier availability of ground vegetation is expected to advance caribou migration timing.

The timing of reproduction affects the number of offspring that survive and reproduce (fitness). Insectivorous birds must time breeding to coincide with peak abundance of their insect food. A study on great tits (Parus major), published in 2006, measured insect biomass over a 20-year period and found the annual peak in caterpillar biomass correlated with temperatures recorded between 8 March and 17 May, while egg-laying dates correlated with temperatures over an earlier period (16 March–20 April). Historically, the caterpillar food peak and the peak chick demand were synchronised, but due to climate change this synchrony has been disrupted in recent warm decades, with consequences for the number and mass of fledglings. Models predicting caterpillar and great tit phenology from 2005–2100 (using an IPCC warming model) predict that great tits will start breeding earlier, broadly tracking the earlier food peak.

Higher Level D4.3.11 Increases in the Number of Insect Life Cycles per Year Due to Climate Change

Life cycle A life cycle is the series of changes in the life of an organism, including reproduction.

Beetles (order Coleoptera) are the most diverse and abundant insect group on Earth, inhabiting every ecosystem except the oceans. While most beetles play vital ecological roles, some are pests: bark beetles (more than 2000 species) feed on tree plantations, damaging and killing trees. The great spruce bark beetle (Dendroctonus micans) is a non-native pest of spruce and pine in Europe, while the Eurasian spruce bark beetle (Ips typographus) is one of Europe’s most destructive forest insects, having killed more than 50 million m3 of Norway spruce (Picea abies) since the 1940s.

Insects follow one of two life-cycle types: (1) eggs develop directly into nymphs, then through intermediate stages (instars) into adults — as in locusts and grasshoppers; or (2) eggs produce larvae (e.g. caterpillars), which form a pupa in which internal changes (metamorphosis) replace larval structures with adult structures. Beetles, including bark beetles, follow this second (complete metamorphosis) life cycle.

Figure D4.3.16: Life cycle of a bark beetle A circular diagram shows the four life-cycle stages of a bark beetle in sequence: eggs → larva → pupa → adult bark beetle, illustrating complete metamorphosis.

Bark beetle females bore into tree bark and tunnel to the phloem, laying eggs beneath the bark. Emerging larvae feed on phloem sap and tissue; after pupation, new adults bore new holes through the bark to emerge. Because phloem transports sucrose and other organic compounds around the plant, larval feeding damage weakens the tree and can lead to its death.

Figure D4.3.17: Trees killed by spruce bark beetle, Colorado, USA A photograph shows a large area of forest with numerous dead, reddish-brown standing spruce trees killed by bark beetle infestation.

Insects are sensitive to temperature, have short life cycles and are highly mobile, so climate change can be expected to affect their rate of development and geographical distribution. Warming summer and winter temperatures strongly affect beetle populations and increase the chance of outbreaks across the USA and Europe, and can also drive range expansion into new areas as temperatures warm. Research in Norway shows that higher temperatures favour bark beetles and cause a transition from one to two generations per year at northern latitudes. With two generations per year there are two attack periods on spruce (one in spring, one in July/August), increasing the chance that the critical beetle density needed to kill a tree is reached; a second generation can also kill trees more easily, since fewer beetles are needed to overwhelm already weakened, less-resistant trees.

Higher Level D4.3.12 Evolution as a Consequence of Climate Change

Changes in the environment can alter the selective pressures acting on a species, potentially leading, over time, to the evolution of new species. Evidence suggests that tawny owls (Strix aluco, also called brown owls) in southern Finland are evolving in response to milder winters over recent decades. Milder winters mean less snow cover, so paler-feathered owls are now at a selective disadvantage — they are more visible to their prey and so catch less food. Darker brown tawny owls, which formerly made up only about 30% of the Finnish population, now make up around 50%. In years with more severe winter weather, brown owls suffer higher mortality, possibly because they are more visible to predators against thick snow cover. This is considered the first evidence of a population evolving in direct response to climate change.

This mirrors the earlier, classic example of industrial melanism in the peppered moth, where industrial pollution shifted the balance of colour morphs. In both cases the colour variants remain within the same species, but given sufficient time, natural selection acting on such variation could eventually lead to the evolution of new species.
