Enzymes as Catalysts

Many chemical reactions do not occur spontaneously, or occur only very slowly. In a laboratory or industrial process, reactions can be made to happen by applying high temperatures, high pressures, extremes of pH, high concentrations of reactants, or inorganic catalysts. Without these drastic conditions, very little product would form. By contrast, in cells and organisms, many reactions occur simultaneously, at very low concentrations, at normal temperatures, and under the mild, almost neutral, aqueous conditions found in cells. It is the presence of enzymes that enables these reactions to occur at relatively high rates, in an orderly manner, yielding the products an organism requires, when they are needed.

Key terms Catalyst: a substance that speeds up the rate of a chemical reaction. Catalysts are effective in small amounts and remain unchanged at the end of the reaction.
Enzyme: mainly proteins (some are RNA) that function as biological catalysts.

In general, catalysts are effective in small amounts and remain unchanged at the end of the reaction. Many enzymes are always present in cells, but some are produced only under particular conditions, at certain developmental stages, or when a particular substrate molecule is present. By making some enzymes and not others, cells can control which chemical reactions happen in the cytoplasm.

Figure C1.1.1: Can a reaction occur without an enzyme? Diagram showing sucrose and water molecules colliding at random. Most random collisions happen at the wrong angle or the wrong speed for reaction, so under normal conditions the uncatalysed hydrolysis of sucrose to glucose and fructose is an insignificant event. However, in the presence of just one molecule of the enzyme sucrase (invertase), approximately 3.0 × 104 molecules of sucrose are hydrolysed every minute, illustrating the enormous rate enhancement enzymes provide.
Common mistake It is incorrect to use the term “amount” when discussing variables in practical procedures. Use more precise terms, such as “concentration” or “volume”.

Role of Enzymes in Metabolism

There are many thousands of chemical reactions taking place within cells and organisms. Metabolism is the name given to these reactions collectively. Metabolic reactions can only occur at a significant rate in the presence of specific enzymes — if an enzyme is not present, the reaction it catalyses only occurs very slowly. The molecules involved in metabolism are collectively called metabolites. Many metabolites are made within organisms, but others are imported from the environment, such as food substances, water, carbon dioxide and oxygen.

Many different enzymes are required by living organisms, and control over metabolism can be exerted through them. Each enzyme is specific to one substrate (or a small group of closely related substrates), so metabolic processes can be closely controlled. Enzymes are usually classified according to the type of reaction they catalyse, and named according to their substrates (the reactants enzymes act on).

Metabolism consists of chains (linear sequences) and cycles of enzyme-catalysed reactions, as seen in respiration, photosynthesis, and protein synthesis, among many other pathways. All these reactions can be classified into one of two types, according to whether they build up or break down organic molecules — these are anabolism and catabolism, respectively.

Anabolic and Catabolic Reactions

Key terms Anabolism: the synthesis of complex molecules from simpler molecules, including the formation of macromolecules from monomers by condensation reactions.
Catabolism: the breakdown of complex molecules into simpler molecules, including the hydrolysis of macromolecules into monomers.

In anabolic reactions, larger molecules are built up from smaller molecules by condensation reactions. Examples include the synthesis of proteins from amino acids, the synthesis of polysaccharides from simple sugars (e.g. glycogen formation), and photosynthesis.

Catabolism is the breakdown of larger molecules into simpler ones, involving hydrolysis reactions. Examples include the hydrolysis of macromolecules into monomers during digestion, and the oxidation of substrates in respiration.

metabolism = anabolism + catabolism

Enzyme Structure: Globular Proteins and the Active Site

Enzymes are typically large, globular protein molecules in which the tertiary structure gives the molecule a generally rounded shape. In an enzyme-catalysed reaction, the starting substance is called the substrate, and what it is converted to is the product.

Key terms Substrate: the starting substance (reactant) in a reaction catalysed by an enzyme; the molecule the enzyme reacts with.
Product: what the substrate is converted to in a reaction catalysed by an enzyme.
Active site: the region of an enzyme molecule where the substrate molecule binds and catalysis occurs.
Enzyme–substrate (ES) complex: a temporary structure formed when a substrate binds to the active site of an enzyme.

An enzyme works by binding to the substrate molecule(s) at a specially formed pocket in the enzyme called the active site. As the enzyme and substrate form an enzyme–substrate (ES) complex, the substrate is raised to a high-energy transition state. This complex has the briefest of existences before the substrate is converted into product(s) by the catalytic properties of the active site. The product(s) are then released, together with the unchanged enzyme, which is available for reuse.

E + S → ES → ES‡ → Pr + E
Figure C1.1.4: The enzyme–substrate complex and the active site A sequence diagram showing an enzyme (a large protein molecule, drawn as a “lock”) with a substrate molecule (the “key”) approaching its active site — the pocket where the substrate is held and reaction occurs. The substrate binds to form the enzyme–substrate complex, in which the substrate molecule is raised to a transition state. The reaction proceeds and product molecules are released, leaving the enzyme unchanged and available for reuse.

The active site is a pocket or crevice in the protein where the substrate binds and catalysis occurs. It may be formed from only a few amino acids, but interactions between amino acids across the whole three-dimensional structure of the enzyme ensure that the active site has the properties needed for catalysis: binding to the substrate, holding it in place during the reaction, and lowering the energy of the transition state. Most substrate molecules are small relative to the enzyme; even for large substrates such as polysaccharides, typically only one bond is in contact with the active site at a time. The active site occupies a relatively small part of the total volume of the enzyme.

Common mistake When describing enzyme-catalysed reactions, do not omit key details: always mention the active site, the substrate, and the enzyme–substrate complex.

Induced-Fit Binding

Induced-fit The binding of the substrate to the enzyme causes a change in the shape of the enzyme and the substrate, resulting in the proper alignment of the catalytic groups on its surface, which enables catalysis to take place.

Enzymes are highly specific in their action, distinguishing them from most inorganic catalysts. At the active site, the arrangement of a few amino acids in the enzyme matches certain groupings on the substrate molecule, enabling the enzyme–substrate complex to form. As it forms, a slight change of shape is induced in both enzyme and substrate (the induced-fit model); it is this shape change that helps raise the substrate to the transition state in which it can react. Meanwhile, other amino acids of the active site bring about the specific catalytic mechanism, perhaps breaking particular bonds in the substrate and forming new ones. Different enzymes have different arrangements of amino acids in their active sites, so each enzyme catalyses either a single reaction or a group of closely related reactions.

Figure C1.1.5: Enzyme specificity and the active site A polypeptide of 49 amino acid residues forming a simple enzyme, folded so that certain residues (e.g. residues 3–4–5 and 24–25–26) line the active site (a pocket/crevice) and match particular groupings on the substrate molecule, allowing the ES complex to form. Other residues (e.g. 20 and 41) are catalytic/“reactive” residues that induce the breaking of bonds and formation of products, while the remaining amino acids interact to maintain the overall three-dimensional structure of the enzyme. The diagram illustrates that specificity depends both on which amino acids allow the substrate to “fit” and on which amino acids bring about the particular chemical changes.
Figure C1.1.6: Induced-fit catalysis by hexokinase A computer-generated molecular model of the enzyme hexokinase, which catalyses glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP. Two polypeptide chains make up the enzyme, forming an active site between them. As the substrate (glucose) fits into the active site, a slight change in the shape of both the active site and the substrate occurs, and the reaction then takes place.

In the original lock-and-key model, the active site is the complementary shape of the substrate, fitting it precisely — the enzyme is the “lock” and the substrate the “key”. In the induced-fit model, the active site undergoes a conformational change to improve binding with the substrate, comparable to a hand entering a glove, where both hand and glove change shape for an optimal fit (the “hand-in-glove” model). Most enzymes appear to follow the induced-fit model.

Top tip! Both substrate and enzyme change shape when binding occurs.

Molecular Motion, Collisions and Enzyme Immobilization

Movement is needed for a substrate molecule and an active site to come together. The greater the kinetic energy of enzyme and substrate, the greater the chance of collisions between molecules and the formation of enzyme–substrate complexes.

Immobilized enzyme An enzyme attached to an inert, insoluble material, enabling recovery, reuse and improved enzyme stability.

Sometimes large substrate molecules are immobilized, and enzymes can also be immobilized, for example by being embedded in membranes. Immobilized enzymes are often more stable than free enzymes in solution, and may provide a better environment for enzyme activity. Enzymes immobilized on or in a membrane (EIM) are widely used in food processing, pharmaceuticals and wastewater treatment, since this facilitates recycling of the enzyme and can enhance its stability and viability.

In industrial processes, enzymes may be used as cell-free preparations added to a reaction mixture, or immobilized, with reactants passed over them. Immobilization involves attaching enzymes to insoluble materials that provide support — for example, entrapment between inert fibres, or covalent bonding to a matrix. In both cases, the enzyme molecules are prevented from being leached (washed) away.

Figure C1.1.7: Enzyme immobilization techniques Five methods of attaching an enzyme (with its active site free to bind substrate) to a support are shown: a) entrapment of the enzyme between inert fibres; b) adsorption of the enzyme onto a membrane surface; c) covalent bonding of the enzyme directly to a matrix; d) cross-linking of enzyme molecules to one another; e) affinity binding, in which the enzyme is attached via a specific binding interaction to the support.

The advantages of using an immobilized enzyme in industrial processes are: it permits reuse of the enzyme preparation; the product is enzyme-free; and the enzyme may be much more stable and long-lasting, due to protection by the inert matrix.

Active Site Structure, Enzyme–Substrate Specificity and Denaturation

Denaturation occurs when the weak intramolecular interactions within a globular protein, formed between different amino acid residues, break. This changes the three-dimensional shape of the active site. Peptide bonds are not hydrolysed or broken during denaturation — only the weaker bonds and interactions that maintain tertiary structure are disrupted. As a result, the structure of the active site changes, so the enzyme and substrate can no longer bind together.

Figure C1.1.8: The effect of pH and temperature on enzymes A diagram comparing an active enzyme, whose active site has a shape complementary to its substrate, with a denatured enzyme. When temperature or pH shifts away from the optimum, the three-dimensional shape of the active site is disrupted, so the substrate no longer fits — the shapes are no longer complementary.

Effects of Temperature, pH and Substrate Concentration on Enzyme Activity

Generalized sketches of the relationship between an enzyme’s rate of reaction and a variable such as temperature, pH or substrate concentration are examples of models in biology; these models can be evaluated using results from real enzyme experiments. In each case, the precise shape of the graph is specific to the reaction being investigated.

Top tip! Accurate, quantitative measurements in enzyme experiments require replicates to ensure reliability. When plotting data, ensure the independent variable is on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis, that data points fill the available area of the graph, and that an appropriate linear scale is chosen.

Effect of temperature

The effect of temperature on enzyme activity can be investigated using the hydrolysis of starch by the enzyme amylase. When starch is hydrolysed by amylase, the product is maltose, a disaccharide. Starch gives a blue–black colour with iodine solution, while maltose gives a yellow or orange-brown colour, indicating no starch is present.

Investigating the effect of temperature on amylase activity Samples of amylase solution and starch solution are first brought to the temperature of a water bath separately (pre-incubation), then mixed. At half-minute intervals, a drop of the reaction mixture is removed on a glass rod and tested with iodine solution on a white spotting tile (the rod is rinsed between tests to avoid contaminating the mixture with iodine). Initially a strong blue–black colour appears, confirming starch is present; as maltose accumulates, an orange colour predominates. The end-point is reached when all starch colour has disappeared. Using fresh reaction mixture each time, the investigation is repeated at a series of temperatures (e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C), and the time taken for complete hydrolysis at each temperature is used to calculate a rate.
Figure C1.1.9: The effects of temperature on hydrolysis of starch by amylase Apparatus diagram: a water bath holds a boiling tube containing 5 cm³ pre-incubated amylase solution mixed with 5 cm³ pre-incubated starch solution, alongside a stop clock. Samples are removed on a glass rod at intervals and tested against iodine solution on a white spotting tile, with the colour changing from blue–black (starch present) to yellow–brown (all carbohydrate now present as maltose). The experiment is repeated at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C. A control tube of 5 cm³ starch solution plus 5 cm³ distilled water (replacing the enzyme) is included and tested for starch at each temperature.

A characteristic curve results (Figure C1.1.10), though the optimum temperature varies between reactions and enzymes. As temperature increases, molecules gain kinetic energy, move more rapidly, and are more likely to collide and react — increasing the reaction rate. The temperature coefficient, Q10, measures the rate of change of a reaction for a 10 °C rise in temperature; many enzymes have a Q10 of about 2, meaning the rate approximately doubles for every 10 °C rise. However, in enzyme-catalysed reactions the effect of temperature is more complex, because proteins are denatured by heat, and the rate of denaturation increases above the optimum temperature. As temperature rises further, the amount of active enzyme progressively decreases, slowing the rate. These two opposing effects together produce an apparent optimum temperature for an enzyme. Human enzymes typically have an optimum at or near normal body temperature.

Figure C1.1.10: Temperature and the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction A graph of rate of enzyme-catalysed reaction (y-axis, arbitrary units) against temperature at which the rate was measured (x-axis, 10–60 °C), with other variables (enzyme and substrate concentration) held constant. Up to about 40 °C the rate increases, with an approximate doubling for each 10 °C rise. Beyond the optimum, the rate falls steeply as the enzyme is progressively denatured and active sites are destroyed, so that the substrate molecules no longer fit the active site.

Not all enzymes share the same optimum temperature: bacteria living in hot thermal springs have enzymes with optima between 80 and 100 °C or higher, whereas seaweeds of northern seas and tundra plants have enzymes with optima closer to 0 °C. This feature of enzymes is often exploited commercially and industrially.

Effect of pH

Each enzyme has a limited range of pH at which it functions efficiently, often at or near neutrality (pH 7.0). This occurs because the structure of a protein, and therefore the shape of its active site, is maintained by various bonds and weak intermolecular forces. A change in pH away from the optimum alters these bonding patterns, progressively changing the three-dimensional shape of the molecule; the active site may quickly become inactive. Unlike the effects of temperature, the effects of pH on the active site are normally reversible (provided the change is not too extreme) — as pH reverts to the optimum, the active site may reform.

The accompanying graph plots rate of enzyme-catalysed reaction against pH for three human enzymes: pepsin, with an optimum pH around 2 (active in the acidic stomach); trypsin, with an optimum pH around 7.8–8 (active in the neutral/alkaline duodenum and small intestine); and most enzymes in human cells, with an optimum close to pH 7.

Effect of Substrate Concentration and Measurements in Enzyme-Catalysed Reactions

The effect of substrate concentration on reaction rate can be shown using the enzyme catalase, which catalyses the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide, a toxic, powerful oxidizing by-product of metabolism:

2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2

Catalase occurs very widely in living things, protecting the delicate biochemical machinery of cells by inactivating hydrogen peroxide as it forms, before damage can occur.

When measuring the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction, the amount of substrate that has disappeared, or the amount of product accumulated, is measured over time. With catalase it is convenient to measure the volume of oxygen produced at fixed intervals (e.g. every 30 seconds).

Figure C1.1.12: Measuring the rate of reaction using catalase Apparatus diagram: catalase solution is mixed with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution (10 volume) in a flask, and the oxygen produced is collected by downward displacement of water in an inverted measuring cylinder, timed with a stop clock; the test tube is tipped to mix enzyme and substrate at time zero. An accompanying graph plots volume of oxygen produced (cm³) against time (s): the rate is greatest at the start (the initial rate), and the curve becomes progressively less steep. The initial rate (volume of oxygen produced per second, cm³ s−1) is calculated from the slope of the tangent to the curve at the start of the reaction.

Over time the initial rate of reaction is not maintained but falls off quite sharply — typical of enzyme reactions studied outside their normal location in the cell. This fall-off is most commonly because the substrate concentration in the mixture has fallen. Consequently, it is the initial rate of reaction — the slope of the tangent to the curve at the start — that is measured.

To investigate the effect of substrate concentration, the experiment is repeated at different substrate concentrations while other variables (temperature, enzyme concentration) are kept constant, and the initial rate is plotted against substrate concentration. The resulting curve shows two phases: at lower concentrations, rate increases in direct proportion to substrate concentration; at higher concentrations, the rate becomes constant and shows no further increase.

This is explained by the formation of a short-lived enzyme–substrate complex. At low substrate concentration, all substrate molecules can find an active site without delay — there is effectively an excess of enzyme, so the rate is set by how much substrate is available, and increasing substrate increases the rate. At higher substrate concentrations, there is more substrate than enzyme; substrate molecules must, in effect, “queue up” for access to an active site, since all active sites are already engaged in catalysis. Adding more substrate simply increases the number of molecules waiting, so the rate no longer increases.

Figure C1.1.13: The effect of substrate concentration A graph of initial rate of reaction (y-axis) against substrate concentration (x-axis), low to high. At low substrate concentration, with few substrate molecules and many active sites free, increasing substrate concentration increases the rate. At intermediate concentration, more active sites become engaged, approaching the maximum rate. At high (excess) substrate concentration, all active sites are already engaged in catalysis, so a further increase in substrate concentration does not change the rate — the curve plateaus.

For example, the initial rate in Figure C1.1.12 is found by extrapolating a straight line from the first part of the curve and dividing the volume of oxygen produced by the corresponding time. Effect of Enzymes on Activation Energy

An enzyme (E) is a large molecule that works by reacting with a substrate (S) to form a short-lived enzyme–substrate complex (ES) at the active site. This complex exists at a local energy minimum and is fairly stable. The transition state (TS) is the point of maximum energy, existing at the top of the energy profile only transiently. Almost instantly, the product (P) is formed and the enzyme is released unchanged, ready to take part in another reaction.

Activation energy Energy required by a substrate molecule before it can undergo a chemical change.

Energy is released when substrate becomes product, but a small amount of energy is needed initially to break or weaken bonds in the substrate to form the transition state — this is the activation energy, a small but significant energy barrier that must be overcome before the reaction can proceed. Enzymes work by lowering the amount of energy required to activate the reacting molecules, providing a new, alternative reaction pathway. Virtually all enzyme-catalysed reactions still have an activation energy barrier — it is simply much smaller; the enzyme lowers the “hump” of activation energy, it does not remove it.

Figure C1.1.14: Activation energy An energy profile diagram (energy on the y-axis, reaction progress on the x-axis) for the reaction sucrose + water → glucose + fructose (catalysed by sucrase). Without a catalyst, a large amount of activation energy must be put in to reach the transition state before the reactant can fall to the lower energy level of the products, releasing the free energy change. With the enzyme, the activation energy “hump” is much lower, so the reaction proceeds far more readily to the same lower-energy products. An accompanying “boulder on a hillside” analogy shows a boulder (substrate) held back by a small hump (activation energy) on a slope; the boulder can be pushed over the hump, or the hump can be dug away (the enzyme’s effect, lowering the activation energy), allowing the boulder to roll down and shatter into products at a lower energy level.
Top tip! Molecules collide more frequently at higher temperatures; however, the main reason the reaction speeds up is that more molecules have enough energy to get over the activation energy barrier.

Higher Level Intracellular and Extracellular Enzyme-Catalysed Reactions

Key terms Extracellular enzyme: an enzyme secreted by a cell that functions outside the cell.
Intracellular enzyme: an enzyme that functions within the cell in which it was produced.

Some enzymes are exported from cells, packaged into vesicles and secreted by exocytosis to work externally — these are extracellular enzymes. Chemical digestion in the gut, using digestive enzymes, is an example of an extracellular reaction. Most enzymes, however, remain and work within cells, as intracellular enzymes: many are found inside organelles, in organelle membranes, in the fluid medium around organelles, in the plasma membrane, and in the cytoplasm (e.g. the enzymes of glycolysis). Two of the main metabolic processes of respiration, glycolysis and the Krebs cycle, are intracellular enzyme-catalysed reactions.

Higher Level Generation of Heat Energy by the Reactions of Metabolism

When glucose is oxidized to carbon dioxide and water in aerobic cell respiration, energy is transferred from a store of chemical potential energy to heat energy (the kinetic energy of molecular motion) — energy that is no longer in store but “on the move”, or active energy. Only part of the stored energy in a molecule is available to do work; this is known as free energy. Reactions that release free energy are exergonic reactions, such as the oxidation of glucose.

Heat generation is inevitable in metabolic reactions, because metabolic reactions are not 100% efficient in energy transfer. Mammals, birds and some other animals depend on this heat production to maintain a constant body temperature, and are described as endotherms (“warm-blooded”); animals that cannot control their metabolism in this way are ectotherms (“cold-blooded”). Reactions in which energy is absorbed, so that the system has more energy at the end than at the start, are endergonic reactions — the synthesis of a protein from amino acids is an example.

Figure C1.1.15: Exergonic and endergonic reactions Two reaction profiles side by side. In the exergonic (“downhill”) reaction, reactants C and D have more stored energy than the products, so free energy is given off as work done, heat, or both, as C + D are converted to CD. In the endergonic (“uphill”) reaction, reactants A and B have less stored energy than the product, so energy must be put in (free energy input) to convert A + B into AB, which has more stored energy than the reactants.

The many endergonic reactions of metabolism are made possible by being coupled to exergonic reactions, through adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the energy currency molecule of biology. ATP acts as a common intermediate, linking energy-absorbing and energy-yielding reactions; metabolic processes mostly involve ATP, directly or indirectly.

Higher Level Cyclical and Linear Pathways in Metabolism

Key terms Metabolic pathway: a sequence of enzyme-catalysed biochemical reactions in cells and tissues.
Linear metabolic pathway: a series of enzyme-catalysed reactions that run in one direction, from reactant to product.
Cyclical metabolic pathway: a circular series of enzyme-catalysed reactions with no end to the series — one reaction leads to the next and eventually back to the starting point.

Metabolism consists of series of reactions in which the product of one reaction is the substrate (intermediate) of the next, and so on. Many pathways are linear (straight chains) of reactions, while others are cyclical. Both types occur within respiration (a catabolic process) and photosynthesis (an anabolic process).

Figure C1.1.16: Some metabolic pathways and the roles of enzymes A diagram showing three pathway types together. A linear metabolic pathway runs from metabolite A (the substrate of enzyme a) through enzymes b and c1 to metabolite C. A branched metabolic pathway shows two enzymes, c1 and c2, competing for the same metabolite C: c1 converts C onward within the linear pathway to D, while c2 diverts C towards X and Y. How much of C is converted to X or D depends on the relative amounts of enzymes c1 and c2, and how readily each forms its enzyme–substrate complex. A separate cyclical metabolic pathway is shown as a closed loop of enzymes (d through h) converting a substrate through intermediates E, I, G, F and H and back to the start.

In respiration, glycolysis is a linear metabolic pathway and the Krebs cycle is a cyclical one. Photosynthesis has light-dependent and light-independent reactions; the light-independent reactions are driven by the Calvin cycle, another example of a cyclical pathway.

Higher Level Enzyme Inhibitors: Competitive and Non-Competitive Inhibition

Key terms Enzyme inhibitor: a substance that slows or blocks enzyme action.
Competitive inhibitor: a substance that binds to the active site of an enzyme, slowing or blocking enzyme action.
Non-competitive inhibitor: a substance that does not bind to the active site but to another part of the enzyme, slowing or blocking enzyme action.

The actions of enzymes may be inhibited by other molecules, some formed within the cell and others absorbed from the environment. Studying inhibitors has helped scientists understand the chemistry of active sites, the natural regulation of metabolism (and which pathways operate), and how certain commercial pesticides and drugs work — by inhibiting specific enzymes and preventing particular reactions.

Molecules that sufficiently resemble the substrate in shape may compete to occupy the active site: these are competitive inhibitors. For example, rubisco, the enzyme that catalyses the reaction between carbon dioxide and its acceptor molecule in photosynthesis, is competitively inhibited by oxygen in the chloroplast.

Alternatively, an inhibitor may be unlike the substrate yet still combine with the enzyme at some other part of the molecule, probably close to the active site — a non-competitive inhibitor. Here, the inhibitor either partly blocks access to the active site, or causes the active site to change shape so it can no longer accept the substrate. Because non-competitive inhibitors do not compete for the active site, adding excess substrate does not overcome their inhibiting effect.

Figure C1.1.17: Competitive and non-competitive inhibitors Two diagrams of enzyme–inhibitor interactions: with a competitive inhibitor in place, the substrate is prevented from binding because the inhibitor occupies the active site; with a non-competitive inhibitor in place, the inhibitor binds elsewhere and causes a change in shape of the active site, preventing catalytic activity even though the site is not directly blocked. An accompanying graph plots initial rate of reaction against substrate concentration for three conditions: no inhibitor (reaching the highest maximum rate); a competitive inhibitor present (substrate and inhibitor compete for active sites, so excess substrate overcomes inhibition, and the maximum rate can eventually still be reached); and a non-competitive inhibitor present (the inhibitor does not compete for active sites, so excess substrate cannot overcome inhibition, and the maximum rate achievable is permanently lowered).

The key difference between the two types of inhibition lies in the interaction between substrate and inhibitor, and therefore in the effect of increasing substrate concentration: in competitive inhibition, substrate and inhibitor compete for active sites, so excess substrate overcomes the inhibition; in non-competitive inhibition, the inhibitor does not compete for active sites, so excess substrate does not overcome the inhibition.

Higher Level Allosteric sites and non-competitive inhibition

Allosteric regulators Molecules that change the shape and activity of an enzyme by reversibly binding at a site on the enzyme, typically some distance from the active site.

Binding of an allosteric activator temporarily stabilizes the enzyme’s shape as an active, effective catalyst; binding of an allosteric inhibitor changes the enzyme’s shape into an inactive form. Allosteric regulation can therefore be seen as a form of reversible non-competitive inhibition or activation of an enzyme. Subtle fluctuations in the concentrations of activators and inhibitors can fine-tune the activity of a critical pathway as the changing conditions or requirements of cell metabolism demand. Only specific substances, known as effectors, can bind at an allosteric site; binding causes interactions within the enzyme that lead to conformational changes, altering the active site enough to prevent catalysis. This binding is reversible.

Higher Level Competitive inhibition: statins

Statin Any of a class of drugs that lower the level of low-density lipoproteins in the blood by inhibiting the activity of an enzyme involved in the production of cholesterol in the liver.

Statins are competitive inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase (HMGR), an enzyme crucial for the synthesis of cholesterol. Statins are similar in structure to HMGR’s substrate, HMG-CoA, so they can occupy part of the enzyme’s binding site, blocking access of the substrate to the active site.

Health consequences of the lipid content of diets

Cholesterol, a steroid lipid, is a component of the diet, particularly where animal fats are present. It is carried in the blood in two forms: low-density lipoproteins (LDLs), complexes of thousands of cholesterol molecules bound to proteins (“bad cholesterol”), and high-density lipoproteins (HDLs), or “good cholesterol”. Excess blood cholesterol, present as LDLs, may cause atherosclerosis — the progressive degeneration of artery walls — directly increasing the likelihood of blood clot formation and circulation. Statins are taken to reduce LDLs in the blood, and therefore to reduce the risk of coronary heart disease and other effects of high blood cholesterol.

Higher Level Regulation of Metabolic Pathways by Feedback Inhibition

End-product inhibition When the product of the last reaction in a metabolic pathway inhibits the enzyme that catalyses the first reaction of the pathway.

Individual metabolic pathways may be switched off by their final product acting as a reversible inhibitor of the enzyme that catalyses the first step of the pathway. As product molecules accumulate, the steps in their own production are switched off. If these product molecules then become the substrate of subsequent reactions, the accumulated product will be removed and production of new product can recommence — an example of the regulation of a metabolic pathway by negative feedback.

Figure C1.1.21: Regulation of a metabolic pathway by end-product inhibition A linear pathway from a substrate molecule A, via enzymes a, b, c and others, through intermediates B, C, D, E, to end-product F. The end-product molecule F inhibits enzyme a, the enzyme catalysing the first step of the pathway, switching off further production of intermediates and end-product until the concentration of F falls.
Common mistake A common misconception is that a competitive inhibitor of an intermediate enzyme in a pathway would prevent any final product from being formed. This is unlikely, because the substrate of the inhibited enzyme would still sometimes manage to bind to the active site, so some final product would still be formed.

End-product inhibition of the pathway from threonine to isoleucine

Bacteria synthesize the amino acid isoleucine from threonine via a five-enzyme pathway. Isoleucine acts as a non-competitive inhibitor by binding to the allosteric site of threonine deaminase, the essential enzyme catalysing the first stage of the pathway; inhibiting this enzyme turns off further isoleucine production, regulating its concentration. Initially, when isoleucine concentration is low, the pathway proceeds because non-competitive inhibition is low. As isoleucine accumulates, inhibition increases and the pathway is progressively switched off. As isoleucine is used up (for example in protein synthesis), its concentration falls, the allosteric sites of threonine deaminase are no longer occupied, and the enzyme can once again catalyse the conversion of threonine to isoleucine.

Figure C1.1.22: Isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase, acting as a non-competitive inhibitor A pathway diagram: threonine, acted on by enzyme 1 (threonine deaminase), is converted via intermediates A, B, C and D (each formed by a separate enzyme, 2 to 5) into isoleucine. At high levels, isoleucine (the allosteric inhibitor) binds to an allosteric site on threonine deaminase distinct from its active site, inhibiting the enzyme; at low levels of isoleucine, the enzyme is not inhibited and the pathway proceeds normally.
Top tip! Isoleucine is an essential amino acid: it cannot be made by the human body and so must be obtained from food.

Higher Level Mechanism-Based Inhibition

Mechanism-based inhibition A process that occurs when unreactive molecules are transformed into an active form through catalytic reactions; these active forms inhibit the enzyme, typically through covalent modification of the active site. It is an irreversible form of enzyme inhibition.

A substrate analogue is a molecule with a structure similar enough to the substrate that it can bind covalently to the enzyme’s active site. Once bound, the enzyme can modify the analogue to produce a reactive group, which reacts irreversibly to form a stable inhibitor–enzyme complex — mechanism-based inhibition.

Penicillin, the first group of antibiotics developed to fight bacterial disease, works by this mechanism. Bacterial cell walls are made of peptidoglycan (murein): polysaccharide chains linked by short peptides. The bacterial enzyme DD-transpeptidase forms cross-links between these strands, strengthening the wall. The structure of penicillin resembles part of the growing peptide chain of peptidoglycan, so instead of binding a peptide chain, transpeptidase binds penicillin; the enzyme modifies penicillin’s structure, forming an inactive enzyme–penicillin complex that blocks further cross-linking. Because the penicillin substrate itself is modified through this interaction, the inhibition is irreversible.

Without a strong, cross-linked cell wall, a bacterial cell is vulnerable to water moving in by osmosis, which kills the bacterium by lysis (the increased internal water pressure bursts the membrane).

Figure C1.1.23: Penicillin blocks the formation of peptide cross-links between polysaccharide chains of peptidoglycan Two polysaccharide chains of peptidoglycan are shown linked by peptide cross-links formed by active DD-transpeptidase. Alongside, penicillin is shown binding to and inactivating DD-transpeptidase, preventing it from forming further peptide cross-links between the polysaccharide chains.

Bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics such as penicillin: mutations in the bacterial genome can alter the active site of some transpeptidases, reducing or abolishing penicillin’s affinity for the enzyme and so promoting antibiotic resistance. When such mutations occur in plasmid DNA, resistance can spread rapidly between bacteria through conjugation.

Figure C1.1.24: Genetic mutations can result in structural changes in the active site of transpeptidases, leading to antibiotic resistance Two versions of transpeptidase are compared: in the non-mutated enzyme, the active site is “opened” and accepts penicillin, which then inactivates it; in the mutated enzyme, a structural change has left the active site “closed” to penicillin, so the antibiotic can no longer bind and the enzyme continues to function, conferring resistance.
