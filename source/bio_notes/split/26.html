Pathogens and Infectious Disease

Many disease-causing organisms can infect humans. Such an organism is called a pathogen, a term usually reserved for viruses, bacteria (eubacteria), fungi and protists. Archaea are not known to cause any human disease — possibly because of a special biochemistry, or simply because they were only recognized as a separate domain about 40 years ago and pathogenic species have not yet been found.

A disease is caused when another organism or virus particle invades the body and lives there parasitically. The pathogen is the invader; the infected organism (a human, in this context) is the host. Pathogens may pass from a diseased host to healthy organisms, so diseases caused this way are infectious or communicable diseases.

Key terms Pathogen: disease-causing organism.
Infection: the process of infecting, or the state of being infected by a pathogen.
Disease: any harmful deviation from the normal structural or functional state of an organism, causing illness.
Infectious/communicable disease: a disease capable of being transmitted from one organism to another.

Disease is broadly defined as an “unhealthy condition of the body”, and this covers distinctly different forms of ill health. Non-infectious (non-communicable) diseases are caused by unfavourable environmental conditions and include cardiovascular disease, malnutrition and cancer. Other diseases are genetic in origin, such as phenylketonuria and Down syndrome.

The World Health Organization (WHO)

Good health is more than the absence of the harmful effects of disease. The WHO, founded in 1948 as the United Nations agency responsible for international public health, defines health as a “state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity”. The WHO monitors and communicates information on the spread and containment of infectious diseases such as COVID-19.

Which organisms are pathogens?

Disease-causing organisms that infect humans include certain bacteria and fungi, some protozoa (single-celled animals), certain invertebrate animals (flatworms and roundworms), and many viruses. Not all bacteria or fungi are parasitic and pathogenic — only relatively few species are. However, no virus can function outside a host organism, so all viruses are parasitic. Once inside a host cell, a virus can take over the machinery of protein and nucleic acid synthesis, forcing the cell to manufacture more virus components from which new viruses self-assemble.

Nature of science: careful observation and germ theory

Careful observation can drive major progress in disease control. In August 1854, an outbreak of cholera in Soho, London, killed 616 people within a month; it was believed that “bad air” from rotting matter was responsible. Physician John Snow mapped the deaths and noticed they clustered around a water pump on Broad Street. Acting on his observation, the local council removed the pump handle, and deaths fell sharply. It later emerged the water had been contaminated by sewage carrying the cholera bacterium.

In Vienna in the 1840s, Ignaz Semmelweis observed that women giving birth in a ward attended by medical students (who had come straight from dissecting cadavers, often without washing their hands) died of childbed fever (puerperal septicaemia) far more often than those attended by midwives (who did not perform autopsies). In 1847 he proposed that anyone delivering a baby should first wash their hands. His conclusion was close to the truth, but conflicted with the medical opinion of the time and was rejected, causing further avoidable deaths. It was only once Louis Pasteur confirmed germ theory (that certain diseases are caused by infection with micro-organisms) and Joseph Lister developed antiseptic surgical methods that hand-washing and hygiene became standard medical practice. This history illustrates how current scientific knowledge is shaped by, and sometimes held back by, its historical development and prevailing opinion.

Skin and Mucous Membranes as a Primary Defence

The skin and mucous membranes (the internal linings of the lungs, trachea and gut) form the body’s primary defence against pathogens — the initial barriers that prevent pathogens from causing infection.

Key terms Mucous membrane (mucosa): the inner lining of some organs and body cavities (e.g. nose, mouth, lungs, intestines); glands here make mucus.
Primary defence: the initial barriers that prevent pathogens from causing infection.
Mucus: a watery solution of glycoprotein with protective and lubrication functions.
Cilium (plural cilia): a motile, hair-like outgrowth from the surface of certain eukaryotic cells that moves rhythmically to propel substances such as mucus.

The skin

The skin acts as both a physical and a chemical barrier. The external skin is covered by keratinized protein from the dead cells of the epidermis — a tough, impervious layer that is an effective barrier to most organisms unless the surface is broken, cut or deeply scratched. Periodic shedding of the epidermis removes microbes. However, permanently moist folds or creases can host micro-organisms that degrade this barrier, causing infections such as athlete’s foot.

Figure C3.2.1: The skin is a protective barrier Shows a cross-section of skin cells at the surface. The outermost layer consists of dead keratin (keratinized) cells that are connected to each other, with the gaps between them filled by a lipid-rich matrix. Together the keratinized cells and the lipid filling form a strong barrier that keeps pathogens out.

Lysozyme is an enzyme secreted by the skin during perspiration (sweating). It acts as a chemical defence by breaking down bacterial cell walls, effectively acting as a natural antibiotic.

Mucous membranes

The internal surfaces of the breathing apparatus (trachea, bronchi, bronchioles) and the gut are lined by moist epithelial cells known as mucous membranes (mucosa). These vulnerable internal surfaces are protected by the secretion of large quantities of mucus. Cilia project from the surface of certain epithelial cells lining the air tubes (bronchi) serving the lungs, and beat to sweep the fluid mucus layer across the epithelial surface, away from the delicate air sacs of the lungs.

Figure C3.2.2: Mucous membrane in the trachea Shows ciliated epithelial cells and goblet cells forming the tracheal lining. Goblet cells secrete a layer of mucus that sits above the epithelial cells; pathogens become trapped in this mucus. The cilia on the ciliated epithelial cells beat rhythmically, moving the mucus (carrying trapped pathogens) upward, away from the lungs and towards the mouth, where it can be swallowed or expelled.

Sealing of Cuts in Skin by Blood Clotting

When a blood vessel is ruptured, the blood-clotting mechanism is activated. This produces localized clotting that prevents further blood loss, prevents a significant fall in blood pressure, and reduces the chance of pathogens invading through the wound. Once clotting has occurred, tissue repair can take place. Note the precise wording: clotting prevents the entrance of pathogens — it is imprecise to simply say it “stops diseases getting in”.

Key term Platelets: tiny cell fragments found in the blood that lack a nucleus; they are involved in the blood-clotting mechanism. They are disc-shaped, about 2 µm in diameter, formed in the bone marrow, and consist of a sac of cytoplasm rich in enzyme-containing vesicles, surrounded by a plasma membrane.

The clotting cascade

Blood clot formation is triggered by a “cascade” of events at the site of a broken vessel:

  1. Platelets collect at the site. They stick to the damaged tissue and clump together, changing shape from sacs into flattened discs with tiny interlocking projections. This alone seals off the smallest breaks.
  2. Clotting factor release. The collecting platelets (and the damaged tissue itself) release a clotting factor, the protein thromboplastin. Together with vitamin K and calcium ions (always present in the plasma), thromboplastin converts the soluble plasma protein prothrombin into the active proteolytic enzyme thrombin.
  3. Fibrin formation. Thrombin converts the soluble blood protein fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin fibres at the site of the cut. Red blood cells become trapped within this mesh of fibres, forming the blood clot.

Platelets collect, adhere and release clotting factor (thromboplastin), forming a platelet plug sufficient to block minor breaks; constriction of the damaged vessel (aided by local hormones, prostaglandins) reduces blood flow. Common mistake: do not confuse fibrin (the insoluble fibres that form the clot) with fibrinogen (the soluble plasma protein precursor). Learn the cascade sequence carefully: thromboplastin release → prothrombin converted to thrombin → fibrinogen converted to fibrin.

Innate vs Adaptive Immunity: An Overview

The immune response is the body’s main defence once invasion by harmful micro-organisms or foreign materials has occurred. It begins with a first response from the innate immune system, carried out by phagocytes (a type of white blood cell) that engulf and destroy many different foreign organisms by phagocytosis. At the same time, the primary phase of the adaptive immune system response begins, involving specialized white blood cells called lymphocytes. This is followed by a secondary phase in which specific white blood cells retain a memory of the pathogen so that a rapid response can be mounted on reinfection.

Innate vs adaptive immune system Innate immune system: responds to broad categories of pathogens; does not change during an organism’s life; carried out by phagocytes.
Adaptive immune system: responds in a specific way to particular pathogens; builds up a memory of pathogens encountered so the response becomes more effective over time; carried out by lymphocytes.

Top tip! White blood cells are called leucocytes. They divide into two groups: phagocytes (which engulf foreign organisms, part of the innate system) and lymphocytes (involved in the adaptive immune system). Both circulate in the blood and are contained in lymph nodes.

Phagocytes and Innate Immune Defence

Key term Phagocytes: white blood cells that engulf, absorb and digest pathogens, foreign particles and cell debris.

Certain white blood cells engulf foreign material, including invading bacterial cells. Some are short-lived cells of the plasma; others are long-lived, “rubbish-collecting” cells distributed throughout body tissues. Both types take up material into their cytoplasm in a manner resembling how the protozoan Amoeba feeds, by phagocytosis. Amoeboid movement (a crawling-like movement using cytoplasmic protrusions) allows phagocytes to move from the blood to sites of infection, where they recognize pathogens, engulf them by endocytosis, and digest them in a controlled way using enzymes from lysosomes.

The diagram notes that “rubbish-collecting” leucocytes distributed widely in the body engulf larger debris and damaged cells and dispose of them.

Lymphocytes: B-Cells and T-Cells

Key terms Lymphocytes: white blood cells in the adaptive immune system that cooperate to produce antibodies.
B-lymphocytes (B-cells): lymphocytes that produce antibodies and are responsible for presenting antigens to T-cells; once activated they can mature into plasma cells or memory B-lymphocytes.
T-lymphocytes (T-cells): white blood cells processed by the thymus that are responsible for cell-mediated immunity.
Plasma: the clear, yellowish liquid part of the blood that carries blood cells.

Lymphocytes are responsible for the specific immune response, making up about 20% of the white blood cells circulating in the blood plasma or tissue fluid. They circulate in the blood and are also contained in lymph nodes (part of the lymphatic system). Lymphocytes can detect any foreign matter entering from outside the body (macromolecules as well as micro-organisms) as different from the body’s own cells and proteins.

There are two types of lymphocyte: B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes. Both types originate in the bone marrow from stem cells, but mature differently in preparation for their distinctive roles.

Figure C3.2.5: T- and B-lymphocytes Shows lymphocytes arising by mitosis (cell division) from a stem cell of the bone marrow. B-cells mature within the bone marrow itself. T-cells migrate during maturation to the thymus gland, where any T-lymphocytes that would react against the body’s own cells are destroyed. Mature lymphocytes of both types then circulate in the blood, escape into the tissue fluid surrounding body cells, and collect in the lymph system; many are stored in lymph nodes, which occur all over the body but are concentrated in the neck, armpits and groin. Each mature T-lymphocyte or B-lymphocyte then awaits a chance encounter with its specific antigen. The diagram also indicates that B-lymphocytes are activated by T-lymphocytes.

T-lymphocytes

T-lymphocytes leave the bone marrow soon after formation and migrate to the thymus gland, found in the chest just below the sternum. The thymus is active and enlarged in early growth and development. While present in the thymus, any T-cells that would react against the body’s own cells are removed and destroyed. Surviving T-cells are released to circulate in the blood plasma, and many are stored in lymph nodes. The thymus shrinks by puberty, its task complete.

B-lymphocytes

B-lymphocytes complete their maturation in the bone marrow before circulating in the blood; many are also stored in lymph nodes. After activation by T-cells, most B-cells form clones of plasma cells that secrete antibodies into the blood system; memory cells are also formed. An individual has a very large number of B-lymphocytes, each making a specific type of antibody.

Key terms Thymus gland: a small organ in the upper chest under the breastbone in which T-lymphocytes grow, multiply and mature; part of the lymphatic system.
Plasma cells: immune cells that make large amounts of a specific antibody; develop from activated B-cells.
Memory cells: long-lived lymphocytes capable of responding to a particular antigen on its reintroduction, long after the original exposure that stimulated their production.
Immunoglobulin: a protein made by B-lymphocytes and plasma cells that helps the body fight infection.

Top tip! Antibodies are proteins produced by B-lymphocytes that attach to foreign, non-self material, allowing the immune system to remove it. Antigens are substances (usually glycoproteins or other proteins) capable of binding specifically to an antibody.

Antibody Structure and Function

An antibody is a globular protein called an immunoglobulin. It is made of four polypeptide chains (two identical light chains and two identical heavy chains) held together by disulfide bridges (–S–S–), forming a Y-shaped molecule. The top of each “arm” contains an antigen-binding site, formed from a unique sequence of amino acid residues to which a specific antigen may fit.

Figure C3.2.6: The structure of an antibody Shows the Y-shaped antibody molecule made of two light-chain polypeptides and two heavy-chain polypeptides, linked by disulfide bridges (–S–S–). Each of the two upper “arms” ends in an antigen-binding site made of a unique amino acid sequence, into which a specific antigen fits. The diagram shows that antibodies occur attached to the cell surface membrane of B-lymphocytes, and may also be secreted by exocytosis as free-standing molecules.

Millions of different antibody types can be produced, each by a different type of lymphocyte — there are as many antibodies as there are types of foreign antigen. The amino acid sequence of the antigen-binding site (the “fork” region) differs according to the antigen it binds, giving each antibody its specificity. Antibodies initially occur attached to the B-cell surface membrane but are later mass-produced and secreted by cells derived from that B-cell, once it has undergone activation.

Antigens and Self/Non-Self Recognition

The immune system can recognize “self” (the body’s own cells and proteins) and distinguish it from foreign, “non-self” substances, such as those on an invading organism. Any molecule the body recognizes as foreign is an antigen. Most antigens are glycoproteins or other proteins, usually located on the outer surfaces of pathogens. Lymphocytes recognize antigens and take steps to overcome them; the specificity of antigens allows a response customized to the specific pathogen.

Each type of lymphocyte recognizes only one specific antigen. In the presence of that antigen, the lymphocyte divides rapidly, producing many identical cells known as clones, which then secrete an antibody specific to that antigen. Antigens on the surface of red blood cells may also stimulate antibody production if transfused into a person with a different blood group.

How are “self” and “non-self” recognized?

Every organism has unique molecules on the surface of its cells, including highly variable glycoproteins on the cell surface membrane. These glycoproteins are known as the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) antigens. In humans, the genes for MHC antigens are located on chromosome 6, and are genetically determined and inherited, so (aside from identical twins) everyone’s MHC antigens are unique. Lymphocytes have antigen receptors that recognize the body’s own MHC antigens and distinguish them from foreign antigens — the basis of the “self”/“non-self” recognition mechanism that is critical to prevent the immune system attacking the body’s own cells.

Key term Major histocompatibility complex (MHC): a group of genes that code for proteins found on the surfaces of cells, which help the immune system recognize foreign substances.

The Immune Response: Activation and Clonal Selection

When infection occurs, the white blood cell population immediately increases and many cells collect at the site of invasion.

Activation of B-lymphocytes by helper T-lymphocytes

Top tip! T- and B-cells have molecules on their outer cell surface membrane that recognize antigens, but each individual lymphocyte has only one type of surface receptor, so each lymphocyte can recognize only one type of antigen.

  1. When a specific antigen enters the body, B-cells whose surface receptors (antibodies) recognize the antigen bind to it.
  2. On binding, the antigen is taken into the B-cell’s cytoplasm by endocytosis, then expressed and displayed on the B-cell surface membrane (an antigen-specific B-cell).
  3. Meanwhile, phagocytic cells called macrophages (found in the plasma, lymph, tissue fluid, liver, spleen and alveoli) engulf any antigens they encounter. Macrophages (and T-cells) secrete cytokines — small signalling molecules (usually protein or glycoprotein) that act on neighbouring cells to alter their behaviour, assisting the immune response. Once engulfed, antigens are presented externally on the macrophage surface, attached to MHC molecules — this is antigen presentation.
  4. As T-cells encounter macrophages and briefly bind to them, they are immediately activated, becoming “armed” or activated helper T-cells.
  5. Activated helper T-cells bind to antigen-specific B-cells displaying the same antigen. As a result, the B-cell is activated (an “armed” or activated B-cell).
Key terms Antigen-specific B-cell: a lymphocyte that will form either antibody-secreting cells or memory B-cells after infection or vaccination.
Macrophage: a phagocytic white blood cell that surrounds and kills pathogens, removes dead cells and stimulates other immune system cells.
Antigen presentation: the process where an antigen is taken into a white blood cell by receptor-mediated endocytosis, digested, complexed with MHC II molecules, and presented on the cell surface for interaction with T-cells.
Helper T-cell: a lymphocyte that recognizes a foreign antigen and then activates T-cell and B-cell production.
Activated B-cell: a B-cell that has absorbed an antigen, presented pieces of it via MHC, and been recognized and activated by a helper T-cell, resulting in differentiation into memory B-cells or plasma cells.

Common mistake: do not confuse the roles of T- and B-cells. B-cells secrete antibodies. Helper T-cells do not secrete antibodies — their role is to further activate B-cells after being activated themselves by contact with an antigen presented by a macrophage.

Top tip! B-cells become memory cells only once activated. Activation requires both direct interaction with the specific antigen and contact with a helper T-cell activated by the same antigen.

The generation of a large number of plasma cells producing one specific antibody type is known as clonal selection. Antibodies are normally produced in such numbers that the antigen is overcome, either by neutralizing it or by making it a clear target for phagocytic cells. Once the disease threat is overcome, the antibodies disappear from the blood and tissue fluid, as do the bulk of the specific B- and T-cells that produced them.

Immunological Memory

Key term Immunity: resistance to the onset of a disease after infection by the causative agent.

Long-lived and specific immunity results from the long-term survival of lymphocytes able to make the specific antibodies needed to fight a particular infection: memory cells, retained after a previous infection by that pathogen. Memory cells are specifically activated B-cells that, unlike plasma cells and other activated B-cells, are long-lived. They enable an early and effective response if the body is reinfected by the same antigen — the basis of natural immunity.

Figure C3.2.10: Profile of antibody production in infection and reinfection A graph of antibody concentration against time. The primary response to an initial infection shows a delay of about 5 days before antibody concentration rises, produced by short-lived plasma cells, before falling away. On a later reinfection by the same antigen, the secondary response occurs much faster (a shorter lag) and reaches a substantially higher antibody concentration, because memory cells retained in the lymph nodes allow a quick and specific response — this rapid, strong response constitutes the person’s immunity.

Common mistake: explanations of the second exposure to an antigen often lack detail — make sure to state that antibodies are produced both more rapidly and to a higher concentration on reinfection.

HIV and AIDS

Key terms HIV (human immunodeficiency virus): a virus that attacks the body’s immune system; if untreated, it can lead to AIDS.
AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome): the name for a number of potentially life-threatening infections and illnesses that occur when the immune system has been severely damaged by HIV.
Retrovirus: a virus with RNA as its nucleic acid, which uses the enzyme reverse transcriptase to copy its genome into the DNA of the host cell’s chromosomes.

HIV was first identified in 1983 as the cause of AIDS. It is a tiny virus, less than 0.1 µm in diameter, consisting of two single strands of RNA and enzymes enclosed within a protein capsid, itself surrounded by a membrane derived from the host cell as the new virus particle (virion) buds off.

Figure C3.2.11: Electron micrograph of white blood cell with HIV budding off A colourized electron micrograph showing a white blood cell (shown in blue) with several new HIV virions (shown in yellow) in the process of budding off from its surface.

HIV is a retrovirus: it reverses the normal flow of genetic information (DNA → mRNA, the “central dogma”) by using the enzyme reverse transcriptase to convert its RNA into DNA inside the host cell; this DNA then integrates into a chromosome in the host cell’s nucleus.

Transmission of HIV in body fluids

HIV is transmitted through contact with the blood or body fluids of infected people, such as during sexual intercourse, sharing of hypodermic needles among intravenous drug users, or breastfeeding of a newborn. Blood transfusions and organ transplants can also transmit HIV, though donors are now screened in most countries. HIV is not transmitted by contact with saliva on a shared drinking glass or towel, and female mosquitoes do not transmit HIV when feeding on human blood.

Common mistake: it is incorrect to say that AIDS is transmitted. HIV (the virus) is transmitted; AIDS (the disease it causes) is not.

Infection of lymphocytes by HIV, with AIDS as a consequence

HIV is an enveloped virus with embedded glycoproteins that act as antigens once the virus is in the bloodstream. These glycoproteins attach the virus to protein (CD4) receptors on the surface of T-lymphocytes, allowing the virus core to penetrate into the cytoplasm of these important regulatory immune cells. Macrophages may also be infected by HIV.

Figure C3.2.12: HIV infection of host cell — CD4 T-helper cells Shows three steps: (1) a binding protein on the HIV capsule attaches to the CD4 receptor on a T-lymphocyte; (2) this binding leads to fusion of the viral capsule with the cell surface membrane; (3) the virus core enters the lymphocyte, while HIV antigens remain on the surface of the now-infected cell.
Figure C3.2.13: Activation of the HIV genome and production of newly replicated HIV virions Shows the genes of HIV integrated into the host chromosome. A copy of viral RNA is made as a messenger RNA copy, which travels to ribosomes where viral proteins are formed; an RNA core is also formed. Viral proteins and RNA are assembled into new HIV “cores”, which then bud off from the cell, taking part of the host cell surface membrane with them to form the new virus capsule (the “budding” process).

Newly synthesized HIV virions leave the host cell by lysis and go on to invade and kill other lymphocytes. Without treatment, this rapidly depletes the body’s reserve of lymphocytes (macrophages also form an important reservoir of HIV). As active lymphocyte numbers fall, the body loses its ability to produce antibodies, and eventually no infection — however trivial — can be resisted; death follows. This is why AIDS is described as an “immunodeficiency syndrome”: a reduction in specific lymphocytes limits antibody production and the ability to fight opportunistic infections.

An HIV vaccine that removes both infected lymphocytes and free HIV particles would be ideal, but a major obstacle is that, in the latent state of infection, infected lymphocytes frequently change their membrane marker proteins due to the presence of the HIV genome within the cell — effectively, HIV can hide from the immune response by changing its identity.

Following initial infection there is a spike in HIV RNA and early symptoms as T-lymphocyte numbers fall from the normal range. Over subsequent years, T-lymphocyte numbers become significantly depressed, and HIV RNA copies rise again as T-lymphocyte depletion becomes severe; eventually an opportunistic infection (e.g. pneumonia) takes hold as the immune system becomes hopelessly compromised, resulting in death.

Antibiotics

Key term Antibiotics: chemicals that block processes occurring in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells.

Antibiotics are substances, obtained from fungi or bacteria, that slow down or kill micro-organisms; at low concentrations they inhibit the growth of other micro-organisms. Many bacterial diseases of humans and other animals can be successfully treated with them. Before antibiotics, hospital wards were filled with patients suffering from pneumonia, typhoid fever, tuberculosis, meningitis, syphilis and rheumatic fever, and these bacterial diseases claimed many lives. In the 1930s, about 40% of patients with bacterial pneumonia died of the disease; today, about 5–10% die, an improvement due to antibiotic drugs. Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, so viral pneumonia and viral meningitis are not treatable with them.

The discovery of penicillin

In 1929, Scottish bacteriologist Alexander Fleming (1881–1955), studying the bacterium Staphylococcus, noticed that an older culture plate had become accidentally contaminated with a fungal colony, and that bacteria were killed in the areas surrounding the mould, which he identified as Penicillium notatum. He cultured the mould in broth and discovered that a substance from it, which he named penicillin, was bactericidal and did not harm human blood cells.

Nature of science: the global impact of science and safety testing

Australian pathologist Harold Florey (1898–1968) and German biochemist Ernst Chain (1906–79) went on to isolate penicillin in a stable form for therapeutic use. Their key test used mice: eight mice were injected with a lethal suspension of bacteria; four received no further treatment and all four died, while the other four were also given penicillin and all four survived. By modern standards this experiment would not be compliant with drug-testing protocols, illustrating how scientists today have an internationally recognized obligation to assess the risks associated with their work.

Figure C3.2.15: Early steps in the discovery and use of penicillin as an antibiotic Illustrates the culturing method (a sterile inoculating loop dipped into a micro-organism culture and streaked onto sterilized nutrient agar), Fleming’s original Petri dish showing Staphylococcus colonies with a zone where bacterial colonies have been killed around an accidental mould (Penicillium notatum) contamination, and Florey and Chain’s mouse experiment described above (4 untreated mice died; 4 penicillin-treated mice survived).

Since penicillin’s discovery, over 4000 different antibiotics have been isolated, but only about 50 have proved safe for use as drugs. Antibiotics effective across a wide range of pathogens are called broad-spectrum antibiotics; others are effective against just a few pathogens. Many modern antibiotics are semi-synthetic (natural starting materials chemically converted into final products), giving activity against resistant bacteria, a greater spectrum of activity, or fewer side effects.

How antibiotics work

Most antibiotics disrupt the metabolism of prokaryotic cells, quickly suppressing whole bacterial populations, while leaving the cells of the (eukaryotic) human host unaffected.

Mechanism targetedEffects
Cell wall synthesisThe antibiotic interferes with synthesis of the bacterial cell wall. Once the wall is destroyed, the delicate plasma membrane is exposed to the destructive force of excessive water uptake by osmosis, causing lysis (bursting) of the cell. Penicillin, ampicillin and bacitracin bind to and inactivate specific wall-building enzymes so the bacterial wall falls apart (the most effective mechanism).
Protein synthesisThe antibiotic inhibits protein synthesis by binding with ribosomal RNA. Prokaryotic ribosomes are made of different RNA subunits from the larger eukaryotic ribosomes. Streptomycin, chloramphenicol, the tetracyclines and erythromycin all bind to prokaryotic ribosomal RNA subunits unique to bacteria, terminating their protein synthesis.

Viruses are non-living particles with no metabolism of their own, and reproduce using the metabolic pathways of their host cell, so they have no function that antibiotics can inhibit — antibiotics cannot be used to prevent viral diseases. Common mistake: it is incorrect to say antibiotics fail against viruses because viruses “hide inside the host cell” — the real reason is that antibiotics act on bacterial enzymes and cell wall synthesis, neither of which viruses possess.

Evolution of Antibiotic Resistance

Evolution by natural selection is the process by which genetic variation in organisms is selected for by their environment, ultimately leading to speciation; it explains the development of antibiotic resistance in bacteria. Patients infected with a bacterium are commonly treated with antibiotics. In a large bacterial population, some individuals may carry a gene for resistance to the antibiotic (typically on a plasmid), arising by spontaneous mutation or acquired through conjugation with resistant bacteria of another population.

Most of a bacterial population is not adapted to an environment that has changed through the addition of an antibiotic, but a resistant variant has a selective advantage under those conditions (though no advantage, and a competitive disadvantage, in the antibiotic’s absence). When the antibiotic is present, most bacteria are killed, but resistant bacteria are very likely to survive and become the basis of the new population — all its members now carry the resistance gene, so the genome of the population has changed abruptly.

Nature of science: science as a shared endeavour

The development of penicillin as an antibiotic led to a wide range of further antibiotics targeting specific bacteria. Modern techniques use machine-learning algorithms to survey over a hundred million chemical compounds to identify potential antibiotics that kill bacteria via new mechanisms, using internationally developed chemical libraries — a global effort in the face of increasing resistance. Researchers hope computer models can also help optimize existing antibiotics so they target only certain bacteria, avoiding harm to beneficial gut bacteria.

Zoonotic Diseases

Key terms Zoonosis (plural zoonoses): an infectious disease that can transfer from other species to humans.
Virulent: the ability of a virus (or bacterium) to cause rapid and severe disease.
Novel virus: a virus that has not previously been recorded.

Pathogens are often specific in their choice of host — for example, humans are the only host for the pathogens causing syphilis, gonorrhoea, measles and poliomyelitis. Other pathogens, however, can cross species barriers and infect a range of hosts; these are zoonoses. Generally, infectious diseases are most often shared between closely related species inhabiting the same geographic area. The incidence of zoonoses increases when humans live in close contact with animals or encounter animals in new geographical regions. The SARS virus (75% genetically similar to SARS-CoV-2) originated in South East Asia in 2002 from a bat coronavirus that spread to humans directly or via civet cats.

Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by the rod-shaped bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. In Illinois, USA, elephants died of M. tuberculosis in the 1990s, and DNA fingerprinting confirmed the same strain infected an animal handler with active TB — demonstrating transmission between humans and elephants. Zoonotic TB in humans is predominantly caused by the closely related Mycobacterium bovis, which causes bovine tuberculosis (bTB) and can be transmitted directly by contact with infected animals or indirectly through contaminated material (e.g. raw milk, contact with infected tissue at abattoirs). In cattle, the usual infection route is inhalation of aerosols expelled by coughing. In Europe, badgers can be infected with the same bTB strains as local cattle; transmission occurs in both directions, though nearly ten times more transmission occurs from badgers to cattle than the reverse. M. tuberculosis and M. bovis are grouped in the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), now considered a single species with variants; human-to-cattle transmission of MTBC, though less often recognized than the reverse, has been increasingly documented.

Rabies

Rabies is a viral disease affecting many carnivorous animals (dogs, cats, foxes, skunks, jackals, wolves), both wild and domestic. It is transmitted through direct contact (e.g. via broken skin or mucous membranes of the eyes, nose or mouth) with saliva or nervous system tissue from an infected animal; humans are usually infected via the bite of a rabid animal. Rabies causes progressive, fatal inflammation of the brain and spinal cord; once symptoms appear, it is fatal, although the disease is avoidable through vaccines and other interventions. Despite this, rabies still kills approximately 59,000 people annually across more than 150 countries, with 95% of cases in Africa and Asia; the WHO states dog bites cause approximately 99% of human rabies transmission.

Japanese encephalitis

Japanese encephalitis (JE) is an infectious disease of the central nervous system caused by the Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), a zoonotic virus prevalent across much of Asia and the Western Pacific. It is transmitted to humans through the bite of infected mosquitoes, particularly Culex tritaeniorhynchus. Encephalitis (inflammation of brain tissue caused by infection) causes brain swelling, leading to headache, stiff neck, light sensitivity, mental confusion and seizures. JEV causes disease in horses, donkeys and pigs; pigs and wading birds are the main carriers, while other animals (wild mammals, reptiles, amphibians, birds, and domesticated cattle, sheep, goats, dogs and cats) can be infected without showing illness. There is no cure; treatment focuses on relieving symptoms. Safe and effective vaccines exist, and mass immunization has made JE relatively rare even in Japan today.

AIDS as a zoonosis

AIDS is caused by two closely related zoonotic viruses, HIV-1 (more virulent, more easily transmitted, and the cause of most infections) and HIV-2. Both arose from cross-species transmission of simian immunodeficiency viruses (SIVs) naturally infecting African primates. HIV/AIDS went unidentified and largely unnoticed for decades before spreading globally in the 1980s, driven by factors such as affordable international air travel, unscreened blood transfusions, sexual behaviour patterns, and intravenous drug use.

COVID-19

COVID-19 is a disease that recently transferred from another species, with profound consequences for humans, and is one of three novel infectious respiratory coronaviruses to have emerged in recent decades: SARS, MERS and COVID-19. SARS-CoV-2, the coronavirus responsible for COVID-19, was first reported in Wuhan, China, in 2019; it is related to the SARS virus and research suggests an origin in a bat coronavirus, though the exact origin of the pandemic has not been confirmed. The global pandemic led to widespread border closures, travel restrictions, lockdowns and school closures.

Nature of science: scientific collaboration in vaccine creation

Vaccines traditionally take 10–15 years to develop due to the complexity of development and the clinical testing required to check for side effects. During the COVID-19 pandemic, international collaboration — shared research information, extensive government financial support, and running several clinical trial stages simultaneously rather than consecutively — allowed the first vaccine to be administered in the UK on 8 December 2020, within the same year the pandemic was declared. This combination of global scientific collaboration and mRNA vaccine development has been compared to the scale of achievement of the Moon landing, and created a blueprint for rapid future vaccine development in a global medical crisis.

Vaccines and Immunization

Key term Vaccination: conferring immunity from a disease by injecting an antigen of attenuated (weakened) micro-organisms, an inactivated component, or a nucleic acid (mRNA), so that the body acquires antibodies prior to potential infection.

Vaccines contain antigens, or nucleic acids (DNA or mRNA) with sequences that code for antigens. These trigger the development of immunity to a specific pathogen without causing the disease itself. Vaccines may be administered by injection, nasal spray or by mouth. Without causing infection, they cause the immune system to make antibodies against the disease and retain the appropriate memory cells, establishing active artificial immunity. The subsequent antibody-production profile on re-exposure to the antigen is normally the same as if immunity had been acquired by overcoming a natural infection.

Vaccines are made from: dead or attenuated (weakened) bacteria; purified polysaccharides from bacterial walls; inactivated viruses; toxoids (inactivated toxins); recombinant DNA produced by genetic engineering; or messenger RNA (mRNA). In communities where vaccine uptake reaches 85–90% of the relevant population, previously common, dangerous diseases can be reduced to rare occurrences — though this success can lead the public to become casual about the ongoing threat.

Common mistake: it is incorrect to say all vaccines are given by injection — some, such as the polio vaccine, are given orally (easier and more economical to administer), and some, such as certain flu vaccines given to children, are given by nasal spray.

Types of immunity

Active vs passive, natural vs artificial immunity Active, natural: the body responds to invasion by a pathogen, producing its own antibodies and memory cells.
Active, artificial: immunity develops after injection of killed or weakened antigens (a vaccine) or nucleic acids that code for antigens, stimulating the body to produce its own antibodies and memory cells.
Passive, natural: maternal antibodies pass to the foetus through the placenta (the recipient does not make their own antibodies).
Passive, artificial: ready-made antibodies are injected directly into the body.

Viral vaccines

Traditional viral vaccines were whole-virus vaccines of two types. Live attenuated vaccines use a weakened form of the virus that can still replicate but does not cause illness. Inactivated vaccines contain viruses whose genome has been destroyed, so they cannot infect cells or replicate, but can still generate an immune response. Both cause the body to briefly make antibodies and memory cells, establishing active artificial immunity. New vaccines may be needed if a virus is unstable and undergoes rapid genetic change.

Some COVID-19 vaccines are mRNA-based, injected into the bloodstream and carried into cells by lipid micro-vesicles (liposomes). The target antigen coded by the mRNA is either an intact spike protein or fragments of it — a glycoprotein that projects from the envelope of coronaviruses and facilitates entry of the virion into the host cell, and which the immune system identifies as foreign. Once inside the cell, the mRNA is translated to produce spike protein antigens, which are displayed on the cell surface. These are detected by the immune system, triggering a response involving killer T-cells (which destroy infected cells), antibody-producing B-cells, and helper T-cells that support antibody production; memory cells are also made, retaining the ability to produce specific antibodies quickly on future infection.

Antivirals

Most antiviral drugs resemble nucleosides and interfere with viral nucleic acid synthesis. Some antivirals developed against SARS-CoV-2 target the viral replication process, which is less tolerant to mutation than the spike protein. Azidothymidine (AZT) inhibits HIV reproduction by interfering with DNA synthesis by reverse transcriptase. Multi-drug “drug cocktails” — commonly a combination of two nucleoside mimics and a protease inhibitor (which interferes with an enzyme needed to assemble virus particles) — are currently the most effective treatments against HIV, and make it less likely that any new mutant HIV strain can overcome all the inhibitory effects at once.

The effectiveness of vaccination programmes — epidemiological data

Key term Epidemiology: the study of the occurrence, distribution and control of infectious diseases.

A study of community disease patterns can provide reliable evidence, but when community-wide vaccine administration is closely followed by a fall in a targeted disease, a causal relationship is not automatically proven, since many uncontrolled variables affect community health. TB remains a major worldwide public health problem, historically present in the earliest human communities and persisting where people live in crowded conditions; almost 14 million people globally have active TB, primarily affecting young adults in economically developing countries. In economically developed countries, TB’s recent rising incidence is largely among people with HIV/AIDS (who are immunocompromised) and among people arriving from other countries. The Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine, prepared from a strain of attenuated live bovine tuberculosis bacillus, is most often used to prevent the spread of TB among children.

Smallpox — an infectious disease eradicated by vaccination

Smallpox, a highly contagious disease caused by the DNA variola virus, once killed or disfigured all those who contracted it. British doctor Edward Jenner (1749–1823) developed a vaccine from a harmless but related virus, vaccinia, used in a “live” state that could be freeze-dried for transport and storage, making it easy to handle and stable in tropical climates. Smallpox has since been eradicated (the last case occurred in Somalia in 1977), the outcome of a determined WHO programme beginning in 1956 involving careful surveillance of cases, even in isolated communities and countries affected by war.

TOK: inductive and deductive reasoning in medicine

Medical science often uses deductive reasoning, moving from general principles to specific predictions — for example, double-blind, randomized, controlled trials used to test new drugs (such as remdesivir for hospitalized COVID-19 patients), which remain the scientific “gold standard”. Medical science also uses inductive reasoning, moving from specific observations to a generalized hypothesis. Edward Jenner’s discovery of the smallpox vaccine is a classic example: he observed that milkmaids exposed to cowpox seemed immune to smallpox, and reasoned that a vaccine (from the Latin vacca, “cow”) made from cowpox lesions could be protective. Smallpox itself, caused by two virus variants (Variola major and Variola minor), carried a 30% risk of death (higher in babies), with survivors often left scarred or blind; it was eradicated by 1980 through a global vaccination programme.

Herd Immunity and the Prevention of Epidemics

Key term Herd immunity: indirect protection from an infectious disease that occurs when the majority of a population is immune, either through vaccination or immunity developed through previous infection.

To control a contagious disease, it is not essential for the entire population to be immunized. The more people in a population who are immune, the lower the risk of a susceptible person becoming infected, since transmission is greatly impeded. Herd immunity depends on the interdependence of members of a population: if all but one person in a country is immune, the chance of that one susceptible person being exposed to the pathogen is almost zero. This means that people who cannot receive vaccines (e.g. due to a compromised immune system) can still be protected if the majority of the population is vaccinated.

Nature of science: peer review, pragmatism and public trust

Scientists publish research for peer review by other scientists, helping to ensure published research is accurate and valid; however, media often report on research while evaluation is still ongoing, and the public should be aware of this. Vaccines are rigorously tested, and the risk of side effects is minimal but not nil. Pragmatism — truth established through practical usefulness and majority consensus within a scientific community — differs from absolute certainty; the COVID-19 vaccine was fully tested and represented a pragmatic solution to the pandemic, and vaccine hesitancy was, in part, the result of some people not appreciating the difference between pragmatism and certainty. Science aims to generate theories that match observations and produce repeatable results, but is pragmatic rather than absolutely certain, and theories can be overturned by new data.

TOK: knowledge, context and acceptance

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted tensions between the scientific community and the wider public: vaccine reluctance often stemmed from a poor understanding of how vaccines work or of the statistics demonstrating their effectiveness, as well as from political, social, cultural or religious beliefs about the role of government in encouraging vaccination, and from the role of social media in spreading flawed information about vaccines.

Evaluation of Data Related to the COVID-19 Pandemic

Percentage change measures the change in a variable over time, whereas percentage difference calculates the difference between two averages.

increase = new number − original number
% change = (change ÷ original number) × 100

A negative result indicates a percentage decrease.

average = (first value + second value) ÷ 2
% difference = (difference ÷ average) × 100
Key terms Percentage change: the change in a variable over time; the percentage change in a value compared to its initial value.
Percentage difference: the difference between two values, divided by the average of the two values, shown as a percentage.

Worked example: vaccine efficacy

Vaccine efficacy refers to the effectiveness of a vaccine in reducing the incidence of a disease — a measure of the percentage change in the number of people with the disease following vaccination. In a trial of the mRNA vaccine mRNA-1273, 30,420 volunteers were randomly assigned to receive either the vaccine or a placebo (15,210 in each group). Symptomatic COVID-19 was confirmed in 185 participants in the placebo group and 11 participants in the vaccinated group.

Calculation % change = [(185 − 11) ÷ 185] × 100 = 94.1%
Vaccine efficacy was therefore 94.1%.

Worked example: comparing incidence between countries

If the daily incidence of COVID-19 in one country was 40 and in another it was 20, the percentage difference between the two countries can be calculated as follows.

Calculation Difference between the two values = 40 − 20 = 20.
Average value = (40 + 20) ÷ 2 = 30.
Percentage difference = (20 ÷ 30) × 100 = 66.7%.

Top tip! Make sure you can calculate both percentage difference and percentage change, and can identify which is appropriate for a given comparison — percentage change for a variable’s change over time (e.g. before and after vaccination), and percentage difference for comparing two separate values (e.g. two different countries at the same time).
