Generation of new cells by cell division

Cell theory states that all cells arise from pre-existing cells. Multicellular organisms begin life as a single cell (the zygote), which grows and divides repeatedly. Initially, stem cells can become any other cell type, but during embryonic development cell specialization (differentiation) occurs. In all living organisms, a parent (“mother”) cell divides to produce two daughter cells.

Key termsA parent cell is a cell that divides to form daughter cells by mitosis or meiosis. Daughter cells are the cells produced when a cell divides.

There are two types of nuclear division that lead to cell division: one produces genetically identical daughter cells (mitosis) and the other produces genetically varied cells (meiosis).

Cytokinesis

In cell division, both the nucleus and the cytoplasm divide. Division of the cytoplasm is called cytokinesis, and it follows the final stage of nuclear division. During division, organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts are distributed between the two daughter cells — these are the only organelles that can be made only by division of a pre-existing structure, so each daughter cell must receive at least one.

DefinitionCytokinesis: splitting of cytoplasm in a parent cell between daughter cells, following division of the nucleus.
Figure D2.1.1: Cytokinesis – division of the cytoplasmShows an animal cell in which the cytoplasm divides by in-tucking (invagination) of the plasma membrane at the equator of the cell, progressively pinching the cytoplasm into two halves until the two daughter cells separate.

In animal cells, division occurs by in-tucking of the plasma membrane at the equator of the cell, “pinching” the cytoplasm in half. A ring of actin, myosin and other proteins forms a contractile ring beneath the membrane that pinches the cell membrane together to split the cytoplasm.

In plant cells, this cannot happen because of the rigid cellulose cell wall. Instead, the Golgi apparatus forms vesicles containing new cell wall materials. These vesicles collect along the line of the equator of the spindle (a region called the cell plate), where they fuse to form the new plasma membranes and cell walls separating the two daughter cells.

Figure D2.1.2: Cytokinesis in a plant cellShows a plant cell after the nucleus has divided into two daughter nuclei. The endoplasmic reticulum secretes vesicles of wall-forming enzymes and the Golgi apparatus secretes vesicles of wall components; these vesicles collect at the midline (the cell plate, at the equator of the remains of the spindle) and fuse to form new cell membrane and cellulose cell wall layers, ultimately forming two separate daughter cells, each with its own cytoplasm.
Animal vs plant cytokinesisCytokinesis differs between animal and plant cells because plant cells have a cell wall and animal cells do not. Animal cells use a contractile ring of actin and myosin that pinches the cell membrane inward (a cleavage furrow). Plant cells cannot form a cleavage furrow, so instead they build a cell plate at the equator from Golgi-derived vesicles of membrane and wall material, which fuses to divide the old cell into two new ones.

Equal and unequal cytokinesis

When cells divide to form new cells within a body tissue, cytoplasmic division is usually equal, producing two daughter cells of the same size.

In some cases, however, division of the cytoplasm is unequal. In human egg production (oogenesis), one large egg (ovum) is formed along with several small cells that never go on to be fertilized. Oogenesis begins in the ovaries of the foetus before birth, but final development of the oocytes is only completed in adult life. An oocyte is an immature egg cell. When a primary oocyte divides, the cytoplasmic division that follows is unequal, forming a tiny polar body and a larger secondary oocyte. When the secondary oocyte divides, another polar body is formed. The polar bodies degenerate, and their cytoplasm and cellular contents are absorbed by the final ovum, so that one large egg forms rather than four equally small cells. The ovum needs a large cytoplasm to provide nutrients and energy for the growing embryo.

Figure D2.1.3: Unequal division of the cytoplasm in human oogenesisShows a primary oocyte dividing unequally to give a secondary oocyte (large) and a first polar body (small); the secondary oocyte then divides unequally again to give the ovum (large) and a second polar body (small). The polar bodies are shown as small cells that eventually degenerate.

Roles of mitosis and meiosis in eukaryotes

Nuclear division occurs by a very precise process, ensuring correct distribution of chromosomes between daughter cells. Nuclear division must happen before cell division, otherwise one daughter cell would end up with a nucleus and the other would not (an anucleate cell).

There are two types of nuclear division: mitosis and meiosis. Mitosis maintains the chromosome number and genome of cells, whereas meiosis halves the chromosome number and generates genetic diversity.

DefinitionsMitosis: nuclear division in which the daughter nuclei have the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Meiosis: nuclear division producing daughter cells (gametes) containing half the number of chromosomes of the parent cell. Diploid (2n): cells with nuclei containing two sets of chromosomes. Haploid (n): cells with nuclei containing one set of chromosomes.

In mitosis, daughter cells have the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell (typically two of each type — the diploid, 2n, state). Mitosis occurs when an organism grows, when old cells are replaced, and during asexual reproduction. In meiosis, daughter cells contain half the chromosome number of the parent cell (one of each type — the haploid, n, state). Meiosis occurs during sexual reproduction, normally when gametes are formed.

Figure D2.1.4: Mitosis and meiosis – the significant differencesShows a single cell about to divide (with nuclear membrane, nucleolus, and chromatin visible), with chromosomes first appearing as thin threads. One branch shows mitosis (replication): occurs when organisms grow, develop, and repair or replace tissues, forming two daughter cells with nuclei identical to the parent and diploid (2n). The other branch shows meiosis (reduction division): occurs during sexual reproduction (e.g. gamete formation in mammals and plants), forming four cells each with half the chromosome number, haploid (n).
Mitosis vs meiosis (overview)Mitosis produces two genetically identical, diploid daughter cells and occurs in growth, replacement and asexual reproduction. Meiosis produces four genetically varied, haploid daughter cells (gametes) and occurs in sexual reproduction. Mitosis and meiosis only occur in eukaryotes — prokaryotes divide by binary fission instead.

DNA replication as a prerequisite for mitosis and meiosis

DNA replication occurs during interphase, a stage of the cell cycle before nuclear division. Replication produces double-stranded chromosomes: each strand is called a chromatid, and the two sister chromatids are held together by a centromere, a specialized DNA sequence visible as the constricted region of the chromosome. During nuclear division, sister chromatids separate and move into different cells.

DefinitionsInterphase: the period between nuclear divisions when the nucleus controls and directs cell activity and replicates its chromosomes. Chromatid: one of two copies of a chromosome after replication, joined at a centromere. Centromere: constriction of the chromosome that attaches to spindle fibres during nuclear division.
Figure D2.1.5: Changes in chromosomes during interphase and mitosisShows a chromosome consisting of a single chromatid before interphase. During interphase, DNA replication produces a duplicated chromosome made of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. During mitosis, the centromere divides and the sister chromatids separate to become individual daughter chromosomes, one moving into each new nucleus.
Terminology checkRefer to the two parts of a replicated chromosome as sister chromatids while they remain attached at a centromere. Once separated into different cells during nuclear division, they should be called distinct chromosomes.

Condensation and movement of chromosomes

The total length of DNA in all 46 human chromosomes is over 2 m. A typical 5 μm chromosome contains DNA that would be about 50 mm long stretched out — roughly 50 000 μm of DNA packed into 5 μm of chromosome. Most chromosome protein has a support and packaging role, although some proteins are enzymes involved in DNA copying and repair.

DNA in the nucleus is packaged with proteins into a complex called chromatin. The coiled DNA double helix of each chromosome is looped around beads of histone protein, called nucleosomes. Histones are basic (positively charged) proteins rich in amino acids with basic side groups (–NH2), such as lysine and arginine, which allows them to bind the negatively charged DNA. The resulting “beads on a thread” structure (the chromatin fibre) is itself coiled, and these coils loop around a non-histone “scaffold” protein fibre. This whole structure is folded again (supercoiled) into the highly condensed metaphase chromosome.

DefinitionsHistone: protein rich in arginine and lysine that forms the scaffolding of chromosomes and is used in chromosome condensation to form nucleosomes. Nucleosome: the basic unit of eukaryotic chromosome structure, consisting of eight histone molecules wrapped by about 220 base pairs of DNA. Supercoiling: further twisting of the DNA double helix about itself within nucleosomes, forming a tightly coiled structure.
Figure D2.1.6: Histone proteins forming tightly packed nucleosomesShows the DNA double helix looped around a core of eight tightly packed histone molecules, forming a bead-like nucleosome; a chain of these nucleosomes forms tightly packed chromatin. Histone tails protrude from the nucleosome core; acetyl groups (–COCH3) can attach to lysine residues in the histone tails. Acetylation loosens the packing of nucleosomes, giving transcription enzymes access to the DNA, whereas tightly packed (non-acetylated) nucleosomes exclude transcription enzymes.
Figure D2.1.7: The packaging of DNA in the chromosomesAn electron micrograph/diagram showing the hierarchy of DNA packaging: the double helix wraps around histones to form nucleosomes (“beads on a string”), which coil into the chromatin fibre, which loops around a non-histone protein scaffold, which is itself supercoiled to form the highly condensed metaphase chromosome.

Chromosomes are moved during cell division using microtubules and microtubule motor proteins. Microtubules are cytoskeletal fibres that lengthen and shorten by polymerization and depolymerization of the protein tubulin. Motor proteins carry chromosomes along the microtubules to the middle of the cell in readiness for nuclear division.

Phases of mitosis

When cell division occurs, the nucleus divides first. In mitosis, the chromosomes (already present as sister chromatids formed during interphase) are separated and precisely distributed to two daughter nuclei, so that each daughter cell is genetically identical to the parent cell. Mitosis is described here as four phases for convenience, but it is actually a continuous process with no breaks between the phases.

Prophase

Chromosomes become visible as long thin threads, then progressively shorten and thicken by supercoiling. Only near the end of prophase can the two sister chromatids (joined at the centromere) be distinguished. The nucleolus gradually disappears and the nuclear membrane breaks down.

Metaphase

Centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell. Microtubules in the cytoplasm form a spindle radiating out from the centrioles. Microtubules attach to the centromeres of each pair of chromatids, and the chromosomes become arranged at the equator of the spindle. (In plant cells, an identical spindle forms, but without centrioles.)

Anaphase

Centromeres divide, spindle fibres shorten, and the sister chromatids are pulled by their centromeres to opposite poles. Once separated, each chromatid is now referred to as an individual chromosome.

Telophase

A nuclear membrane reforms around each group of chromosomes at opposite ends of the cell. The chromosomes decondense (uncoil), becoming chromatin again, and the nucleolus reforms in each new nucleus. Cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm) follows.

Figure D2.1.8: Mitosis in an animal cellA sequence of six diagrams for a cell containing a single pair of homologous chromosomes (for simplicity): (1) Interphase — nuclear membrane, nucleolus and chromatin visible; centrioles duplicate. (2) Prophase — chromosomes condense and become visible (each already divided into two chromatids joined by a centromere, though this is not immediately visible); the nucleolus disappears; the nuclear membrane breaks down; a spindle of microtubule fibres forms, radiating from a pair of centrioles at each pole. (3) Metaphase — chromatids attach to the spindle at the equatorial plate (3D view shows spindle fibres converging on centrioles at each pole). (4) Anaphase — centromeres divide and chromatids are pulled apart by the microtubules towards opposite poles. (5) Telophase — chromosomes uncoil, the spindle disappears, and the nucleolus and nuclear membrane reappear at each pole. (6) Cytokinesis — the cytoplasm divides, forming two separate daughter cells.
DefinitionsProphase: first stage of nuclear division where chromosomes condense. Metaphase: stage where chromosomes become arranged at the equator of the spindle. Anaphase: stage where chromosomes move to opposite poles. Telophase: stage when the nuclear membrane reforms around daughter nuclear material.

Identification of phases of mitosis

Actively dividing plant tissue, such as root tips, contains many cells undergoing mitosis. This tissue can be isolated, stained with acetic orcein stain, squashed, and examined under a high-power microscope. Interphase nuclei stain red-purple with almost colourless cytoplasm, while the condensed chromosomes of dividing cells are clearly visible, allowing the proportion of cells at each stage of mitosis to be assessed.

Meiosis as a reduction division

Meiosis involves two divisions of the nucleus: meiosis I and meiosis II. The parent cell is diploid, and meiosis produces daughter cells that are haploid. As in mitosis, chromosomes replicate into chromatids during interphase before meiosis begins. Early in meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair up; by the end of meiosis I, homologous chromosomes have separated, but the chromatids that make up each chromosome do not separate until meiosis II. There is no further interphase (and so no further DNA replication) between meiosis I and meiosis II — meiosis consists of two nuclear divisions but only one round of chromosome replication.

Stages of meiosis I

1. Chromosome replication occurs during the preceding interphase, forming sister chromatids.

2. As meiosis begins, the diploid parent cell’s homologous chromosomes (each already consisting of two sister chromatids) pair up closely along their length. Each pair is called a bivalent. The members of the bivalent continue to shorten (condense).

3. During condensation, chromatids frequently break; when non-sister chromatids from homologous chromosomes break and rejoin at exactly corresponding points, a cross-shaped structure called a chiasma forms. This event, called crossing over, exchanges lengths of genes between chromatids and generates genetic variation.

4. A spindle forms. Bivalents attach by their centromeres to spindle fibres at the equatorial plate.

5. Spindle fibres pull the homologous chromosomes of each bivalent apart to opposite poles — the chromosome number is halved — but the sister chromatids of each chromosome remain joined at their centromere.

Stages of meiosis II

Meiosis I ends with two cells, each containing a single set of chromosomes (still made of two sister chromatids). These cells do not enter interphase, but continue smoothly into meiosis II, which occurs at right angles to meiosis I and proceeds exactly like mitosis: centromeres divide and individual sister chromatids move to opposite poles. Following cytokinesis, four haploid cells are produced, each with half the chromosome number of the original parent cell.

Figure D2.1.10: What happens to chromosomes during meiosis?Shows a cell with a single pair of homologous chromosomes (2n) through the stages: during interphase, chromosomes replicate (copying occurs); during meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair up, crossing over occurs (visible as chromosomes separate), and homologous chromosomes separate into different cells, halving the chromosome number (n); during meiosis II, the sister chromatids of each chromosome separate and move into daughter cells; cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm, producing four haploid (n) cells as the final product of meiosis.
DefinitionsBivalent: a pair of duplicated homologous chromosomes, held together by chiasmata during meiosis. Chiasma (plural chiasmata): site of crossing over between homologous chromosomes during meiosis. Crossing over: exchange of genetic material between two homologous chromosomes during meiosis.
Accurate terminology for reduction divisionUse precise vocabulary when describing meiosis: “homologous”, “maternal”, “paternal”, “diploid (2n)”, “haploid (n)” and “random” help describe chromosome movement and how it generates genetic variety during the reduction division.

Germ-line cells, found in the gonads (testes and ovaries), undergo meiosis as part of the life cycle of every sexually reproducing organism. Halving the chromosome number in gametes is essential because fertilization doubles the number again, restoring the diploid state in the zygote. Without this reduction division, chromosome number would double every generation.

Down syndrome and non-disjunction

Very rarely, errors occur in the precisely controlled movement of chromosomes during meiosis. Chromosomes that should separate and move to opposite poles instead move together to the same pole. This malfunction is called non-disjunction, and it results in some gametes with more than, and some with less than, the haploid number of chromosomes.

DefinitionNon-disjunction: the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during meiosis. Non-disjunction can result in either an additional or a missing chromosome — not only an extra one.

People with Down syndrome have an extra chromosome 21, giving a total of 47 chromosomes (a condition called trisomy, where an extra copy of a chromosome is present). Symptoms of Down syndrome are variable but, when severe, include congenital heart and eye defects. The incidence of chromosomal abnormalities increases significantly with maternal age, so pregnant people over 40 are advised to have the foetus’s chromosomes screened. Non-disjunction resulting in trisomy also occurs (less commonly) in chromosomes 18 and 13, and occasionally other pairs.

In meiosis II, the two chromatids of that chromosome fail to separate and both go into the daughter cell that forms the secondary oocyte, producing gametes with an extra chromosome 21 (as well as gametes short of a chromosome). If a gamete with the extra chromosome takes part in fertilization, the zygote has a chromosome mutation (three copies of chromosome 21). Meiosis as a source of variation

The four haploid cells produced by meiosis are genetically different from each other for two main reasons: crossing over and random orientation (independent assortment) of chromosomes.

Crossing over

As chromosomes thicken early in meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair up along their length, forming a bivalent. As the chromosomes continue to shorten and thicken, the individual sister chromatids of each homologue become visible. During this coiling and shortening, breaks frequently occur in non-sister chromatids at corresponding points along their length. The broken ends rejoin almost immediately but, where the repair is between non-sister chromatids, segments of the chromatids are swapped — hence “crossing over”. The point of attachment after crossing over is called a chiasma, which stabilizes the bivalent. Virtually every pair of homologous chromosomes forms at least one chiasma, and having two or more chiasmata in the same bivalent is common. Chiasmata increase genetic variability because they exchange DNA between maternal and paternal chromosomes, producing new combinations of alleles on the chromosomes of the haploid cells eventually formed.

Figure D2.1.12: Formation of chiasmataA five-step sequence: (1) homologous chromosomes begin pairing to form a bivalent as they shorten and thicken by coiling; (2) breakages occur in parallel non-sister chromatids at identical points; (3) rejoining of non-sister chromatids forms chiasmata; (4) the positions of chiasmata become visible later, as the tight pairing of homologous chromosomes ends; (5) when homologous chromosomes move apart in anaphase I, the effect of crossing over becomes fully apparent, with chromatids showing new combinations of maternal and paternal segments.

Random orientation (independent assortment)

The way bivalents line up at the equator of the spindle in meiosis I is entirely random: which chromosome of a given homologous pair goes to which pole is independent of the behaviour of chromosomes in other pairs.

Figure D2.1.13: Genetic variation due to random orientationShows the nucleus of a diploid parent cell with two pairs of homologous chromosomes (four bivalents, carrying allele combinations such as A/a and B/b). In meiosis I, the bivalents move to the equator of the spindle and can line up in either of two alternative random orientations. Depending on orientation, homologous chromosomes separate to the nearest pole in one of two possible ways, so that meiosis II ultimately produces one of two alternative sets of four haploid gametes — in one orientation the gametes are AB and ab, in the other they are Ab and aB. The more bivalents present, the greater the possible variation: with 23 pairs of chromosomes in humans, there are over 8 million (2^23) possible combinations from random orientation alone.
DefinitionRandom orientation: the way chromosomes line up at the equator of the cell during meiosis, with each chromosome of a pair behaving independently of chromosomes in other pairs, so that each gamete gets a different combination of maternal and paternal chromosomes.

Meiosis II also contributes to variation: crossing over in meiosis I has already made sister chromatids non-identical, and during meiosis II these chromatids separate and are randomly distributed to the gametes, so each gamete can have a unique combination of genetic material. Finally, fertilization — the fusion of gametes from two different parents — adds further genetic variation.

Higher Level

Cell proliferation for growth, cell replacement and tissue repair

Both animals and plants grow from a single cell (the zygote) by repeated cell divisions to form an embryo. Proliferation (rapid increase in cell number) then occurs in animal early-stage embryos and in plant meristem tissue.

DefinitionsProliferation: increase in the number of cells as a result of cell growth and cell division. Meristem: a group of cells in plants that retains the ability to divide by mitosis and form new cells and tissues throughout the life of the plant.

Plant meristems

Once a plant has passed the early embryo stage, all further growth occurs at restricted points called meristems. Meristem cells are small, with thin cellulose walls and dense cytoplasm, and mostly lack the large vacuoles typical of mature plant cells. Meristems occur either at terminal (apical) growing points, or laterally.

Apical meristems occur at the tips of stems and roots and are responsible for primary growth: cells produced by division first enlarge rapidly, and this cell enlargement phase is followed by cell differentiation. Lateral meristems form from cambium cells (undifferentiated, indefinitely dividing meristematic tissue) located between the phloem (outer) and xylem (inner) in vascular bundles. Growth of the lateral meristem causes secondary growth — an increase in the girth (and strength) of the stem.

Early-stage animal embryos

Following fertilization, the zygote divides rapidly by mitosis to form a ball of cells. After about six days, a blastocyst has formed, from which the placenta and the main body of the organism develop. At this stage all cells are undifferentiated, with the potential to become any type of cell or tissue.

Figure D2.1.15: Cell proliferation in the early-stage embryo of an animalShows the sequence of rapid mitotic divisions of the zygote, forming first two, then four, then increasing numbers of cells, producing a solid ball of undifferentiated cells that ultimately hollows out to form a blastocyst.

Cell proliferation during routine cell replacement and wound healing

Cells in the body are constantly being worn out and replaced — the average adult human loses about 500 million skin cells per day, and red blood cells at a rate of 2 million per second. Cell replacement is routine for almost all cell types, except those which cannot undergo mitosis (such as mature nerve cells). Stem cells in the basal layer of the skin epidermis remain undifferentiated and continue dividing throughout life; their daughter cells differentiate and move up, leaving the basal layer, while the self-renewing basal cells maintain the process.

Wound healing also involves cell proliferation. After blood clotting, inflammation increases blood flow to the wound, allowing macrophages (white blood cells) and fibroblasts to travel rapidly to the damaged area. Macrophages remove infection, while fibroblasts produce proteins that help close the wound.

Higher Level

Phases of the cell cycle

The cycle of growth and division of cells is the cell cycle, which achieves cell proliferation. It has three main stages: interphase (which includes G1, S and G2 phases), division of the nucleus (mitosis), and division of the cytoplasm and whole cell (cytokinesis).

DefinitionCell cycle: repeating process of an orderly sequence of events where cells arise by division of existing cells, grow, and then divide.
Figure D2.1.17: The stages of the cell cycleShows the cell cycle as a circle divided into G1, S, G2 (together forming interphase), M (mitosis, itself subdivided into prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase) and C (cytokinesis), which then repeats. A graph beneath plots cell volume and quantity of DNA (both in arbitrary units) against time through the cycle: cell volume rises steadily throughout interphase and drops sharply at cytokinesis, while the quantity of DNA stays constant through G1, doubles during S phase, stays constant through G2 and M, then halves at cytokinesis. Labels describe each phase: G1 (first growth phase) — cytoplasm active, new organelles formed, intense biochemical activity; S (synthesis) — chromosomes copied (replicated) into sister chromatids; G2 (second growth phase) — further growth, then preparation for mitosis; M — mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase); C — cytokinesis, division of cytoplasm, two cells formed.

The steps of interphase

During G1 (first growth phase), new organelles are synthesized in the cytoplasm; this is a time of intense biochemical activity and accumulation of energy stores. During S phase (synthesis), each chromosome makes a copy of itself (replicates), forming two identical sister chromatids that remain attached until mitosis. During G2 (second growth phase), biochemical activity and cytoplasmic growth continue, in preparation for mitosis.

Higher Level

Cell growth during interphase

Interphase is always the longest part of the cell cycle, but its length is extremely variable. In fast-growing tissue (a developing human embryo, or the growing point of a young stem) interphase may last about 24 hours or less. In mature, infrequently dividing cells (e.g. liver, bone, kidney and lung cells) it lasts much longer. Some differentiated cells, such as brain cells, rarely or never divide again, remaining permanently in interphase.

Although the nucleus at interphase appears “resting” under a light microscope, it is metabolically very active: proteins and DNA are synthesized, and the numbers of mitochondria and chloroplasts increase by growth and division of these organelles. During interphase, chromosomes are actively involved in protein synthesis — mRNA copies of particular genes are made for use by ribosomes in the cytoplasm, where proteins are assembled in the sequence dictated by the mRNA. The compact chromosomes visible during mitosis become dispersed during interphase and are then called chromatin. Within the chromatin, one or more dark-staining nucleoli (singular nucleolus) are visible; these consist of protein and RNA and are the site of ribosome synthesis, after which the ribosomes migrate into the cytoplasm.

Higher Level

Control of the cell cycle using cyclins

The cell cycle is regulated by a molecular control system with key checkpoints — stop points that must be overridden by a “go-ahead” signal before the cycle can proceed. Three checkpoints are recognized: at G1, at G2, and in M. For example, at the G2 checkpoint, receiving the go-ahead signal allows the cell to proceed to M then C.

The molecular control signals are proteins called kinases and cyclins. Kinases are enzymes that activate or inactivate other proteins; they are present in the cytoplasm at all times, sometimes in an inactive state. Kinases are activated by specific cyclins, so they are called cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). Cyclin concentrations in the cytoplasm rise and fall constantly; as cyclin concentration increases, cyclin combines with CDK to form a complex called mitosis-promoting factor (MPF). As MPF accumulates, it triggers chromosome condensation, fragmentation of the nuclear membrane, and spindle formation — mitosis is switched on. By anaphase, destruction of cyclins begins (though CDKs persist in the cytoplasm). There is a threshold level of a specific cyclin required to pass each checkpoint.

DefinitionCyclin: regulatory protein whose concentration rises and falls at specific times during the eukaryotic cell cycle.

Synthesis of fresh cyclin begins in late S-phase; accumulated cyclin molecules combine with recycled CDK to produce MPF (mitosis-promoting factor); MPF triggers mitosis, with MPF concentration peaking in metaphase; cyclin is then broken down (degraded), leaving free CDK to be recycled for the next cycle.
Higher Level

Consequences of mutations in genes that control the cell cycle

When mutations occur in genes that control the cell cycle, normal cell-cycle controls are lost and cancer can result. Two types of genes are involved in initiating cancer when they mutate.

Proto-oncogenes vs tumour-suppressor genes

Proto-oncogenes encode proteins that stimulate normal cell division. Mutation converts a proto-oncogene into an oncogene, increasing the amount of the protein product or producing a permanently activated protein, causing uncontrolled cell division. Example: the Ras gene codes for a G protein that relays a growth-factor signal to a kinase cascade leading to normal cell division; the Ras oncogene codes for a permanently activated Ras protein that triggers the cascade even without growth factor. This mutation is dominant — only one allele needs to mutate for an effect, because the mutant masks the normal allele.

Tumour-suppressor genes encode proteins that inhibit cell division or promote apoptosis (controlled cell death) when damaged DNA is detected. Mutation causes loss, reduction, or permanent deactivation of the protein product, again resulting in uncontrolled division. Example: the p53 gene codes for a transcription factor that triggers cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis when DNA damage is detected; mutated p53 produces no functional protein, so damaged cells continue dividing. This mutation is recessive — both alleles must be mutated to have an effect, since one normal dominant allele still encodes functional protein.

DefinitionsProto-oncogene: gene that codes for proteins that stimulate the cell cycle and promote cell growth and proliferation. Oncogene: a gene that, when activated, can potentially make a cell cancerous — typically a mutant form of a proto-oncogene. Tumour-suppressor gene: a gene that in normal tissue inhibits cancerous behaviour; loss or inactivation of both copies can cause a cell to behave as a cancer cell.
Remembering the distinctionWhen a proto-oncogene mutates, it becomes a cancer-causing oncogene (gain of function). Tumour-suppressor genes normally prevent cancer; when they mutate (loss of function), they can lead to tumour formation.
Higher Level

Differences between tumours in rates of cell division and growth

Cancer is not a single disease but a group of diseases affecting different tissues, characterized by uncontrolled cell division by mitosis. In more economically developed countries, about one in three people will suffer from cancer at some point in their life and about one in four will die from it; common cancers include lung cancer in men and breast cancer in women, though rates vary greatly worldwide.

In cancer, the rate of cell multiplication greatly exceeds the rate of cell death, forming an irregular mass of cells called a tumour. Benign tumours grow slowly and do not spread to other parts of the body (non-cancerous). Malignant tumours grow rapidly, destroy nearby normal tissue, and spread throughout the body (cancerous).

Figure D2.1.19: Steps in the development of a malignant tumourShows normally dividing cells subjected to prolonged exposure to carcinogens (tobacco smoke, ultraviolet light, asbestos, X-rays). A carcinogen causes a mutation in a cell, which then undergoes repeated rapid mitosis because the cell cycle is no longer inhibited; the resulting abnormal cells do not respond to signals from other cells and are not removed by the immune system. A benign tumour absorbs nutrients and enlarges, possibly compressing surrounding tissue, but does not spread and can be surgically removed. A malignant tumour, however, consists of cells that secrete signals triggering growth of new blood and lymph vessels to supply the tumour; the tumour cells lose their attachments to each other, and malignant cells (with unusual chromosomes and disabled metabolism) may be carried via blood vessels or lymph vessels around the body, setting up secondary growths that impair or obstruct the function of invaded organs. This spread of cancer cells to new locations is called metastasis.
DefinitionsBenign tumour: grows slowly and does not spread to other parts of the body. Malignant tumour: grows rapidly, invades and destroys nearby tissue, and spreads throughout the body. Primary tumour: cancer growing at the site where the abnormal growth first occurred. Secondary tumour: formed when cancerous cells detach from the primary tumour, penetrate blood or lymph vessel walls, and circulate to form tumours elsewhere. Metastasis: movement of cells from a primary tumour to set up secondary tumours.
Tumour vs cancer“Tumour” is not equivalent to “cancer”. Tumours are growths caused by uncontrolled cell division, but benign tumours are not cancerous. Only malignant tumours are cancerous.

Calculating the mitotic index

The mitotic index measures the proportion of cells in a sample that are undergoing mitosis compared to the total number of cells.

Mitotic index = (number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells) × 100

Worked example: in a micrograph with 95 visible cells, of which 10 are undergoing mitosis, the mitotic index = (10 ÷ 95) × 100 = 0.105 × 100 = 10.5%. For accuracy, the mean of three or more samples of 100 cells should be used. The mitotic index is used to distinguish benign from malignant tumours (benign tumours have a lower mitotic index than malignant tumours); a high mitotic index indicates a rapidly dividing cell mass, a possible sign of tumour formation. It can also be used to monitor response to chemotherapy — a falling mitotic index indicates successful treatment.
