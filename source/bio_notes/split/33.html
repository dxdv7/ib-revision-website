Gene expression and its effect on the phenotype

Gene expression is the mechanism by which information in genes has effects on the phenotype. Information in the genetic code is transcribed into mRNA, which is translated into proteins at ribosomes; the proteins produced by an organism determine its phenotype. The most common stages of gene expression are transcription, translation, and the function of the protein product (such as an enzyme).

DefinitionPhenotype: observable traits of an organism resulting from genotype and environmental factors. Do not confuse genotype (the genetic makeup of an individual) with phenotype (the outward effect of the genotype on the body).

Gene expression underlies many processes across the course: transcription is the key stage at which a gene’s expression can be switched on or off; non-coding DNA sequences include regulators of gene expression; different patterns of gene expression underlie cell differentiation, often triggered by environmental changes; biochemical gradients affect gene expression in early embryos; intracellular receptors alter gene expression when a signalling chemical binds and the activated receptor binds specific DNA sequences; and auxin regulates gene expression mechanisms controlling plant growth and development.

Regulation of transcription by DNA-binding proteins

Transcription factors are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences to control transcription. Each transcription factor has a DNA-binding site enabling it to bind specific regulatory sequences called enhancer or promoter sequences. Enhancer sequences, when bound by transcription factors, increase transcription of an associated gene; they can be thousands of base pairs away from the gene, usually upstream (before) but sometimes downstream (after) of it. Regulation of transcription is the most common form of gene control, allowing unique expression of each gene in different cell types and at different developmental stages.

Promoter regions occur on DNA just before the start of a gene’s coding sequence. Only once a transcription complex (RNA polymerase enzyme plus transcription factors) has assembled at the promoter can transcription of the gene’s template strand begin. The rate of transcription can be increased or decreased by transcription factors binding to the enhancer site for a gene; when activator proteins bind the enhancer, a new complex forms and contacts the polymerase–transcription factor complex at the promoter, increasing the rate of gene expression.

DefinitionsTranscription factor: a protein that binds to specific DNA sequences to control the transcription of mRNA. Enhancer: regulatory DNA sequences which increase the rate of transcription when activator proteins bind to them.

Control of mRNA degradation as a means of regulating translation

After transcription, mRNA is examined, modified and transported to ribosomes for translation. mRNA is eventually broken down (degraded) by nucleases; in human cells it may persist from minutes to days. The 3′ poly-A tail must be degraded (shortened) before the rest of the mRNA molecule is broken down, and poly-A tail shortening is one of the key steps that initiates degradation once the mRNA is no longer needed for translation. Degradation of mRNA ensures that proteins are synthesized only when needed and that mRNA is removed once its job is done.

Epigenesis

Epigenesis is the development of patterns of differentiation in the cells of a multicellular organism, from an undifferentiated zygote, determined by the genome and by gene expression. Environmental factors can change the activity of genes, and these changes can be passed on to offspring, affecting their development.

DefinitionsEpigenesis: the development of patterns of differentiation in the cells of a multicellular organism. Epigenetics: the study of heritable changes in gene activity that are not caused by changes in DNA base sequences (epi = outside); mechanisms include DNA methylation and histone modification.

Epigenetic modifications change the way cells “read” genes rather than altering the DNA base sequence itself (which would be a mutation). Because DNA base sequences are unchanged by epigenetic modification, the phenotype — but not the genotype — can be altered.

Genome, transcriptome and proteome

The proteome of an organism is all the proteins expressed within a cell, tissue or organism at a given time. The genome (the whole of an organism’s genetic information) is the same in every cell of a multicellular organism, because all cells derive from one original cell by mitotic cell division. The genome instructs the expression of proteins, but because individual tissues have specific structures related to their function, different proteins are expressed in different tissue types (e.g. digestive enzymes in the gut, collagen in bone). No cell expresses all of its genes at once; gene expression determines which genes are switched on or off at a given time, since expressing genes costs energy and it would be wasteful to express unneeded genes.

Because the proteome depends on which genes are expressed, it varies with the function, location and environmental conditions of the cell (via cell differentiation), even though the genome remains the same. The genome is thus stable, while the proteome is dynamic.

The transcriptome is the range of mRNA transcripts produced in a specific cell or tissue type at a particular time, depending on differential gene expression; the transcriptome determines which proteins are synthesized, which in turn depends on which parts of the genome are activated.

Genome vs transcriptome vs proteomeThe genome is stable and identical in every cell of an organism (with the exception that monozygotic twins share an identical genome to each other). The transcriptome actively changes, varying with developmental stage and environmental conditions, because it reflects which genes are being transcribed at a given time. The proteome is even more variable and reflects the final protein output of the cell, shaped by transcription, alternative splicing, mRNA editing and post-translational modification — so every individual (other than monozygotic twins) has a unique proteome.

Methylation of the promoter and histones in nucleosomes

Nucleosomes are stable, but not static, protein–DNA complexes; they can inhibit or facilitate transcription. Methylation is the reversible addition of a methyl group (–CH3) within chromatin, usually to the DNA molecule itself at the promoter region (though sometimes to histone tails). These chemical additions are called epigenetic tags. Enzymes add methyl groups to the base cytosine while the DNA is wrapped around histone proteins. Extensive methylation usually inactivates a gene, repressing transcription of the gene downstream; removing methyl groups can switch the gene back on. Methylation of amino acids in histones can either enable or disable the recruitment of regulatory proteins to the chromatin, activating or repressing transcription.

The diagram indicates other sites along the DNA double helix where methylation has occurred, illustrating how methylation marks are distributed at multiple positions in the chromatin.
DefinitionsMethylation: the reversible addition of a methyl group (–CH3) within chromatin. Epigenetic tag: chemical tags (such as methyl or acetyl groups) added directly to DNA or to histone proteins to regulate gene expression, blocking or allowing access to a gene’s “on” switch.

Once a gene has been methylated, this state can persist from one cell division to the next. External conditions adverse to a cell or organism can leave a lasting mark on DNA through extensive methylation, leading to genes being switched off.

Epigenetic inheritance

Epigenetic inheritance is the acquisition of characteristics gained by a parent during their lifetime and passed on to their offspring, brought about by modification of chromatin via epigenetic tags such as DNA methylation or histone modification. Phenotypic changes in a cell or organism can be passed to daughter cells or offspring without any change to the DNA nucleotide sequence. This can occur if epigenetic tags remain in place during mitosis or meiosis; if the tags are removed during cell division, epigenetic inheritance does not take place.

DefinitionEpigenetic inheritance: an inheritance pattern in which a modification to chromatin alters gene expression in an organism; a parent’s experiences, in the form of epigenetic tags, can be passed down to offspring.

Methylation can last a lifetime and can be transmitted to offspring, and sometimes to further generations. A well-documented example: in 1944, wartime hostilities deprived communities in the western Netherlands of food for over six months (the Dutch famine). Babies born to starving pregnant mothers were underweight at birth but, despite being fed normally after the war, grew up to be heavier than average as adults with higher rates of obesity — it seems the parents’ starvation was imprinted onto their children’s DNA, causing the children to develop greater body mass as a survival strategy. This challenges the older view (from Mendel through to Watson and Crick) that genes are unchangeable by external factors, with the environment’s only influence being on natural selection of alleles rather than on gene performance itself; poor diet (for example) now appears able to interfere with gene performance in succeeding generations via inherited epigenetic markers.

Environmental effects on gene expression

Many external factors can affect gene expression, including diet, oxygen levels, humidity, light cycles, drugs, temperature, and exposure to mutagens. One well-studied example is air pollution.

Traffic-related air pollution includes particulate matter (small particles from diesel vehicles that can enter lung tissue and cause damage), ozone (O3), carbon monoxide (CO) and nitrogen oxides (NOx). Air pollution contributes to cardiovascular disease and lung conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Research indicates that air pollution exposure can modify DNA and histone methylation patterns, which in turn can affect inflammation and disease development. Air pollution exposure is correlated with long-term negative respiratory outcomes, including interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) — diseases causing inflammation and scarring/thickening of lung tissues (bronchioles, alveoli, and surrounding capillaries), reducing the rate of oxygen diffusion into the blood and, in the bronchioles, causing asthma. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), a common form of ILD triggered by inhaling organic and inorganic substances (including air pollutants), is influenced by such epigenetic modifications. Exercise and B vitamins have been suggested as ways to reduce the impact of air pollution on methylation patterns and health.

Removal of epigenetic tags from ovum and sperm, and imprinting

Many organisms retain and pass on epigenetic marks from environmental exposure via gametes to the next generation. In humans and other mammals, however, egg and sperm cells undergo extensive reprogramming during development, which removes (“silences”) nearly all epigenetic marks. This reprogramming likely evolved to prevent environmentally caused methylation changes from being transmitted across generations. Any tag that is not removed may affect the development and function of the next generation.

For a small minority of genes (about 1%), epigenetic tags survive this reprogramming and pass unchanged from parent to offspring, through a process called imprinting. Imprinting involves adding epigenetic tags to DNA in sperm and eggs during gametogenesis (gamete formation by meiosis), switching “on” some parts of the DNA and “off” other parts. For most genes, two working copies are inherited (one from each parent), but for imprinted genes only one working copy is inherited, because differential silencing means either the maternal copy or the paternal copy is epigenetically silenced, depending on the gene. In sperm development, maternal tags are silenced; in egg development, paternal tags are silenced.

DefinitionImprinting: process by which only one copy of a gene in an individual (from either mother or father) is expressed, while the other copy is suppressed by epigenetic addition of chemical tags to DNA during gamete formation.

Imprinting in tigons and ligers

Tigons and ligers are lion–tiger hybrids. A male tiger crossed with a female lion produces a tigon, about the same size as or smaller than either parent. A female tiger crossed with a male lion produces a liger, which grows to a much greater size than either parent species.

Figure D2.2.3: Different patterns of epigenetic inheritance in lions and tigers explains different phenotypes in ligers and tigonsShows a female lion (120–180 kg) crossed with a male tiger (160–230 kg) producing a tigon that is the same size as or smaller than its parents, because the maternal (lion) growth gene is switched off (an anti-growth imprint) while the paternal (tiger) growth gene is switched on — but with no unopposed paternal growth signal, growth stays limited. In the reciprocal cross, a female tiger (140–180 kg, with no anti-growth imprinting defence) crossed with a male lion (170–230 kg, contributing a growth-promoting paternal imprint) produces a liger exceeding 400 kg, because the paternal growth gene is switched on and unopposed by any maternal anti-growth imprint.

This difference is explained by differing patterns of genetic imprinting between the two species, thought to have evolved because of their different mating systems. Female lions may mate with multiple males, so a male lion’s genes benefit from encouraging extra growth in his own cubs relative to potential half-siblings in the litter; in response, female lions have evolved anti-growth imprinted genes, since a lioness is equally related to all her cubs and benefits from distributing resources equally to maximize the number that survive. Tigers, in contrast, are typically monogamous per litter (a female tiger mates with one male, who is equally related to all her cubs), so male tigers have not evolved genes that encourage extra growth, and female tigers have not evolved anti-growth imprinting defences. When a female tiger (lacking anti-growth imprinting) is crossed with a male lion (which does contribute growth-encouraging genes), the offspring — the liger — grows unusually large.

Monozygotic twin studies

No two members of a family are genetically identical, except identical twins. Differences between individuals can be due to genetic factors (e.g. blood group), environmental factors (e.g. a suntan), or a combination of both (e.g. height and weight). The “nature or nurture” question asks whether phenotypic variation is due to genetic code (“nature”) or environment (“nurture”). Studies of monozygotic twins are designed to measure the relative contribution of genetics versus environment for a given trait.

DefinitionsMonozygotic twins: identical twins resulting from the fertilization of a single egg by a single sperm, with the fertilized egg then splitting in two; they share the same genome and are always the same sex. Monozygotic: derived from a single ovum, and so genetically identical.
Figure D2.2.4: Comparing monozygotic (identical) and dizygotic (non-identical) twinsShows monozygotic twins developing from a single zygote that splits into two genetically identical embryos sharing one placenta, versus dizygotic twins developing from two separately fertilized eggs, each genetically distinct, each with its own separate placenta.

Dizygotic (non-identical) twins develop from two separately fertilized eggs, each with a different combination of alleles, so DNA fingerprinting can distinguish them — unlike monozygotic twins, which cannot be distinguished by DNA fingerprinting because they are genetically identical.

Twin studies compare twins raised together or apart, measuring specific phenotypic traits and attributing variation to genotype, environment, or both. If separated monozygotic twins show similar characteristics, the trait is probably mainly genetic; if there are significant differences, environment is probably more important. Comparing monozygotic and dizygotic twin results further refines this: if monozygotic twins resemble each other more closely than dizygotic twins do, genetic factors likely play a role; if both types of twins resemble each other closely, shared environment is likely responsible; and because monozygotic twins share all their genes, differences between them must be due to non-shared environmental influences.

Although monozygotic twins share the same genotype, most are not perfectly identical (e.g. in physical features or disease predisposition), which can be explained by epigenetic changes — differences in DNA methylation and histone acetylation that accumulate over the twins’ lifetimes (methylation levels tend to increase with age).

In a 1990 twin study, geneticist Claude Bouchard fed male monozygotic twins an extra 1000 calories a day for three months to examine the importance of genes in body-fat storage and obesity. Every participant gained weight, but weight gain within pairs of twins was much more similar than weight gain between different twin pairs, and twins in each pair tended to gain weight in the same body regions — suggesting that genetic factors strongly influence fat storage patterns.

External factors impacting gene expression

The effect of a hormone

Steroid hormones such as oestradiol, progesterone and testosterone affect gene expression by binding to receptors in the cytoplasm; these receptor complexes act as ligand-gated transcription factors that bind to DNA (with the help of co-activators and co-repressors) to alter DNA transcription.

The effect of lactose in bacteria: the lac operon

In bacteria, gene expression is most commonly controlled at the level of transcription (regulating how much mRNA is made), although translational-level control (regulating the rate of translation of mRNA into protein) also occurs. In prokaryotes, operons are regions of DNA containing a group of closely related genes, consisting of structural genes plus regulatory elements: a promoter, an operator, and a terminator. Structural genes encode the enzymes of a metabolic pathway; the operator regulates whether these structural genes are transcribed.

E. coli, found in the human gut, has access to lactose when we drink milk. The lac operon ensures that E. coli only produces lactose-metabolizing enzymes when lactose is present, avoiding wasted energy and resources. The mechanism involves a repressor molecule (coded by a regulator gene), an operator gene next to the structural gene being regulated, and a promoter.

How the lac operon works

When lactose is absent: no lactose is available to bind the repressor, so the repressor molecule binds to the operator and blocks RNA polymerase from binding to the promoter, preventing transcription of the structural gene for the lactose-metabolizing enzyme (lactase). The gene is turned off.

When lactose is present: lactose binds to the repressor protein, changing its shape and inhibiting its ability to bind the operator. RNA polymerase can then bind to the promoter and transcribe the structural gene; the resulting mRNA passes to ribosomes in the cytoplasm where lactase is synthesized. The gene is turned on. Once all the lactose is metabolized, the repressor is free to bind the operator again, switching the gene back off.

Figure D2.2.5: The function of the lac operon in bacteriaTwo panels are shown. In the absence of lactose: the repressor is bound to the operator, physically obstructing RNA polymerase from binding the promoter, so the gene for lactase is not transcribed. In the presence of lactose: lactose has combined with and inactivated the repressor, so RNA polymerase can bind the promoter and transcribe the structural gene for lactase, whose mRNA passes to ribosomes for lactase synthesis.
DefinitionsOperon: a sequence of adjacent bacterial genes all under the transcriptional control of the same operator. Operator: a DNA region at one end of an operon that acts as the binding site for a repressor protein; when the operator is bound by the repressor, transcription is prevented. Lac operon: an inducible operon including three loci involved in the uptake and breakdown of lactose in E. coli. Repressor: the protein product of a regulator gene that controls transcription of an operon by binding the operator and blocking RNA polymerase. Inducer: a molecule capable of activating transcription of a gene by combining with, and inactivating, a genetic repressor.

The lac operon is an inducible operon: transcription is normally switched off but is turned on by the presence of a small effector molecule called the inducer (lactose). The repressor’s ability to bind the operator depends on its conformation, which is allosterically regulated by the inducer, so the concentration of inducer determines the operon’s activity. Operons are only found in prokaryotes; regulatory mechanisms in eukaryotes are more complex.

The effect of tryptophan in bacteria: the trp operon

E. coli synthesizes the amino acid tryptophan from a precursor through a series of enzyme-catalysed steps. The five coding regions for the tryptophan-synthesizing enzymes are arranged sequentially (starting with trpE) in the trp operon, and when E. coli needs to make its own tryptophan, all the enzymes in the pathway are synthesized together.

The trp operon is a repressible operon: its transcription is normally switched on but can be repressed when a small molecule (tryptophan) binds allosterically to a regulatory protein. The trp repressor is the product of a regulatory gene (trpR), located some distance from the operon it controls and with its own promoter. The trp repressor is synthesized in an inactive form with little affinity for the trp operator; only when tryptophan binds to the repressor does it change to an active form that can bind the operator and inhibit transcription of the structural genes.

Figure D2.2.6: The function of the trp operonTwo panels, each showing the promoter, operator, and the five structural genes (trpE, trpD, trpC, trpB, trpA). In the absence of tryptophan: the repressor (inactive form) does not bind the operator, so RNA polymerase binds the promoter and mRNA synthesis of the five tryptophan-synthesizing genes can proceed. In the presence of tryptophan: tryptophan molecules bind to the repressor, activating it; the active repressor binds the operator and blocks RNA polymerase, so mRNA synthesis is blocked.

Tryptophan acts as a co-repressor (in contrast to lactose, which acts as an inducer in the lac operon) — it cooperates with the repressor protein to switch the operon off. As tryptophan accumulates, more tryptophan molecules bind the trp repressor, which then binds the trp operator and inhibits synthesis of the enzymes of the tryptophan biosynthetic pathway.
