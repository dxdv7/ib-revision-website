Phospholipid bilayers: the basis of cell membranes

A plasma membrane is a structure common to all cells. It encompasses and maintains the integrity of the cell, holding its contents together, and it is also the barrier across which all substances entering or leaving the cell must cross. The lipid of membranes is predominantly phospholipid, together with other lipid types.

A phospholipid molecule has a hydrophilic head, which attracts water, and a hydrophobic tail, which repels water. When added to the surface of a water layer, phospholipid molecules arrange themselves into a monolayer, with their hydrophilic heads dissolved in the water and their hydrophobic tails sticking outwards, away from the water. When phospholipid molecules are instead mixed into water, they spontaneously arrange themselves into a bilayer, in which the hydrophobic tails on the inside are attracted to each other and shielded from water, while the hydrophilic heads face outward into the surrounding water on both surfaces.

Figure B2.1.1: The response of phospholipid molecules in waterOn the left, phospholipid molecules added to the surface of water form a single monolayer, heads down in the water and tails pointing up into the air. On the right, phospholipid molecules mixed into water form a double-layered bilayer, with hydrophilic heads facing outward on both surfaces (in contact with water) and hydrophobic tails facing inward, away from water, on both sides of the bilayer.

Monolayers can be extensive and cover a water surface, whereas bilayers need to be continuous, and so they spontaneously form closed structures called vesicles.

Lipid bilayers as barriers

The lipid bilayer is about 4–10 nm in thickness, thin enough to encompass a prokaryotic cell of 0.1–5 micrometres in diameter or a eukaryotic cell of 10–100 micrometres in diameter. Within the bilayer, attractions between the hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails on the inside, and between the hydrophilic glycerol/phosphate heads and the surrounding water on the outside, make a stable, strong barrier. The hydrophobic hydrocarbon chains that form the core of the membrane have low permeability to large and hydrophilic molecules, including ions and polar molecules. Membranes therefore function as effective barriers between aqueous solutions — for example, between the cytoplasm and the fluid outside a cell.

Molecules such as water, respiratory gases (oxygen and carbon dioxide), nutrients (e.g. glucose), essential ions and excretory products continually move into or out of cells; cells may also secrete substances such as hormones and enzymes, or receive growth substances and hormones. Plants secrete the chemicals that make up their walls through their cell membranes and assemble the wall outside the membrane, while certain mammalian cells secrete structural proteins, such as collagen, that are assembled outside the cell.

Simple diffusion across membranes

DiffusionThe free passage of molecules (and atoms and ions) from a region of their higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

Atoms, molecules and ions of liquids and gases undergo continuous random movements, which result in the even, homogeneous distribution of the components of a gas mixture or the atoms, molecules and ions in a solution — so that a small random sample of a solution has the same composition as the whole solution. “Membrane diffusion” describes the movement of molecules through the phospholipid bilayer of membranes. Oxygen and carbon dioxide, being very small molecules, are examples of substances that undergo simple diffusion across membranes: oxygen diffuses into cells from a higher concentration in blood plasma, through tissue fluid, into cells where it is used in aerobic respiration, while carbon dioxide travels in the opposite direction.

Where a concentration gradient exists in a gas or liquid, random molecular movements carry molecules from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration until the particles become evenly dispersed; the energy for this process comes from the kinetic energy store of the molecules themselves.

Figure B2.1.4: Diffusion in a liquidA three-step sequence shows a crystal of potassium permanganate (potassium manganate(VII), KMnO4) placed in distilled water. As the crystal dissolves, its potassium and permanganate ions are dispersed through the water by random molecular movements. Over time, the ions become evenly distributed throughout the water; random movements continue, but with no further net movement in any particular direction.

Diffusion across the cell membrane occurs where the plasma membrane is fully permeable to the solute; the lipid bilayer itself is permeable to non-polar substances, including steroids, and to oxygen and carbon dioxide in solution, all of which diffuse quickly by this route. The term “diffusion”, when applied to movement across membranes, specifically refers to movement across the phospholipid part of the membrane (as distinct from facilitated diffusion through proteins).

Integral and peripheral proteins in membranes

Membrane proteins have diverse structures, locations and functions, falling into two main categories: integral and peripheral proteins.

Peripheral proteinA protein that is attached to the surface of the bilayer — either the outer surface (facing outside the cell) or the inner surface (facing the cytoplasm). Peripheral proteins may shuttle between integral proteins on the membrane surface, act as scaffold proteins to help hold the cell’s shape, or act as receptors for extracellular signals.
Figure B2.1.5: Peripheral and integral proteins of the plasma membraneA section of phospholipid bilayer is shown with an integral membrane protein fully embedded within it, a lipid molecule with a protein attached, and two separate peripheral membrane proteins attached to the outer and inner surfaces of the bilayer respectively, without being embedded within it.

Proteins embedded in one or both of the lipid layers of the membrane are described as integral proteins. Integral membrane proteins may act as channels for the transport of metabolites, as enzymes or carriers, or as receptors or antigens.

Osmosis and the role of aquaporins

OsmosisThe diffusion of free water molecules from a region where they are more concentrated (low solute concentration) to a region where they are less concentrated (high solute concentration), across a partially permeable membrane.

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion. Since water makes up 70–90% of living cells, and cell membranes are partially permeable, osmosis is very important in biology — too much or too little water in the blood creates osmotic effects that can be damaging, causing cells either to burst (too much water) or shrink (too little water).

Dissolved substances attract a shell of polar water molecules around them through hydrogen bonding, a process called hydration; this greatly reduces the tendency of both the solute and its surrounding water molecules to move randomly. The more concentrated a solution, the more water molecules are held nearly stationary in this way, whereas in pure water all water molecules are free to move about randomly.

Figure B2.1.6: The process of osmosisA bag made from dialysis tubing (a partially permeable membrane), containing a concentrated sucrose solution, is lowered into distilled water. Water molecules, free to move about, diffuse through the tubing, but the slow-moving sucrose molecules, each surrounded by attached water molecules, cannot escape into the surrounding water. As a result there is a net flow of water into the bag, and the volume of the bag increases as it becomes stretched with water.

When a solution is separated from water (or a more dilute solution) by a water-permeable membrane, free water molecules diffuse across it, but the dissolved solute molecules hardly move at all, producing a net flow of water from the more dilute solution into the more concentrated solution. Osmosis can therefore be defined as the net movement of water molecules from a region of high water concentration to a region of lower water concentration, across a selectively permeable membrane — or equivalently, as the passive movement of water molecules from a region of lower solute concentration to a region of higher solute concentration.

Common mistakeOsmosis is the movement of water molecules, not simply “particles”, from a region of lower solute concentration to a region of higher solute concentration across a partially permeable membrane. Because the membrane is effectively impermeable to solutes (which are too large or too highly charged to cross the phospholipid bilayer), any change in a cell’s mass is due to water moving by osmosis, not to solute movement.

There are protein-lined pores in the plasma membrane, called aquaporins, that allow water to pass through readily. Water also diffuses across the plasma membrane through tiny, transient spaces between phospholipid molecules, especially where the membrane contains phospholipids with unsaturated hydrocarbon tails, since these tails are spaced more widely apart.

AquaporinA water channel pore (protein) in a membrane.

Facilitated diffusion via channel proteins

Facilitated diffusionThe movement of particles from a region of higher to lower concentration through integral proteins (carrier or channel proteins); movement is passive.

Particles that cannot diffuse across the plasma membrane through the phospholipid bilayer directly are helped across by integral channel proteins (made of globular protein) that span the membrane and form pores large enough to allow diffusion. Channel proteins provide hydrophilic channels for polar and charged molecules, such as carbohydrates, amino acids and ions, to pass through; these pores close again once the substance is no longer present.

Figure B2.1.8: Channel proteins in the plasma membraneA channel protein spanning the plasma membrane is shown providing a passage for a specific substance to move through the bilayer; each type of channel allows only one specific substance to pass.
Figure B2.1.9: Facilitated diffusionA globular protein embedded in the lipid bilayer is shown forming a temporary pore. When substance X is present at a higher concentration outside the membrane, its presence triggers the pore to open, and substance X diffuses through the specific pore from its region of high concentration to its region of low concentration; when substance X is no longer present, the pore closes again.

The channel proteins’ structure makes membranes selectively permeable, allowing specific ions to diffuse through only when the relevant channels are open. As with all diffusion, the energy for facilitated diffusion comes from the kinetic energy store of the moving molecules — energy from metabolism (ATP) is not required. Important examples of facilitated diffusion include the movement of ADP into mitochondria and the exit of ATP from mitochondria.

Channel proteinA pore in the membrane that allows specific charged particles (e.g. ions) and polar substances (i.e. all hydrophilic substances) to diffuse through the membrane into or out of the cell.

Active transport via pump proteins

Active transportMovement of particles from a lower to a higher concentration, using energy from ATP created during respiration; movement is through carrier proteins.

Many substances required by cells must be absorbed from a dilute external concentration into cells that already contain a higher concentration of that substance — uptake against a concentration gradient, which cannot occur by diffusion and instead requires an input of energy. In active transport, metabolic energy stored as ATP is transferred to drive the transport of molecules and ions across membranes; pump proteins (and some carrier proteins) use this energy to move specific particles across membranes against their concentration gradient.

Figure B2.1.10: Pump proteins in the plasma membraneA pump protein spanning the membrane is shown using energy released from the reaction ATP → ADP + Pi to move one (or two) specific substances selectively across the membrane, against their concentration gradient.

Active transport is a feature of most living cells, seen for example in absorption in the gut, active uptake of ions by plant roots, urine formation in kidney tubules, and impulse propagation in nerve fibres. Active transport has three characteristic features that distinguish it from diffusion:

  1. It occurs against a concentration gradient: valuable molecules and ions (such as nitrate ions in plant cells, or calcium ions in muscle fibres) are actively absorbed into cells even when their concentration outside the cell is lower than inside.
  2. Active uptake is highly selective: most membrane pumps are specific to particular molecules or ions, so a substance is only transported if the corresponding pump molecule is present — for example, an animal cell offered potassium chloride is more likely to absorb the K+ ions it needs than the Cl− ions, while a plant cell offered sodium nitrate is more likely to absorb the NO3− ions it needs than the Na+ ions.
  3. Active transport involves pump molecules: pump molecules span the lipid bilayer, pick up specific molecules or ions on one side, and release them on the other side; this movement requires a reaction with ATP that transfers metabolic energy to the process.
Figure B2.1.11: Active transport of a single substanceA four-step cycle is shown for a carrier protein in the lipid bilayer. (1) A molecule or ion to be transported into the cell binds to the carrier protein. (2) The carrier protein is activated by reacting with ATP. (3) The reaction causes a change in the shape and position of the carrier protein, moving the bound molecule across the membrane. (4) ADP and Pi are released from the carrier protein, the molecule or ion is released on the other side, and the carrier protein reverts to its original, receptive shape ready to repeat the cycle.

Some pump proteins transport a single type of molecule or ion in one direction; others transport two substances (such as Na+ and K+) in opposite directions (as in the sodium–potassium pump); occasionally, two substances such as Na+ and glucose are transported together in the same direction.

Common mistakeWhen describing what active transport requires, do not simply state that “energy is needed”. You should specifically mention that ATP is used, and that this ATP is produced by respiration.

Selectivity in membrane permeability

Permeability by simple diffusion is not selective and depends only on the size and hydrophilic or hydrophobic properties of the particles concerned. In contrast, facilitated diffusion and active transport allow membranes to be selectively permeable. Large polar molecules or charged molecules cannot pass through the hydrophobic interior of the membrane, whereas small non-polar molecules can; for many substances, movement across the phospholipid bilayer can therefore only be controlled by altering the concentration gradient across the membrane — for example, oxygen movement into the lungs can be controlled by adjusting the breathing rate, since the membrane itself is not selectively permeable to oxygen.

Substances moved across the membrane by transmembrane proteins, however, can be selectively transported — such as the ions that control the generation of electrical impulses in nerve cells. Such selective transport is essential for processes including the movement of minerals into plant roots and the absorption of nutrients in the animal intestine.

Colorimetry is a technique often used to investigate how conditions such as temperature affect membrane permeability. A colorimeter passes light of a set colour (wavelength) through a sample and records the light absorbed; absorbance is linearly related to solute concentration, while transmittance (the percentage of light that passes through) is related to it exponentially. In one classic investigation, the pigment betalain, contained within beetroot cell vacuoles, leaks out through the plasma membrane and tonoplast as heat or extreme pH damages the phospholipid bilayer, so measuring the absorbance of green light (betalain’s complementary colour) in the surrounding solution at different temperatures gives a measure of how much membrane damage has occurred.

Figure B2.1.13: A colorimeterA light source shines through a coloured filter (chosen to match the colour absorbed by the sample) to produce light of one wavelength, which passes through a cuvette containing the solution under test. The solution absorbs some of this light, and the remaining transmitted light reaches a light detector, with the result shown on a digital display as an absorbance reading.

Glycoproteins, glycolipids and the fluid mosaic model

The carbohydrate molecules of the membrane are relatively short-chain polysaccharides that occur only on the outer surface of the plasma membrane. Some are attached to proteins (forming glycoproteins) and some to lipids (forming glycolipids); together they are known as the glycocalyx. The glycocalyx functions in cell–cell recognition and in binding cells into tissues (cell adhesion); it is highly hydrophilic and attracts large amounts of water to the cell’s surface, helping the cell interact with its watery environment and obtain dissolved substances.

The plasma membrane is made almost entirely of protein and lipid, together with a small, variable amount of carbohydrate. This overall view of membrane structure is known as the fluid mosaic model: “fluid” because the lipid and protein components are constantly in motion, and “mosaic” because the proteins are scattered about within the bilayer in a patchwork pattern.

Integral proteins are embedded within the bilayer, including a channel protein with a central pore and a protein that traverses the membrane and is exposed at both surfaces; a peripheral protein is attached only to the surface of the bilayer. A molecule of cholesterol sits within the bilayer. On the outer surface, a glycoprotein (a polysaccharide chain attached to a protein) and a glycolipid (a polysaccharide chain attached to a lipid) project outward from the membrane into the extracellular space.
Roles of each membrane componentPhospholipids are the main structural component of the cell membrane and act as barriers to the passage of hydrophilic molecules and ions. Proteins have several roles depending on their location and structure: transport, receptors, anchorage, cell recognition, intercellular joining and enzymatic activity. Carbohydrates on the outer surface are involved in cell recognition and cell adhesion.
Fluid mosaic modelThe accepted view of the structure of the plasma membrane, comprising a phospholipid bilayer with proteins embedded but free to move about.

Understanding of the plasma membrane’s composition and structure built up from evidence gathered over many decades, drawing on cytology, cell biochemistry and cell physiology. Early observations — that cell contents flow out when the cell surface is ruptured, that water-soluble compounds enter cells less readily than lipid-soluble ones, and that phospholipids spontaneously form a stable bilayer in water — supported the idea of a lipid membrane barrier. In the 1930s (revised in 1954), James Danielli and Hugh Davson proposed that membranes consist of a phospholipid bilayer evenly coated on both surfaces with a layer of globular protein, with pores present in places; early electron micrographs, which showed two dark lines with a lighter band in between, appeared to support this model.

Figure B2.1.15: TEM of the cell surface membrane of a red blood cellA transmission electron micrograph (magnification ×700,000) of a red blood cell membrane in cross-section shows two dark parallel lines with a lighter band between them — the appearance that was originally interpreted as supporting the Davson–Danielli model of a lipid bilayer sandwiched between two continuous protein layers.

Later evidence led Singer and Nicolson to propose the fluid mosaic model in 1972. Attempts to extract protein from plasma membranes showed that while some proteins occurred on the external surface and were easily extracted, others were buried within or across the lipid bilayer and were much harder to extract. Freeze-etching studies, in which a membrane is split open along its mid-line, confirmed that some proteins are indeed buried within or across the lipid bilayer.

Figure B2.1.16: Plasma membrane structure — evidence from freeze-etchingPart (a) shows a cell membrane in cross-section as a lipid bilayer with protein molecules and attached polysaccharides. Part (b) shows an electron micrograph of a freeze-etched cell membrane, split along the line shown in part (a), revealing the inner, fractured face of the bilayer studded with protein particles embedded within the membrane, rather than lying only on its surface — evidence against the Davson–Danielli model of continuous surface protein layers.

Further evidence came from “tagging” experiments, in which specific membrane components were labelled with marker chemicals (typically fluorescent dyes) and shown to be continually on the move within the membrane, confirming that the membrane is strong but fluid. It was also found that lipid bilayers contain the unusual lipid cholesterol, which disturbs the close packing of phospholipids and whose quantity varies with ambient temperature, and that carbohydrate molecules on the outer surface form complexes with membrane proteins (glycoproteins) and lipids (glycolipids).

Common mistakeWhen drawing a plasma membrane, take care with the position of components: cholesterol should be drawn smaller than the hydrophilic heads and embedded within the bilayer, and peripheral proteins should be drawn on the membrane surface, not partially or fully embedded within it.

Higher Level Fatty acid composition, cholesterol and membrane fluidity

Membranes contain a mixture of phospholipids. Some have saturated fatty acid tails (no double bonds between carbon atoms), and some have unsaturated fatty acid tails (with double bonds). Unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points because their kinked hydrocarbon tails do not pack together as closely as saturated tails do, so lipid bilayers rich in unsaturated fatty acids are more fluid and flexible at the temperatures a cell experiences. Saturated fatty acids have higher melting points and make membranes stronger (less fluid) at higher temperatures. If membrane temperature falls, the membrane becomes less fluid, and at some point may solidify; membranes must remain sufficiently fluid for many of their proteins to move about and function correctly.

Some organisms vary the balance of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids (and the amount of cholesterol) in their membranes as ambient temperature changes, in order to maintain a properly functioning membrane even in the cold. For example, in the lake sturgeon (Acipenser fulvescens), a North American freshwater fish that experiences long overwintering periods, a decrease in temperature from 16°C to 1°C causes both mono- and polyunsaturated fatty acids in membrane phospholipids to increase significantly while saturated fatty acids decrease, helping the fish survive in cold conditions.

Homeoviscous adaptationThe adaptation of cell membrane lipid composition to maintain adequate membrane fluidity. As temperature decreases, phospholipid fatty acid composition is expected to become more unsaturated to maintain homeoviscosity, with the reverse occurring in warmer conditions.

Cholesterol is an essential lipid component of all animal cell plasma membranes, in addition to phospholipids. It is a steroid, with a hydrophilic hydroxyl (−OH) group at one end and a hydrophobic hydrocarbon chain at the other end of its carbon ring structure.

Figure B2.1.17: Molecular structure of cholesterolCholesterol is drawn with a ring-shaped steroid core, a hydrophilic hydroxyl (−OH) group at one end (the polar, hydrophilic part of the molecule), and a non-polar hydrocarbon tail at the other end (the hydrophobic part of the molecule).
Figure B2.1.18: The interaction between cholesterol and the phospholipid bilayerA cholesterol molecule is shown positioned within the phospholipid bilayer: its polar hydroxyl group is attracted to, and sits alongside, the phosphate heads of the phospholipids near the outer surface, while its non-polar hydrocarbon tail and carbon rings sit alongside, and are attracted to, the hydrophobic fatty acid tails of the phospholipids in the interior of the membrane.

By disturbing the close packing of phospholipids, cholesterol increases membrane flexibility; it acts as a modulator of membrane fluidity, stabilizing membranes at higher temperatures while preventing stiffening at lower temperatures. In mammalian membranes, cholesterol overall reduces fluidity and reduces permeability of the membrane to some solutes. At low temperatures, cholesterol maintains fluidity by forcing phospholipids apart and maintaining distance between them, sustaining movement of membrane components; at higher temperatures, bonds between cholesterol and phospholipids maintain the structural integrity of the membrane, preventing it from becoming too fluid or disintegrating. The amount of cholesterol present in a membrane varies with the ambient temperatures a cell experiences.

Higher Level Membrane fluidity, vesicle formation and cytosis

Bulk transport across the plasma membrane occurs through the movement of vesicles of solid or liquid matter, in processes known generally as cytosis. Uptake of matter into a cell is called endocytosis, and export of matter out of a cell is called exocytosis. The strength and flexibility of the fluid mosaic membrane makes this activity possible, and energy from metabolism (ATP) is required.

Key termsEndocytosis: formation of vesicles as the plasma membrane pinches inward, taking material into the cell. Exocytosis: vesicles fuse with the membrane and material is exported out of the cell.
Figure B2.1.19: Transport by endocytosis and exocytosisA cell is shown with its nucleus and cytoplasm. On one side, the plasma membrane is shown pulling inward at the point where a vesicle forms, enclosing either solid matter (phagocytosis, “cell eating”) or liquid (pinocytosis) to bring it into the cell as endocytosis. On the other side, a vesicle from within the cytoplasm is shown fusing with the plasma membrane and releasing its contents outside the cell by exocytosis.

When solid matter is taken in by phagocytosis, part of the plasma membrane at the point where the vesicle forms is pulled inward while the surrounding membrane and cytoplasm bulge outward, enclosing the solid matter in a small vesicle. Vesicles are also used to transport materials within cells, for example between the rough endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus, and on to the plasma membrane. In the human body, phagocytic cells called macrophages engulf and dispose of the debris of damaged or dying cells by endocytosis — around 2 × 1011 red blood cells are broken down and disposed of by macrophages every 24 hours. White blood cells that produce antibodies secrete them by exocytosis, and neurons release neurotransmitter chemicals, such as acetylcholine, into the synaptic cleft by exocytosis.

Common mistakeDo not confuse membrane fluidity with membrane permeability — fluidity refers to how freely the components of the membrane can move and reorganize, while permeability refers to how readily particular substances can cross the membrane.

Higher Level Gated ion channels in neurons

A nerve impulse is transmitted along the axon of a nerve cell by a momentary reversal in electrical potential difference across the axon membrane, brought about by rapid movements of sodium and potassium ions through facilitated diffusion via pores called gated ion channels. These are channel proteins that span the membrane and have a central channel that can open and close; each channel is exclusively permeable to one type of ion.

Neurotransmitter-gated ion channels

Synapses are small gaps between neurons. Because an action potential cannot cross a synapse directly, the signal must be transduced from an electrical signal into a chemical signal — a neurotransmitter, such as acetylcholine (ACh) — that can diffuse across the gap. The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor protein, found in the central and peripheral nervous system, muscle and many other tissues, contains binding sites for acetylcholine and is ionotropic: when ACh binds, positively charged ions (mainly sodium) can flow through the transmembrane protein channel. Because these channels can be closed (“gated”) and are opened by the neurotransmitter ACh, they are known as neurotransmitter-gated ion channels.

With no acetylcholine bound, the central ion channel is closed. When acetylcholine molecules bind to the receptor from the synaptic cleft side, the channel opens, allowing Na+ ions to flow from the synaptic cleft into the cytoplasm. Neurotransmitter-gated ion channelA channel protein that temporarily opens when a specific neurotransmitter binds to it.

Voltage-gated channels

Potassium and sodium channels in nerve cell membranes are voltage-gated channels: they open or close depending on whether a certain threshold membrane potential has been reached. When a stimulus is received, sodium channels open and sodium ions enter the axon, depolarizing its interior so that the membrane potential shifts from about −70 mV to about +40 mV, initiating the nerve impulse (action potential). Almost immediately afterwards, potassium channels open and sodium channels close, allowing potassium ions to exit the axon down their electrochemical gradient. As the interior of the axon becomes less positive again, the potassium channel is first closed mechanically by a “ball and chain” device — a flexible strand of amino acid residues (the “chain”) attached to a globular protein (the “ball”) — before the channel itself returns fully to its closed state once the axon interior is more positive outside than inside again.

Figure B2.1.21: Voltage-gated K+ channel — facilitated diffusion in an axonThree stages of a voltage-gated potassium channel in the axon membrane are shown. In the resting state (net negative charge inside the axon, net positive charge outside), positively charged voltage-sensing paddles on the channel are attracted to the negatively charged interior surface, holding the channel mechanically closed so no K+ ions pass. Once the membrane depolarizes (net positive charge inside, net negative outside), the paddles are attracted to the now-negative outside and repelled by the positive interior, opening the channel gates so K+ ions diffuse out down their electrochemical gradient. Finally, a “ball and chain” structure attached to the interior of the channel protein swings into the open channel and blocks further diffusion of K+ ions while the exterior is still negatively charged, remaining in place until the interior of the axon becomes negatively charged again and the gate itself closes.
Voltage-gated channelsA type of transmembrane protein that forms ion channels activated by changes in the electrical membrane potential of a neuron.

Higher Level Sodium–potassium pumps as exchange transporters

Sodium–potassium pumps are globular proteins that span the axon membrane of nerve cells and are an example of exchange transporters. When preparing an axon for the next nerve impulse, this pump actively transports potassium (K+) ions into the axon and sodium (Na+) ions out of the axon, using energy transferred from ATP. Over time, this activity concentrates sodium and potassium ions on opposite sides of the membrane, playing an important role in generating membrane potentials. The pump works in a repeating cycle:

  • With the interior surface of the pump open to the inside of the axon, three sodium ions load onto specific binding sites.
  • The globular protein reacts with ATP, attaching a phosphate group to the pump; this triggers the pump to close to the interior and open to the exterior.
  • The three sodium ions are released outside the axon, and simultaneously two potassium ions load onto specific binding sites from outside.
  • With the potassium ions loaded, the phosphate group detaches, triggering the pump protein to reverse shape, reopening to the interior and releasing the potassium ions inside the axon.
  • The cycle then repeats.
Figure B2.1.22: The sodium–potassium ion pumpA carrier protein in the lipid bilayer is shown cycling through its states: three Na+ ions bind on the inside; the carrier protein is activated by reaction with ATP, which is converted to ADP and Pi; a change in the shape and position of the carrier protein occurs, releasing the three Na+ ions outside the cell while two K+ ions load onto the protein from outside; the K+ ions are then released inside the cell as the protein reverts to its original shape.

Higher Level Sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters: indirect active transport

Sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters are a family of glucose transporters found in the epithelium of the small intestine and the proximal tubule of kidney nephrons, where they play an important role in glucose absorption. These cotransporter proteins move glucose into the cell while simultaneously moving two sodium ions in the same direction, for each glucose molecule transported.

Active transport of sodium at the base of the cell, using sodium–potassium pumps, removes sodium from the cell and so builds up a sodium concentration gradient, allowing sodium to re-enter through the cotransporter protein down its own concentration gradient at the microvillus surface. As sodium moves down its gradient through the cotransporter, glucose is drawn into the cell through the same protein, even though this moves glucose against its own concentration gradient. Because glucose is not directly powered by ATP but is instead moved in conjunction with the active transport of sodium ions elsewhere in the cell, this overall process is known as indirect active transport. Once inside the cell, glucose then leaves via facilitated diffusion at the base of the cell, through a separate glucose transporter protein, on its way into the blood.

Figure B2.1.23: Epithelial cell with sodium-dependent glucose transporterAn epithelial cell is shown with a microvillus surface at the top (facing the intestinal lumen) and a basolateral surface at the bottom (facing the blood). At the microvillus surface, a sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter protein has a glucose-binding site and Na+ binding sites; two Na+ ions and one glucose molecule enter the cell together through this protein, with sodium moving down its concentration gradient and glucose moving against its own gradient (indirect active transport). At the basolateral surface, a sodium–potassium pump uses ATP to pump Na+ out of the cell and K+ into the cell, maintaining the low intracellular sodium concentration that drives the cotransporter, while a separate glucose transporter protein allows glucose to leave the cell by facilitated diffusion into the surrounding tissue.

Higher Level Adhesion of cells to form tissues

Cells in animal tissues are held together by cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs) — proteins embedded in the surface membranes of cells — which is essential for maintaining tissue structure and function. Binding between CAMs causes cells to adhere to one another, forming a structure called a cell junction. Some CAMs bind cells to one another or to other CAMs; other types bind cells to the extracellular matrix (ECM), the non-cellular scaffolding present within all tissues and organs (for example, collagen fibres and bone mineral make up the ECM of bone tissue).

Key termsCell-adhesion molecule (CAM): a protein located on the cell surface involved in binding cells with other cells or the extracellular matrix. Extracellular matrix (ECM): a large network of proteins and other molecules that surround, support and give structure to cells and tissues.
Figure B2.1.24: Types of CAMs and cell junctions in epithelial cellsA layer of epithelial cells is shown, with microvilli on their upper surface and an extracellular matrix beneath them. Between adjacent cells, several types of cell–cell junction are shown, including tight junctions, anchoring junctions and gap junctions. Cell junctions can be divided into two overall types: cell–cell junctions (usually involving cadherin proteins) and cell–extracellular matrix junctions (usually involving integrin proteins). Both cadherins and integrins are transmembrane proteins. Junctions can also be classified by function: anchoring junctions strengthen contact between cells; tight junctions seal gaps between cells and act as an impermeable or semipermeable barrier controlling the movement of membrane transport proteins between the apical and basal layers of epithelia; gap junctions link the cytoplasm of adjacent cells, allowing transport of molecules between them; and signal-relaying junctions include synapses.

Flowering plants contain relatively few CAMs; instead, plant cells are held together rigidly by extensive interlocking of the cell walls of adjacent cells. The cytoplasm of adjacent cells is often connected by functionally similar but structurally different “bridges”: gap junctions in animals and plasmodesmata in plants. These structures allow cells to exchange small molecules, including nutrients and chemical signals, coordinating the function of cells within a tissue.
