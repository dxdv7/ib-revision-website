Differences Between Sexual and Asexual Reproduction

Reproduction ensures the continuity of a species through time. There are two fundamentally different strategies: asexual and sexual reproduction.

Asexual reproductionReproduction not involving gametes and fertilization. Eukaryotic cells undergo mitosis, and a structure breaks away from the main body and grows into a new organism. All offspring produced by asexual reproduction are genetically identical to the parent and to each other.
Sexual reproductionReproduction involving the production and fusion of gametes (e.g. eggs and sperm). Offspring are genetically unique.

Asexual reproduction in different organisms

Single-celled organisms tend to reproduce asexually because they cannot have specialized reproductive organs. A yeast cell simply divides in two, a process called budding. Single-celled protists, such as Amoeba, also divide asexually.

Figure D3.1.1: A yeast cell reproducing asexuallyThe diagram shows a yeast cell producing a smaller bud that grows out from the main cell body by mitotic division; the bud eventually separates to become a new, genetically identical yeast cell — this process is called budding.
Figure D3.1.2: Asexual reproduction in hydraThe diagram shows a hydra, a small aquatic animal, with its mouth at the upper centre surrounded by tentacles used to capture food drifting past on the current. A daughter polyp is shown budding off from the side of the parent’s body wall; the daughter eventually detaches to live independently as a genetically identical clone of the parent.

Examples of asexual reproduction in plants include runners (e.g. in strawberries) that grow into clones of the parent plant, bulbs that divide to produce clones, and tubers (e.g. the potato) that can split to produce several genetically identical new plants. Some animals reproduce asexually too, including many insects such as greenfly, and aquatic hydra (which buds off daughter polyps, Figure D3.1.2).

Sexual reproduction and its two stages

Sexual reproduction is the production of offspring from two parents using gametes. The cells of the offspring have two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent. Sexual reproduction involves two stages:

  • Meiosis — the special cell division that makes gametes with half the number of chromosomes (one set).
  • Fertilization — the fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote (restoring two sets of chromosomes).

DNA from one generation passes to the next through gametes. Sexual reproduction is a source of genetic variation: DNA from a mother is “shuffled” with DNA from a father, and the fusion of gametes is random (any sperm can fertilize the egg). Variation is therefore produced both by new combinations of genes within gametes and by the random fusion of gametes.

Some animals use external fertilization, releasing gametes into water (e.g. spawning in many fish species). Most land animals use internal fertilization, where males transfer sperm into the female’s body.

Top tipSexual reproduction passes on a species’ genetic code with modification — from meiosis and random fertilization — producing variation that allows a population to adapt to a changing environment. In asexual reproduction, offspring are genetically identical to the parent, allowing rapid population growth in an unchanging environment. For example, aphids (greenfly) reproduce asexually in spring and summer when conditions are stable and predictable, but sexually in autumn, generating variation to help the population cope with changing conditions.

Role of Meiosis and Fusion of Gametes in the Sexual Life Cycle

In sexual reproduction, a male and a female gamete fuse to form a zygote, which grows into a new individual. During gamete formation, meiosis halves the normal chromosome number. Gametes are haploid, and fertilization restores the diploid number.

Figure D3.1.3: Meiosis maintains the chromosome number across generationsThe diagram shows an egg (ovum) with 23 chromosomes and a sperm with 23 chromosomes undergoing fertilization to form a zygote with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs; the zygote then develops into an embryo, also with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs. This shows how the halving of chromosome number at meiosis, followed by fertilization, keeps the chromosome number constant from one generation to the next — without meiosis, the chromosome number would double every generation.
GameteA haploid male or female sex cell able to unite with one of the opposite sex to form a zygote.
FertilizationThe fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote.

During meiosis, paternal and maternal chromosomes are randomly assorted into new haploid cells, breaking up parental combinations of alleles. Due to random assortment and crossing over, gametes have unique sequences of alleles not present in either parent. The fusion of gametes is also random — any male gamete can fertilize the egg, and each egg has a unique set of alleles. This produces new, unique combinations of alleles in the offspring.

Common mistakeThe chromosome numbers in Figure D3.1.3 (23 and 46) apply only to Homo sapiens. Other species have different chromosome numbers — it is incorrect to define a haploid cell generally as having 23 chromosomes.

Differences Between Male and Female Sexes in Sexual Reproduction

Male vs. female gametesMale gametes travel to the female gamete, so they are small with few energy reserves; large numbers must be produced for at least one to reach the egg, which is a further reason for their small size. Female gametes are fewer and larger, providing nutrients and energy for the growing embryo.

Because females generally produce relatively few eggs compared with the vast numbers of sperm produced by males, females in the animal kingdom tend to choose their mate carefully and avoid multiple partners. In mammals, once pregnant, a female feeds and nurtures the growing offspring, there is no reproductive benefit to further mating, and there are periods when she cannot reproduce further. Males, by contrast, produce huge numbers of sperm and can fertilize eggs from multiple females, so they can have several mates and reproduce continually throughout life.

Anatomy of the Human Male and Female Reproductive Systems

Organisms carrying out sexual reproduction have specialized reproductive organs so that male gametes can reach female gametes. In mammals, as in most vertebrates, the sexes are separate. Because the reproductive and urinary systems are closely linked, especially in the male, biologists refer to this as the urinogenital system.

Male reproductive system

  • Two testes (singular, testis) sit in the scrotal sac (scrotum), outside the main body cavity, keeping them 2–3°C below normal body temperature — the optimum for sperm production. As well as producing spermatozoa (sperm), the testes secrete the male sex hormone testosterone, so they are also endocrine glands.
  • The epididymis stores sperm; the sperm ducts carry sperm in seminal fluid (semen) to the outside during ejaculation.
  • Exocrine glands (the seminal vesicles and prostate gland) secrete a nutritive seminal fluid (alkali, proteins and fructose) in which sperm are transported. Semen therefore contains sperm plus fluid from these glands.
  • The urethra carries semen (during ejaculation) and urine (during urination) to the outside.
  • The penis contains spongy erectile tissue that fills with blood during sexual stimulation, producing an erection; the erect penis penetrates the vagina during intercourse.

Female reproductive system

  • The ovaries lie near the base of the abdominal cavity. As well as producing ova (egg cells), they are endocrine glands, secreting oestradiol (formerly called oestrogen) and progesterone.
  • A pair of oviducts (fallopian tubes) extend from the uterus and open as funnels near the ovaries. The oviducts transport egg cells and are the site of fertilization.
  • The uterus, shaped roughly like an inverted pear with a thick muscular wall, has an inner lining, the endometrium, richly supplied with arterioles. This lining is rebuilt each month, on an average 28-day cycle, in preparation for implantation; if fertilization does not occur, it disintegrates and menstruation begins.
  • The vagina is a muscular tube that can enlarge to allow entry of the penis and exit of a baby at birth. It connects to the uterus at the cervix and opens to the exterior at the vulva.

Changes During the Ovarian and Uterine Cycles and Their Hormonal Regulation

Concept – Change: changes in the blood concentration of four hormones (FSH, LH, oestradiol and progesterone), controlled by feedback loops, coordinate the menstrual cycle.

The menstrual cycle consists of two linked cycles: the ovarian cycle, concerned with the monthly preparation and shedding of an egg cell from an ovary, and the uterine cycle, concerned with building up the uterine lining. Together they average 28 days. By convention, day 1 is the first day of menstruation.

Figure D3.1.6: Hormone regulation of the menstrual cycleThe diagram summarizes the control loop: the hypothalamus (part of the forebrain) releases a hormone that controls release of FSH and LH from the anterior pituitary gland. (1) FSH secretion stimulates (2) growth of an ovarian follicle, which secretes oestradiol. (3) LH triggers ovulation. (4) The corpus luteum that remains secretes progesterone (and some oestradiol). Oestradiol and progesterone together feed back to inhibit LH and FSH secretion (negative feedback). (5) Falling LH and FSH levels allow the corpus luteum to degenerate, so progesterone and oestradiol levels fall, the endometrium breaks down, and a new cycle restarts.
Mechanism: the hormonal control cycle, step by step
  1. FSH, secreted by the pituitary gland, stimulates several immature egg cells (in primary follicles) to develop in the ovary; usually only one completes development into a mature egg cell in an ovarian follicle.
  2. The developing follicle secretes oestradiol, which (a) stimulates build-up of the endometrium in the uterus, ready for possible implantation, and (b) increases the number of FSH receptors on the follicle, boosting its own oestradiol output further — an example of positive feedback.
  3. Oestradiol rises to a peak just before mid-cycle. At its highest level it inhibits further FSH secretion from the pituitary, preventing further follicles developing — an example of negative feedback.
  4. The high oestradiol level stimulates a surge of LH from the pituitary. LH triggers ovulation (release of the mature egg cell from the follicle) on day 14, and converts the emptied follicle into a temporary endocrine gland, the corpus luteum.
  5. The corpus luteum secretes progesterone (and some oestradiol). Progesterone (a) continues building up the endometrium, and (b) inhibits further LH and FSH secretion from the pituitary — a second example of negative feedback.
  6. FSH and LH levels fall rapidly, causing the corpus luteum to degenerate; progesterone and oestradiol levels fall as a result. The extra endometrial lining is no longer maintained, breaks down, and is lost as menstruation over the first five days or so of the new cycle. Falling progesterone triggers renewed FSH secretion.
  7. A new cycle begins.
  8. If the egg is fertilized, the developing embryo itself becomes an endocrine gland, secreting a hormone (hCG) that maintains the corpus luteum for at least 16 weeks of pregnancy; eventually the placenta takes over as the endocrine gland secreting oestradiol and progesterone, preventing ovulation and maintaining the endometrium.

Common mistakeDo not confuse the roles of FSH and LH. FSH alone stimulates follicle development. LH promotes oestradiol secretion by the developing follicle and triggers ovulation — the LH surge is therefore a good predictor of ovulation for someone trying to conceive.

Fertilization in Humans

At fertilization, the sperm’s cell membrane fuses with the egg cell membrane. The sperm nucleus enters the egg, but the tail and mitochondria remain outside and are ultimately destroyed — so all the mitochondria in a human cell are derived from the egg (maternal inheritance).

After the two cells fuse, the nuclear membranes of the sperm and egg nuclei break down. The newly joined homologous chromosome pairs (one set from the egg, one from the sperm) condense and take part in a joint mitosis to produce two diploid nuclei.

In mammals, including humans, fertilization — fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote — is internal, occurring in the upper part of the oviduct.

Mechanism: the steps of IVF treatment
  1. Down-regulation shuts down the menstrual cycle by stopping secretion of pituitary and ovarian hormones (usually via a nasal-spray drug), over about two weeks, allowing better control of superovulation.
  2. Superovulation: high doses of FSH are injected over about 10 days to stimulate development of multiple follicles.
  3. When follicles reach 15–20 mm diameter, an injection of hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin, normally secreted by the developing embryo, which maintains the corpus luteum) starts final maturation; after about 36 hours, follicles (typically 8–12) are collected from the ovaries under general anaesthetic.
  4. Eggs removed from the follicles are combined with sperm under sterile conditions.
  5. Successfully fertilized eggs are incubated before implantation.
  6. For about two weeks before implantation, the woman takes progesterone (to maintain the endometrium) to aid implantation; this continues until pregnancy testing and, if positive, until 12 weeks of gestation.
  7. Embryos at the eight-cell stage may be placed in the uterus; if one or more embed, a normal pregnancy may follow.

Because the natural success rate of implantation is only around 40%, usually two or three blastocysts are implanted, which is why IVF treatment carries a high chance of multiple pregnancies. The first “test-tube baby” was born in 1978; IVF is now considered a routine procedure.

Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

Flowering plants carry their reproductive organs in the flower, which is often a hermaphrodite structure with both male and female parts — but reproduction in flowering plants is always sexual, even in hermaphroditic species.

The ovum is contained within an ovule, which also stores nutrients; once fertilized, the ovule becomes the seed. The male reproductive cells are pollen, which travel to the female part of the same or a different plant, carried by insects or the wind.

Key termsOvule: the structure in the ovary that, after fertilization, grows into the seed. Pollen: microspore produced in anthers, containing the male gamete(s). Pollination: transfer of pollen from anther to stigma. Pollen tube: grows from a pollen grain attached to a stigma, down through the style, to the embryo sac.

Fertilization can only occur after an appropriate pollen grain lands on the stigma and germinates. The pollen grain produces a pollen tube that grows down between the cells of the style into the ovule.

Figure D3.1.8: Fertilization in a flowering plantThe diagram shows a germinated pollen grain on the stigma, with a pollen tube growing down through the style, into the ovary and through the micropyle (a gap in the ovule wall) into the ovule. The pollen tube carries two male nuclei: one fuses with the egg cell nucleus in the embryo sac, forming the diploid zygote (fertilization); the other fuses with a separate nucleus in the ovule, triggering formation of a food store for the developing embryo. This “double fertilization” is unique to flowering plants.

Features of an Insect-Pollinated Flower

Flower parts occur in rings, or whorls, attached to the swollen tip of the flower stalk, the receptacle.

  • Sepals (collectively the calyx) enclose the flower bud; usually small, green and leaf-like.
  • Petals (collectively the corolla) are often coloured and conspicuous, attracting insects or other animals.
  • Stamens are the male parts, consisting of the anther (housing pollen grains) and the filament (stalk).
  • Carpels are the female parts (there may be one or many). Each carpel consists of an ovary (containing ovules), a stigma (receives pollen) and a connecting style.

Methods of Promoting Cross-Pollination

Cross-pollinationPollen transferred from the anther of one flower to the stigma of another flower of the same species.

Transfer of pollen is often carried out by animal pollinators — insects (e.g. butterflies, bees), or sometimes birds or bats — attracted by colour or scent, and rewarded with nectar and pollen as food. In return, they accidentally transfer pollen between flowers, a mutualistic relationship between pollinator and plant. Pollen may also be transferred by wind or, occasionally, running water.

Figure D3.1.11: Pollinators at workPhotographs show insect and other animal pollinators visiting flowers to feed on nectar and pollen, incidentally picking up and transferring pollen grains as they move between flowers of the same species.

Plants encourage cross-fertilization (fertilization with a different flower rather than self-pollination) by:

  • the stamens and stigma maturing at different times;
  • the stigma and anthers being positioned at different heights within the same flower;
  • having separate male and female flowers.

This time separation prevents self-pollination within the same flower.

Self-Incompatibility Mechanisms to Increase Genetic Variation Within a Species

Self-pollinationWhen pollen is transferred to the stigma of the same flower.
Inbreeding from self-pollinationAdvantages: preserves good genome combinations already suited to a stable environment. Disadvantages: reduces genetic variation; increases the chance that two undesirable recessive alleles come together; decreases genetic diversity overall.

Because male and female reproductive organs often occur in the same flower, gametes from the same individual could fuse at fertilization — self-pollination. Many plant species have genetic mechanisms ensuring the gametes that fuse come from different plants. Self-incompatibility is the recognition and rejection of pollen by the carpel of the same flower: this genetically controlled mechanism inhibits growth of the pollen tube down the style if the pollen came from the same flower as the style, preventing the pollen from fertilizing the ovum. Self-incompatibility therefore prevents inbreeding and promotes outcrossing.

Dispersal and Germination of Seeds

The seed develops from the fertilized ovule and contains an embryo plant plus a food store. After fertilization:

  • The zygote grows by repeated mitotic division to form an embryonic plant, consisting of an embryo root, an embryo stem, and either one or two cotyledons (seed leaves).
  • Formation of stored food reserves is triggered; in many seeds (e.g. peas and beans) this food store is absorbed into the cotyledons rather than remaining separate. Food reserves are only formed if fertilization has occurred.

As the seed matures, the outer layers of the ovule become the protective seed coat, or testa, and the whole ovary develops into the fruit. The seed’s water content then decreases and it enters a dormancy period; in a mature, fully dormant seed, water makes up only 10–15% of seed weight.

Dispersal of seeds

Seed dispersalThe carrying of the seed away from the parent plant, needed to ensure offspring eventually germinate some distance away, reducing competition with the parent for space, light, nutrients and water. Seed dispersal is a different process from pollination: pollination transfers pollen grains from anther to stigma, whereas seed dispersal moves fertilized ovules (seeds) to new areas, ready for germination.

Plant structures for dispersal exploit air currents (wind), passing animals, or flowing water. In a few plants, seeds are flung from the ripening fruit by an explosive mechanism, powered by turgor pressure or internal tensions within the fruit. Some plants (e.g. coconuts) use water for dispersal. Seeds are compact, nutritious food packages to animals; many are eaten, dropped, or stored and forgotten, aiding dispersal.

Figure D3.1.15: Methods of seed dispersalFour examples are shown: (a) wind — the winged seed of the South East Asian hardwood Dipterocarpus obtusifolius spins away on air currents; (b) water — the coco de mer or sea coconut (Lodoicea maldivica), native to the Seychelles, produces the largest seed on Earth and is dispersed by water; (c) mechanical/explosive — a high-speed photo shows seeds of Himalayan balsam (Impatiens glandulifera) being flung out as the ripe fruit bursts; (d) animal — a cedar waxwing (Bombycilla cedrorum) eats a mulberry fruit, later dispersing the seeds it swallows.

Germination of seeds

GerminationThe resumption of growth by an embryonic plant in a seed or fruit, at the expense of stored food.

Seed dormancy may be imposed by: incomplete (immature) embryo development that is overcome with time; a plant growth regulator such as abscisic acid that inhibits development until it disappears from seed tissues; an impervious seed coat that must be made permeable (e.g. by abrasion with soil or microbial action); or a requirement for pre-chilling under moist conditions (some seeds need to be held at or below 5°C for up to 50 days, mimicking winter).

Once dormancy is overcome, germination requires suitable external conditions: full hydration of the embryo (water uptake); a high enough partial pressure of oxygen to sustain aerobic respiration, providing ATP for growth; and a suitable temperature, close to the optimum for enzymes mobilizing stored food, translocating solutes, and synthesizing new cell components (e.g. wheat germinates over 1–35°C, maize over 5–45°C).

Higher Level Control of the Developmental Changes of Puberty by GnRH and Steroid Sex Hormones

For roughly the first 10 years of life, the human reproductive systems remain juvenile. At puberty, secondary sexual characteristics develop as production of sex hormones by the gonads increases sharply — testosterone in the testes; oestradiol and progesterone in the ovaries. These are chemically similar molecules, all manufactured from the steroid cholesterol (made in the liver and absorbed from the diet).

PubertyThe stage in life when an individual becomes sexually mature, marking the transition from childhood to adulthood.

Because they stimulate muscle protein formation and bone growth, testosterone, oestradiol and progesterone are known as anabolic steroids (“anabolic” means build up). The effect is less marked in females than the effect of testosterone in males. Puberty begins, on average, about two years earlier in females than males.

Higher Level Spermatogenesis and Oogenesis in Humans

GametogenesisThe production of sex cells (gametes): first by mitosis, to proliferate cells, and then by meiosis, to form haploid gametes.

The process of meiosis

Meiosis is a continuous process of nuclear division, conventionally described in four phases (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) with no real breaks between them. There are two successive divisions, meiosis I and meiosis II, with no interphase between them; the arrival of homologous chromosomes at opposite poles marks the end of meiosis I. Meiosis II closely resembles mitosis.

Gametogenesis: three shared phases

Gamete formation in both testes and ovaries follows the same sequence: a multiplication phase, in which gamete mother cells divide repeatedly by mitosis; a growth phase, in which each developing sex cell enlarges; and a maturation phase, involving meiosis, which produces the haploid gametes. The products of meiosis I are secondary spermatocytes and secondary oocytes; the products of meiosis II are spermatids and ova.

Spermatogenesis

In the human foetus, the testes develop high on the posterior abdominal wall and migrate to the scrotum by about the seventh month of pregnancy; testes that fail to migrate do not later produce sperm, as the lower scrotal temperature (2–3°C below body temperature) is essential for sperm production. Spermatogenesis begins at puberty and continues throughout life.

Each testis contains many seminiferous tubules, lined by repeatedly dividing germinal epithelial cells, draining via a system of channels to the epididymis and then the sperm duct. Between the tubules is connective tissue with blood capillaries and groups of hormone-secreting interstitial cells (making the testis an endocrine gland too). The testes are suspended by a spermatic cord containing the sperm duct and blood vessels.

Within the seminiferous tubules, germinal epithelial cells and nutritive Sertoli cells are attached to the basement membrane. Developing sperm cells (spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, secondary spermatocytes, spermatids) are lodged on the surface of the large Sertoli cells, which nourish them until they mature into spermatozoa.

Sperm are immobile when first formed. They pass from the seminiferous tubules into the much-coiled epididymis, where maturation and storage occur. During ejaculation, waves of muscular contraction move sperm along the sperm ducts; nutritive fluid from the seminal vesicles and prostate gland is added where the sperm ducts join the urethra, producing a slightly alkaline semen. During ejaculation, the sphincter muscle at the base of the bladder closes.

Common mistakeDo not confuse semen production with spermatogenesis. Semen is sperm plus the fluid from the prostate and seminal vesicles that the sperm travel in; spermatogenesis is the process of sperm formation itself.
Mechanism: hormonal control of spermatogenesisAt puberty, a hypothalamic hormone (GnRH) triggers FSH and LH secretion from the anterior pituitary. FSH initiates sperm production in the testes. LH stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone. Testosterone and FSH then act together to maintain ongoing sperm production and growth of the Sertoli cells that support developing sperm. Testosterone secretion continues throughout life and is regulated by negative feedback: a high blood testosterone level inhibits LH secretion, and testosterone production only resumes once LH has fallen significantly (similarly, over-activity of the nutritive/Sertoli cells briefly inhibits FSH secretion).

Oogenesis

The paired ovaries (about 3 cm long, 1.5 cm thick) are suspended by ligaments near the base of the abdominal cavity, and are endocrine glands secreting oestradiol and progesterone as well as producing egg cells. Oviducts extend from the uterus, open as funnels near the ovaries, transport oocytes, and are the site of fertilization.

Oogenesis begins in the foetal ovary but is only completed in adult life. The germinal epithelium lining the ovary’s outer surface divides mitotically to form oogonia, which migrate into the ovary’s connective tissue, grow and enlarge into oocytes. Each oocyte becomes surrounded by follicle cells to form a primary follicle. By mid-pregnancy, oogonia production in the foetus ceases, by which time there are several million per ovary; very many degenerate throughout life, so that by puberty only about 250,000 primary oocytes remain, of which fewer than 1% will ever complete development into secondary oocytes or ova.

Between puberty (about age 11) and menopause (about age 55), several primary follicles begin developing each month, though usually only one matures, enlarging and moving to the outer part of the ovary. The primary follicle then undergoes meiosis I, but cytoplasmic division is unequal, producing a tiny polar body and a secondary oocyte. Meiosis II then begins but arrests before completion; in this state the secondary oocyte is released from the ovary at ovulation, when the follicle wall ruptures. Meiosis II is only completed if a sperm nucleus later penetrates the oocyte’s cytoplasm.

Higher Level Mechanisms to Prevent Polyspermy

PolyspermyFertilization of an egg by more than one sperm.

Of the few sperm that reach a secondary oocyte, one (or occasionally more) passes between the surrounding follicle cells. Fertilization includes a mechanism that prevents polyspermy.

First, the sperm must cross the zona pellucida, the glycoprotein coat surrounding the oocyte. The sperm’s acrosome, a membrane-bound sac of hydrolytic enzymes at its tip, releases its contents on contact with the zona pellucida, digesting a path to the oocyte membrane — the acrosome reaction.

Next, the plasma membrane around the sperm head fuses with the oocyte’s plasma membrane, and the male nucleus enters. This triggers cortical granules in the oocyte’s outer cytoplasm to release their contents outside the cell by exocytosis — the cortical reaction. This alters the oocyte membrane so it can no longer be crossed by another sperm, preventing polyspermy.

As the sperm nucleus enters, completion of meiosis II is triggered and the second polar body is released. The male and female haploid nuclei then fuse to form the diploid zygote nucleus, completing fertilization.

Figure D3.1.24: Fertilization of a human secondary oocyteA numbered sequence shows: (1) a sperm passes between the follicle cells and reaches the jelly coat (zona pellucida) around the secondary oocyte; (2) capacitation changes the sperm head surface, releasing hydrolytic enzymes; (3) enzyme digestion creates a tiny path through the zona pellucida to the oocyte’s plasma membrane (the acrosome reaction); (4) the plasma membranes of the secondary oocyte and sperm fuse; (5) cortical granules in the oocyte’s outer cytoplasm cross the plasma membrane by exocytosis, preventing entry of a second sperm (the cortical reaction); (6) the secondary oocyte’s nucleus is stimulated to complete meiosis II; (7) the resulting haploid male and female nuclei fuse to form a diploid nucleus, and the polar bodies break down; (8) the diploid nucleus undergoes mitosis; (9) the cytoplasm then divides, forming the first two diploid cells of the embryo.
Key termsZona pellucida: the protective glycoprotein coat of the egg; after one sperm enters it becomes stronger, blocking further sperm entry. Acrosome: the sac at the sperm head containing protease enzymes. Cortical reaction: the exocytosis of cortical granules that prevents polyspermy.
Common mistakeWhen describing fertilization, focus on events beginning with the arrival of sperm at the oocyte surface, including the acrosome reaction and cortical reaction — not earlier stages such as ejaculation.

Higher Level Development of a Blastocyst and Implantation in the Endometrium

Fertilization occurs in the upper oviduct. As the zygote is carried down the oviduct by ciliary action, mitosis and cell division (cleavage) begin — the division of the zygote into a mass of daughter cells without any increase in overall mass. By the time the embryo reaches the uterus, it is a solid ball of tiny cells; further division produces a fluid-filled ball, the blastocyst.

By day 7, the human blastocyst consists of about 100 cells and begins to embed in the endometrium — a process called implantation, which takes from about day 7 to day 14. At this stage, part of the blastocyst forms the inner cell mass, which will become the foetus; other parts become the placenta. Once implanted, the embryo begins receiving nutrients directly from the endometrium.

Higher Level Pregnancy Testing by Detection of Human Chorionic Gonadotropin Secretion

Once fertilized, the developing embryo secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin), which circulates in the blood and maintains the corpus luteum as an endocrine gland for at least 16 weeks of pregnancy. hCG appears in the urine from about seven days after conception, providing the basis of pregnancy testing.

Monoclonal antibodies

Antibodies destroy antigens in the body, but the plasma cells that secrete them (products of B-lymphocytes) have very short lifespans and cannot themselves divide, so antibodies normally cannot be produced outside the body long-term.

Monoclonal antibodyAn antibody produced by a single clone of B-lymphocytes, consisting of a population of identical antibody molecules; it is stable and can be used over time. The short lifespan of a plasma cell is overcome by fusing the specific lymphocyte with a cancer cell (which divides indefinitely), producing a hybridoma cell. Hybridoma cells are virtually immortal and divide to form a persistent clone that continues secreting the specific antibody in large quantities.

Pregnancy testing

A pregnant person has a significant concentration of hCG in their urine, while a non-pregnant person has a negligible amount. Monoclonal antibodies against hCG are engineered to carry coloured granules, so a simple test kit gives a visual, coloured-strip result.

Higher Level Role of the Placenta in Foetal Development Inside the Uterus

From early in development, the embryo is contained, supported and protected by a membranous, fluid-filled sac. The outer tissue layers of the embryo grow to form both these membranes and the placenta. By the end of two months of development, the beginnings of the principal adult organs are present and the placenta is operational; from this point, the developing offspring is called a foetus rather than an embryo (conventionally, from the 11th week of pregnancy, i.e. the 9th week after fertilization). The placenta allows the foetus to remain in the uterus to a later stage of development than in mammals without a placenta (e.g. marsupials such as the kangaroo and wombat).

Figure D3.1.27: Human embryo at the six-week stageA photograph of an embryo at six weeks shows the developing placenta and the umbilical cord already connecting the embryo to it.

The placenta: structure and function

The placenta is a disc-shaped structure of maternal (endometrial) and foetal membrane tissues, bringing maternal and foetal blood circulations very close together over a huge surface area without the two blood supplies mixing. The placenta and foetus are connected by two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein in the umbilical cord.

Exchange across the placenta occurs by diffusion and active transport:

  • Respiratory gases are exchanged: oxygen diffuses from maternal to foetal haemoglobin; carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction.
  • Water crosses by osmosis; glucose crosses by facilitated diffusion; ions and amino acids are transported actively.
  • Excretory products, including urea, leave the foetus for the mother to excrete.
  • Maternal antibodies cross freely, giving the foetus passive immunity, initially protecting it from the same diseases as the mother.

The placenta is a barrier to bacteria, though some viruses can cross it.

Higher Level Hormonal Control of Pregnancy and Childbirth

Pregnancy

The placenta is also an endocrine gland, initially producing hCG (which appears in the urine from about seven days after conception and is the basis of pregnancy testing). hCG is secreted first by cells of the blastocyst and later entirely by the placenta; its role is to maintain the corpus luteum, and thus progesterone secretion, for the first 16 weeks of pregnancy. Once the corpus luteum breaks down, the placenta itself secretes oestradiol and progesterone. Progesterone maintains the uterine lining and so maintains the pregnancy; without maintained hormone levels, conditions favourable to the foetus are lost and a spontaneous abortion (miscarriage) results.

Childbirth

Immediately before birth, progesterone levels decline sharply, removing progesterone’s inhibition of uterine muscle contraction. The posterior pituitary begins releasing oxytocin, which relaxes the elastic fibres joining the pelvic girdle bones (aiding cervical dilation for the head to pass through) and stimulates rhythmic contractions of the uterine muscle wall.

OxytocinA hormone released by the (posterior) pituitary gland that increases contraction of the uterus during childbirth.
Mechanism: the positive feedback loop of labourContractions of the uterus wall force the baby’s head into the cervix, stretching it. Stretch-sensitive receptors in the cervix send a signal to the brain (control centre), which causes the posterior pituitary gland to release oxytocin. Oxytocin makes the uterine wall muscles contract more forcefully, which stretches the cervix further, triggering release of even more oxytocin — a self-reinforcing positive feedback loop. This cycle of escalating contractions and increasing oxytocin release continues, becoming progressively more frequent and powerful (the process of labour), until the baby is born. At birth, stretching of the cervix decreases sharply, breaking the positive feedback cycle and terminating oxytocin release. Afterward, less powerful uterine contractions separate the placenta from the endometrium, expelling the placenta and umbilical remnants as the afterbirth.

Higher Level Hormone Replacement Therapy and the Risk of Coronary Heart Disease

MenopauseThe period in life (typically ages 45–55) when menstruation ceases; a person is considered to have reached menopause once 12 consecutive months have passed without a period.

Menopause results from the ovaries reducing oestradiol and progesterone production, so fertility declines. After menopause the ovaries no longer respond to FSH and LH, and stop releasing eggs and oestradiol; the resulting loss of negative feedback affects the pituitary gland. Because oestradiol affects many body systems (blood vessels, heart, bone, uterus, urinary system, skin, brain), its loss is linked to menopausal symptoms including brief episodes of raised body temperature, increased heart rate, sudden perspiration, joint and muscle stiffness, and changes in concentration.

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)Medication containing female hormones (oestradiol and progesterone) taken to relieve menopausal symptoms by replacing the hormones the body stops producing; it can be taken as tablets, patches, creams or gels, under medical advice.
NOS: epidemiological vs. clinical evidence, and correlation vs. causationEpidemiological studies provide circumstantial evidence of health risks across populations; they suggest connections but cannot establish a direct cause or biochemical mechanism. Clinical studies of individual patients attempt to establish causal relationships, though truly “controlled” experiments are often not possible for ethical reasons. Early epidemiological studies suggested HRT reduced the incidence of coronary heart disease (CHD) and this was taken to be a cause-and-effect relationship. However, subsequent randomized controlled trials — in which subjects are randomly assigned to an experimental group (receiving the treatment being tested) or a comparison/control group (receiving an alternative treatment), then compared for outcomes — showed that HRT actually led to a small increase in CHD risk. The original correlation was not a cause-and-effect relationship: HRT patients tended to have higher socioeconomic status (SES), and higher SES independently correlates with lower CHD risk (possibly because people of higher SES use health services, including preventative measures like HRT, more comprehensively and regularly). This is an important lesson that correlation between two variables does not, by itself, establish causation.
