Chemical properties of the carbon atom

MacromoleculeLiving organisms contain many large macromolecules made from smaller molecules. The most common atoms in biomolecules are carbon (C), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O) and nitrogen (N); some biomolecules also contain sulfur (S) and phosphorus (P). A macromolecule is a very large organic molecule such as a protein, nucleic acid or polysaccharide.

Carbon has unique properties that make life possible:

  1. Atoms bond to achieve a stable, complete outer electron shell. The first electron shell holds up to 2 electrons; the second holds up to 8. Carbon has 4 electrons in its second shell and forms 4 strong, stable covalent bonds, each formed when two atoms share a pair of outer electrons.
  2. Covalent bonds are the strongest bonds in biological molecules, requiring the most energy to break, so they give great stability to large biological molecules. Carbon also forms covalent bonds with oxygen, nitrogen and sulfur, creating organic molecule groups with distinctive properties. Molecules are additionally attracted to each other by much weaker intermolecular forces that are easily broken and reformed.
  3. Covalent bonds are only broken in specific chemical reactions.
  4. Carbon’s ability to form 4 strong covalent bonds with other carbon atoms allows stable chains, branched chains and cyclic (ring) structures.
  5. The four covalent bonds of a carbon atom point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron, because the four electron pairs repel each other and position themselves as far apart as possible. Where four different groups are attached to a carbon atom, two different (mirror-image) arrangements are possible; a carbon atom bonded to four different atoms or groups is described as asymmetric — another source of variety among organic molecules.
  6. Carbon atoms can form more than one bond between them, e.g. a double bond, formed by sharing two pairs of electrons; nitrogen and oxygen also form double bonds. Carbon compounds containing carbon–carbon double bonds are called unsaturated.
  7. Many biomolecules carry functional groups attached to a carbon skeleton, which make the molecule reactive.

At least 2.5 million organic compounds exist — more than the total known compounds of all other elements combined. Organic compounds include amino acids (which form proteins), fatty acids (a component of lipids) and carbohydrates such as glucose.

Functional group and R-groupA functional group is the chemically active part of a member of a series of organic molecules. Carbohydrates contain the hydroxyl group, fatty acids the carboxyl group, and lipids the ester group. All carbon compounds fit into a relatively small number of “families” (homologous series) identified by their functional group, which gives them their characteristic chemical properties. The rest of the molecule, apart from the functional group, has little effect on its chemical properties and is called the R-group.

Organic and inorganic compounds

Organic molecules are made of carbon and hydrogen, such as urea, proteins, sugars and lipids found in living organisms; hydrocarbons such as methane are organic even though not produced by living organisms. Some simple carbon compounds are not considered organic, such as carbon dioxide, carbonic acid and hydrogen carbonate ions. Inorganic compounds include all other compounds, such as water and phosphoric(V) acid.

Condensation and hydrolysis reactions

Condensation vs hydrolysisCondensation and hydrolysis are two of the most important reactions in metabolism, responsible for forming (and breaking down) proteins, nucleic acids, triglycerides and polysaccharides such as starch and cellulose. During condensation, a new covalent bond forms between two monomers and a molecule of water is produced as a by-product; the process is enzyme-controlled and requires energy input. Hydrolysis (“splitting by water”) is the reverse: a water molecule acts as a reactant, being split to supply an –H and an –OH group that break a covalent bond, releasing the monomers; it is also enzyme-catalysed, but by a different enzyme from the one that catalyses the corresponding condensation reaction.
Figure B1.1.7: Condensation versus hydrolysisShows the general scheme: monomer A–H plus HO–monomer B combine by condensation (releasing H2O) to form monomer A–monomer B; the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, adds H2O back across the bond to regenerate monomer A–H and HO–monomer B.

When two monosaccharides combine to form a disaccharide, a water molecule is released as a product, making it a condensation reaction; the covalent linkage formed between monosaccharide residues, after removal of H–O–H, is called a glycosidic linkage. These reactions can occur without enzymes but only very slowly; catalysis by an enzyme greatly increases the rate. In the reverse (hydrolysis) reaction, disaccharides are “digested” back to their monosaccharides, again catalysed by a (different) enzyme.

Figure B1.1.8: Disaccharides and the monosaccharides that form themShows three condensation/hydrolysis pairs: glucose plus fructose combine (releasing water) to form sucrose via a glycosidic linkage; galactose plus glucose combine to form lactose; and glucose plus glucose combine to form maltose. Each reaction is reversible, with hydrolysis (adding water) breaking the disaccharide back into its two monosaccharides.
Key termsMonosaccharide: any of the class of sugars (e.g. glucose) that cannot be hydrolysed to give a simpler sugar. Disaccharide: a sugar formed by condensation of two monosaccharides (e.g. maltose). Glycosidic linkage: a covalent bond between monosaccharide residues in disaccharides and polysaccharides.

Production of macromolecules by condensation reactions

Polysaccharides are built from many monosaccharide molecules joined by condensation into glycosidic bonds — often thousands of monosaccharides make up a single polysaccharide, which is an example of a giant molecule called a polymer (a large organic molecule made of repeating subunits, or monomers). Normally each polysaccharide contains only one type of monomer, e.g. starch is built entirely from glucose. Polypeptides are formed by condensation reactions between many amino acids, occurring at ribosomes in the cytoplasm and on the rough endoplasmic reticulum. The formation of nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) also proceeds by condensation reactions between nucleotides.

Digestion of polymers into monomers by hydrolysis reactions

To release the energy-rich monomers in polysaccharides such as starch and glycogen, the macromolecules must be digested by hydrolysis reactions, in which glycosidic bonds are broken with the addition of water; the rate of these reactions is increased by enzyme action. Amylase, secreted by the salivary glands and pancreas of mammals, hydrolyses polysaccharides in food.

Figure B1.1.9: Hydrolysis of starch into smaller sugar moleculesShows a starch polysaccharide chain being progressively broken down by the enzyme amylase, with water added across each glycosidic bond, releasing maltose and glucose units.

Proteins (polypeptides) are similarly digested into shorter peptide chains and, ultimately, amino acids by hydrolysis reactions catalysed by protease enzymes.

Figure B1.1.10: Hydrolysis of a peptide bondShows a polypeptide chain being split by a protease enzyme, with a water molecule added across a peptide bond to release a shorter peptide and a free amino acid, each bearing an amine group, an R-group, and a carboxyl group.

In nucleotides, the phosphodiester bond links the third carbon (3' carbon) of one sugar molecule to the fifth carbon (5' carbon) of the adjacent sugar molecule. When this bond is hydrolysed, a water molecule is added, the phosphodiester bond breaks, and a 3'–OH–deoxyribose–5'–phosphate group is formed at the cut ends.

Form and function of monosaccharides

Introduction to carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are the largest group of organic compounds in living things, including sugars, cellulose and starch, with the general formula Cx(H2O)y, where x = 3, 4, 5 (pentoses) or 6 (hexoses); some heptoses (x = 7) also exist, e.g. mannoheptulose in avocados. Examples include D-fructose (C6H12O6), D-ribose (C5H10O5) and D-maltose (C12H22O11), which are cyclic molecules with hydroxyl groups bonded to the ring carbons.

Glucose: structure and properties

Monosaccharides are relatively small carbohydrate molecules; they taste sweet and are soluble in water. Glucose is an especially important monosaccharide because: all green leaves synthesize it in chloroplasts using light energy; the body transports it in the blood; all cells use it as a respiratory substrate; and it is the building block for larger molecules such as cellulose and starch in plants and glycogen in animals. Glucose is polar and dissolves in water through hydrogen bonding with its slightly charged hydroxyl groups; the negatively charged oxygen in water attracts the positively charged hydroxyl hydrogens in glucose, allowing glucose to be transported in blood plasma. The carbon atoms of the glucose ring each form four covalent bonds at the optimum tetrahedral angle of 109.5 degrees, giving a very stable structure; forcing these bonds into different angles would make the molecule less stable. Glucose is the main respiratory substrate: it can be completely broken down by aerobic respiration to release large amounts of energy as ATP, releasing carbon dioxide and water as waste products, whereas substrates derived from lipids and proteins must first be processed before entering these pathways.

Glucose has the molecular formula C6H12O6, making it a 6-carbon sugar (hexose); this formula tells us the atoms present but not the molecule’s structure. Glucose can fold into a ring (cyclic) form, existing in solution in equilibrium with its linear form; as the ring forms, the oxygen on carbon-5 attaches to carbon-1, and the ring contains five carbon atoms plus one oxygen atom.

Figure B1.1.12: The differences between alpha- and beta-glucoseShows the structural formulae of alpha-glucose and beta-glucose as six-membered (pyranose) rings. The two forms differ only in the orientation of the –H and –OH groups attached to carbon-1: in alpha-glucose the –OH on carbon-1 points below the ring plane, while in beta-glucose it points above the ring plane (on the same side as the –CH2OH group on carbon-5).

Isomers

IsomerCompounds with the same component atoms but a different arrangement of those atoms are isomers. In the ring form of glucose, interchanging the positions of the –H and –OH groups on carbon-1 gives two isomers, alpha-glucose and beta-glucose, known as anomers (a type of geometric variation found at certain carbohydrate carbon atoms). These small structural differences barely change the sugar’s general chemical properties but are recognized by enzymes and can therefore have major effects on the polymers built from them. Two compounds that are identical except for being mirror images of each other are optical isomers: they have identical chemical properties but rotate the plane of polarized light in opposite directions (D- rotates it to the right, L- to the left).

Polysaccharides as energy storage compounds

Starch

Starch is a mixture of two polysaccharides, both built from alpha-glucose: amylose, an unbranched chain of several thousand 1,4-linked alpha-glucose units, and amylopectin, which has shorter 1,4-linked chains but also branch points formed by alpha-1,6 glycosidic links. In starch, the bonds between glucose residues cause the molecule to coil into a helix, stabilized by many hydrogen bonds between component glucose units.

Figure B1.1.14: StarchShows amylose as a straight-chain polymer of alpha-glucose units joined by alpha-1,4-glycosidic bonds, and amylopectin as a branched-chain polymer of alpha-glucose with additional alpha-1,6-glycosidic bonds at branch points.

Starch is the major storage carbohydrate of most plants, laid down as compact grains in plastids such as chloroplasts. Its usefulness lies in the compactness and insolubility of the molecule (giving it no osmotic effect on the cell), while remaining readily hydrolysed to sugar when energy is required. Manufactured “soluble starch” consists of starch molecules broken into shorter lengths that dissolve more easily. Starch can be detected using iodine in potassium iodide solution: iodine molecules fit into the centre of the starch helix, producing an intense blue-black colour.

Figure B1.1.15: Benedict’s test colour changesShows the gradient of colour change produced by Benedict’s reagent (an aqueous solution of copper(II) sulfate, sodium carbonate and sodium citrate) when heated with a reducing sugar: from blue (no reducing sugar present) through green and yellow (low concentration) to an orange/red precipitate of copper(I) oxide (high concentration), giving a semi-quantitative indication of the amount of reducing sugar present.
Food testsStarch is tested for using iodine in potassium iodide, which forms a blue-black starch-polyiodide complex. Monosaccharides and disaccharides (except sucrose) are tested using Benedict’s reagent: because they reduce copper(II) ions, they are called reducing sugars, and heating produces an orange/red precipitate of copper(I) oxide whose density indicates the approximate quantity present.

Glycogen

Glycogen is a polymer of alpha-glucose, chemically very similar to amylopectin but larger and more highly branched, formed by condensation reactions between alpha-glucose monomers. It is one of the body’s energy reserves, hydrolysed and respired as needed; it is also made by some fungi and bacteria.

Figure B1.1.16: The structure of glycogenShows the highly branched chain structure of glycogen, built from alpha-glucose subunits joined by 1,4-glycosidic bonds along chains and 1,6-glycosidic bonds at frequent branch points.

Glycogen’s insolubility means it has no osmotic effect on cells storing large amounts of glucose. Its extensive branching gives a compact structure that allows more glucose to be stored per unit volume, and the many non-reducing ends created by the branches allow rapid, enzyme-controlled hydrolysis to release glucose quickly when energy demand rises. Glycogen granules are found in liver cells, muscle fibres and most body tissues, with the notable exception of brain cells, which store virtually no energy reserves and instead require a constant supply of glucose from the blood.

Starch and glycogen as storage compoundsGlycogen and starch make good storage compounds because of their relative insolubility (due to large molecular size, avoiding osmotic effects) and the relative ease of adding or removing alpha-glucose monomers by condensation and hydrolysis to build or mobilize the energy store.

Structure of cellulose related to its function

Cellulose is by far the most abundant carbohydrate, making up more than 50% of all organic carbon, found mainly in the cell walls of green plants and plant debris in soil. Cellulose molecules are polymers of beta-glucose joined by glycosidic bonds between carbon-4 of one beta-glucose and carbon-1 of the next; because a glycosidic bond forms by removing water from adjacent –OH groups, each successive beta-glucose molecule must be rotated 180 degrees relative to its neighbour to keep the –OH groups correctly oriented, producing straight, uncoiled, unbranched chains. Hydrogen bonds between adjacent glucose units within a strand, between strands in a fibril, and between fibrils, stabilize and strengthen the structure; in plant cell walls, additional strength comes from cellulose fibres laid down in layers running in different directions.

Figure B1.1.17: CelluloseShows beta-glucose molecules forming straight, unbranched chains via 1,4-glycosidic bonds, with each successive glucose rotated 180 degrees relative to its neighbour. Hydrogen bonds hold the strands straight and pack them together into fibrils; many fibrils combine to form fibres, which are laid down at different angles for strength (illustrated with an inset electron micrograph of cellulose in a plant cell wall at times 1500 magnification).

Because of this structure, cellulose is extremely strong: insoluble, tough, durable and slightly elastic. Extracted cellulose fibres have many industrial uses, including cotton, paper, rayon fibres for clothing, nitrocellulose for explosives, cellulose acetate, and cellophane for packaging.

Starch/glycogen vs cellulose: form and functionBecause the structure of cellulose differs from that of starch and glycogen, its function differs too. Starch and glycogen are used for energy storage, whereas cellulose is used primarily for structural support and mechanical strength in plant cell walls. The alternating orientation of beta-glucose units in cellulose allows hydrogen bonding both within and between parallel strands, strengthening the polymer for its structural role, whereas the helical coiling of alpha-glucose chains in starch and glycogen produces compact, readily hydrolysed energy stores instead.

The role of glycoproteins in cell–cell recognition

The plasma membrane has three major components: phospholipids, proteins and carbohydrates. The carbohydrate molecules of the cell surface membrane are relatively short-chain polysaccharides, some attached to proteins (glycoproteins) and some to lipids (glycolipids), found only on the outer surface of the plasma membrane. These carbohydrate components are collectively called the glycocalyx (“sugar coating”).

Key termsGlycoprotein: a membrane protein with a glycocalyx attached. Glycolipid: a lipid with a glycocalyx attached. Glycocalyx: long carbohydrate molecules attached to membrane proteins and membrane lipids.

Along with peripheral proteins, carbohydrates allow cells to recognize each other via their unique shapes. This recognition function lets the immune system distinguish body cells (“self”) from foreign cells or tissues (“non-self”), so that the immune system does not attack the organism’s own cells while still identifying and dealing with foreign cells and viruses; the glycoprotein/glycolipid types on virus surfaces may change frequently, helping viruses evade immune recognition. The roles of the glycocalyx are: cell–cell recognition; acting as receptor sites for chemical signals such as hormones; and assisting in binding cells together to form tissues. It is also important for embryonic development.

Antigens, antibodies and blood transfusions

Key termsAntigen: a substance (usually a glycoprotein or other protein) capable of binding specifically to an antibody; it is recognized by the body as foreign and stimulates an immune response. Antibody: a protein produced by blood plasma cells derived from B lymphocytes in the presence of a specific antigen, which then binds to that antigen, aiding its destruction.

The glycocalyx can act as an antigen because the carbohydrate pattern on the outer membrane surface is genetically determined, allowing the shape of these surface macromolecules to distinguish the body’s own cells from non-self material such as bacteria or viruses. Antigen specificity arises from the variety of possible amino acid sequences, allowing immune responses customized to specific pathogens.

Human blood cells carry antigens on their plasma membrane, of which the ABO system is one of the most clinically important for transfusions. People carry an antibody in their plasma against whichever ABO antigen they lack, even without prior contact with that antigen. Hydrophobic properties of lipids

LipidsLipids are fats (semi-solid) and oils (liquid). They are poorly soluble in water — a property described as hydrophobic — but dissolve in less-polar organic solvents such as ethanol and propanone. Lipids contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, but relatively fewer oxygen atoms than carbohydrates. Fats and oils differ only in that, at around room temperature, oils are liquid and fats are solid.

The simplest lipids are triglycerides, formed from a trihydric alcohol called glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol, with three hydroxyl groups) and fatty acids — long-chain carboxylic acids with long hydrocarbon “tails”. Fatty acids may be saturated (no carbon–carbon double bonds) or unsaturated (one or more carbon–carbon double bonds in the tail).

Figure B1.1.20: Fatty acids and glycerol, the building blocks of lipidsShows the structure of palmitic acid (molecular formula CH3(CH2)14COOH), a 16-carbon fatty acid with a hydrocarbon tail and a carboxyl group at the end (which ionizes weakly in water, releasing a hydrogen ion), and glycerol (molecular formula C3H5(OH)3), a three-carbon molecule bearing three hydroxyl groups.
Common mistakeGlycerol is not a fatty acid, even though it combines with fatty acids to form a triglyceride. It is also not a sugar, since its carbon:hydrogen:oxygen ratio (3:8:3) does not match the 1:2:1 ratio required of a sugar; glycerol belongs to the alcohol family of organic compounds.

Hydrocarbon tails in fats and oils are typically 16–18 carbons long, though any even number between 14 and 22 is possible. It is the hydrocarbon tails that give triglycerides their hydrophobic properties. A triglyceride molecule is large but small compared with polymers such as starch or cellulose — it is only because of their hydrophobic behaviour that triglyceride molecules aggregate into large globules in water, giving the appearance of polymers. The carboxyl (–COOH) functional group of a fatty acid ionizes slightly in water to produce hydrogen ions (–COOH → –COO− + H+), which is why fatty acids are described as acids. This carboxyl group reacts with a hydroxyl group of glycerol to form an ester bond.

Formation of triglycerides and phospholipids by condensation reactions

Building a triglycerideTriglycerides form by condensation reactions between fatty acids and glycerol, with water removed at each step. A bond forms between the carboxyl group of a fatty acid and one hydroxyl group of glycerol to give a monoglyceride (releasing water); the reaction is repeated with a second fatty acid to give a diglyceride, and a third time to give a triglyceride. Three fatty acids combine with one glycerol to form each triglyceride molecule, and the three fatty acids may be identical or different. In cells, enzymes catalyse both the condensation reaction that forms triglycerides and the hydrolysis reaction that breaks glycerides down.
Figure B1.1.21: Formation of a triglyceride from three fatty acids and one glycerolShows the stepwise condensation reaction: glycerol reacts with the first fatty acid (releasing water) to form a monoglyceride with an ester linkage; a second condensation reaction with another fatty acid forms a diglyceride; a third condensation reaction with a third fatty acid forms the complete triglyceride, with three water molecules released in total across the three ester bonds formed.

Glycerol can also bond with a phosphate group plus two fatty acids (rather than three fatty acids) to form a phospholipid, which plays a central role in plasma membranes.

Differences between saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids

Double bonds cause a bend (“kink”) in the hydrocarbon chain, which prevents neighbouring chains from packing closely and interacting strongly, weakening the intermolecular forces between molecules and so lowering the melting point. Saturated fats are built exclusively from saturated fatty acids (major constituents of butter and cocoa butter). Unsaturated fats contain one or more unsaturated fatty acids; where a fatty acid’s carbon chain has a single double bond it is a monounsaturated fatty acid, and where it has two or more double bonds it is a polyunsaturated fatty acid (common in vegetable seed oils such as maize, soya and sunflower oil, and making up about 70% of the lipid in olive oil). Because their kinked tails pack less closely, fats with unsaturated fatty acids melt at lower temperatures than saturated fats, which matters in manufacturing spreadable margarines and butter-type spreads. Polyunsaturated fats matter for the health of arteries.

Figure B1.1.25: Saturated and unsaturated fatty acids and the triglycerides formed from themCompares palmitic acid (C15H31COOH, saturated, straight hydrocarbon tail) with oleic acid (C17H33COOH, unsaturated, with a kink in the tail caused by its double bond), each shown as a structural and space-filling model. It also compares the triglycerides tristearin (built from saturated fatty acids, melting point 72 degrees Celsius) and triolein (built from unsaturated fatty acids, melting point −4 degrees Celsius), showing how the kinks introduced by double bonds prevent close packing and greatly lower the melting point.

Unsaturated fatty acids are used for energy storage in plants and cold-blooded (ectothermic) animals, which benefit from their lower melting points, allowing them to remain liquid at lower environmental temperatures (typically down to around 5 degrees Celsius or lower). Saturated fatty acids are used by warm-blooded (endothermic) animals, which maintain higher body temperatures at which saturated fats remain appropriately liquid.

Health consequences of dietary lipid content

Diets with excess lipids and fatty acids supply more energy than the body needs, leading to storage of excess fat in adipose tissue around body organs and under the skin; chronic overweight increases the likelihood of type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure (hypertension). Diets consistently very low in energy-rich foods (lipids and carbohydrates) also cause major health risks: without sufficient fatty acids, the body respires amino acids from protein digestion instead of using them to build and maintain tissue, so on a continuing low-energy diet muscle proteins are broken down and the body wastes away — a constant danger for nutritionally deprived communities in less economically developed countries.

Triglycerides in adipose tissue: energy storage and thermal insulation

Adipose tissueAdipose tissue is a tissue found beneath the skin layer, containing fat cells (adipocytes). Because fats and oils are insoluble in water, they can be safely stored in cells and tissues without osmotic consequences.

Buoyancy and thermal insulation

Figure B1.1.26: Adipose tissueIllustrates fat cells (adipocytes) that make up adipose tissue, typically found under the skin as subcutaneous fat.

Fat is stored in animals as adipose tissue, typically as subcutaneous fat under the skin; in aquatic diving mammals this layer is thick enough to be identified as blubber. Because fat is less dense than muscle or bone, it gives buoyancy to the body. Where fat reserves under the skin have a restricted blood supply, so that body heat is not conducted to them, subcutaneous fat also functions as a heat-insulating layer — the large fat stores in marine mammals insulate them against heat loss to cold surrounding water.

Energy source and metabolic water

When triglycerides are oxidized in respiration, a large amount of energy is transferred to ATP. Mass for mass, fats and oils release more than twice as much energy as carbohydrates on respiration, because fats are more reduced (have more hydrogen relative to carbon and oxygen) than carbohydrates, and more of the oxygen used in fat oxidation comes from the atmosphere rather than from within the molecule itself (as is the case for carbohydrates). Fat therefore forms a concentrated, insoluble energy store, typical of animals that must endure long, unfavourable seasons using stored reserves; oils are also a major energy store in plant seeds and fruits, commercially used as edible oils (e.g. maize, olives, sunflower). Complete oxidation of fats produces far more metabolic water than the same mass of carbohydrate. Desert animals such as camels and desert rats retain much of this metabolic water to help survive without drinking water; developing bird and reptile embryos also benefit from metabolic water released by oxidizing the fat stored in egg yolk, and whales, surrounded by undrinkable salt water, rely solely on metabolic water.

Formation of phospholipid bilayers

In a phospholipid molecule, two of the three –OH groups of glycerol bond to fatty acids, while the third bonds to phosphoric acid, itself bonded to another small group. This phosphate group is ionized and therefore water-soluble, so phospholipids combine the hydrophobic properties of their hydrocarbon tails with the hydrophilic properties of their phosphate-containing head.

AmphipathicAmphipathic means having both hydrophobic and hydrophilic parts on the same molecule. A phospholipid’s glycerol/phosphate “head” is hydrophilic (readily hydrogen-bonds with water), while its two fatty-acid hydrocarbon “tails” are hydrophobic (repelled by water).
Monolayers and bilayersA small quantity of phospholipid placed on a solid surface (such as a clean glass plate) remains as a discrete bubble, but a similar drop added to water instantly spreads across the entire surface: molecules float with their hydrophilic heads in contact with water and their hydrocarbon tails exposed above and away from the water, forming a monolayer. When more phospholipid is added, the molecules arrange as a bilayer, with the hydrocarbon tails facing each other — the arrangement found in the plasma membrane. Additional attractions between the hydrophobic tails on the inside, and between the hydrophilic heads and surrounding water on the outside, create a stable, strong barrier.
Figure B1.1.27: Phospholipid monolayer and bilayer formation in waterShows a single phospholipid molecule with a hydrophilic head (attracted to water) and a hydrophobic tail (repelled by water). In contact with water, phospholipid molecules form a monolayer, with heads dissolved in the water and tails sticking outward, away from the water surface. When mixed thoroughly with water, phospholipid molecules instead arrange themselves into a bilayer, in which the hydrophobic tails cluster together in the centre, away from water, while the hydrophilic heads face outward on both surfaces, in contact with water.

Cholesterol also forms part of the plasma membrane; it is a steroid with a hydroxyl group and hydrocarbon chain on either side of its carbon ring structure.

Ability of non-polar steroids to pass through the phospholipid bilayer

The lipid bilayer of the plasma membrane is permeable to non-polar substances, including steroids, because non-polar molecules can pass through the fatty acid interior of the membrane, which is itself non-polar. This includes the sex hormones oestradiol and testosterone.

Key termsSteroid: a group of four-ring hydrocarbons, of which cholesterol, oestradiol and testosterone are examples. Oestradiol: a steroid hormone; a sex hormone of female mammals. Testosterone: a steroid hormone; the main sex hormone of male mammals.

Oestradiol coordinates the menstrual cycle and the development of secondary sexual characteristics in females; testosterone drives male secondary sexual development. Both hormones are chemically very similar molecules manufactured from the steroid cholesterol, which is made in the liver and also absorbed from the diet. The most noticeable effect of increased secretion of these sex hormones is stimulation of muscle protein formation and bone growth, so testosterone and oestradiol (along with progesterone) are known as anabolic steroids (“anabolic” meaning “build up”); these effects are less marked for the female sex hormones than for testosterone in males.
