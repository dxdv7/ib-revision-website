Organelles as discrete, specialized subunits

Organelles are discrete (separate) subunits within cells, including nuclei, chloroplasts, mitochondria, vesicles, ribosomes and the plasma membrane. The only organelles common to both eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells are the cell membrane and ribosomes. The cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm are not considered organelles.

Top tipMost organelles are surrounded by membrane and are described as “membrane-bound”, including the rough endoplasmic reticulum, smooth endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and the double membrane surrounding the nucleus. Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotic cells do not.

New experimental techniques often bring breakthroughs in scientific knowledge; the study of individual organelle function became possible only after the invention of the ultracentrifuge and the development of cell fractionation methods for using it. Centrifugation serves two basic purposes in cell biology: as a preparative technique to separate one type of material from others, and as an analytical technique to measure physical properties (such as molar mass, density, shape and binding ability) of macromolecules.

Figure B2.2.1: The principle of centrifugation applied to a cell homogenateBefore centrifugation, a cell homogenate — a suspension of cells with broken plasma membranes but intact organelles — is evenly mixed throughout the tube. After centrifugation, the tube is shown separated into a pellet of larger, more-dense components that have settled at the bottom, and a supernatant of smaller, less-dense components remaining suspended near the top.
How cell fractionation worksA centrifuge spins fluids at very high speed, subjecting suspended particles to centrifugal forces as great as 1,000,000 times the force of gravity, which can sediment particles with molar masses as small as 10,000 g/mol; modern ultracentrifuges reach speeds of 150,000 revolutions per minute or higher. More-dense particles settle at the bottom of the tube while less-dense particles remain higher up. Cell fractionation of animal cell homogenates into their various organelles was first achieved in the 1940s: at low speeds, nuclei fall to the bottom of the tube; at a slightly higher speed, mitochondria can be collected; and at even higher speeds and longer run times, ribosomes can be collected. These techniques let biologists study biological processes, free from the complex side reactions of a living cell, using purified cell-free systems — for example, the mechanism of protein synthesis (translation) was discovered using a cell homogenate able to translate mRNA into protein, and ultracentrifugation was also central to the Meselson–Stahl experiment on DNA replication.
Common mistakeCentrifugation does not separate organelles based on their size, but on their relative density.

Theodor Svedberg, the Swedish chemist who designed the first ultracentrifuge in 1925, studied haemoglobin and found that a centrifuged sample revealed a single, sharp band with a molar mass of 68,000 g/mol, strongly supporting the theory that proteins are true macromolecules rather than mixtures. Svedberg also gave his name to the non-SI Svedberg unit (S), which measures the time taken for a particle of a given size to settle at the bottom of a solution, measured at exactly 10−13 seconds; a particle’s mass, density and shape all affect its S value. This is how ribosomes are differentiated by their sedimentation rate: the 70S ribosome found in chloroplasts, mitochondria and prokaryotes has a sedimentation rate of 70 × 10−13 seconds.

Advantages of separating the nucleus and cytoplasm

The nuclear membrane keeps DNA separate from the rest of the cell, protecting it from the many chemical reactions occurring in the cytoplasm, and it also allows gene transcription and translation to be kept separate in space and time. Many eukaryotic genes contain non-coding sequences: the sections of a gene that carry meaningful, protein-coding information are called exons, while the non-coding sequences that interrupt them are called introns. While genes split in this way are common in higher plants and animals, some eukaryotic genes contain no introns at all.

When a gene containing exons and introns is transcribed, the resulting mRNA at first contains the sequence of introns and exons exactly as they occur in the DNA. If this unmodified mRNA were translated directly, it would cause serious problems during protein synthesis. Instead, an enzyme-catalysed reaction called post-transcriptional modification removes the introns as soon as the mRNA is formed — a process called RNA splicing — leaving a shortened, “mature” mRNA that passes out of the nucleus into the cytoplasm to be translated at the ribosomes. This is an advantage for eukaryotic cells because variants of a protein can be produced from a single gene through different patterns of splicing. In prokaryotes, there is no nuclear membrane separating DNA from the cytoplasm, so mRNA may meet ribosomes immediately; prokaryotic genes therefore do not have introns, and prokaryotic cells lack the enzyme machinery needed for splicing.

Like other cell membranes, the nuclear membrane is a phospholipid bilayer, permeable only to small, non-polar molecules; other molecules cannot diffuse through it directly. Lamins, intermediate protein filaments that are a major component of the cytoskeleton, are important in nuclear membrane function.

Nuclear poreA protein-lined channel in the nuclear envelope that regulates the transport of molecules between the nucleus and the cytoplasm.

The nuclear pores are tiny, about 100 nm in diameter, but so numerous that they make up about one-third of the nuclear membrane’s surface area — suggesting just how important communication between the nucleus and cytoplasm is. Unlike the mechanisms that transport proteins into most other organelles (where proteins must unfold to cross the membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts), nuclear pores can transport proteins in their fully folded conformation, and can transport ribosomal components as already-assembled particles. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is assembled with ribosomal proteins in the nucleolus, and the resulting ribosomal subunits are then transported into the cytoplasm; export of RNA through nuclear pore complexes is an active process that requires energy, since proteins made in the cytoplasm are essential for gene expression to proceed efficiently.

Advantages of compartmentalization in the cytoplasm

Each organelle performs a different function, carrying out a specific biological process and a distinct set of chemical reactions; at the most basic level, biochemical reactions within organelles are what sustain life. Organelles separate the biochemical reactions needed to support the cell’s function, so that each fulfils a different role while all cooperate together. Compartmentalization — the division of the cell interior into separate, discrete areas — allows the correct concentration of metabolites and appropriate enzymes to be maintained for specific metabolic processes, so that otherwise incompatible biochemical processes can be kept separate.

Top tipCompartmentalization means the reactions of life can be controlled without interfering with each other — similar to the way different operations in a factory are carried out in different areas (for example, engine assembly in one area, vehicle-frame assembly in another).

Lysosomes are tiny spherical vesicles bound by a single membrane, containing digestive (hydrolytic) enzymes that are only active in the lysosome’s acidic interior. Because the rest of the cell is neutral to slightly alkaline, this pH-dependence protects the cell from self-digestion if a lysosome should leak or rupture — a clear example of how compartmentalization by lysosomes allows a function (controlled digestion) that would not otherwise be possible or safe within the wider cell interior.

Phagocytosis is a process used by specific cells, such as phagocytic white blood cells, to capture and ingest foreign particles such as bacteria, then digest and destroy them. Phagocytosis proceeds through recognition of the foreign particle by receptors and its ingestion into a phagocytic vacuole; fusion of the phagocytic vacuole with a lysosome to create a phagolysosome; and finally destruction of the ingested particle. The temporary phagocytic vacuole, followed by the phagolysosome, provides a contained space within the cell where antimicrobial activity can occur at low pH without disrupting the cell’s other metabolic processes.

Figure B2.2.3: The process of phagocytosisA phagocytic cell is shown engulfing a bacterium: (1) receptors on the cell surface recognize the bacterium, and the plasma membrane folds around it to form a phagocytic vacuole; (2) the phagocytic vacuole fuses with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome; (3) the phagolysosome breaks down the bacterium, producing soluble debris that is removed from the cell by exocytosis.
Key termsPhagocytosis: the process by which a phagocyte engulfs and destroys foreign substances, such as bacteria. Phagocytic vacuole: a vesicle formed around a particle engulfed by a phagocyte via phagocytosis.

Higher Level Adaptations of the mitochondrion for aerobic respiration

Respiration is a key life process occurring in all cells of all organisms: the breakdown of glucose to release the energy in its bonds, converting this energy to ATP while releasing carbon dioxide and water. Respiration begins in the cytoplasm in an anaerobic process called glycolysis, producing pyruvate, which diffuses into the mitochondria where aerobic respiration takes place.

Mitochondria are formed from two membranes, an inner and an outer membrane. The outer membrane contains transport proteins that move pyruvate into the mitochondrion, where carbon is removed from pyruvate as carbon dioxide (decarboxylation) and hydrogen is also removed (oxidation), splitting into protons (H+) and electrons. The electrons pass down a sequence of proteins in the inner membrane, the electron transport chain (ETC), releasing energy that is used to pump protons into the intermembrane space between the two mitochondrial membranes, where they accumulate quickly and create an electrochemical gradient. As well as the ETC, the inner membrane contains ATP synthase, a transmembrane multi-protein assembly through which accumulated protons pass, generating ATP. The inner membrane is highly folded into structures called cristae, which increase the surface area available for the ETC and ATP synthase, increasing ATP production.

The Krebs cycle — the sequence of enzyme-controlled stages in which hydrogen and carbon atoms are removed from derivatives of glucose — occurs in the matrix of the mitochondria, which contains the appropriate enzymes and pH for this to happen. In this way, mitochondria achieve compartmentalization of the enzymes and substrates of the Krebs cycle within the matrix, and of the ETC and ATP synthase within the highly folded inner membrane.

Figure B2.2.4: Electron micrograph of a mitochondrion, with an interpretive drawingAn electron micrograph of a mitochondrion (with a 1 micrometre scale bar) is shown alongside an interpretive line drawing labelling its structures: a smooth outer membrane forming the boundary of the organelle; an inner membrane that is extensively infolded to form cristae; the space between the inner and outer membranes; and the matrix, which contains the metabolites and enzymes of the Krebs cycle.
Common mistakeIt is inaccurate to say simply that mitochondria carry out “respiration”, since anaerobic glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm. It is correct to say that mitochondria carry out aerobic respiration.

Higher Level Adaptations of the chloroplast for photosynthesis

Just as mitochondria are the site of the Krebs cycle and respiratory ATP formation, chloroplasts are the organelles where the reactions of photosynthesis occur. Chloroplasts, found in green plants, contain photosynthetic pigments along with the enzymes and electron-transport proteins needed for reducing carbon dioxide to glucose and for ATP formation, using light energy.

A chloroplast has a double membrane around it, plus a third inner membrane that folds extensively to form a system of branching membranes called thylakoids. Thylakoid membranes are organized into flat, compact, circular piles called grana (singular, granum), almost like stacks of coins; between the grana are loosely arranged tubular membranes, called lamellae, suspended in a watery stroma.

Figure B2.2.5: The ultrastructure of a chloroplastA diagrammatic view of a chloroplast shows an outer double membrane surrounding a stroma that contains starch grains, lipid droplets and ribosomes, and a system of internal membranes forming stacked grana connected to one another by lamellae of the stroma. An enlarged stereogram of one granum shows its thylakoid membranes as a compact stack of flattened, circular sacs, with chlorophyll pigments sandwiched between the lipids and proteins of the thylakoid membranes. An accompanying electron micrograph (×38,000) of a granum shows these stacked thylakoid membranes, within which the chlorophyll pigments are held.

Chlorophyll, the photosynthetic pigment that absorbs light energy, exists in the grana, where chlorophyll molecules are grouped into structures called photosystems, held in the thylakoid membranes by proteins. The large surface area of the thylakoid membranes optimizes the chloroplast’s ability to harvest light energy: light excites electrons, which pass down an electron transport chain, pumping protons into the thylakoids, where they build up quickly because of the small volume of fluid inside. Protons then flow down their electrochemical gradient through ATP synthase proteins, from inside the thylakoids into the stroma, generating ATP.

This ATP is used in a biochemical process occurring in the stroma, the Calvin cycle, which involves reduction reactions and ultimately produces glucose; the stroma contains the appropriate enzymes and optimum pH for the Calvin cycle. Compartmentalization within chloroplasts therefore separates the light-harvesting activity in the thylakoids (the light-dependent reactions) from the enzymes and substrates of the Calvin cycle in the stroma (the light-independent reactions).

Higher Level Functional benefits of the nucleus double membrane

The nucleus is the largest organelle in a eukaryotic cell, typically 10–20 micrometres in diameter, and is surrounded by two membranes known together as the nuclear envelope. The outer membrane is continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), which makes it easy for the nucleus to obtain proteins made in the ER without needing energy-requiring endocytosis. The double membrane of the nuclear envelope is really one membrane folded to form a double structure, continuous across the inner and outer surfaces of the envelope, which allows the nuclear envelope to connect directly with the ER.

Figure B2.2.6: The nuclear membrane and its connection to the ERThe nuclear envelope is shown as two membranes (an outer surface and an inner surface) enclosing chromatin and a nucleolus within the nucleus, with nuclear pores passing through both membranes. The outer membrane of the nuclear envelope is shown continuous with the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum, which encloses the ER lumen and carries ribosomes on its outer (cytoplasmic) surface.

The nuclear pores are very small, about 100 nm in diameter, but so numerous that they make up about one-third of the nuclear membrane’s surface area, enabling the rapid movement of molecules such as mRNA out of the nucleus and proteins, ATP and some hormones into the nucleus. The inner membrane contains the DNA and nucleolus, and is lined by the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of protein filaments (lamins) that extends into the nucleus and provides structural support; the lamina is present in animal cells but not in plant or fungal cells. The double membrane therefore allows compartmentalization of the nucleus’s functions.

Common mistakeStudents sometimes draw the nuclear membrane as a single line — this is incorrect. The double membrane needs to be drawn clearly, with nuclear pores shown passing through it.

During both mitosis and meiosis, the nuclear envelope dissociates into small vesicles early in cell division. This allows the chromosomes to come into contact with the cytoplasm and the spindle apparatus, which is central to manipulating chromosomes during division.

Figure B2.2.7: Breakdown of the nuclear membrane during mitosisThe nuclear membranes and nuclear lamina of an animal cell are shown breaking apart at the start of mitosis: the nuclear pores and the continuous double membrane separate into numerous small membrane vesicles, some with lamin proteins still bound to them and some free lamins released into the cytoplasm, dismantling the nuclear envelope so the chromosomes can interact with the spindle apparatus.

Higher Level Free ribosomes and the rough endoplasmic reticulum

Many ribosomes occur freely in the cytoplasm, and are the sites of synthesis for proteins that are retained in the cell to fulfil roles there. It is common for several ribosomes to move along the same messenger RNA molecule at once; this structure of mRNA, ribosomes and their growing protein chains is called a polysome, allowing many copies of a particular polypeptide to be produced simultaneously.

Figure B2.2.9: A polysomeA single strand of mRNA in the cytoplasm is shown with several ribosomes attached along its length, each moving along the mRNA in the same direction and each carrying its own partially completed, growing polypeptide chain of a different length, depending on how far along the mRNA that ribosome has travelled.
PolysomeA group of ribosomes translating the same mRNA molecule, indicating that the cell needs multiple copies of a particular polypeptide.

Other ribosomes are bound to the membranes of the endoplasmic reticulum, forming the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER). These bound ribosomes synthesize proteins that enter the lumen of the RER for vesicular transport within the cell, for subsequent secretion from the cell, or for packaging into lysosomes.

Top tipFree ribosomes produce proteins for use within the cell, while ribosomes attached to the RER produce proteins destined for export from the cell, or for movement to the Golgi apparatus for further modification.

Higher Level Structure and function of the Golgi apparatus

The Golgi apparatus is involved in processing and secreting proteins. It consists of a stack-like collection of flattened membranous sacs called cisternae, which vary in number, shape and organization between different cell types. On one side of the stack, vesicles arriving from the RER fuse with the Golgi membranes; on the opposite side of the stack, new vesicles form from swellings at the margins that pinch off, carrying proteins for transport within the cell or for secretion.

Figure B2.2.10: Flow of membrane and proteins from the ER through the Golgi to the plasma membraneRibosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum are shown synthesizing proteins that enter the ER lumen. Vesicles bud off from the RER and fuse with the cisternae of the Golgi apparatus on the side facing the RER. Proteins pass from one cisterna to the next through the stack of the Golgi apparatus, before leaving as vesicles from the opposite face of the stack, which then travel toward and fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing their contents outside the cell.

Proteins enter the Golgi apparatus on the side facing the RER and exit on the side facing the plasma membrane, being transferred from one cisterna to the next along the way. Each cisterna contains different enzymes that catalyse different types of protein modification, including the addition or removal of sugars (glycosylation), the addition of sulfate groups (sulfation) and the addition of phosphate groups (phosphorylation). Some of these Golgi-mediated modifications act as signals that direct proteins to their final destination within the cell, including the lysosome and the plasma membrane.

Higher Level Structure and function of cell vesicles

Membranes must be able to separate and fuse together so that cells and organelles can bring in, transport and release molecules. Many cells take up molecules through receptor-mediated endocytosis: a protein (or protein complex) first binds to a receptor on the cell surface, after which a specialized protein causes the membrane at that point to invaginate (tuck inward). One such specialized protein is clathrin, which polymerizes into a lattice network around the growing membrane bud to form a clathrin-coated pit; the invaginated membrane eventually breaks off and fuses to form a vesicle, budding from either the Golgi body or the plasma membrane.

As clathrin protein molecules assemble on the inside of the membrane, they form a clathrin-coated pit, which progressively invaginates and pinches off to form a clathrin-coated vesicle inside the cell, carrying the receptor-bound substance. The clathrin coat is then shown being shed, leaving a “naked” vesicle free within the cytoplasm.
InvaginationThe intucking of a surface or membrane.

Some hormones, transport proteins and antibodies use receptor-mediated endocytosis to enter a cell; this pathway is also exploited by viruses and toxins to gain entry into cells. The reverse process — the fusion of a vesicle with a membrane — is a critical step in the release of neurotransmitters from a neuron into the synaptic cleft.
