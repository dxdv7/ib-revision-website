An Introduction to the Blood System

Mass flowLiving cells require a supply of water and nutrients (such as glucose and amino acids) and oxygen, and they must remove the waste products of cellular metabolism. In single-celled and very small organisms, internal distances are small, so movement of nutrients can occur efficiently by diffusion. In larger organisms, an internal transport system is required to meet the needs of cells. Mass flow is the movement of fluids down a pressure gradient, and internal transport systems are examples of mass flow.

There are three types of vessel in the circulatory system:

  • arteries – carry blood away from the heart
  • veins – carry blood back to the heart
  • capillaries – fine networks of tiny tubes linking arteries and veins.
Key termsEndothelium: the innermost lining layer of arteries and veins, and the layer of cells that comprise the capillary; also lines the inside of the heart. Lumen: the hollow interior of a blood vessel, through which the blood passes.

Adaptations of Capillaries for Exchange

Capillaries are the smallest blood vessels and serve the tissues and cells of the body. Blood is under lower pressure (about 35 mm Hg) to prevent the walls of the capillaries bursting. They are narrow tubes, about the diameter of a single red blood cell (approximately 8 µm). Because of the low blood pressure, the flow rate of blood is reduced, which increases the rate of exchange of molecules between blood and tissue.

Adaptations of capillariesAdaptations for effective exchange of materials between blood plasma and tissue fluid include:
  • a large surface area due to branching (to increase diffusion)
  • narrow diameters (so that red blood cells are in close contact with the capillary wall, reducing diffusion distance)
  • thin walls (a single layer of endothelial cells) for fast diffusion
  • fenestrations (gaps in the wall) in some capillaries where exchange needs to be particularly rapid; these gaps are large enough to allow some components of blood to escape and contribute to tissue fluid.
The walls of capillaries consist of endothelium only. Capillaries branch abundantly and bring the blood circulation close to cells – no cell is far from a capillary.

Structure of Arteries and Veins

Both arteries and veins have strong, elastic walls, but artery walls are much thicker and stronger than those of veins. The strength of the walls comes from wall thickness and collagen fibres; elasticity is due to elastic and involuntary (smooth) muscle fibres. Compared to wall thickness, the lumen of an artery is relatively small compared to the lumen of a vein – this enables the pressure inside the artery to be maintained. The large lumen of a vein reduces friction between blood cells and the vessel wall, enabling blood to flow freely under low pressure.

Both vessels are labelled with three wall layers, from outside in: the outer layer (tunica externa) of elastic fibres and collagen; the middle layer (tunica media) of elastic fibres, collagen and smooth muscle fibres; and the endothelium (inner lining) of squamous epithelium – a single layer of cells with a smooth inner surface. The hollow centre of each vessel is the lumen. The artery wall is shown much thicker relative to its lumen than the vein wall is relative to its lumen.
Concept: Form and functionThe form of a blood vessel is related to its function – arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, whereas veins have thinner walls because blood is at lower pressure; veins also contain valves to stop blood backflow. Capillary walls are one cell thick to ensure fast diffusion.

Blood leaving the heart is under high pressure and travels in waves or pulses following each heartbeat. By the time blood reaches the capillaries it is under much lower pressure, without a pulse. This difference in blood pressure accounts for the differences in the walls of arteries and veins.

Adaptations of Arteries for Transport Away from the Heart

AortaThe main artery that leaves the heart is called the aorta, which carries blood from the heart to the head and rest of the body. Blood pressure in the aorta is very high – about 120 mm Hg.

Arteries that divide from the aorta distribute blood under high pressure to regions of the body and to the main organs. The layers of muscle and elastic tissue in artery walls help them withstand and maintain high blood pressures.

Adaptations of arteries
  • overall wall thickness/muscle layer thickness to withstand blood pressure and prevent rupture of the artery wall
  • a thick layer of elastic tissue to even out and maintain blood pressure
  • walls that stretch to accommodate the surge of blood when ventricles contract
  • elastic tissue and collagen fibres of the tunica externa (outer layer) prevent rupture as blood surges from the heart
  • the high proportion of elastic fibres first stretches and then recoils, keeping the blood flowing and propelling it forwards after each pulse passes
  • with increasing distance from the heart, the tunica media (middle layer) progressively contains more smooth muscle fibres and less elastic tissue, as less stretching and recoiling occurs due to smaller differences in blood pressure
  • varying constriction and dilation of arteries maintains blood flow
  • muscle fibres stretch and recoil, evening out pressure, but a “pulse” can still be detected.

Arteries divide into smaller blood vessels called arterioles, which deliver blood to the tissues. Arterioles have a high proportion of smooth muscle fibres, so they can regulate blood flow from arteries into capillaries.

1 mm Hg = 0.13 kPa

Measurement of Pulse Rates

Ventricular contractions of the heart force a wave of blood through the arteries. The expansion of arteries can be felt as a pulse, particularly where the artery is near the skin surface and passes over a bone. The pulse can be taken from the radial artery above the wrist, or from the carotid artery on the neck (located below the ear, on the side of the neck directly below the jaw).

Taking a pulseUse two fingers (not the thumb, which has its own pulse) to compress the artery. Count for a full minute for the most accurate result, or count for 30 seconds and multiply by 2, to determine heart rate in beats per minute. Digital methods, such as data loggers or smartwatches/fitness bands that scan blood flow near the wrist using LEDs, can also be used to measure pulse.

Adaptations of Veins for Return to the Heart

Because of the low pressure in veins, there is a possibility of backflow. Veins have valves at intervals that prevent this. Pressure from the blood opens the valves, which are made from a strong protein called collagen and other material. At the end of a heart contraction, as blood tries to flow backwards, blood pressure pushes against the valves, causing them to close. This ensures blood only moves in one direction, back towards the heart. Valves are especially common in the veins of the limbs, and are also found in the heart.

Figure B3.2.5: Valves in veins prevent backflow of bloodTwo paired diagrams of a vein showing a valve. In the first, blood flows towards the heart and the valve is opened by blood pressure from behind; pressure from movements of surrounding tissues, including contraction of muscles, compresses the vein and helps push blood along. In the second, blood flow is shown reversed, and the valve is closed by blood pressure from in front, preventing backflow.

Veins have thin walls, with the external layer (tunica externa, with elastic and collagen fibres) the thickest layer. The middle layer (tunica media) contains a few elastic and muscle fibres; because of the low blood pressure these do not need to be extensive. The wall of the vein is flexible, allowing surrounding muscles to compress the vein, which helps keep blood moving.

VenuleBlood flows into veins from smaller vessels called venules, formed by the union of several capillaries, with a blood pressure of about 15 mm Hg. Veins have the lowest blood pressure of any blood vessel – approximately 5 mm Hg.

Occlusion of the Coronary Arteries

AtherosclerosisOcclusion means the blockage or closing of a blood vessel. Diseases of the heart and blood vessels are primarily due to a condition called atherosclerosis – the deposition of plaque (a cholesterol derivative) in the inner wall of blood vessels – which causes occlusion of the coronary arteries (the arteries supplying blood to the heart muscle) and progressive degeneration of the artery walls.
Figure B3.2.6: Atherosclerosis, leading to a thrombusA comparison of a healthy artery (pale, smooth endothelium lining, elastic and flexible wall, unobstructed flow of blood) with a diseased artery, which shows plaque built up beneath the endothelium narrowing the lumen, and a blood clot (thrombus) formed where the atheroma has broken through the endothelium, restricting blood flow. An accompanying photomicrograph of a diseased human artery in transverse section shows the artery wall with blood flow greatly impeded by a massive atheroma.
Steps to atherosclerosis
  • Damage to artery walls occurs as fatty tissue (atheromas) is deposited under the endothelium, significantly reducing the diameter of the lumen.
  • Raised blood pressure follows as fat deposits and fibrous tissue impede blood flow, damaging the arterial wall.
  • The damaged region is repaired with fibrous tissue, significantly reducing the elasticity of the vessel wall.
  • Lesion formation occurs when the smooth lining of the artery breaks down; these lesions are called atherosclerotic plaques.
  • If the plaque ruptures, blood clotting is triggered. A blood clot formed within a blood vessel is a thrombus; when a thrombus breaks free and circulates in the bloodstream it is called an embolus.

An embolus may be swept into a small artery or arteriole narrower than the diameter of the clot, causing a blockage. The blood supply to tissue downstream of the block is then deprived of oxygen, and without oxygen tissues die. The coronary arteries, particularly those going to the left ventricle, are especially vulnerable. When sufficient heart muscle dies this way, the heart may cease to be an effective pump – a “heart attack” (myocardial infarction) has occurred. The plaque does not deposit on top of the artery wall, but forms within it, beneath the damaged endothelium.

Damaged coronary arteries can be surgically bypassed in a heart bypass operation, typically using a blood vessel taken from the patient’s leg; survival rates for this increasingly common operation are high.

An adult human on a low-cholesterol diet forms about 800 mg of cholesterol in the liver each day. However, diets high in saturated fatty acids (animal fats) often lead to abnormally high blood-cholesterol levels. Excess blood cholesterol, present as LDL, may cause atherosclerosis, increasing the likelihood of blood clot formation and circulation, leading to strokes and heart attacks.

Epidemiological vs clinical studiesClaims about links between health problems and their causes are based on: epidemiological studies, which provide circumstantial evidence of health risks – they suggest connections but do not establish a cause or biochemical connection; and clinical studies of individual patients, which attempt to show causal relations between diseases and diets (though it is not possible to carry out truly “controlled” experiments on humans for ethical reasons).

Even strong correlations, such as that between saturated fat intake and coronary heart disease, do not prove a causal link. When evaluating evidence for CHD claims, strengths to look for include statistically significant correlations, comparison of means with statistical assessment of differences, and analysis of the spread of the data (more widely spread data reduces the significance placed on a correlation). Limitations to consider include whether the health measure used was valid, sample size and representativeness, whether data came from animal rather than human trials, whether other dietary variables were controlled, and the rigour of the data-gathering method (e.g. the reliability of self-reported survey data). Correlation coefficients quantify the strength of a relationship between variables; a low or absent correlation coefficient can provide evidence against a hypothesis, but even a strong correlation does not prove causation.

Transport of Water from Roots to Leaves

Transpiration is the evaporation of water from the spongy mesophyll tissue of the leaf and its subsequent diffusion out through the stomata – an inevitable consequence of gas exchange in the leaf. The plant must transport water from roots to leaves to replace transpiration losses. Each part of the plant has a specific role: the stem supports leaves in the light and transports organic materials, ions and water between roots and leaves; the root anchors the plant and absorbs water and ions from the soil; the leaf is a photosynthetic organ, consisting of a leaf blade connected to the stem by a leaf stalk.

The transpiration stream

The cohesion–tension theoryWater that evaporates from the walls of mesophyll cells is continuously replaced, mostly from the water in nearby cell walls and then from xylem vessels. This loss of water from cell walls draws water out of xylem vessels by capillary action. As water leaves xylem vessels in the leaf, a tension (negative pressure potential) is set up in the whole water column in the xylem. This tension draws water up the xylem and is transmitted down the stem to the roots because of the cohesion of water molecules. Cohesion and adhesion ensure a continuous, unbroken column of water under tension. This explanation of upward water movement is the cohesion–tension theory. Water flow in the xylem is therefore always upwards; this flow is known as the transpiration stream.

Evidence for the cohesion–tension theory: if a xylem vessel is pierced by a fine needle, a bubble of air immediately enters the column (interrupting water flow) – if the xylem contents had been under pressure rather than tension, a jet of water would instead be released. Further evidence comes from measurement of tree trunk diameter over 24 hours: a large tree shows detectable shrinkage in trunk diameter during the day as xylem vessels narrow under tension (though collapse is prevented by lignified wall thickening); the trunk recovers at night when transpiration virtually stops.

Advantages of transpiration

Transpiration is primarily a consequence of plant structure, nutrition and gas exchange rather than a valuable process in itself – the living plant acts as a “wick” that steadily dries the soil around it. However, it confers advantages: evaporation of water from leaf cells in the light has a strong cooling effect (energy is needed to break hydrogen bonds between water molecules as water vapour forms); the stream of water travelling up the xylem passively carries dissolved ions absorbed from the soil solution by root hairs, required in the leaves and growing points; and lateral movement of water from xylem vessels (via pits) hydrates all living cells of the plant, providing the turgor pressure that supports the whole leaf and, in non-woody plants, the entire aerial system.

Movement of water in the xylem

LigninXylem begins as cells with cellulose walls and living contents, connected end to end. During development the end walls dissolve away, so mature xylem vessels become long, hollow tubes. The living contents are used up in depositing cellulose thickening on the inside of the lateral walls, hardened by deposition of lignin – a complex polymer in which cellulose microfibrils are embedded, giving great strength and rigidity. This makes xylem extremely tough and able to resist negative pressure (suction) without collapsing.

Adaptations of Xylem Vessels for Water Transport

The structure of xylem vessels is seen in transverse (TS) and longitudinal (LS) sections of plant stems; in prepared, stained slides, cell walls strengthened with lignin stand out particularly clearly. Xylem have thickened, lignin-strengthened walls; lignin is waterproof, stopping water escaping from the vessels. Pits in the walls allow entry of water and lateral movement between vessels. After xylem vessels form, the cell contents (cytoplasm, nucleus and other organelles) are removed and the cells join end to end to form dead, hollow tubes.

Concept: FunctionThe adaptations of xylem allow: unimpeded flow of water and minerals (lack of cell contents and incomplete or absent end walls); the ability to withstand tension (lignified walls); and areas for entry and exit of water (pits).

Movement of Water Into and Through the Root

Water uptake occurs in the root system, but the movement of water up the stem is driven by conditions in the leaves. The root system provides a huge surface area in contact with soil because roots branch continually and grow at each root tip, pushing through the soil to access the dilute soil solution around soil particles. Contact with soil is vastly increased by the region of root hairs just behind the growing tip of each root; root hairs are extensions of individual epidermal cells and are relatively short-lived, with fresh soil resources being exploited as root growth continues.

In older parts of the root the walls of the outermost cells become waxy, preventing water uptake in these regions. An enlarged individual mature root hair is shown in contact with soil particles and a surface film of water, with its nucleus, cytoplasm and vacuole labelled, alongside pockets of soil air (essential for healthy root growth). A cross section of the mature root shows the stele (central vascular tissue, containing xylem and phloem) surrounded by a single-cell-layer endodermis, with a high-power detail of the stele also shown.
Three pathways of water movement
  • Apoplast pathway: mass flow of water through the interconnecting free spaces between cellulose fibres of plant cell walls (about 50% of wall volume), and through the water-filled spaces of dead cells and hollow xylem vessels. This pathway entirely avoids the living contents of cells and is the most significant route of water movement through the plant.
  • Symplast pathway: diffusion through the cytoplasm of cells and via cytoplasmic connections between cells (plasmodesmata), with osmosis occurring through the cell membrane. Many organelles in the cytoplasm resist water flow, slowing movement relative to the apoplast pathway.
  • Vacuolar pathway: osmosis from vacuole to vacuole of cells, driven by a gradient in osmotic pressure. Active uptake of mineral ions in roots causes water absorption by osmosis. Not a significant pathway for bulk water transport across the plant, but is how individual cells absorb water.
While all three routes are open, the bulk of water crosses from the root epidermis to the xylem via the apoplast pathway.

The apoplast pathway of water movement (mass flow through space in the wall cellulose, about 50% of wall volume) is shown alongside the symplast pathway (through living cytoplasm, by diffusion, including through cytoplasmic connections between cells), with the plasma membrane (selectively permeable), nucleus, cellulose cell wall and vacuole all labelled in the root hair cell and adjoining root cortex cells.
Casparian stripThe endodermis is a single layer of cells, unique to the root, that surrounds the vascular tissue. At the endodermis, a waxy strip in the radial walls, called the Casparian strip, blocks the passage of water by the apoplastic route. Water must instead pass through the endodermis symplastically, by osmosis. This diversion is significant because the cytoplasm of endodermal cells actively transports ions from the cortex to the endodermis, raising the ion concentration (and osmotic potential) at the centre of the root, which causes passive water uptake by osmosis to follow. All transport must pass through the symplast at the endodermis, allowing control over substances reaching the xylem – though this restriction can be bypassed (“bypass flow”) at the root tip, where endodermis is not yet formed, and where lateral roots emerge.

Higher Level Tissue Fluid: Release and Reuptake in Capillaries

Tissue fluidThe blood circulation delivers nutrients and oxygen to body tissues as blood flows through capillaries between cells. Tiny gaps in capillary walls (which vary in size in different parts of the body) allow a watery liquid, similar in composition to plasma, to pass through – this is tissue fluid: a mixture of water and solutes, forced out of the blood by ultrafiltration, which surrounds body cells. Red blood cells, platelets and most blood proteins are too large to pass through and are retained in the capillaries. Tissue fluid supplies nutrients to cells and carries away carbon dioxide and waste products of metabolism.
Forces for exchange in capillariesGiven the dissolved solutes in plasma (including plasma proteins), the water potential of blood would be expected to limit water loss and even draw water back in by osmosis. However, the force applied to the blood by the heart creates enough hydrostatic pressure (mechanical pressure exerted on or by a liquid, also called pressure potential) to overcome osmotic water uptake at the arteriole end of the capillary bed, where blood pressure is much higher than at the venule end. As blood flows through the capillary bed there is a progressive loss of water and fall in hydrostatic pressure, so that by the venule end there is a net inflow of tissue fluid back into the capillary. As a result, about 90% of tissue fluid returns to the plasma by this route. Blood proteins, particularly albumins, cannot escape the capillaries; they maintain the water potential of the plasma, preventing excess water loss and assisting fluid return.
Figure B3.2.18: Forces for exchange in capillariesA diagram of a capillary running from its arteriole end to its venule end, with tissue fluid surrounding it. At the arteriole end, high hydrostatic pressure drives ultrafiltration of water and small molecules (oxygen, glucose, amino acids) out into the tissue fluid, following the diffusion gradient for oxygen and nutrients required by respiring cells – this produces net outflow, only partly opposed by osmotic movement of water back into the capillary. Along the length of the capillary, hydrostatic pressure is progressively reduced. By the venule end, the diffusion gradient favours movement of waste metabolites (e.g. carbon dioxide) into the capillary, and osmotic movement of water back into the capillary now dominates over the much-reduced hydrostatic pressure, producing net inflow.

Higher Level Exchange Between Tissue Fluid and Cells

Blood plasma is a straw-coloured liquid forming around 55% of blood, and is largely composed of water (95%); water is a good solvent, allowing many substances to be transported in the plasma. Both plasma and tissue fluid contain water and dissolved solutes, but there are many differences in composition – tissue fluid contains far fewer proteins, since many plasma proteins are too large to pass through gaps in capillary walls.

Higher Level Drainage of Excess Tissue Fluid into Lymph Ducts

Lymphatic systemMolecules too large to enter capillaries are removed from tissues via the lymphatic system – a network of fine capillaries throughout the body that drain lymph and return it to the blood circulation. Tiny valves in the walls of lymph capillaries permit fluid entry. The network of lymph capillaries drains into larger lymph ducts (lymphatics); lymph is moved along the lymphatics by contractions of smooth muscle in their walls and by compression from body movements, with backflow prevented by valves, as in veins.

Lymph finally drains back into the blood circulation in veins close to the heart. At intervals along the way, lymph passes through kidney-shaped lymph nodes, part of the body’s defences against disease, where fixed macrophages engulf bacteria and cell debris by phagocytosis, and where certain cells of the immune system (lymphocytes and plasma cells) are made.

Tissue fluid is formed by pressure filtration (ultrafiltration) of plasma in capillaries, promoted by the higher pressure of blood from arterioles; lower pressure in the venules allows tissue fluid to drain back into capillaries. Of the tissue fluid that does not return directly to capillaries, about 10% instead becomes lymph.

Higher Level Single Circulation of Fish vs Double Circulation of Mammals

Mammals and fish both have a closed circulation, in which blood is contained inside blood vessels and pumped by a powerful, muscular heart in one direction from the heart through the system before returning to the heart.

Single vs double circulationBony fish have a single circulation: blood passes through the heart once in each complete circuit – it enters from the body, is pumped to the gills to be oxygenated, and then flows directly to the rest of the body before returning to the heart, via a two-chambered heart. Mammals have a double circulation: blood passes twice through a four-chambered heart in each complete circuit. The right side of the heart pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs (the pulmonary circulation) to be oxygenated, returning it to the left side of the heart, which pumps oxygenated blood around the rest of the body (the systemic circulation) and back to the right side.
Figure B3.2.21: Closed circulatory systems of bony fish and mammalsTwo diagrams comparing closed circulation systems. The single circulation of fish shows blood pumped by a two-chambered heart to the gills first, then on to the rest of the body, passing once through the heart in each complete circulation. The double circulation of mammals shows blood passing twice through a four-chambered heart in each complete circulation, via separate pulmonary circulation (to the lungs) and systemic circulation (to body tissues).
Figure B3.2.20: The circulatory systems of a) fish, b) amphibians and c) mammalsThree schematic diagrams comparing circulatory plans. a) Fish: a single-circuit heart with one ventricle (V) and one atrium (A) pumping blood through gill capillaries and then systemic capillaries in series. b) Amphibians: a heart with one ventricle but two atria, with a partial separation of pulmonary/skin circulation (lung and skin capillaries) from the systemic circuit, allowing some mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood. c) Mammals: a fully divided heart with two ventricles and two atria, with a completely separate pulmonary circuit (to lung capillaries) and systemic circuit (to systemic capillaries), preventing mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.

The major advantages of the mammalian double circulation are simultaneous high-pressure delivery of oxygenated blood to all regions of the body, and that oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix, so oxygenated blood reaches respiring tissues undiluted.

In the systemic circulation, organs are supplied with blood by many arteries, all branching from the aorta. Within each organ, arteries divide into arterioles, which supply capillary networks; capillaries drain into venules, which join to form veins, which join the vena cava, returning blood to the heart. The branching sequence is: aorta → artery → arteriole → capillary → venule → vein → vena cava. Vessels are often named after the organs they serve – for example, the liver is supplied by the hepatic artery, and also receives blood directly from the small intestine via the hepatic portal vein, which brings products of digestion absorbed by the villi capillaries.

Higher Level Adaptations of the Mammalian Heart

An adult human heart is about the size of a clenched fist, lying in the thorax between the lungs, beneath the sternum. It is a hollow, muscular organ contained in a tough, non-elastic membrane called the pericardium, which anchors the heart within the thorax. The heart wall is well supplied with oxygenated blood from the aorta via the coronary arteries.

Cardiac muscle consists of cylindrical, branching columns of fibres, forming a unique three-dimensional network that allows contraction in three dimensions. Each fibre has a single nucleus and appears striated under the microscope; fibres are surrounded by a special plasma membrane, the sarcolemma, and are well supplied by mitochondria and capillaries.

Chambers of the heartThe heart cavity is divided into four chambers, with the right side completely separate from the left. The two upper chambers are thin-walled atria, which receive blood into the heart. The two lower chambers are thick-walled ventricles, with the left ventricle wall much thicker than the right, although the volumes of blood on the right and left sides are identical. The ventricles pump blood out of the heart.
Figure B3.2.23: The structure of the heartTwo views of the heart. The external view, from the front with the pericardium removed, is labelled with the vena cava from the head, vena cava from the lower body, right and left pulmonary arteries, right and left pulmonary veins, the aorta, and the coronary artery and coronary vein on the heart’s surface. The internal (longitudinal section) view is labelled with the pacemaker (sinoatrial node) in the right atrium wall, the right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, semilunar valves, tendinous chords (anchoring the atrioventricular valves to the ventricle walls), cardiac muscle, and the septum separating the right and left sides.
The valves of the heartHeart valves prevent backflow of blood, maintaining one-way flow, and are opened and closed by differences in blood pressure – opening when pressure on the input side exceeds that on the output side, and closing when pressure on the output side exceeds that on the input side. The atrioventricular valves are large valves that prevent backflow of blood from the ventricles into the atria; their edges are supported by tendons (chordae tendineae, or “heart strings”) anchored to the ventricle walls, preventing the valves folding back under the huge pressure of each heartbeat. On the right side is the tricuspid valve; on the left is the bicuspid valve. A different type of valve, the pocket-like semilunar valves, separate the ventricles from the pulmonary artery (right) and aorta (left), stopping backflow into the ventricles as they relax between beats.
Figure B3.2.24: The role of the heart valvesA four-part sequence diagram illustrating a full cardiac cycle. During diastole (atrial and ventricular), the muscles of atrial and ventricular walls are relaxed, pressure in both chambers is low, the semilunar valves are shut, and blood flows passively from the veins into the atria and ventricles. During atrial systole, atrial walls contract, raising atrial pressure; the venae cavae and pulmonary veins close; the atrioventricular valves are pushed open and blood flows into the still-relaxed, low-pressure ventricles; meanwhile high pressure in the aorta and pulmonary arteries keeps the semilunar valves shut. During ventricular systole, the ventricular walls contract strongly, raising ventricular pressure; the atrioventricular valves close and the semilunar valves open, pushing blood into the aorta and stretching the arteries; the atrial walls relax and atrial pressure falls, with the venae cavae and pulmonary veins now open so blood flows into the atria again.
Concept: FunctionThe structures of the mammalian heart enable: separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood (the septum separating left and right atria and ventricles); continuing contraction of muscle throughout life, in multiple directions (cardiac muscle); one-directional blood flow, from atria to ventricles and then to the arteries (atrioventricular and semilunar valves); supply of blood to the heart muscle itself (coronary arteries); and control of heart contraction (the pacemaker) – cardiac muscle is myogenic, meaning it is self-excitable and does not require electrical impulses from the central nervous system.

Higher Level Stages in the Cardiac Cycle

Cardiac cycleThe cardiac cycle is the sequence of events of a heartbeat by which blood is pumped around the body. The heart beats at about 60–100 times per minute, so each cycle is about 0.8 seconds long, divided into two stages: systole (contraction of heart muscle) and diastole (relaxation of heart muscle). When chamber walls contract, chamber volume decreases, raising pressure on the blood and forcing it towards a region of lower pressure; valves prevent backflow, so blood always flows in one direction through the heart.
Sequence of events (left side of heart)
  1. Atrial systole (about 0.1 s): atrial walls contract, pushing blood past the atrioventricular valve into the ventricles, which are under low pressure. Backflow from the aorta into the ventricle is prevented by the closed semilunar valves; backflow from the atria into the vena cava and pulmonary veins is prevented because atrial wall contraction seals off these veins (which also contain valves of their own).
  2. Ventricular systole (about 0.5 s): the ventricle contracts, generating high pressure that slams shut the atrioventricular valve (producing the “lub” heart sound) and opens the semilunar valve, forcing blood into the aorta and generating a detectable pulse. This is followed by relaxation of the atrium (atrial diastole, about 0.7 s).
  3. Ventricular diastole: the ventricles relax; falling ventricular pressure allows the aortic pressure to close the semilunar valve (producing the “dup” heart sound), and the bicuspid valve reopens as ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure.

Origin and control of the heartbeat

Steps in control of the cardiac cycle
  • The heartbeat originates in the sinoatrial node (SA node), or pacemaker, in the wall of the right atrium.
  • A wave of excitation (electrical impulses) spreads out across both atria, causing the muscle of both atrial walls to contract simultaneously (atrial systole).
  • A narrow band of non-conducting fibres at the base of the atria blocks the excitation wave from spreading immediately to the ventricles. Instead, the stimulus is picked up by the atrioventricular node (AV node) at the base of the right atrium.
  • After a delay of 0.1–0.2 s, excitation passes from the AV node via the bundle of His (heart muscle involved in electrical conduction) to the base of both ventricles via tiny bundles of conducting fibres called Purkinje tissue.
  • When stimulated, ventricle muscle starts to contract from the base of the heart upwards (ventricular systole).
  • The AV node delay allows the atria to finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles contract, preventing simultaneous contraction of atria and ventricles.
  • After every contraction, cardiac muscle has a refractory period – a period of insensitivity to stimulation (enforced diastole) during which the heart passively refills with blood; this relatively long period enables the heart to beat throughout life.
Figure B3.2.27: Control of heart rateA diagram of the heart’s internal conducting system, labelling the sinoatrial node in the right atrium wall, the atrioventricular node at the base of the right atrium, the atrioventricular bundle (bundle of His), the right and left bundle branches running down the septum, and the Purkinje fibres spreading from the bundle branches up through the walls of both ventricles. The arch of the aorta, right and left atria and right and left ventricles are also labelled for orientation.

The heart’s own intrinsic rhythm, set by the SA node, is about 60–100 beats per minute; this can be overridden by conditions in the body, with the resting rate of 50–60 beats per minute potentially rising to up to 200 beats per minute during very strenuous exercise. The delay at the AV node (about 100 ms) results from: AV node cells taking longer to become excited than SA node cells; the smaller diameter of AV cells slowing conduction; fewer sodium ion channels and a more negative resting potential in AV cells than SA cells; and fewer gap junctions in the intercalated discs of AV node cells. This delay allows the atria to contract fully, adding to the volume of blood delivered to the ventricles, and prevents simultaneous contraction of atria and ventricles – which would otherwise prevent the chambers filling properly, reducing cardiac output and blood pressure.

Higher Level Generation of Root Pressure in Xylem Vessels

Root pressureEndodermal cells of the root contain suberin, a waterproof substance that blocks the apoplast pathway (in addition to the Casparian strip), directing water through the symplast pathway so that water flows into the xylem and not out. Endodermal cells also actively pump salts into the xylem, lowering the water potential of the xylem (increasing solute concentration) so water moves down a water potential gradient into the xylem by osmosis. This creates a positive pressure potential, moving water and dissolved minerals a short distance up the xylem – this is called root pressure: force generated in the roots that helps drive water upwards into xylem vessels.

When high humidity prevents transpiration, or in spring before deciduous leaves have opened, root pressure is generated to cause water movement in roots and stems when transpiration-driven transport is insufficient.

Common mistakeDiffusion is not the main method of mineral absorption in roots – active transport is the means by which minerals are absorbed. If plants are able to absorb water by osmosis, they must have higher solute concentrations inside their cells than outside, which can only be achieved by active transport.

Higher Level Phloem Sieve Tubes and Companion Cells for Translocation

Phloem structurePhloem tissue consists of sieve tubes and companion cells, served by transfer cells in the leaves. Sieve tubes are narrow, elongated elements connected end to end to form tubes; their end walls, sieve plates, are perforated by pores. A mature sieve tube’s cytoplasm has no nucleus and lacks many other organelles. Each sieve tube is connected to a companion cell by plasmodesmata passing through pits in the walls; companion cells are believed to service and maintain the cytoplasm of the sieve tube, which has lost its nucleus.

A high-power LS shows a sieve tube element with end walls perforated as a sieve plate, containing a lining layer of cytoplasm with small mitochondria and some endoplasmic reticulum but no nucleus, ribosomes or Golgi apparatus, connected by plasmodesmata (cytoplasmic connections) to an adjacent companion cell, whose cytoplasm contains a nucleus, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus. A sieve plate in surface view shows its perforating pores, and an accompanying electron micrograph in LS shows a companion cell alongside a sieve plate.

Phloem is a living tissue with a relatively high rate of aerobic respiration during transport; transport of manufactured sucrose and amino acids in the phloem is an active process using energy from metabolism.

The process of translocation

Translocation and the pressure–flow hypothesisTranslocation is the movement of manufactured food (e.g. sucrose and amino acids) in phloem tissue. At a source (e.g. mesophyll cells in the leaf, where sugar is made and accumulates), sucrose is actively loaded into companion cells and then into sieve tube elements; this raises the solute potential, so water follows by osmosis, creating high hydrostatic pressure in the sieve tubes of the source area. At a sink (e.g. roots, where sucrose may be converted into insoluble starch deposits), sucrose flows out of the sieve tubes, lowering solute potential; water then diffuses out and hydrostatic pressure falls. According to the pressure–flow hypothesis, this difference in hydrostatic pressure between source and sink drives mass flow of sugar solution down the pressure gradient along the sieve tube. Sources and sinks can vary by season – for example, roots act as sources after winter dormancy, mobilizing stored starch to sucrose for transport to growing leaves (the sinks).
