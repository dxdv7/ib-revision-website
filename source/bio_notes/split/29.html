DNA Replication: An Overview

Key term DNA replication: the production of exact copies of DNA with identical base sequences.

A copy of each chromosome must pass into daughter cells formed by cell division, so the chromosomes must first be copied (replicated). DNA replication produces exact copies of DNA with identical base sequences, and is required both for reproduction and for growth and tissue replacement in multicellular organisms. The base pairing of DNA allows this process to proceed accurately and, in general, without errors — although errors (mutations) do occur occasionally.

Concept: continuity DNA replication enables the genetic code of an organism to be copied into new cells. This allows continuity within the genome of the organism and ensures the genetic blueprint is passed on to new cells in a way that maintains their integrity and function. DNA replication takes place in the interphase nucleus, well before the events of nuclear division.

Semi-Conservative Nature of DNA Replication

Key term Semi-conservative replication: each strand of an existing DNA double helix acts as the template for the synthesis of a new strand from free nucleotides.

At the end of DNA replication, each new pair of DNA strands reforms as a double helix. One strand of each double helix comes from the original chromosome, and one is newly synthesized. This arrangement is semi-conservative replication, because half of the original molecule stays the same. If replication were “conservative”, one DNA molecule formed would consist of two entirely new strands and the other of the two original strands.

Figure D1.1.1: Semi-conservative versus conservative replication Compares two hypothetical models across generations of parent DNA. If replication were conservative, an entirely new double helix would form alongside the original in the first generation, so that one original double helix molecule would remain fully intact (unzipped) even in later generations. In the actual, semi-conservative model, one strand of each new double helix comes from the parent chromosome and one is newly synthesized, so that half of the original molecule is conserved in each first-generation molecule, and this pattern continues (with an increasing proportion of entirely new DNA) into the second generation. The figure notes that Watson and Crick proposed semi-conservative replication, and that this was later confirmed experimentally using bacterial DNA labelled with a heavy nitrogen isotope.

Complementary base pairing, together with the retention of one strand of the original DNA double helix, allows a high degree of accuracy in copying base sequences.

Nature of science: the evidence for semi-conservative replication

Watson and Crick proposed semi-conservative replication but had no direct evidence for it — scientific knowledge must be supported by evidence. Meselson and Stahl designed an experiment to test this model. They first grew a culture of the bacterium E. coli in a medium where the only available nitrogen was the heavy isotope 15N, so the bacterium’s DNA became entirely “heavy”. These cells were then transferred to a medium containing the lighter, more abundant isotope 14N (both are stable, non-radioactive isotopes of nitrogen); newly synthesized DNA was now made using 14N. The change in the ratio of 15N to 14N in DNA of successive generations was measured (bacterial cell divisions in an E. coli culture are naturally synchronized, dividing every 60 minutes).

DNA extracted from samples of each generation was separated by placing it on top of a salt solution of increasing density in a centrifuge tube. On centrifuging, DNA molecules moved down to the level where the salt solution matched their own density: DNA with heavy nitrogen ended up nearer the base of the tube, while DNA with light nitrogen stayed nearer the top — allowing the proportions of heavy, light and hybrid (one heavy, one light strand) DNA in each generation to be identified, confirming the semi-conservative model.

Role of Helicase and DNA Polymerase

Key terms Helicase: an enzyme that unwinds the DNA double helix at replication forks.
DNA polymerase: an enzyme that links nucleotides together to form a new DNA strand, using the pre-existing strand as a template.

The process of DNA replication described here is the (simpler) prokaryotic system. The first step is disruption of the hydrogen bonds holding the two DNA strands together (“unzipping”). The enzyme helicase unwinds the DNA double helix at one region, breaking the hydrogen bonds there and temporarily keeping the strands separated, exposing unpaired nucleotides surrounded by a pool of free-floating nucleotides.

Next, both strands act as templates: complementary nucleotides line up opposite each exposed base (adenine with thymine, cytosine with guanine), and hydrogen bonds form between the complementary bases, holding them in place. Finally, a condensation reaction links the sugar and phosphate groups of adjacent nucleotides, forming the new strands; this reaction is catalysed by the enzyme DNA polymerase. DNA polymerase also has a “proofreading” role, correcting mistakes such as incorrectly paired bases as they occur, so that the two strands formed are identical to the original.

Figure D1.1.2: DNA replication Shows a length of DNA double helix with a replication site opening along it. Helicase unwinds the helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds (“unzips” the molecule), exposing free nucleotides present in excess at the replication site. The steps shown are: (1) the DNA molecule unwinds and unzips as hydrogen bonds break; (2) the single strands act as templates for new strands; (3) free nucleotides with complementary bases slot into place by complementary base pairing and are held by hydrogen bonds; (4) the sugar–phosphate “backbone” is formed by a condensation reaction, catalysed by DNA polymerase, which attaches the new polynucleotide strands; (5) the replicated (daughter) DNA molecules each rewind into a double helix as replication sites join up.
Common mistake It is incorrect to say that “helicase is in charge of elongation of DNA”. Helicase only unwinds DNA; DNA polymerase then acts on the exposed single strands.

Nature of science: tools shape the knowledge produced

Research on human DNA replication has benefited from using the DNA virus SV40 as a “tool”. The initial use of the viral SV40 system in vitro identified polymerases and other proteins but entirely missed identifying helicase, since this activity was supplied by the SV40 large T-antigen — the tool used gave only partial knowledge of DNA replication. A more recently developed tool, cryogenic electron microscopy (CEM), gives atomic-resolution images of proteins that can be reconstructed in three dimensions, allowing researchers to see the shapes replication proteins adopt as they perform their biological function.

PCR and Gel Electrophoresis as Tools for Amplifying and Separating DNA

Key terms Polymerase chain reaction (PCR): a technique for amplifying selected regions of DNA by multiple cycles of DNA synthesis.
Taq polymerase: heat-stable DNA polymerase enzyme used in PCR, extracted from the thermophilic bacterium Thermus aquaticus.
Gel electrophoresis: a process used to separate fragments of DNA or proteins, on a polymer gel, according to size and overall charge.

DNA can be analysed to identify the individual it was taken from — used, for example, in forensic science (identifying someone from a blood or body fluid sample, or from pollen/plant fragments) and to establish genetic relatedness, or whether individuals of an endangered species were bred in captivity or captured in the wild. Sometimes only a very small sample of DNA is available: the discovery of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) allows fragments of DNA to be copied repeatedly, faithfully and quickly, in a fully automated process. A heat-tolerant DNA polymerase, Taq polymerase, obtained from an extremophile bacterium, is used; note that Taq polymerase does not perform proofreading, so mistakes in complementarity may occur.

Once DNA has been amplified, fragments can be separated using gel electrophoresis so the genotypes of different individuals can be compared.

Top tip! Taq polymerase is named after the heat-tolerant bacterium it was isolated from, Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs and hydrothermal vents. This heat stability makes Taq polymerase ideal for PCR, since temperature cycling is central to the technique.

Polymerase chain reaction

In PCR, double-stranded DNA is amplified (many copies are made) entirely automatically, in vitro. A single DNA molecule is sufficient as starting material. The process involves a repeated heating and cooling cycle, each repetition (in the presence of excess nucleotides) doubling the number of copies of the original DNA.

Figure D1.1.3: The polymerase chain reaction Shows the PCR cycle in six numbered steps: (1) double-stranded DNA with known end sequences is the starting material; (2) the two strands are separated by heating to 95°C; (3) short single-stranded primers, made with base sequences complementary to the end sequences of the fragment to be copied, are added and the mixture cooled to 40°C to let primers bind to each strand; (4) heat-tolerant Taq polymerase and excess nucleotides are added and the mixture heated to 72°C, so nucleotides are added to the primers using the single strands as templates; (5) two new double-stranded DNA molecules result; (6) the whole process is repeated using the two new double strands as templates, doubling the DNA again each cycle. The figure notes that primers are short synthetic single strands of DNA complementary to the 3′ end of the target DNA, and that DNA polymerase synthesizes a new strand only in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

Two limitations of PCR are noted: it requires prior knowledge of the DNA or amino acid sequence of the target gene or protein in order to design the flanking primers; and because primers are short sequences, they may not be specific enough, so a non-target DNA sequence can sometimes be amplified instead of the intended one.

Gel electrophoresis

Gel electrophoresis is used to separate molecules such as proteins and nucleic acid fragments. It is central to DNA sequencing (determining the order of bases in a length of DNA) and to genetic profiling (identifying individual organisms or species). In electrophoresis, DNA or RNA fragments are separated according to their overall charge and mass, on the basis of two factors: differential migration through a supporting medium (agarose gel, a very pure form of agar, or polyacrylamide gel), whose tiny pores act as a molecular sieve so small fragments move faster than large ones; and the electrical charge carried by the molecules — the phosphate groups of DNA give it a net negative charge, so in an electric field DNA migrates towards the positive pole (anode).

DNA fragments of different lengths are produced by restriction enzymes, which cut at specific base sequences wherever these occur along the DNA. A series of wells are cut near one end of the gel, which is submersed in a conducting salt (buffer) solution; small samples are loaded into the wells (several mixtures can be separated on one gel at a time).

Figure D1.1.4: Electrophoretic separation of DNA fragments Shows the electrophoresis apparatus: a gel (of agarose or polyacrylamide) sits in a reservoir of buffer solution, connected to a power supply (maximum voltage about 45 volts) via a negative electrode (carbon fibre) and a positive electrode. DNA samples, treated with a restriction enzyme, are loaded into wells cut near one end of the gel. Once the potential difference is applied, DNA fragments move through the gel towards the positive electrode, with smaller fragments migrating faster/further than larger fragments, so the DNA separates into bands of different-sized fragments (a DNA electrophoretogram). The bands are made visible after the run by adding a dye that binds to and colours the DNA.

After separation, fragments are not immediately visible (they are tiny and transparent). They can be identified using: gene probes — single-stranded DNA with a base sequence complementary to a particular fragment or gene of interest, made radioactive (so exposure to X-ray film reveals its position) or fluorescently labelled (so it fluoresces distinctively under UV light); or stains, which immediately locate all DNA fragments once applied — ethidium bromide (a potential carcinogen, fluorescent under short-wave UV once bound to DNA) or methylene blue (less sensitive, and the colour fades quickly).

Top tip! Gel electrophoresis can be used to separate fragments of proteins as well as DNA.

Applications of PCR and Gel Electrophoresis

Key terms Genetic profiling: the identification of individual organisms or species using DNA.
Variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs): short base sequences that show variation between individuals in number of repeats; these major lengths of non-coding DNA are used in genetic profiling.

Genetic profiling (“genetic fingerprinting”) is used to identify organisms, species or individuals using DNA, combining both PCR and gel electrophoresis. Most of our DNA does not code for proteins; extensive non-gene regions contain short base sequences repeated many times, some scattered along the DNA and many joined together in major clusters. These non-coding, “nonsense” sequences, called satellite and microsatellite DNA, or variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs), are used in genetic profiling. We each inherit a distinctive combination of VNTRs, half from our mother and half from our father, giving each of us (except identical twins) a unique nucleotide pattern.

To produce a genetic profile, a DNA sample is cut with a restriction enzyme acting close to the VNTR regions, and electrophoresis separates the fragments by length, producing a pattern of bands.

Figure D1.1.5: Steps to genetic profiling Shows Southern blotting: DNA extracted and cut into fragments with a restriction enzyme is separated by electrophoresis, then the gel is treated with alkali to make the fragments single-stranded. The fragments are transferred (blotted) from the gel onto a nylon membrane by drawing buffer solution up through the gel and membrane using layers of blotting paper, a glass block and weight, with the buffer travelling from a buffer tray, through a wick, through the gel and membrane, into the blotting paper. Heat treatment then binds the DNA copies permanently to the membrane. A radioactively labelled DNA probe (an artificially synthesized single strand of DNA complementary to a sequence of interest, made using nucleotides containing radioactive phosphorus-32 or carbon-14) is added; where its sequence is complementary to a bound DNA fragment, the strands pair and the probe binds, and excess unbound probe is washed away. The membrane is then overlaid with X-ray film, which is selectively “fogged” by radioactive emission from the bound probe; developing the film reveals the position (as dark bands) of the DNA fragments to which the probe has bound.
Concept: change Although the genetic code is relatively consistent within a species, changes occur so that individuals have different alleles from one another, including in the number of short tandem repeats (STRs) and VNTRs. These differences allow genetic profiling techniques to identify similarities and differences between DNA samples from different individuals.

Applications of genetic fingerprinting

  • Identification of suspects: DNA samples are taken from crime scenes and profiled alongside samples from victims, suspects, and uninvolved individuals used as controls; the greatest care is needed to avoid any cross-contamination.
  • Identification of corpses: bodies too decomposed for visual recognition, or body parts remaining after violent incidents or natural disasters, can be identified by comparing DNA profiles with those of close relatives or with DNA recovered from the person’s belongings.
  • Determining paternity: DNA samples from possibly related people are analysed side by side and their banding patterns compared; since a child inherits half its DNA from its mother and half from its father, bands in the child’s profile that do not match the mother’s must come from the father.
Figure D1.1.6: DNA profiles used to investigate relatedness Shows two applications side by side. In forensic investigation, DNA profiles from a crime scene sample are compared against profiles of several suspects (S1–S4); a matching banding pattern identifies which suspect should be interviewed further. In paternity/family investigation, DNA profiles of a mother (F), children (C) and a possible father (M) are compared: bands in a child’s profile are first matched against the mother’s profile and discounted; any remaining, unmatched bands in the child’s profile should match those of the biological father.

Nature of science: reliability through repeated measurement

Reliability is enhanced by increasing the number of measurements made. In DNA profiling, increasing the number of markers used (DNA sequences with a known physical location on a chromosome, including single-nucleotide polymorphisms, SNPs, and STRs) reduces the probability of a false match, since more bands give more points of comparison between profiles.

Top tip! Short tandem repeats (STRs) can also be used in DNA profiling. An STR is a section of DNA organized as repeating units of 2 to 13 nucleotides, whereas VNTR repeating units consist of 10 to 100 nucleotides. Both STRs and VNTRs are widely used in forensic studies.

Higher LevelDirectionality of DNA Polymerases

The DNA double helix has direction: one chain runs 5′ to 3′, while the other runs 3′ to 5′ — the two chains are antiparallel, which matters both for replication and for transcription of the genetic code into mRNA.

DNA polymerase requires a free 3′–OH group to start synthesizing a new strand, adding the 5′ end of a new nucleotide to the 3′ end of the existing chain. This means it can synthesize in only one direction: it moves along the template strand in the 3′ to 5′ direction while synthesizing the new strand in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

Top tip! Higher Level DNA replication of the new strand occurs only in the 5′ to 3′ direction, because the shape of the active site of DNA polymerase requires the 3′–OH end of the template strand to initiate replication.

The proofreading mechanism of DNA replication also explains why DNA polymerases synthesize only in the 5′ to 3′ direction: a hypothetical polymerase synthesizing 3′ to 5′ would be unable to proofread, because removing an incorrectly paired nucleotide would leave a chain that could no longer be elongated. For a DNA polymerase to act as a self-correcting enzyme, removing its own errors as it moves along the DNA, it must proceed only in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

Higher LevelLeading Strand and Lagging Strand Replication

Key terms RNA primer: a short length of RNA made at the beginning of the synthesis of each DNA fragment; subsequently removed and filled in with DNA.
DNA polymerase III: the primary enzyme involved in DNA replication.
Leading strand: at a replication fork, the DNA strand made by continuous synthesis in the 5′ to 3′ direction.
Primase: an RNA polymerase that uses DNA as a template to produce an RNA fragment that serves as a primer for DNA synthesis.
Lagging strand: a single DNA strand that is discontinuously replicated in the opposite direction to the replication fork, forming Okazaki fragments.
Okazaki fragments: short lengths of DNA produced on the lagging strand during DNA replication; adjacent fragments are rapidly joined by DNA ligase to form a continuous strand.
DNA ligase: an enzyme that joins two DNA fragments together via the formation of a 3′–5′ phosphodiester bond.

Replication is initiated by an RNA primer — probably a “relic” of the RNA world. Semi-conservative replication is actually initiated at many points along the DNA double helix, called replication forks, where the strands separate (a “bubble” forms) through the action of helicase. DNA gyrase assists by relieving the strain caused as the double helix is unwound, while single-strand binding proteins attach to the separated strands and prevent them from rejoining. The unwound sections of both strands then act as templates for synthesis of new, complementary strands.

Both strands are replicated simultaneously, but because DNA polymerase III can add nucleotides only to a free 3′ end, strands can elongate only in the 5′ to 3′ direction. Because the two template strands are antiparallel, the details of replication differ between them.

Higher LevelLeading strand

The exposed 5′ to 3′ strand is the leading strand. DNA polymerase III adds nucleotides by complementary base pairing to the free 3′ end of the new strand, in the same direction as the moving replication fork. This process is continuous, occurring immediately behind the advancing helicase as fresh template is exposed. The initial nucleotide chain formed is a short length of RNA, the primer, synthesized by the enzyme primase; new DNA then starts from the 3′ end of this RNA primer.

Top tip! Higher Level An RNA primer is required to start synthesis of a new strand because DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to an existing chain with a free 3′–OH group. Primase, unlike DNA polymerases, does not require a free 3′–OH end, so it can synthesize a short complementary RNA primer from scratch, giving DNA polymerase the starting point it needs.

Higher LevelLagging strand

On the lagging strand, replication is discontinuous, proceeding in the direction opposite to the moving replication fork. A series of relatively short lengths of DNA, called Okazaki fragments, are formed, each primed separately — this is necessary so that both strands can be replicated at the same time. Unlike the leading strand, which is initiated by a single RNA primer, the lagging strand must be initiated repeatedly, so there are multiple primers/primase actions along it. The enzyme DNA ligase joins together adjacent Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand.

Figure D1.1.7: The steps of DNA replication Higher Level Shows the DNA double helix uncoiled for clarity around a replication fork, with antiparallel parent strands. Replication is initiated at many points; helicase uncoils the DNA at the replication fork, while DNA gyrase and single-strand binding proteins prevent the separated strands from rejoining. On the leading strand, RNA primase adds a short RNA primer once, and DNA polymerase III then adds nucleotides continuously (from a “pool” of free nucleotides, attaching by base pairing) in the 5′ to 3′ direction of replication, matching the direction the fork is opening. On the lagging strand, RNA primase repeatedly adds short RNA primers at intervals, and DNA polymerase III synthesizes short Okazaki fragments from each primer (again 5′ to 3′, but overall running away from the fork); DNA polymerase I then removes each RNA primer and replaces it with DNA, and ligase seals the gaps between adjacent Okazaki fragments. Eventually all the replication forks along the DNA molecule join up, producing two complete daughter DNA molecules.

Nature of science: discovery of Okazaki fragments

Okazaki fragments are named after Japanese molecular biologists Reiji and Tsuneko Okazaki, who discovered them using a pulse–chase experiment: replicating DNA was exposed to a brief “pulse” of isotope-labelled nucleotides, followed by a “chase” period of unlabelled nucleotides of varying length. With short chases, labelled radioactivity appeared mostly in small, slowly sedimenting (“slow”) DNA fragments; as chase length increased, radioactivity increasingly appeared in larger, faster-sedimenting (“fast”) DNA, with little further increase in the slow DNA. This showed that short fragments of newly made DNA on the lagging strand are progressively joined into longer, continuous strands over time.

Higher LevelFunctions of enzymes in replication

In summary (for the prokaryotic system): an RNA primer is formed by primase; DNA polymerase III then attaches nucleotides to the primer, forming a fragment; DNA polymerase I replaces the RNA nucleotides at the start of each fragment with DNA nucleotides; and finally DNA ligase joins the Okazaki fragments together. In this way, short lengths of DNA are synthesized and joined into a continuous strand.

Higher LevelDNA Proofreading

Key term Proofreading: the process by which DNA polymerase corrects its own errors as it moves along DNA.

Proofreading of DNA is carried out by prokaryotes and eukaryotes alike. DNA polymerase III removes any mismatched base from the 3′ terminal, then replaces it with a correctly matched nucleotide. Proofreading ensures mismatched nucleotides are removed before replication proceeds further; it is carried out by a nuclease activity that cleaves the phosphodiester bond. Polymerization and proofreading are coordinated, carried out by different catalytic domains within the same polymerase molecule.

DNA proofreading mechanism Higher Level
  1. DNA polymerase occasionally adds an incorrect (mismatched) base to the growing strand, opposite a template base it does not correctly pair with.
  2. The polymerase’s proofreading (nuclease) activity detects the mismatch and removes the incorrect base from the 3′ end of the new strand, cleaving the phosphodiester bond.
  3. The polymerase then adds the correct, complementary base in its place, and normal 5′ to 3′ synthesis continues.
Figure D1.1.8: DNA proofreading Higher Level Shows a template DNA strand being copied by DNA polymerase, with the growing new strand ending in a free 3′–OH group. In one panel, an incorrect base has just been added, mismatched with the template base opposite it. In the next panel, the incorrect base is removed by the polymerase’s proofreading activity. In the final panel, the correct, complementary base has been added in its place, restoring correct base pairing along the whole strand, so that replication can continue accurately.
