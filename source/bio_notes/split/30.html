Genes, Proteins and the Genetic Code

Proteins are linear sequences of amino acids condensed together. Most proteins contain several hundred amino acid residues, but all are built from only 20 different amino acids. The unique properties of a protein depend on (1) which amino acids are involved in its construction and (2) the sequence in which these amino acids are condensed together. The sequence of bases in DNA dictates the order in which specific amino acids are assembled and combined.

The DNA molecule in each chromosome is very long and codes for a large number of proteins. Within this molecule, the relatively short lengths of DNA that code for single proteins are called genes. Proteins vary greatly in size and, therefore, so do genes: a very few genes are as short as 75–100 nucleotides, but most are at least 1000 nucleotides long, and some are much longer.

Key term Gene expression is the process by which the information encoded in a gene is used to direct the synthesis of a protein molecule. Not all genes in a cell are expressed at any given time – typically less than 25% of the protein-coding genes in human cells are expressed at any one moment, even though every nucleus in a multicellular organism contains the full genome. Transcription, being the first stage of gene expression, is a key stage at which expression of a gene can be switched on and off.

Transcription

All proteins are formed in the cytoplasm, either at free-floating ribosomes or at ribosomes embedded in the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER). A mobile copy of the information in a gene must therefore be made and transported to these sites. That copy is made of RNA and is called messenger RNA (mRNA). It is formed by a process called transcription, catalysed by the enzyme RNA polymerase, which forms a complementary copy of the genetic code of a gene.

Key terms Transcription: the synthesis of messenger RNA using a DNA template.
RNA polymerase: the enzyme that catalyses the synthesis of RNA molecules from a DNA template.

Coding strand versus template strand

Information in DNA lies in the sequence of the bases cytosine (C), guanine (G), adenine (A) and thymine (T). This sequence determines which specific amino acids are assembled and combined. Of the two strands of the DNA double helix, only one – the template strand (also called the non-coding strand) – is used to synthesize mRNA. The other strand, the coding strand, is not used as a template, but its base sequence corresponds to that of the mRNA produced (except that the mRNA contains uracil where the coding strand has thymine).

Coding strand vs template strand The coding strand is not used to make mRNA, but its sequence matches the mRNA sequence (with U replacing T). The template strand is the strand actually read by RNA polymerase, in the 3’ to 5’ direction, to synthesize mRNA by complementary base pairing. A common mistake is to assume the coding strand is the one copied – it is the template strand that serves this role.

Role of hydrogen bonding and complementary base pairing in transcription

In transcription, the hydrogen bonds between the complementary strands of DNA are broken, allowing the DNA double helix of a particular gene to unwind. There is always a pool of free nucleotides (nucleoside triphosphates) present in the nucleus. One strand of the DNA, the template strand, becomes the template for transcription: a single-stranded molecule of RNA is formed by complementary base pairing, with free RNA nucleotides aligning against the template strand and being held temporarily by hydrogen bonds before being joined together by RNA polymerase. In RNA synthesis, uracil pairs with adenine (rather than thymine). The mRNA strand leaves the nucleus through nuclear pores and passes to ribosomes in the cytoplasm, where the information can be “read” and used in protein synthesis.

Process: transcription
  1. RNA polymerase binds to DNA and the hydrogen bonds between the two DNA strands are broken, unwinding the double helix of the gene being transcribed.
  2. One strand, the template strand, is exposed and read by RNA polymerase in the 3’ to 5’ direction.
  3. Free RNA nucleotides align opposite their complementary bases on the template strand (A with U, T with A, C with G, G with C) and are held in place temporarily by hydrogen bonds.
  4. RNA polymerase links the 5’ end of each new nucleotide to the 3’ end of the growing RNA strand, synthesizing mRNA in the 5’ to 3’ direction.
  5. The DNA double helix reforms behind the moving RNA polymerase as the mRNA strand is released.

The template strand (read in the 3’ to 5’ direction) is shown paired with a pool of free nucleotides that align by complementary base pairing and are held by hydrogen bonds. Behind the enzyme, the DNA helix reforms as the polymerase moves along, while ahead of it the double helix is unwound. The result is a single mRNA strand, a copy of the coded information of the gene, that is a complementary copy of the template strand and matches the coding strand (except U replaces T).

Stability of DNA templates

The sugar–phosphate backbone of the DNA molecule ensures the stability of the base sequence, and hydrogen bonds between the two strands of the double helix maintain the integrity of the molecule. Single DNA strands are used as the template for transcribing the base sequence without the DNA base sequence itself changing. It is essential that the same code is replicated exactly from one DNA molecule to the next, so that cells retain the same genome throughout the life of the organism. Some somatic cells, such as neurons, skeletal and cardiac muscle cells, mature bone cells (osteocytes) and retinal receptor cells, do not divide during the lifetime of the organism. In such cells, the base sequence must be conserved throughout the life of the cell to ensure the ongoing functioning of the cell through transcription and translation.

Transcription and the expression of genes

Organisms control which of their genes are expressed at any one moment; this genetic information is used selectively both during development and in the mature organism. The expression of genes is related to when and where the proteins they code for are needed.

Translation

The sequence of bases in DNA, transcribed to mRNA, is expressed at the ribosomes to form proteins. The genetic code determines the sequence in which amino acids are assembled, and hence the polypeptide produced. Gene expression, completed in the process of translation at ribosomes, is therefore a function of the complementary base pairing seen in nucleic acids.

Key term Translation: the information of mRNA is decoded into protein (an amino acid sequence).

Roles of mRNA, ribosomes and tRNA

Amino acids from the pool available for protein synthesis are activated by combining with short lengths of a different type of RNA, transfer RNA (tRNA). It is tRNA that translates a three-base sequence into an amino acid. All tRNAs have a clover-leaf shape, but there is a different tRNA for each of the 20 amino acids. At one end of a tRNA molecule is a site where one particular amino acid can be bonded covalently; at the other end is a sequence of three bases called an anticodon, which is complementary to the codon of mRNA that codes for that amino acid.

The amino acid becomes attached to its tRNA by a specific enzyme in a reaction that also requires ATP. This process, amino acid activation, takes place in the cytoplasm. The specificity of the enzymes ensures the correct amino acids are used in the right sequence. Ribosomes are made from two subunits: in translation, mRNA binds to the small subunit of the ribosome, allowing two tRNAs to bind simultaneously to the large subunit.

Figure D1.2.3: An amino acid is linked to a specific tRNA The diagram shows amino acid1 combining with ATP in the presence of a specific enzyme1, releasing AMP and two inorganic phosphate groups (2Pi), and forming a tRNA–amino acid1 complex. The tRNA specific for amino acid1 carries an anticodon specific for that amino acid: three consecutive bases in the tRNA that are complementary to a codon on the mRNA (for example, the anticodon AAA is complementary to the mRNA codon UUU).

Complementary base pairing between tRNA and mRNA

A ribosome moves along the mRNA, “reading” the codons from the start codon. The sequence of three bases in the codon is complementary to the sequence of bases in the anticodon: in the ribosome, for each mRNA codon, the complementary anticodon of the tRNA–amino acid complex slots into place and is held temporarily in position by hydrogen bonds. While held there, the amino acids of neighbouring tRNA–amino acid complexes are joined by a peptide linkage. This frees the first tRNA, which moves back into the cytoplasm for reuse, and the ribosome moves on to the next mRNA codon. This continues until a ribosome meets a stop codon.

Key term Anticodon: a set of three consecutive nucleotides in a tRNA molecule that recognizes, through base pairing, the three-nucleotide codon on a messenger RNA molecule.
Figure D1.2.4: Translation The diagram shows a strand of mRNA (having come from the nucleus) being translated at a ribosome. Reading begins at a start codon (AUG, coding for Met) and continues towards a stop codon (UGA). A tRNA–amino acid complex carrying glutamic acid (Glu), with its anticodon exposed, is shown arriving so that its anticodon forms complementary base pairs, held by hydrogen bonds, with the mRNA codon being read. The ribosome acts as a supporting framework and as the site for the enzymes that catalyse peptide bond formation.

The growing polypeptide chain

During translation, the ribosome moves in steps along the mRNA, moving three bases (one codon) at a time. Consecutive amino acids are linked together via condensation reactions, forming peptide bonds. By these repeated steps, a polypeptide is formed and elongated, eventually emerging from the large subunit. Several ribosomes may move along the same mRNA molecule at once; this structure of mRNA, ribosomes and growing protein chains is called a polysome.

Features of the genetic code

All organisms use the same codons to specify the placement of each of the 20 amino acids in protein formation. This supports the idea of a common origin of life on Earth – that the first DNA has sustained an unbroken chain of life from the first cells to all organisms alive today, with only the most minor variations arising since life originated around 3500 million years ago. The processes of reading the code and synthesizing protein, using RNA and ribosomes, are very similar in prokaryotes and eukaryotes. This is called the universality of the genetic code.

Most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon; this is known as the degeneracy of the genetic code. The degenerate code provides enough different combinations to code for all amino acids and makes allowance for the possibility of mutation. If the third nucleotide in a triplet changes through mutation, it is possible that the amino acid coded for will not change, because for some amino acids the first two bases alone provide enough information. This means the third nucleotide can be relatively tolerant to mutation, reducing the chance that a synthesized protein will be altered when a base mutates.

Key term Degenerate code: the genetic code contains more codons than there are amino acids to be coded for, so most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon. (Note: “degenerate” here does not mean “deteriorated” – it means several codons share the same meaning, e.g. any codon beginning UC codes for serine regardless of the third base.)

The code uses three, not two, bases per codon. A codon size of two would give only 16 possible combinations – not enough to code for all 20 amino acids, with no allowance for further growth. A triplet code gives 64 combinations – more than enough to include all amino acids, with capacity to cope with any expansion in the number of amino acids used during evolution.

Mutations That Change Protein Structure

DNA can change: normally the sequence of nucleotides is maintained without change, but occasionally alterations do happen. If a change occurs, a mutation has taken place. A point mutation occurs when a single base pair is added, deleted or changed (e.g. A for T, or C for G) within a DNA molecule. Because the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide is controlled by coded instructions stored in DNA and mediated via mRNA, changing just one amino acid in a protein’s sequence can alter its properties, often quite drastically.

Key term Point mutation: occurs in DNA when a single base pair is added, deleted or changed.

Worked example: sickle-cell anaemia

Haemoglobin, the oxygen-transporting pigment of red blood cells, is made of four polypeptide molecules – two called alpha haemoglobin and two called beta haemoglobin – that interlock to form a compact molecule. The gene coding for beta haemoglobin, on chromosome 11, is prone to a change of base A to T in the codon for the sixth amino acid, normally glutamic acid. This base substitution causes the amino acid valine to appear at that position instead.

The non-polar valine creates a hydrophobic spot in the otherwise hydrophilic outer surface of the protein. This attracts other haemoglobin molecules to bind to it, and in tissues with low partial pressures of oxygen (e.g. tissue with a high rate of aerobic respiration), the sickle-cell haemoglobin molecules readily clump together into long fibres within capillaries. These fibres distort red blood cells into sickle shapes that cannot transport oxygen effectively and may block small capillaries, preventing normal circulation. This unusual haemoglobin is called haemoglobin S. People with a single allele for haemoglobin S have sickle-cell trait and are only mildly anaemic; those with both alleles have sickle-cell anaemia, a serious condition that may also trigger heart and kidney problems.

Higher Level

Directionality of Transcription and Translation

The phosphate groups along each strand of the DNA double helix bridge the carbon-3 of one sugar molecule and the carbon-5 of the next. One chain runs from 5’ to 3’ while the other runs from 3’ to 5’. As with DNA polymerase, polymerase enzymes work in the 5’ to 3’ direction: in transcription, RNA polymerase synthesizes the RNA strand in the 5’ to 3’ direction while reading the template DNA strand in the 3’ to 5’ direction. RNA polymerase requires a free 3’–OH group to start synthesis of a new RNA strand, adding the 5’ end of an incoming RNA nucleotide to the 3’ end of the growing strand. In translation, the codons of mRNA are likewise read from the 5’ end to the 3’ end by tRNAs, and polypeptide chains are synthesized from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.

Higher Level

Initiation of Transcription at the Promoter

In eukaryotes, before mRNA can be transcribed by RNA polymerase, the enzyme first binds together with a small group of proteins called general transcription factors at a sequence of bases known as the promoter. Promoter regions occur on DNA just before the start of a gene’s base sequence (the promoter is itself an example of non-coding DNA with a special function). Transcription of the template strand can only begin once this transcription complex (enzyme plus factors) has assembled. Once initiated, RNA polymerase moves along the DNA, untwisting the helix and exposing DNA nucleotides so RNA nucleotides can pair and the mRNA strand can be formed and released.

Key term Promoter: a DNA sequence that initiates gene transcription; it includes sequences recognized by RNA polymerase.

The rate of transcription may be increased (or decreased) by the binding of specific transcription factors at the enhancer site of a gene, located some distance “upstream” of the promoter and gene sequence. When activator proteins bind to the enhancer, a new complex is formed that makes contact with the polymerase–transcription factor complex at the promoter, increasing the rate of gene expression. Regulator transcription factors, activators and RNA polymerase are all proteins, coded for by other genes – illustrating that protein–protein interactions play a key part in the initiation of transcription.

Higher Level

Non-Coding Sequences in DNA

Protein-coding sequences account for only approximately 1.5% of human DNA. Some further DNA sequences regulate the expression of protein-coding genes (regulatory DNA sequences), but this still leaves around 70% of DNA with other or no obvious roles (at one time these regions were described as “junk” or “nonsense” DNA). These extensive non-gene regions of eukaryotic chromosomes include:

  • Introns: non-coding nucleotide sequences, one or more of which interrupt the coding sequences (exons) of eukaryotic genes.
  • Telomeres: special nucleotide sequences, typically multiple repetitions of one short sequence, occurring near the ends of DNA molecules to “seal” the ends of linear DNA and stop erosion of genes that would otherwise occur with each round of replication.
  • Genes for tRNA: parts of the DNA template that code for relatively short lengths of RNA, formed in the nucleus and passed to the cytoplasm, where they transfer amino acids from the pool there to a growing polypeptide at a ribosome.
  • Major lengths of non-coding DNA: short sequences of bases repeated many times; where these occur together in major clusters they are known as variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs), which are exploited by genetic engineers in DNA profiling (genetic fingerprinting).
Figure D1.2.10: Eukaryotic genes consist of exons and introns The diagram shows a single gene as a length of DNA in which coding sequences (exons) alternate with non-coding sequences (introns) along its length.

Telomeres

Telomeres protect an organism’s genes from being lost with each cycle of DNA replication, which otherwise leaves a gap at the 5’ end of each replicated DNA strand. They protect chromosomal ends from degradation by binding proteins to form telomere caps, and prevent the ends of chromosomes from attaching to each other, which would otherwise activate the cell’s DNA-damage monitoring system and trigger apoptosis (programmed cell death). Recognition sites for the enzyme telomerase also enable telomeres to be lengthened.

Key term Telomere: made of repetitive sequences of non-coding DNA, located at the end of chromosomes, that protect the chromosome from damage.
Higher Level

Post-Transcriptional Modification in Eukaryotic Cells

The sections of a gene that carry meaningful information (coding for amino acids) are called exons; the intervening non-coding sequences are called introns. While genes split in this way are common in higher plants and animals, some eukaryotic genes contain no introns at all. When a gene consisting of exons and introns is transcribed, the resulting mRNA initially contains the sequence of introns and exons exactly as they occur in the DNA – but if this unmodified mRNA were translated at a ribosome, it would cause serious problems for protein synthesis. Because introns are non-coding, they must be removed from the mRNA.

Key term Post-transcriptional modification: changes that occur to a newly transcribed mRNA after transcription has occurred and before it is translated into a protein.
Process: RNA splicing
  1. Introns in the pre-mRNA are spliced out by a spliceosome – a large RNA–protein complex with enzymatic properties (itself under the control of a gene).
  2. The spliceosome recognizes highly conserved splice sites between the 3’ end of an exon and the 5’ end of an intron, and cleaves the phosphodiester bond between the relevant nucleotides.
  3. All the exons are joined together to form the coding sequence for one polypeptide from the gene.
The resulting shortened mRNA is described as mature; it is this form that passes into the cytoplasm to the ribosomes for protein synthesis. Note that the genes of prokaryotes have no introns, and prokaryotic cells lack the enzyme machinery for splicing – so a eukaryotic gene copied into a bacterial chromosome by a genetic engineer must be intron-free.
Key term Cleave: a reaction that breaks one of the covalent sugar–phosphate linkages between nucleotides that form the sugar–phosphate backbone of a nucleic acid.
Figure D1.2.11: Post-transcriptional modification of mRNA The diagram shows mRNA, produced by transcription, containing both exons and the short lengths of intervening “nonsense” sequence called introns. Enzymes present in the nucleus (forming the spliceosome) remove the introns from the mRNA in a process called splicing. What remains, the exons, are joined up to form mature mRNA, which then passes out of the nucleus through pores in the nuclear membrane to ribosomes in the cytoplasm.

Polyadenylation and 5’ capping

To complete post-transcriptional modification, both ends of the mRNA are modified to increase the molecule’s stability. At the 3’ end, the pre-mRNA is cleaved to free a 3’ hydroxyl group, and the enzyme poly-A polymerase adds a long chain of adenine nucleotides (the poly-A tail, typically 100–250 residues long) in a process called polyadenylation; this section of mRNA is not itself encoded in the genome. The 5’ end is capped by the addition of a modified guanine nucleotide. Together, these modifications produce a mature mRNA that is protected from enzymatic degradation and can be exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm for translation.

Higher Level

Alternative Splicing of Exons

The lengths of mRNA (exons) remaining after introns are removed may be spliced together in different combinations, so that a single gene can code for more than one type of polypeptide. This process is called alternative splicing. Many genes give rise to two or more different polypeptides depending on the order in which exons are assembled, and even more variety can result if an intron is sometimes treated as an exon during RNA processing. As a consequence, the number of proteins produced by an organism can be greater than the number of its genes – which may help explain why the human genome has a similar (low) number of genes to some small invertebrate animals. This process is itself under the control of specific genes, often located on other chromosomes.

Key term Alternative splicing: the process of splicing exons in different combinations to produce variants of a protein from a single gene.
Figure D1.2.12: Alternative RNA splicing The diagram shows mRNA produced by transcription, with introns “edited out” between three exons (labelled 1, 2 and 3). It illustrates that these exons can be assembled in alternative combinations – for example all three in order (1-2-3), or with an exon skipped (e.g. 1-3 without exon 2) – each combination coding for a different polypeptide product from the same original gene.

Worked example: the troponin T gene

Alternative splicing of transcripts of the troponin T (TnT) gene in foetal and adult heart muscle illustrates this process. Troponin plays a central role in the contraction of vertebrate striated muscle. The alternative splicing of the TnT gene is developmentally regulated: foetal heart tissue uses one form of spliced mRNA, and as the heart matures a different form is used in the adult. The two resulting mRNAs are translated into different but related muscle proteins, known as isoforms, that prepare the heart for the increased demand experienced at birth.

Higher Level

Initiation of Translation

Activation of amino acids

The 20 amino acids used in protein synthesis are each activated by combination with a specific tRNA, in a reaction requiring ATP. There are 20 different tRNA molecules, one for each amino acid. All tRNAs have a clover-leaf shape but differ in the anticodon sequence exposed on one of the “clover leaves”, which is complementary to a codon of mRNA. The enzyme catalysing formation of the amino acid–tRNA complex, a tRNA-activating enzyme, recognizes only one type of amino acid and its corresponding tRNA (there are 20 different tRNA-activating enzymes).

Process: amino acid activation
  1. A specific amino acid and a molecule of ATP bind to a tRNA-activating enzyme; the amino acid is activated by hydrolysis of ATP and bonding of AMP (adenosine monophosphate), releasing a linked pair of phosphate groups (P-Pi).
  2. The tRNA specific to that amino acid binds to the active site of the enzyme.
  3. The amino acid binds to the attachment site on the tRNA (the CCA sequence at its 3’ end), and AMP is released.
  4. The activated tRNA with its attached amino acid is released from the enzyme.

Nature of science Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei (1962) deduced the first codon of the genetic code experimentally. Using the enzyme polynucleotide phosphorylase (which randomly joins RNA nucleotides), they generated artificial “poly(U) RNA” made entirely of a repeating UUU sequence. This was added, along with ribosomes, activating enzymes and tRNAs, to 20 separate tubes, each containing one of the 20 amino acids radioactively labelled. Only the tube containing labelled phenylalanine produced a protein product, showing that the codon UUU codes for phenylalanine. Similar experiments with poly(C) and poly(A) RNA showed that CCC codes for proline and AAA for lysine.

Ribosomes – the site of protein synthesis

A ribosome consists of a large and a small subunit, both composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein. There are three sites within the ribosome where tRNAs interact:

  • A site – where a codon of incoming mRNA binds to a specific tRNA–amino acid complex via complementary base pairing with its anticodon.
  • P site – where the amino acid attached to its tRNA is condensed with the growing polypeptide chain by formation of a peptide linkage.
  • E site – where the tRNA leaves the ribosome, after transferring its amino acid to the growing protein chain.

Three labelled tRNA-binding sites are shown side by side: the A site (the amino acid–tRNA binding site), the P site (which holds a tRNA while peptide bonds form) and the E site (from which a discharged tRNA exits the ribosome), together with an exit tunnel through which the growing polypeptide emerges. Initiation, elongation and termination

Translation begins when an mRNA molecule binds to the small ribosomal subunit at an mRNA binding site near the 5’ end of the mRNA. An initiator tRNA, carrying the amino acid methionine, then binds at the start codon AUG, followed by attachment of the large ribosomal subunit; the initiator tRNA occupies the P site of the assembled ribosome. The next codon, now present in the A site, becomes available to a tRNA with the appropriate anticodon; its arrival brings two activated amino acids (in the P and A sites) into position, and a peptide bond forms between them by a condensation reaction catalysed by enzymes in the large subunit, forming a dipeptide.

During elongation, the ribosome moves three bases along the mRNA. The tRNA in the P site moves to the E site and is released, while the next codon now occupies the vacant A site, allowing a tRNA with the appropriate anticodon to bind. This brings a further amino acid alongside the growing chain, and another peptide bond forms. By these repeated steps, the ribosome progresses along the mRNA in the 5’ to 3’ direction, codon by codon, and a polypeptide is formed and emerges from the large subunit. Eventually a stop codon (UAA, UAG or UGA) is reached: the completed polypeptide is released from the ribosome into the cytoplasm, and the ribosome’s components disassemble.

Figure D1.2.16: Initiation, elongation and termination in translation The figure shows three stages. In initiation, an initiator tRNA carrying methionine (Met) binds the start codon AUG at the mRNA binding site on the small ribosomal subunit, and the large ribosomal subunit then binds, with the initiator tRNA occupying the P site. In elongation, a tRNA carrying the next amino acid (e.g. Glu) binds at the vacant A site, a peptide bond forms between the amino acids held in the P and A sites, then the ribosome moves three bases along the mRNA so that the spent tRNA shifts from the P site to the E site and exits, while the next codon occupies the now-vacant A site for another tRNA to bind and the cycle to repeat, building a growing chain (e.g. Met–Glu–Ala). In termination, once a stop codon (UAG, UAA or UGA) enters the A site, the process halts and the completed polypeptide is released. Throughout, the ribosome moves along the mRNA from the 5’ to the 3’ end.
Higher Level

Modification of Polypeptides into Their Functional State

When a protein exits translation at a ribosome, it may take up its active three-dimensional shape and become functional immediately – for example, becoming active as an enzyme in an essential, continuous biochemical pathway. Many other polypeptides, however, must be modified before they can function; these steps occur after translation and are called post-translational modifications. Some proteins are produced as inactive precursors requiring further processing, since it is often important for a protein to become active only at a particular site or time. For example, the protein-digesting enzyme trypsin is produced in the pancreas in an inactive form, trypsinogen, which does not digest the proteins of the pancreas cells in which it is formed. Insulin is another protein requiring modification: it undergoes a two-stage modification, from pre-proinsulin to proinsulin, and from proinsulin to insulin.

Key term Post-translational modification: a biochemical modification that occurs to one or more amino acids on a protein after the protein has been translated by a ribosome.
Post-transcriptional vs post-translational modification Post-transcriptional modification refers to changes made to a newly transcribed mRNA, before it is translated into protein (e.g. splicing, capping, polyadenylation). Post-translational modification refers to changes made to a protein after it has already been translated by a ribosome (e.g. cleavage of an inactive precursor into its active form).

From this common starting point, two possible pathways are shown: one in which the protein adopts its final shape and becomes immediately active (e.g. as an enzyme), and another in which the inactive protein undergoes post-translational modification by enzymes before it becomes an active protein (e.g. an enzyme).
Higher Level

Recycling of Amino Acids by Proteasomes

To maintain the proteome of an organism, proteins are continually synthesized by translation and broken down by hydrolysis reactions, a process known as proteolysis. This constant turnover of proteins helps maintain optimally functioning proteins. While amino acids are constantly absorbed by organisms, recycling of existing amino acids also occurs to ensure sufficient turnover. Breakdown of proteins is carried out by proteasomes – complexes made of protein into which a protein substrate marked for degradation is fed. Proteasomes degrade proteins that are damaged, misfolded or no longer needed by the cell.

Key terms Proteolysis: a hydrolysis reaction of peptide bonds in which proteins are broken down into smaller peptides and/or individual amino acids.
Proteasome: protein complexes that degrade unneeded, misfolded or damaged proteins by proteolysis.
Process: protein breakdown by a proteasome
  1. Proteins destined for degradation are marked by attachment of a short chain of a regulatory protein called ubiquitin.
  2. The ubiquitin-marked protein binds to the cap (regulatory particle) at the top of the proteasome, and the ubiquitin is released.
  3. The target protein is threaded into the core particle of the proteasome, where it is broken down into short peptides.
  4. These peptides are further processed to provide a pool of amino acids from which new proteins can be synthesized.

The tagged protein binds to a cap (regulatory particle) at the top of the barrel-shaped proteasome; the ubiquitin is released at this stage. The target protein is then threaded into the core particle of the proteasome, where it is broken down into short peptides, which emerge from the structure.
