Introduction to Viruses

All living organisms (eukaryotes and prokaryotes) have nucleic acids that make up their genomes. Viruses also contain nucleic acids, but they require living cells to reproduce — they do not have ribosomes or the metabolic enzymes needed for protein synthesis. This makes viruses obligate parasites: they can only reproduce inside a host cell.

Key definitionsVirus: a non-cellular parasite of animals, plants and bacteria that consists of nucleic acid surrounded by a protein coat (a capsid). Parasite: an organism that lives on or in another organism (its host) for most of its life cycle, deriving nutrients from its host. Micro-organism: a living organism that cannot be seen by the naked eye, such as bacteria, protists and single-celled fungi (note: viruses are not classed as living organisms).

Viruses infect all cellular organisms and are highly diverse in shape and structure. Unlike LUCA (the last universal common ancestor) for cellular organisms, there is no presumed single common ancestor for viruses. One method of classifying viruses is based on the genome they contain: the nucleic acid present is either DNA or RNA, which may be single- or double-stranded, and may have positive or negative polarity.

Common mistakeA virus is not a cell. A virus is considered ‘non-cellular’, meaning that no part of it is cellular.

Top tip! Viruses are not part of the Linnaean system of classification or the three-domain system, since they are not considered to be cellular living organisms.

Nature of science — observations and science as a shared endeavour: viruses were first observed directly in 1931, when electron microscopes were developed, but their existence had already been inferred from careful observation. Fine filters could remove bacteria from infected tissue, yet the filtered tissue remained infective, showing that an infectious agent smaller than bacteria was responsible. In 1915 Frederick Twort discovered bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) after observing clear spots within bacterial colonies. Edward Jenner (smallpox vaccine, 1798) and Louis Pasteur (rabies vaccine) developed vaccines before viruses were even known to exist. Since 1966 the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) has maintained a universal taxonomic scheme for viruses, using a modified version of the Linnaean system that starts at order and goes down to species — for example HIV is placed in the family Retroviridae because it is a retrovirus containing RNA and reverse transcriptase.

Structural Features Common to Viruses

Concept: UnityAll viruses share the following common structural features: a small, fixed size range of 20–400 nm in diameter; nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) as genetic material; a capsid made of protein that surrounds the nucleic acid; no cytoplasm; and few or even no enzymes.
Common mistakeViruses do not have a nucleus or a nucleoid — these terms apply only to eukaryotes and prokaryotes respectively. A virus simply has a viral genome, consisting of RNA or DNA.

Diversity of Structure in Viruses

Concept: DiversityViruses are highly diverse in their shape and structure. Genetic material may be RNA or DNA, which can be either single- or double-stranded. Some viruses are enveloped in host cell membrane and others are not enveloped.

Viruses differ from each other in host range (the number of species and cell types they can infect), type of genome, structure, and method of replication.

The Viral Genome

DNA viruses include adenoviruses (a cause of eye disease) and most bacteriophages (viruses that parasitize bacteria). RNA viruses include HIV and coronaviruses. Because the genetic code is shared between viruses and living organisms, host cells can transcribe viral DNA into mRNA and translate it into protein.

Positive-sense vs negative-sense RNAPositive-sense RNA is viral RNA with the same base sequence as mRNA, so it can be translated immediately by host ribosomes (e.g. SARS-CoV-2). Negative-sense RNA is complementary to viral mRNA, so it cannot be translated directly — it must first be converted into positive-sense RNA by RNA polymerase before translation can occur (e.g. influenza viruses).

Nucleic acid replication location also varies: in SARS-CoV-2 it occurs entirely in the cytoplasm, whereas influenza viruses replicate their RNA in the nucleus. In some viruses, such as HIV, RNA acts as a template for synthesizing viral DNA in the cytoplasm using the viral enzyme reverse transcriptase.

The viral genome may be linear (e.g. coronaviruses) or circular (e.g. hepatitis B); single-stranded (e.g. hepatitis E virus and coronaviruses) or double-stranded (e.g. HIV and SV-40). Some viruses have more than one copy of their genome (e.g. HIV and influenza viruses).

The genomes of simple viruses, such as bacteriophage Qbeta, may be only a few thousand nucleotides long and contain just four genes. More complex viruses contain many more genes: bacteriophage T4 has 289 genes, largely involved in building its complex capsid. Rapid replication is favoured by a small genome size — the smaller the genome, the faster it can replicate. Viruses with prokaryotic hosts tend to replicate especially rapidly, reflected in compact genomes with overlapping genes.

Figure A2.3.3: Coloured TEM of T4 bacteriophageThe micrograph shows the T4 bacteriophage, which has a large, complex icosahedral (polyhedral) head containing its DNA, connected to a tail structure used to attach to and inject DNA into a host bacterium. T4’s comparatively large genome (289 genes) is reflected in the structural complexity of its capsid.

Top tip! There is a roughly inverse relationship between genome size and dependence on the host for essential functions in DNA replication — the smaller the viral genome, the more the virus depends on host-cell machinery.

Capsid

Capsid and capsomereCapsid: the protein coat of a virus, which encloses the viral genome. Capsomere: one of the individual protein subunits that make up a viral capsid.

Capsids come in a variety of shapes: helical (e.g. tobacco mosaic virus, TMV), conical (e.g. HIV) and polyhedral (e.g. adenovirus). Some viruses carry viral enzyme molecules within their capsids.

Figure A2.3.4: The tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), which infects the tobacco plantThe figure shows the helical capsid of TMV: a protein coat made of polypeptide building blocks arranged in a spiral around a central canal that contains a single strand of RNA (running from its 3’ to 5’ end). A transmission electron micrograph (negatively stained, ×40 000) shows the hollow tube construction of the virus in side view and end view, alongside an enlarged drawing of part of the virus. The figure also contrasts a healthy tobacco leaf with an infected leaf showing the characteristic mosaic pattern of disease.

Some of the most complex capsids are found in bacteriophages. Phage capsids have elongated icosahedral heads enclosing their genome, built from equilateral triangles fused together into a 20-sided (icosahedral) spherical shape — the optimal way to form a closed shell from identical protein subunits. Joined to the head is a tail shaft with fibres used to attach to the bacterial cell wall.

Figure A2.3.5: Unfolded two-dimensional (left) and folded three-dimensional (right) icosahedronThe figure shows a flat net of 20 identical equilateral triangular faces on the left, and the same net folded into a closed, roughly spherical 20-sided solid (icosahedron) on the right — illustrating how a capsid can be built as a closed shell from many identical protein subunits.

Envelope

Envelope, nucleocapsid, virionEnvelope: a membrane, typically derived from the host cell plasma membrane, studded with viral glycoproteins. Nucleocapsid: the capsid of a virus that has an envelope. Virion: an isolated but infectious virus particle found outside the host cell.

Viruses can be classified as enveloped (e.g. HIV, influenza viruses and coronaviruses) or non-enveloped (e.g. bacteriophages and poliovirus). Enveloped viruses typically contain host cell phospholipids embedded with virally encoded spike glycoproteins — usually taken from the host plasma membrane, although coronaviruses take their envelope from internal host cell membranes instead. The envelope protects the virion from enzymes and other chemicals, giving enveloped viruses an advantage over non-enveloped virions. Glycoproteins on the envelope help the virus bind to receptor molecules on the host cell surface, enabling recognition and possible uptake of the virion into the cell.

Nature of science — patterns and trends: for many years, viruses could only be studied by observing the effects of virus-infected extracts on living animals. An approximate measure of infectivity could be estimated by diluting an extract and testing how far it could be diluted while still causing infection.

Viral Reproductive Life Cycles

Virus particles cannot carry out life processes independently, so they rely on the mechanisms of the host cell for energy supply, nutrition, protein synthesis and other functions. The general stages of viral reproduction are:

  • attachment (or adsorption) to host cells
  • entry (or penetration)
  • replication of the viral genome
  • transcription of viral mRNA and protein synthesis using host cell ribosomes
  • maturation and assembly of new viruses, followed by release from the host cell
Common mistakeA virus cannot move independently. Viruses move passively, carried through phloem, blood, other body fluids or the air. When a virus reaches a host cell, it binds to receptors on the cell surface, which causes the virus particle to be drawn into the cell.

Bacteriophage Lambda

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that infect bacteria. They played a central role in the development of molecular biology, particularly our understanding of gene structure and expression, and are used in the laboratory as vectors for cloned DNA. Bacteriophages are highly specific to the bacteria they infect: bacteriophage lambda only infects E. coli. The wild type has a temperate life cycle, meaning it can either persist in the bacterial genome via lysogeny, or enter a lytic cycle that kills the host cell.

Figure A2.3.6: Structure of bacteriophage lambdaThe diagram shows an icosahedral capsid (head) containing double-stranded linear DNA, connected via a collar to a tail structure consisting of a sheath and core, ending in a conical part with a tail fibre used for attachment to the host bacterium.

Lytic Cycle

Key termsLytic: a phage life cycle in which a phage attaches to a host bacterium, injects its DNA which undergoes replication to form new virions, which then lyse the cell. Lysis: breakdown, typically of cells. Virulent: the ability of a virus (or bacterium) to cause rapid and severe disease.
Stages of the lytic cycle1. The tail fibre of bacteriophage lambda binds to specific receptors on the surface of E. coli (attachment). 2. The phage injects its double-stranded DNA genome into the host cell (penetration); the protein capsid is left outside. 3. The viral DNA enters the bacterium and remains as a separate molecule, replicating independently of the host bacterial DNA. 4. The bacterial DNA is broken down (DNA hydrolysis) while phage DNA is replicated (biosynthesis). 5. Viral genes are expressed using host enzymes to produce viral proteins, which self-assemble into mature bacteriophage lambda virions (maturation). 6. Lysis of the bacterial cell wall releases the new virus particles, killing the host cell.
Figure A2.3.7: An overview of the lytic cycle of a virusA six-step diagram: (1) the phage attaches to the surface of the bacterium; (2) the phage is adsorbed onto the bacterium and injects its DNA; (3) the viral DNA enters the bacterium; (4) phage DNA is replicated while bacterial DNA is hydrolysed; (5) phage proteins are made and assembled into many new phage particles; (6) the cell lyses (bursts) and the newly made phage particles are released.

The lytic cycle results in the destruction of the infected cell and its membrane. Bacteriophages that only use the lytic cycle are called virulent phages.

Lysogenic Cycle

Key termsLysogenic: a phage life cycle in which a phage attaches to a bacterium and injects its genome, but does not undergo a full replication cycle; instead it becomes resident within the bacterial host, maintained in a dormant state. Prophage: a bacteriophage in an inactive state in which the genome is typically integrated into the chromosome of the bacterial host.
Stages of the lysogenic cycle1. The phage infects the cell by injecting its DNA. 2. The phage DNA becomes incorporated into the host genome, forming a prophage and altering the genotype of the bacterium (a bacterial host carrying a prophage is called a lysogen). 3. When the bacterial chromosome is copied as the cell divides, the phage’s DNA is also replicated and passed to daughter cells. 4. Under stressful conditions, the phage DNA is excised from the bacterial chromosome and enters the lytic cycle. 5. Phage DNA replicates and phage proteins are made; new phage particles are assembled. 6. The cell lyses, releasing the new phages.
Figure A2.3.8: An overview of the lysogenic cycle of a virusThe diagram follows the same six numbered stages described above, noting that the final two stages (5 and 6) are shared with, and identical to, the lytic cycle — i.e. once induced out of dormancy, a lysogenic infection proceeds through the lytic pathway to release new phage.

In the lysogenic cycle, unlike the lytic cycle, the viral DNA does not remain separate but is incorporated into the host cell’s DNA. The bacteriophage’s DNA then replicates together with the E. coli host DNA whenever the host replicates, and because the prophage carries genes, it can confer novel properties on the host. Bacteria grown in rich medium contain high levels of proteases (protein-degrading enzymes essential for completing viral infection) and are more likely to support lytic replication when infected; poorly growing bacteria have lower protease levels, which favours establishment of lysogeny instead.

Top tip! Bacteriophages that can adopt either a lytic or a lysogenic replication cycle are termed temperate phages.

Lytic vs lysogenic cycleIn the lytic cycle, viral DNA remains separate from host DNA, host DNA is degraded, and the host cell is always destroyed by lysis. In the lysogenic cycle, viral DNA integrates into the host chromosome as a prophage, host DNA is not degraded, and the host cell survives and reproduces normally (passing the prophage to daughter cells) until induction triggers entry into the lytic cycle.

Nature of science — experiments: if a bacteriophage infects bacteria spread on an agar plate, phages released from an individual infected cell can only infect neighbouring bacteria, producing localized clearings of the bacterial lawn called plaques. Counting plaques allows a biologist to estimate the concentration of bacteriophage particles in a suspension (the phage titre), assuming the number of plaques corresponds to the number of phage particles present.

MOI (multiplicity of infection) = number of infectious virus particles ÷ number of target cells present

The multiplicity of infection (MOI) is one factor that determines whether a virus enters the lytic cycle or the lysogenic (dormant) phase. For example, to achieve an MOI of 0.5 using virus particles at a concentration of 2 × 109 cm−3 and bacteria at a concentration of 8 × 108 cm−3, in 0.25 cm3 of bacteria, a scientist would first calculate the number of target bacterial cells present (8 × 108 × 0.25 = 2 × 108 cells), then the number of virus particles needed for an MOI of 0.5 (0.5 × 2 × 108 = 1 × 108 particles), and finally the volume of virus suspension required to supply that many particles at the given concentration.

Evidence for Several Origins of Viruses from Other Organisms

There are many different types of virus depending on the cells they infect, and it seems unlikely that all known viruses share a single common ancestor. Viruses are therefore considered polyphyletic in origin, meaning they have arisen from a few independent origins, almost certainly at different times from cellular organisms. Three main hypotheses have been proposed to explain the evolutionary origin of viruses.

The virus-first hypothesisViruses evolved before, or co-evolved with, their current host cells. Bits of RNA with self-complementary sequences may have folded like a protein, gradually becoming more complex until they could self-replicate. They then began infecting other cells, becoming viruses.
Figure A2.3.9: The escaped genes (progressive) hypothesis for the origin of virusesThe diagram shows a single-celled organism containing RNA and a protein-building structure, enclosed by a protein coat. Unequal cell division produces two mini-cells: one lacks the protein-building structure and cannot survive independently, but the other survives because of its protein coat. This surviving entity — now a virus — is able to replicate itself and infect new organisms.

The escaped genes (progressive) hypothesis suggests that viruses arose from genetic elements (such as transposons, repetitive DNA sequences that can move between chromosomes in eukaryotes) that gained the ability to move between cells.

Figure A2.3.10: The regressive (reduction) hypothesis for the origin of virusesThe diagram shows a single-celled organism (with RNA and a protein-building structure) forming a mutualistic relationship with another organism. Over time, the smaller organism loses its protein-building structures, becoming dependent on its partner, but retains the ability to replicate itself and infect new organisms — it has become a virus.

The regressive (or reduction) hypothesis suggests that viruses are remnants of cellular organisms that have lost non-essential structures through their dependence on a host.

Both the escaped genes and regressive hypotheses assume that cells existed before viruses, but some researchers propose that viruses may have been among the first replicating entities, appearing before LUCA. There is huge diversity among viruses, with no single gene shared by all of them. It has been argued that precursors of a group called nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDV) — which have large genomes containing genes for DNA repair, DNA replication, transcription and translation, and which replicate in both the host nucleus and cytoplasm — may have been involved in the emergence of eukaryotic cells; it is hypothesised that the eukaryotic nucleus arose through endosymbiosis of a complex, enveloped DNA virus into an ancestral eukaryotic cell. Some researchers hypothesise that NCLDV viruses themselves evolved through a regressive process, in which their ancestral cells lost key genes and adopted an obligate parasitic replication strategy; this irreversible loss of genes may have been a dominant force in viral evolution.

No single hypothesis may explain the origin of all viruses. Retroviruses probably arose through a progressive process (mobile genetic elements gaining the ability to travel between cells), while large DNA viruses probably arose through a regressive process (once-independent entities losing essential genes and adopting parasitism). Since all viruses share an extreme form of obligate parasitism on host cells, the structural features they have in common could be regarded as a form of convergent evolution, in which species occupying similar ecological niches adapt in similar ways to similar selective pressures, producing ‘analogous structures’.

Rapid Evolution in Viruses

The evolution rate of a virus can be defined as the number of nucleotide substitutions per nucleotide site, per year. This rate is affected by mutation, recombination, large population size and a rapid life cycle.

Mutation in Viruses

Mutations are a natural by-product of viral replication, occurring at a frequency determined by the error rate of the replication enzymes (adjusted by any proofreading that occurs). Whether a mutant succeeds depends on whether it confers a selective advantage: mutations that improve replication, transmission, or the ability to evade the host immune system increase in frequency, while those that reduce viral fitness are removed from the population. RNA viruses tend to have a much higher mutation rate than DNA viruses because errors in copying their RNA genomes are not corrected by proofreading. Coronaviruses are an exception among RNA viruses, having a lower mutation rate because they produce an RNA polymerase that corrects some replication errors. Even so, viruses generally evolve faster than their host cells, giving them an advantage in overcoming the host immune response.

Case Study: Influenza

There are three types of influenza (flu) virus: A, B and C. Influenza is an enveloped virus with a genome of negative-sense, single-stranded, segmented RNA. It infects the epithelial cells of the respiratory tract (and, in birds, the intestinal tract). Virions recognize target cells through complementary binding between haemagglutinin (a spike protein in the viral envelope) and specific sialic acid receptors on the host cell membrane.

Antigenic drift vs antigenic shiftAntigenic drift consists of small mutations in influenza genes that gradually change the virus’s surface proteins, haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), over time as the virus replicates — leading to surface antigens no longer recognized by the immune system, and so to disease. Antigenic shift is a sudden, major change in an influenza A virus, producing new HA and/or NA proteins and potentially a new influenza A subtype; it results from recombination, in which genetic material from two different virus strains that have co-infected the same host cell is combined to produce new combinations.
Figure A2.3.11: Conceptual illustration of antigenic drift and antigenic shiftThe diagram contrasts antigenic drift, shown as gradual random mutation producing different strains within the same subtype of influenza A, with antigenic shift, shown as genetic shuffling (reassortment) between different subtypes of influenza A co-infecting the same cell, giving rise to an entirely new influenza A subtype.

Antigenic shift can occur if an influenza virus from an animal population gains the ability to infect humans; such animal-origin viruses may carry HA/NA combinations different enough from existing human viruses that most people have no immunity to the novel virus.

Case Study: HIV

Retrovirus, reverse transcriptase, provirusRetrovirus: a virus that has RNA as its nucleic acid and uses the enzyme reverse transcriptase to copy its genome into the DNA of the host cell’s chromosomes. Reverse transcriptase: an enzyme found in HIV and other retroviruses that converts viral RNA into viral DNA (reverse transcription). Provirus: a virus that has integrated into a host’s genome and is replicated whenever the cell replicates its DNA.

Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) is caused by HIV, a retrovirus. After entering the cytoplasm of a host cell, HIV’s RNA is converted into DNA by reverse transcriptase; the resulting viral DNA is then integrated into the host cell nucleus’s DNA to form a provirus.

Figure A2.3.12: The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)The diagram shows an enveloped, roughly spherical virion. The outer lipid membrane (envelope) is studded with glycoprotein spikes. Inside, a conical protein capsid encloses the RNA genome together with viral enzymes, including reverse transcriptase and protease.

HIV has a high mutation rate because reverse transcriptase lacks a proofreading mechanism; AIDS patients often carry many genetically distinct variants of HIV, different from the original infecting virus. This is a major problem for treatment: antiviral drugs that block a single essential viral enzyme work only temporarily, as drug-resistant strains arise rapidly by mutation. However, combinations of drugs (typically three) that target different viral proteins are highly effective at inhibiting the virus and preventing replication. The population size of HIV within a host is the total number of infectious proviruses integrated into cellular DNA at a given time; high mutation rates combined with short generation times and large population sizes allow HIV to evolve rapidly and adapt to its host.

Nature of science — falsificationThe central dogma of molecular biology proposed that genetic information flows only from DNA to RNA to protein. In 1970, David Baltimore discovered the enzyme reverse transcriptase in retroviruses, falsifying this strict one-directional flow by showing that RNA can be copied back into DNA. This discovery underpinned the discovery of HIV in 1984 and the development of effective HIV therapies. The transfer of genetic information from RNA to DNA is now recognized as a general evolutionary principle operating throughout biology, not just in viruses.

Top tip! A variant describes a subtype or strain of a virus (e.g. the delta and omicron variants of SARS-CoV-2) that is genetically distinct from the main strain but not different enough to be classed as a separate strain (as influenza A, B, C and D are).

Consequences of Rapid Viral Evolution

The rapid evolution of viruses has real consequences for treating the diseases they cause. Yearly flu vaccines must be redeveloped to maintain their efficacy (the percentage reduction in disease among a vaccinated group in a clinical trial). During the COVID-19 pandemic, many new variants evolved, sometimes with different symptoms, reducing the efficacy of vaccines developed against earlier strains. Top tip! Emerging diseases such as HIV and COVID-19 are often particularly virulent because they are not yet adapted to their new host species.

Epidemiological Modelling: The SIR Model

Compartmental models are a general modelling technique used in the mathematical modelling of infectious diseases, including COVID-19. The population is divided into labelled compartments — for example S (susceptible), I (infectious) and R (recovered) — and people move between compartments over time.

Figure A2.3.13: SIR model showing the transition between susceptible, infectious and recovered statesThe diagram shows three boxes labelled Susceptible, Infectious and Recovered, connected by arrows showing the one-way progression of individuals from susceptible to infectious to recovered as an epidemic proceeds. This model is accurate for diseases transmitted only human-to-human, where recovery confers long-lasting resistance.
Figure A2.3.14: SIR model showing numbers of susceptible, infectious or recovered in a populationThe graph plots the number of people (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis) for an outbreak. The S curve (susceptible, those who can still catch the infection) starts high and falls over time; the I curve (infectious, those who have the infection and can spread it) rises to a peak and then falls; the R curve (recovered, those who have had the disease but are now better) starts at zero and rises steadily as the epidemic progresses.

Such models try to predict how a disease spreads, the total number infected, and the duration of an epidemic, and to estimate epidemiological parameters such as the reproductive number, R0 — the expected number of secondary cases produced by a single infection in a population that is completely susceptible. Models can also show how interventions such as lockdowns or vaccination might affect the outcome of an epidemic.

Variation Between Organisms

Concept: DiversityAll species show variation. This is one of the pillars on which the theory of evolution by natural selection is based.

In 1831, Charles Darwin was employed as a naturalist on HMS Beagle. During his five-year voyage, particularly his visit to the Galapagos Islands, Darwin noted that all species show large variation — every individual is slightly different from others in the population. This observation, together with his awareness that humans can artificially select for different breeds of animals (such as pigeons), formed a central pillar of his theory of evolution by natural selection: if artificial selection can create new varieties, then, given enough time, nature could also select for new varieties, and ultimately new species.

Figure A3.1.1: Variation in one species of beetleThe figure shows the wing cases of fourteen-spot ladybirds (Propylea 14-punctata), all collected from the same nettle plant, displaying noticeable variation in colouration and spot patterns between individuals of the same species.
Species and variationSpecies: a group of individuals of common ancestry that closely resemble each other and are normally capable of interbreeding to produce fertile offspring. Variation: differences between individuals of a species, which may be caused by environmental and/or genetic factors.

Continuous Variation

Some variation can take any value across a range of measurement for a given characteristic, such as height, and is called continuous variation. Many human features, including body weight and height, are controlled by polygenes (several different genes). A graph of the height of 400 people shows continuous variation between the shortest (160 cm) and tallest (185 cm) individuals, with a mean height of 173 cm.

Figure A3.1.2: Human height as a case of polygenic inheritanceThe figure is a bar graph (histogram) of the number of people against height, grouped into 2 cm bands from 160–161 cm up to 184–185 cm. The results cluster around a mean value, producing a symmetrical bell-shaped curve known as a normal distribution — values grouped symmetrically around a central value. The caption explains that human height is determined genetically by the interaction of alleles of several genes, likely located at loci on different chromosomes.

Environmental conditions can also affect the phenotype (the physical appearance of an organism and its internal physiology) and produce variation: for example, a tall plant may appear stunted if deprived of essential mineral ions, and human physique is affected by levels of nourishment, particularly in childhood. The phenotype of an organism is therefore the product of both its genotype and environmental influences.

Discontinuous Variation

Continuous vs discontinuous variationContinuous variation: a continuum of variation from one phenotype to another (e.g. human height). Discontinuous variation: variation in which the characteristic falls into two or more discrete types with no intermediate forms (e.g. the garden pea plant being tall or dwarf, or human ABO blood grouping). Characteristics showing discontinuous variation are typically controlled by a single gene, usually with two alleles, and are not affected by the environment — they are determined solely by genotype.

Top tip! Discontinuous variation can arise from threshold effects in development or mutations of large effect, including homeotic mutations, which cause discrete changes in form from a single mutation (e.g. loss of one pair of wings in insects, or conversion of petals to stamens in plants).

Distinguishing Between Different Species

Because morphological variation exists within a species, some individuals of one species may resemble individuals of another species that also shows variation. Taxonomists — scientists who define and classify groups of organisms based on shared characteristics — distinguish species by studying large numbers of individuals. Many species show sexual dimorphism, where males and females differ in appearance (e.g. lions), adding further morphological variation within a species. Natural history museums increasingly use DNA profiling to check current classifications, since DNA profiling allows clear parameters to be set for deciding whether individuals belong to the same species.

What is a Species?

Species were originally defined by their appearance — the morphological species concept — based on shape, colour and other distinctive structural traits. This concept was used by Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus in the 1700s and, although much criticised, is still widely used today, particularly for plants.

Morphological species conceptSpecies are groups of organisms with shared traits.
Advantages and limitations of the morphological species conceptAdvantages: can be applied to both asexual and sexual organisms; does not require information on gene flow; can be applied to extinct and fossilized species; is the easiest and fastest concept to apply in the field, since it relies only on appearance. Limitations: relies on subjective criteria, so researchers may disagree on which features distinguish a species; individuals within a species (e.g. males and females) may appear very different from each other.

The Binomial System for Naming Organisms

Concept: UnityThe binomial system provides a universal means of classifying organisms that can be understood worldwide.

Classification is essential in biology because there are too many living organisms (currently around 1.8 million described species) to sort and compare without organizing them into manageable categories. Taxonomy is the science of classification; a taxon is a general term for a group or category within a classification system. The classification scheme must be flexible enough to accommodate newly discovered organisms and should include fossils, since living and extinct species are believed to be related.

Figures A3.1.3 and A3.1.4: Carl Linnaeus and Systema NaturaeFigure A3.1.3 is a portrait of Carl Linnaeus, the Swedish botanist, physician and zoologist who developed binomial nomenclature. Figure A3.1.4 shows the title page of his most famous work, Systema Naturae (1735), which showed for the first time how all life could be classified.

Carl Linnaeus, in the 1730s, developed binomial nomenclature (the binomial system, meaning ‘two-part name’), still used today; he is known as the father of modern taxonomy. He published Systema Naturae in 1734, dividing flowering plants into classes based on the structure of their sexual organs, and in 1749 introduced the binomial system itself. Each organism is given a Latin noun (the genus) followed by an adjective (the species), with the genus written first and capitalized, the species name in lower case, and the whole name conventionally italicised or underlined — for example Homo sapiens, where ‘Homo’ is the genus and ‘sapiens’ the species. Several species can share a genus: humans share the genus Homo with now-extinct species such as the Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis).

Species within the same genus share similar traits, such as larger brain capacity and a smaller face compared to other members of the family Hominidae (the great apes), reflecting similar life histories and environmental adaptations.

Common mistakeThe genus name must start with a capital letter (e.g. Homo, not ‘homo’), and the full species name should be italicised (or underlined when handwritten), e.g. Homo sapiens. After the first full use, the genus may be abbreviated to its initial, e.g. H. sapiens.

Nature of science — science as a shared endeavour: local common names for organisms vary around the world and can cause confusion — for example ‘magpie’ refers to entirely different bird species in Europe, Asia and Sri Lanka. The international binomial system avoids this ambiguity by giving scientists worldwide a single, agreed name for each organism.

The Biological Species Concept and Alternatives

Biological species conceptDeveloped by zoologist Ernst Mayr in 1942: a species is a group of organisms that can breed and produce fertile offspring.

The biological species concept has limitations: it does not apply to organisms that reproduce asexually all or most of the time (e.g. prokaryotes); it does not apply to extinct organisms; and it overemphasizes gene flow while downplaying the role of natural selection, since selection can keep morphologically and ecologically distinct species pairs distinct even when some gene flow occurs between them.

Ecological species conceptA species is a group of organisms adapted to a particular set of resources (its ecological niche — everything about where and how it lives, including diet, reproduction and habitat). This concept explains differences in form and behaviour between species as adaptations to resource availability. Advantages: applies to both asexual and sexual species; emphasizes the role of disruptive natural selection as organisms adapt to different conditions. Limitation: niches are difficult to identify precisely, since a niche involves complex interactions between an organism and both the biotic and abiotic environment.
Evolutionary species conceptA species is a single lineage of populations descending from a common ancestor, maintaining its identity from other such lineages, with its own selection pressures and evolutionary outcome. This concept relies on genetic data comparing evolutionary relationships between organisms.
Species conceptApproach and methodology
Morphological species conceptrelies on morphological data and emphasizes groups of physical traits that are unique to each species
Biological species conceptrelies on behavioural information and emphasizes reproductive isolation between groups
Ecological species conceptrelies on detailed information about how organisms interact with their biotic (biological) and abiotic (non-living) environment
Evolutionary species conceptrelies on genetic data and emphasizes distinct evolutionary links between groups

Difficulties Distinguishing Between a Population and a Species

PopulationInteracting groups of organisms of the same species living in an area, close enough together to be able to interbreed.

Species typically exist as localized populations, although population boundaries can be hard to define.

Figure A3.1.8: The concept of populationThe figure illustrates several populations of garden snails and their predators (thrushes) across a landscape: an ‘open’ population of snails around a compost heap, an ‘open’ population in a flower bed, a ‘semi-open’ population in a vegetable patch, and a ‘closed’ population isolated on a traffic island flower bed, cut off by roadways that act as a barrier to snail migration. A hedge nearby is shown as home to predators of the garden snail.

Some populations are completely ‘open’, with individuals moving freely in and out; others are ‘closed’, almost completely isolated from neighbouring populations of the same species (like the traffic-island snails). Individuals within a local population tend to resemble each other and may differ noticeably from members of other populations. Local populations matter because they are potential starting points for speciation — the process by which one species splits into two or more species, supported by evidence in the fossil record.

SpeciationThe process by which new species form, where one species splits into two or more species.

Barriers arising within local populations are a possible cause of speciation. Before separation, individuals share a common gene pool, but after isolation, processes such as mutation can change one population without affecting the other. A new population may also form from a small, unrepresentative sample separated from a larger population, with many alleles lost from its gene pool. Over time, accumulated genotypic changes can cause previously interbreeding populations to become genetically distinct new species.

Common mistakeThe smallest unit of evolution is a population, not an individual organism. Natural selection acts on individuals, but it is populations that evolve — individual organisms cannot evolve during their own lifetime.

Speciation usually happens gradually, with populations becoming progressively more different in their traits, so it can be an arbitrary decision whether two populations should be regarded as the same or different species. This is compounded by the definition problem: if isolated populations can no longer interbreed, it is impossible to prove experimentally whether they could still, in theory, produce fertile offspring, since they cannot physically meet to test this.

Diversity in Chromosome Numbers of Plant and Animal Species

Concept: Diversity and UnityChromosomes vary in number and shape among organisms — bacteria have circular chromosomes, whereas animals and plants have linear chromosomes; variation in chromosome number can relate to evolutionary origin and functional differences. However, within any one species the number of chromosomes per cell is normally constant.

There are five features of eukaryotic chromosomes that are useful to remember.

1. The Number of Chromosomes per Species is Fixed

The number of chromosomes varies between species but is normally constant within a species. For example, a mouse has 40 chromosomes per cell, an onion has 16, humans have 46, and a sunflower has 34. Each species has a characteristic (and always even) chromosome number.

Top tip! You need to know, as an example of diversity in chromosome numbers, that humans have 46 chromosomes and chimpanzees have 48.

2. The Shape of a Chromosome is Characteristic

Chromosomes are long, thin structures of fixed length. Somewhere along the chromosome is a narrow region called the centromere, which occurs in a fixed position for any given chromosome. The centromere position, chromosome length, and banding pattern after staining together allow scientists to identify particular chromosomes in photomicrographs.

3. Chromosomes Occur in Homologous Pairs

The chromosomes of a cell occur in pairs, called homologous pairs, one of each pair inherited from the male parent and one from the female parent. A cell with chromosomes in homologous pairs has a diploid nucleus, represented as 2n (where n represents one set of chromosomes). A cell with only one chromosome of each pair has a haploid nucleus, represented as n; a sex cell (gamete) is haploid, formed by meiosis.

Centromere, diploid, haploidCentromere: a constriction of the chromosome, the region that becomes attached to spindle fibres during nuclear division. Diploid: cells having two sets of chromosomes (one from each parent organism). Haploid: cells having one set of chromosomes.

4. Genes Occur at Specific Loci

Chromosomes carry genes in a linear sequence; the position of a gene is called a locus (plural loci), and each gene has two or more alternative forms, called alleles. A diploid organism with the same allele at a given locus on both homologous chromosomes is homozygous; one with different alleles at that locus is heterozygous.

5. Chromosomes are Copied Precisely

Between nuclear divisions, while chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin and cannot be seen individually, each chromosome is replicated (copied) during interphase of the cell cycle. The two identical resulting structures are called chromatids, which remain joined at their centromere until separated during nuclear division, after which each is again recognized as a chromosome.

Each chromatid is an identical copy of the other, carrying the same linear series of genes (too small to be individually visible). The centromere is shown as a small constriction that does not itself code for a protein. A further stage shows the centromere dividing and the two chromatids separating during nuclear division to become two individual chromosomes.

Karyotype and karyogramKaryotype: the number and type of chromosomes present in an organism (including their length, banding pattern and centromere position). Karyogram: a diagram or photograph showing the chromosomes of a cell, arranged in homologous pairs in descending order of size.
Figure A3.1.10: Chromosomes as homologous pairs, seen during nuclear divisionThe left-hand image shows a photomicrograph of human chromosomes from a diploid male cell captured during mitosis, at a stage where each chromosome is present as two chromatids joined at the centromere. A karyotype stained to show banding patterns (using Giemsa dye or fluorochromes for fluorescent microscopy) makes it easier to distinguish chromosomes, since the size and location of light and dark bands, reflecting gene positions, are unique to each chromosome pair.

Sex Chromosomes

Sex chromosomeA chromosome that determines sex rather than other body (somatic) characteristics. In humans these are labelled X and Y and are typically shown as the final pair in a karyogram; the other 22 pairs are called autosomes. Karyograms are used by genetic counsellors to detect rare chromosomal abnormalities, such as Down syndrome.

Human and Chimpanzee Chromosome Comparison

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes, but every other living species in the family Hominidae, including the bonobo and chimpanzee, has 24 pairs — even though less than 3% of human DNA differs from that of chimpanzees.

Figure A3.1.12: A comparison of human and chimpanzee chromosomesThe figure aligns the karyograms of humans and chimpanzees, showing that the centromere of human chromosome 2 lines up almost exactly with that of chimpanzee chromosome 2A, suggesting that two ancestral ape chromosomes fused to form one large human chromosome.

Scientists compared banding patterns and found remarkable similarity between human chromosome 2 and the chimpanzee chromosomes originally numbered 12 and 13. Human chromosome 2 contains remnants of a second centromere (most chromosomes have only one), and a segment near this remnant centromere on the long arm closely resembles chimpanzee telomeric DNA — exactly what would be expected if two chimpanzee chromosomes had fused end-to-end.

Figure A3.1.13: Evolution of human chromosome 2The diagram compares human chromosome 2 with chimpanzee chromosomes 2A and 2B, showing the position of the (now vestigial, off-centre) remnant centromere in human chromosome 2 corresponding to where the two ancestral chimpanzee chromosomes joined.

This evidence supports the hypothesis of an ancestral fusion event between the chimpanzee chromosomes originally numbered 12 and 13, now renamed 2A and 2B in recognition of the fusion. The resulting human chromosome, the second largest visible under a microscope, is named chromosome 2. Studies show no genes from the ends of chimpanzee chromosomes 2A and 2B appear to have been lost in the fusion. This is considered a testable hypothesis, since chromosomal observations agree with independent genomic analysis suggesting a common ancestor between humans and chimpanzees around 4 million years ago.

Unity and Diversity of Genomes Within Species

Concept: Unity and DiversityOrganisms in the same species share most of their genome, but variations such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms give some diversity within species’ genomes.
Genome and SNPGenome: the whole of the genetic information of an organism or cell. Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP): polymorphism involving variation of a single base pair.

An SNP is a difference in a single nucleotide — for example, guanine (G) replaced by adenine (A) at one position. SNPs occur throughout the genome, on average about once in every 1000 nucleotides in humans, meaning there may be between 4 and 5 million SNPs in one person’s genome; scientists have identified more than 100 million SNPs across populations worldwide. Most are found in non-coding DNA between genes, and in this way contribute to human diversity. SNPs can act as biological markers, helping scientists locate disease-associated genes; when they occur within a gene or its regulatory region they may directly affect gene function. SNPs can also help predict an individual’s response to particular drugs and their risk of developing certain diseases, and can be used to trace inheritance of genetic diseases within families.

Diversity of Eukaryote Genomes and Comparing Genome Sizes

Genomes vary in overall size (determined by the total amount of DNA) and in base sequence, with variation between species being much larger than variation within a species.

Genome sizeGenome size refers to the amount of DNA in a set of chromosomes in a species, measured in millions of base pairs (Mb). Not all DNA codes for protein.

Larger genomes do not necessarily indicate greater organismal complexity. Some organisms, particularly plants, form new species by combining whole sets of chromosomes (a process called polyploidy), which can produce extremely large genome sizes unrelated to organismal complexity. Genome size can be measured as the ‘C-value’, the mass of DNA in picograms in a haploid set of chromosomes (roughly 1 pg ≈ 1 billion base pairs, or 1000 Mb of DNA), often measured from gametes, and can be looked up in specialist genome-size databases for animals and plants.

Common mistakeDNA in chromosomes, mitochondria, and (in plants) chloroplasts is considered part of the genome, but mRNA and tRNA are not. Also, ‘genome size’ refers to the total amount of DNA measured in base pairs, not the number of genes.

Current and Potential Future Uses of Whole Genome Sequencing

The Human Genome Project

The Human Genome Project (HGP), a publicly funded initiative launched in 1990 to determine the base sequence of the entire human genome, shared work among more than 200 laboratories worldwide. Completion of the human genome sequence was announced on 26 June 2000. Alongside the HGP, scientists sequenced the genomes of other organisms including E. coli, the fruit fly, the mouse, bakers’ yeast and Arabidopsis. Since then, roughly 3500 species of complex life have had their genomes sequenced, with around 100 sequenced at ‘reference quality’ for in-depth research. Sequencing speed has increased while cost has decreased over time.

1. How Many Genes Do We Have, and How Do They Work?

Genes can be located within base-sequence data by detecting regions uninterrupted by ‘start’ and ‘stop’ codons, which are more likely to code for protein. The roughly 3 × 109 bases of the human genome represent only about 20 000 genes — far fewer than expected (the fruit fly Drosophila has almost half our gene number, and rice has more than 40 000 genes). Promoters and enhancers associated with many genes help determine which cells express which genes, when, and at what level. The genetic code itself is not altered by the environment (except through mutation), but gene expression can be regulated by external factors acting through promoters, so our experience may influence how our genes are expressed.

2. Locating the Cause of Genetic Disorders

The HGP also enables scientists to locate genes responsible for human genetic disorders. More than half of all genetic disorders result from mutation of a single, commonly recessive, gene. To prove a gene is linked to a disease, affected patients must be shown to carry a mutation in that gene while unaffected individuals do not; the mutation typically causes loss of the gene’s normal product. Common genetic diseases linked in this way include cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell disease and haemophilia.

Figure A3.1.15: Determining the order of nucleotide bases in a DNA moleculeThe figure shows a largely automated DNA sequencing process, in which the order of nucleotide bases along a DNA molecule is determined by machine.

3. Development of the New Discipline of Bioinformatics

The genomes of many prokaryotes and eukaryotes, as well as humans, have now been sequenced, producing huge volumes of data that require organization, storage and indexing to be put to practical use. Bioinformatics — the storage, manipulation and analysis of biological information using computer science, applied mathematics, informatics and statistics — has developed to meet this need, centred on databases of nucleic acid sequences and their derived proteins.

Whole Genome Sequencing: Current and Potential Future Uses

Currently, genome sequencing is used to research evolutionary relationships between species. In future, advancing technology could enable more widespread personalized medicine, where an individual’s genetic profile guides decisions on disease prevention, diagnosis and treatment.

Malaria, the most significant insect-borne disease, threatens around 40% of the world’s population, with about 90% of fatalities occurring in Africa south of the Sahara. It is caused by Plasmodium, a protist transmitted between people by Anopheles mosquitoes; of the four Plasmodium species, only P. falciparum causes severe illness. The entire genome sequence of P. falciparum has been analysed, revealing 14 chromosomes encoding approximately 5300 genes — a major boost to vaccine development efforts, since sequencing can help identify potential antigens for antibodies to target. However, the complex parasitic lifestyle of Plasmodium, and its ability to rapidly develop drug resistance, make it difficult to eradicate, and an effective malaria vaccine remains a major international challenge.

Higher Level Difficulties Applying the Biological Species Concept to Asexually Reproducing Species and Bacteria

The biological species concept does not work well for organisms that do not reproduce sexually, or where genes can transfer between species, as in prokaryotes.

Genetic Transfer in Prokaryotes

Although bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, their populations show great genetic diversity. Within a bacterial species, a strain is a lineage with genetic differences from another strain — for example, one strain of E. coli may be antibiotic-resistant while another is sensitive to the same antibiotic. Genetic diversity in bacteria arises from two main sources: mutation (which alters the DNA sequence and can change bacterial traits, such as creating a strain that requires an external amino acid source that other strains can synthesize themselves), and genetic transfer (horizontal gene transfer), in which genetic material passes from one bacterial cell to another via transformation, transduction or conjugation.

Binary fissionWhen a cell divides into two daughter cells, typically in asexual reproduction of prokaryotes.
TransformationThe alteration of a bacterial cell’s genotype by uptake of naked, foreign DNA from the surrounding environment, usually released when another bacterium has lysed. Many bacteria have cell-surface proteins that recognize and transport DNA from closely related species into the cell; this foreign DNA can then be incorporated into the genome by insertion or recombination via crossing over at homologous regions.
Figure A3.1.16: Transformation in bacteriaThe figure shows two routes of transformation. In the first, DNA fragments are taken up from the environment and integrated into the bacterial chromosome by recombination, producing stable transformation. In the second, a DNA plasmid is taken up directly by the bacterial cell, again producing stable transformation.
TransductionOccurs when a bacteriophage infects a bacterial cell and transfers some of that cell’s DNA to another bacterium. This results from an error during the phage lytic cycle: as the bacterial chromosome is degraded into small pieces during phage DNA and protein synthesis, newly assembled phage coat proteins occasionally enclose a piece of bacterial DNA instead of phage genetic material, creating an abnormal phage that carries bacterial chromosomal DNA into the next host it infects.
ConjugationInvolves direct physical contact between two bacterial cells and one-way transfer of genetic material from a donor to a recipient bacterium. The ability to form sex pili and donate DNA during conjugation depends on the presence of an F (fertility) plasmid, which carries the genes needed to produce sex pili and may also carry genes conferring a growth advantage. After a sex pilus attaches to a recipient cell and draws the two cells together, a temporary cytoplasmic mating bridge (conjugation tube) forms between them, providing a route for DNA transfer.
Figure A3.1.17: Conjugation in bacteriaThe figure shows the stages of conjugation: (a) a conjugation tube forms between a donor and recipient cell, and an enzyme cuts the donor’s plasmid; (b) plasmid DNA replication begins, and the free DNA strand starts moving through the tube to the recipient; (c) in the recipient cell, replication starts on the transferred DNA strand; (d) the cells separate and the plasmid in each cell re-forms a circle.

A Species Concept for Prokaryotes and Other Asexually Reproducing Species

Because DNA can be exchanged between bacteria via horizontal gene transfer, and rates of genetic transfer vary between populations with constantly changing genotypes, it is difficult to apply the biological species concept (which requires organisms to ‘breed and produce fertile offspring’) to bacteria and other asexually reproducing organisms. Species definitions with an evolutionary (‘phylogenetic’) basis may therefore be more appropriate for prokaryotes.

Higher Level Chromosome Number as a Shared Trait Within a Species

All individuals within a species normally have the same number of chromosomes (with rare exceptions involving chromosome non-disjunction). Chromosomes occur in even numbers because they exist as homologous pairs, one from each parent. To form gametes, each pair of homologous chromosomes separates into a different daughter cell during meiosis, so each gamete carries only one set of chromosomes; at fertilization, when a male gamete fuses with a female gamete, the diploid (two of each chromosome) number is restored. If chromosomes did not separate this way, chromosome number would double every generation.

Breeding between different species is generally not possible because a mismatch in gamete chromosome numbers prevents proper chromosome pairing after fertilization (anatomical incompatibilities between species can also prevent reproduction). Cross-breeding between closely related species with different parent chromosome numbers is unlikely to produce fertile offspring, because if the resulting hybrid has an uneven, odd chromosome number, its chromosomes cannot divide equally into gametes during meiosis.

Figure A3.1.18: Cross-breeding between closely related species is unlikely to produce fertile offspring if parent chromosome numbers are differentThe figure shows a cross between a horse (Equus caballus, 64 chromosomes in body cells, 32 in gametes) and a donkey (Equus asinus, 62 chromosomes in body cells, 31 in gametes), which produces a mule with 63 chromosomes in its body cells. Because 63 is an odd number, it cannot be divided equally between gametes, so the mule’s gametes cannot form and mules are infertile.

Higher Level Developing a Dichotomous Key

Dichotomous keyAn identification key in which a group of organisms is progressively divided into two groups of smaller size.

Identifying unknown organisms in fieldwork is important but time-consuming. Identification books with drawings, photographs and habitat information can help, but dichotomous keys offer an alternative that requires careful observation of structural features and builds understanding of how organisms may be related.

To construct a dichotomous key, a representative specimen of each type of organism is chosen and examined carefully, and its most significant structural features are recorded in a presence/absence matrix against every specimen.

Africa), Pignut Hickory (N. America), Osier (Central and W. Europe), Southern Catalpa (N. America) and Indian Horse Chestnut (Himalaya).
Figure A3.1.20: Matrix of characteristics shown by one or more of the sample leavesThe figure is a table with the eight leaf specimens (A–H) as columns and their structural features as rows — such as whether the leaf is undivided (entire) or made of leaflets, whether the blade is narrow with parallel sides or broad, whether leaflets radiate from one point (palmate) or are arranged in two rows, whether the margin is smooth or toothed like a saw, whether the blade is heart-shaped or boat-shaped, whether leaflets are paddle-shaped, and how many leaflets are present — with presence marked for each specimen.

From the matrix, a feature shown by roughly half the specimens is chosen to divide them into two groups, and this process is repeated to build a dichotomous flow diagram that progressively divides the specimens into smaller groups, with each division point labelled by its critical diagnostic feature.

Figure A3.1.21: Dichotomous flow diagram of leaf characteristicsThe figure shows a branching flow diagram starting from all eight leaves, first splitting into ‘leaf entire’ (A, D, F, G) versus ‘leaf divided into leaflets’ (B, C, E, H), then progressively subdividing each branch using features such as blade shape (narrow with parallel sides vs broad), margin type (smooth vs toothed), overall shape (heart-shaped vs boat-shaped), and leaflet arrangement or number, until each of the eight leaves (A–H) is uniquely identified at the end of a branch.

Finally, the flow diagram is converted into a dichotomous key: each division point becomes a pair of alternative statements answered ‘yes’ or ‘no’, each leading either to another numbered pair of statements or to a named leaf.

Figure A3.1.22: Dichotomous key to the sample of eight tree leavesThe figure presents the finished numbered key (statements 1–7), each offering two alternatives (e.g. ‘leaves entire’ vs ‘leaf blade divided into leaflets’; ‘blade margin toothed like a saw’ vs ‘blade margin smooth’) that direct the reader either to the next numbered pair or to the name of one of the eight tree species (Macedonian Oak, Osier, Sweet Chestnut, Southern Catalpa, Horse Chestnut, Indian Horse Chestnut, Pignut Hickory, Picrasma).
Limitations of dichotomous keysThe organism sought might not be included in the key; individuals of one species may vary in morphology (e.g. sexual dimorphism), making identification unreliable; terminology can be difficult for non-experts to interpret; a key may not exist at all for the organisms being studied; and some features needed for identification cannot easily be established in the field (e.g. whether an animal has a placenta, or is endothermic or ectothermic).

Higher Level Identifying Species from Environmental DNA Using Barcodes

Around 1.8 million species have been formally described, but some scientists estimate there may be between 8 and 10 million species on Earth (possibly more), meaning a large number of species still await identification and classification. Traditional taxonomy requires considerable expertise and time, both in short supply as many species face extinction.

DNA barcodingSpecies identification using short sections of DNA. The genes chosen show less intraspecific (within-species) variation than interspecific (between-species) variation; for most animal groups, biologists use the sequence of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase gene. Small DNA samples can be amplified using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Just as a commercial barcode uniquely identifies a product using lines and spaces, the DNA profile produced by gel electrophoresis is unique to the individual it comes from and forms a specific pattern of bars and gaps; overall ‘barcodes’ can be built to represent the genetic makeup of a species even though individuals vary slightly. DNA barcoding offers a fast, accurate alternative to traditional taxonomy, avoiding some of its limitations (such as the inability to identify damaged or partial specimens, and morphological variation within species).

Figure A3.1.23: Species identification data available on the internetThe figure shows the DNA barcoding workflow: a specimen is collected along with collection data and a photograph; a tissue sample is taken and DNA extracted; the DNA is amplified by PCR and sequenced to produce a DNA barcode; the specimen data and DNA barcode are then made web-accessible so scientists worldwide can access information about the species.

Once enough species have established barcodes, environmental samples can be tested to obtain DNA from a variety of organisms present in a habitat, allowing the biodiversity of that habitat to be investigated rapidly — without needing to directly observe or capture every organism present.
