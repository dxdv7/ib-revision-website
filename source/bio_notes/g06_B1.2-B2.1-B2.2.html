Amino acids: building blocks of proteins

Proteins make up about two-thirds of the total dry mass of a cell. Unlike carbohydrates and lipids, proteins contain atoms of nitrogen and usually sulfur, in addition to carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Proteins are crucial biomolecules for basic life processes: they are responsible for transport throughout a cell or organism, for maintaining cellular structures and for basic metabolism, among other roles. Haemoglobin and myoglobin transport and store oxygen; proteins form channels and pumps that move ions, water and other molecules through the cell membrane; proteins carry molecules between the cell membrane and intracellular compartments; and many proteins act as enzymes.

Amino acids are the molecules from which peptides and proteins are built — typically several hundred or even thousands of amino acid molecules combine to form a protein. The terms “polypeptide” and “protein” are often used interchangeably, but once a polypeptide is about 20 amino acid residues long, it is generally agreed to have become a protein.

Key termAn amino acid residue refers to an amino acid once it is bonded into a polypeptide chain.

Amino acids carry two functional groups attached to the same alpha (central) carbon atom: an amino group (−NH2, basic) and an organic acid group (carboxyl group, −COOH, acidic). A side-chain part of the molecule, called an R-group, is also attached to this alpha carbon, together with a hydrogen atom. The 20 different amino acids that make up proteins in cells and organisms differ only in their side chains (R-groups); the R-group determines the chemical character of each amino acid, and therefore of the proteins that contain it.

Forming dipeptides and longer chains of amino acids

Two amino acids combine with the loss of a water molecule to form a dipeptide — a catalysed condensation reaction. The reaction, which occurs in ribosomes, takes place between the amino group (−NH2) of one amino acid and the carboxyl group (−COOH) of another amino acid, forming a dipeptide plus a molecule of water. The two amino acid residues are held together by a covalent bond called a peptide bond or peptide linkage.

Key termsPeptide linkage: a covalent bond formed between the amino group of one amino acid and the carboxyl group of another, with the loss of a molecule of water. A tripeptide is a peptide made of three amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds.
Figure B1.2.3: Peptide linkage formationAmino acid 1 (with its own R-group) and amino acid 2 (with a different R-group) are shown approaching each other. The carboxyl group (−COOH) of amino acid 1 reacts with the amino group (−NH2) of amino acid 2. A hydroxyl (−OH) from the carboxyl group and a hydrogen from the amino group combine to leave as a molecule of water (H2O), while the remaining carbon and nitrogen atoms bond together to form the peptide linkage (−CO−NH−), joining the two amino acids into a dipeptide.

When a further amino acid residue is attached by another condensation reaction, a tripeptide is formed; repeating this process builds long strings of amino acid residues into polypeptides and proteins. The start of an amino acid chain is called the N-terminus (the end with a free amino group) and the end is called the C-terminus (the end with a free carboxyl group). Peptide or protein chains are assembled one amino acid at a time in the presence of a specific enzyme, peptidyl transferase, located in the ribosome.

Proteins differ in the variety, number and order of their constituent amino acids. The order of amino acids in the polypeptide chain is controlled by the sequence of bases in DNA. Changing just one amino acid in the sequence of a protein can alter its properties completely. Each protein folds into a particular three-dimensional conformation depending on its amino acid sequence, and this shape determines its function. Some proteins, such as haemoglobin, consist of more than one polypeptide chain linked together.

A nutrient is a chemical substance found in foods that is used in the body. Some nutrients are essential components of the diet; others are non-essential. An essential nutrient is one that cannot be synthesized by the body and so must be obtained from food. Non-essential nutrients are those that are made in the body, or that have a replacement nutrient able to fulfil the same dietary purpose.

Proteins in the diet are first digested — broken down — by proteases into their constituent amino acids. These amino acids are absorbed into the body and contribute to a pool of amino acids from which new proteins are built, whether the original protein came from animal or plant sources. All 20 amino acids must be present in this pool for protein synthesis to proceed. Any amino acids present in excess cannot be stored: instead they are deaminated, and the resulting −NH2 group is combined with carbon dioxide to form urea, which is excreted. The ten amino acids that humans can synthesize themselves, when necessary, are produced by a process called transamination, in which an amino acid present in excess reacts with another organic acid to form a new amino acid plus a different organic acid.

Non-essential amino acids are made from other amino acids: for example, tyrosine is synthesized from the essential amino acid phenylalanine, and cysteine from the essential amino acid methionine. The infinite variety of possible peptide chains

Each type of protein has a unique sequence of amino acids, and there are many thousands of different proteins in cells. The proteome is the entire set of proteins found in an individual organism. The 20 amino acids used to build proteins are coded for in the genetic code; a gene is a sequence of DNA that encodes a polypeptide or protein, and typically one gene codes for one protein. Peptide chains can be any length, from a few amino acids to many thousands, and the amino acids can occur in any order.

Examples of polypeptides include enzymes (such as amylase, which digests starch), hormones (such as insulin) and proteins found in the cell membrane. Histone proteins are involved in DNA packing in eukaryotic chromosomes.

P = An, where P = the number of different possible polypeptides, A = the number of different types of amino acid available, and n = the number of amino acid residues in the polypeptide.

Using the naturally occurring pool of 20 different amino acids, this formula shows just how enormous the potential diversity of proteins is: a short polypeptide of only 5 residues could already be assembled in 205 (3,200,000) different ways, while a polypeptide of 25 residues could take on around 2025 different sequences, and one of 50 residues around 2050 different sequences — numbers so large they vastly exceed the number of atoms in the observable universe. This calculation illustrates why the amino acid sequence of a protein provides an almost limitless source of biological diversity in form and function.

Effects of pH and temperature on protein structure

Once a polypeptide chain is constructed, a protein takes up a specific shape, and this shape is closely related to its function — especially for proteins that are enzymes. The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is sensitive to environmental conditions. In extreme cases, proteins, including enzymes, may become denatured.

DenaturationA structural change in a protein that results in a loss — usually permanent — of its biological properties, altering its three-dimensional shape. Denaturation occurs when the ionic interactions, hydrogen bonds and other weak intermolecular forces within a globular protein, formed between different amino acid residues, break down, changing the protein’s shape, including the active site of an enzyme.
Figure B1.2.5: Folding of a protein from its denatured stateA correctly folded protein is shown being exposed to high temperature or incorrect pH, causing it to unfold into a denatured protein with a disrupted three-dimensional shape. If the denaturant is then removed, the diagram shows the protein refolding back into its original conformation — illustrating that, for small proteins, the amino acid sequence alone can determine the final three-dimensional structure.
How denaturation happensRises in temperature, or even a small deviation in pH away from the optimum, can denature proteins. Exposure to heat causes atoms to vibrate more violently, disrupting the weak, non-covalent intermolecular forces that hold a protein’s shape together. For many small proteins, once the triggering conditions are removed, the protein reverts to its original shape — suggesting that it is the amino acid sequence of the protein that ultimately decides its three-dimensional structure. However, more extreme denaturation is often irreversible: cooking a hen’s egg denatures the translucent protein albumen in the egg white, which becomes permanently opaque and insoluble. Small changes in pH can be reversed if the optimum pH is restored, but exposure to strong acids or alkalis usually denatures proteins irreversibly.

Higher Level Chemical diversity in the R-groups of amino acids

The reactive groups of an amino acid — the basic amino group (−NH2) and the acidic carboxyl group (−COOH) — are both bonded to the same carbon atom, and it is these groups that take part in the condensation reactions forming peptide bonds. The remainder of the molecule, the R-group, may vary enormously between different amino acids, giving each amino acid a distinct chemical character. The categories of amino acid found in cell proteins are:

  • Acidic amino acids: have an additional carboxyl group in the R-group (e.g. aspartic acid).
  • Basic amino acids: have an additional amino group in the R-group (e.g. lysine).
  • Amino acids with hydrophilic properties (water soluble): have polar or charged R-groups (e.g. serine).
  • Amino acids with hydrophobic properties (insoluble): have non-polar R-groups (e.g. alanine).
Figure B1.2.6: The range of amino acids used in protein synthesisFour generalized amino acid structures are shown, each with the alpha carbon bearing a carboxyl group and an amino group, but with contrasting R-groups: aspartic acid (an acidic amino acid, with an additional carboxyl group in its R-group), lysine (a basic amino acid, with an additional amino group in its R-group), serine (an amino acid with a polar, hydrophilic R-group), and alanine (an amino acid with a small, non-polar, hydrophobic R-group).
Polar vs chargedA polar molecule (such as water) has an uneven distribution of electrons, so it has regions of positive and negative charge, but its overall charge is neutral. In contrast, a group is described as charged when the number of positive and negative charges are not equal — protons and electrons are not balanced.

Water molecules force hydrophobic R-groups together to minimize disruption to the hydrogen-bonded network of water molecules. Hydrophobic amino acids have little or no polarity in their side chains, so they cannot interact with highly polar water molecules; this is sometimes described as a “hydrophobic force”, but the effect is really caused by repulsion from water rather than any attraction between the hydrophobic groups themselves. This repulsion is also important for the assembly of lipid vesicles and membranes, and for protein folding: polar amino acid side chains tend to be displayed on the outside of a folded protein where they can interact with water, while non-polar side chains are buried on the inside.

Higher Level Levels of protein structure: primary and secondary

The sequence of amino acids determines the precise position of each residue within a structure, and therefore the overall three-dimensional shape of the protein. There are four levels of protein structure, each significant in biology: primary, secondary, tertiary and, in some proteins, quaternary.

Figure B1.2.7: The different levels in the hierarchy of protein structureThe diagram shows, in sequence: the primary structure (a simple linear chain showing the sequence of amino acids joined by peptide linkages); the secondary structure (parts of the same chain coiled into a helical shape); the tertiary structure (the coiled chain further folded into a compact three-dimensional shape that creates an active site); and the quaternary structure (two or more separately folded polypeptide chains associating together to form one larger functional protein).

Primary structure

The primary structure of a protein is the linear sequence of amino acid residues joined by peptide linkages, and it determines the overall shape and properties of the protein. The sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain is controlled by the coded instructions stored in DNA, mediated via mRNA. Changing just one amino acid in the sequence, which can arise through mutation, often drastically alters the protein’s properties.

Common mistakePrimary structure is not simply “a string of amino acids” — the idea of a specific sequence or order is essential to the definition.

Secondary structure

The secondary structure of a protein develops when parts of the polypeptide chain take up a particular shape immediately after formation at the ribosome, becoming folded or twisted (or both). The most common shapes are formed by coiling into an alpha-helix, or folding into beta-sheets. These shapes are permanent and held in place by hydrogen bonds.

Figure B1.2.8: The secondary structure of proteinsTwo structures are shown side by side. The alpha-helix is drawn as a rod-like, right-handed coil of the polypeptide backbone, with regularly spaced hydrogen bonds running roughly parallel to the helix axis, linking the C=O group of one peptide linkage to the N−H group of a peptide linkage further along the chain, holding the coil in shape; R-groups project outward from the helix. Beta-sheets are shown as sections of polypeptide chain lying alongside one another in a pleated, zig-zag arrangement, with hydrogen bonds forming between the backbone atoms of adjacent, parallel sections of chain, and R-groups projecting alternately above and below the sheet.
Figure B1.2.9: BipD, an invasion protein from the bacterium Burkholderia pseudomalleiA ribbon model of the protein BipD (which causes the disease melioidosis) shows extensive regions of alpha-helix (coloured red) and beta-sheet (coloured pale green), connected by flexible loop regions (coloured cream) — illustrating how a single protein can contain substantial stretches of both types of secondary structure.

Hydrogen bonding in regular positions helps to stabilize both alpha-helices and beta-pleated sheets.

Super-secondary structure

Super-secondary structure describes recurring patterns that alpha-helices or beta-sheets commonly adopt within proteins. Four alpha-helices, for example, often form a coiled-coil arrangement called a four-helix bundle, with pairs of adjacent helices further stabilized by salt bridges (ionic bonds) between charged amino acids. Two beta-sheets may stack on top of each other in a “beta sandwich”, as seen in the immunoglobulin fold.

Figure B1.2.10: Super-secondary structure in a proteinPart (a) shows a four-helix bundle: four separate alpha-helices arranged side by side and twisted around each other in a coiled-coil. Part (b) shows a “beta sandwich”: two beta-sheets stacked flat on top of one another.

Super-secondary structure often corresponds to a particular domain — a compact, independently folded structure within a polypeptide chain that usually has a specific function, such as DNA-binding capability or the ability to induce dimerisation between two proteins that share the domain. A protein dimer is a macromolecular complex formed from two protein monomers.

Higher Level Tertiary structure of proteins

The tertiary structure of a protein is the precise, compact structure unique to that protein, which arises as the molecule folds further and is held in a particular complex shape. This shape is stabilized by four different types of bonding, established through interactions between R-groups and other parts of the chain: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide covalent bonds, and hydrophobic interactions (including weak London/dispersion forces).

Two of the 20 amino acids — cysteine and methionine — contain sulfur, so disulfide bridges can form between their side chains in a protein’s tertiary structure. Effect of polar and non-polar amino acids on tertiary structure

Amino acids with polar R-groups have hydrophilic properties; when built into a protein in prominent positions, they can influence its properties and function. Amino acids with non-polar R-groups have hydrophobic properties. Hydrophobic amino acids are clustered in the core of globular proteins that are soluble in water. Integral membrane proteins have regions rich in hydrophobic amino acids that help them embed permanently in the membrane; the portions of these proteins found inside the membrane are hydrophobic, while portions exposed to the cytoplasm or extracellular fluid tend to be hydrophilic, matching the non-polar fatty acid tails that form the interior of the membrane.

Higher Level Quaternary structure: conjugated and non-conjugated proteins

The quaternary structure of a protein arises when two or more polypeptide chains, or proteins, are held together to form a single complex, biologically active molecule.

Conjugated proteins — haemoglobin

Conjugated proteinA combination of a protein and a non-protein prosthetic group.

Haemoglobin is a conjugated protein: it consists of four polypeptide chains (two alpha-chains and two beta-chains), and each chain is held around a non-protein haem group (the prosthetic group), which contains an atom of iron. Conjugated proteins in general may have carbohydrate groups attached (such as in the glycocalyx of cell membranes), lipids, bound metal ions, or other organic groups.

Non-conjugated proteins — insulin and collagen

Non-conjugated proteinA protein that is not associated with a non-protein prosthetic group. Insulin and collagen are examples.

In 1951, the English biochemist Frederick Sanger determined the first amino acid sequence of a protein, insulin — a major discovery that established that every protein has a precisely defined amino acid sequence. Insulin is a hormone involved in glucose regulation and is an example of a globular protein. It is composed of an A chain and a B chain, linked together by two disulfide bonds, with an additional disulfide bond formed within the A chain itself.

Collagen is the most abundant protein in animals. It gives structure and strength to the body, and is found in bones, muscles, skin and tendons; it is an example of a fibrous protein. The quaternary structure of collagen consists of three left-handed helices twisted together into one right-handed triple helix.

Each polypeptide chain has a repeated triplet sequence, Gly-X-Y, where X and Y can be any amino acid but are frequently proline (X position) and hydroxyproline (Y position). This regular pattern of glycine allows each helical chain to turn every three amino acids and intertwine around the other two chains to form a compact triple helix, held together by interchain hydrogen bonds to form a unit called tropocollagen. Many triple helices lie parallel to one another in a staggered pattern to form fibrils, cross-linked by covalent bonds between neighbouring triple helices; the fibrils then unite to form fibres.

X-ray crystallography has traditionally been used to obtain high-resolution images of crystalline proteins: diffracted X-rays generate spots on a detector whose intensity and position can be computer-processed into an electron density contour map showing the protein’s structure and shape. An alternative technique, cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), uses the same basic principle as transmission electron microscopy, but cools samples to cryogenic temperatures (using liquid nitrogen) and embeds them in non-crystalline (vitreous) ice. Randomly orientated protein molecules are struck by an electron beam, producing faint images; thousands of these images are computer-averaged to generate a high-resolution three-dimensional density map, allowing different conformations of the same protein to be distinguished and large, intact complexes (such as ribosomes and viruses) to be studied almost to atomic scale. X-ray crystallography, however, remains much quicker and simpler, and the majority of known protein structures still come from X-ray data — a reminder that new technology alone does not always determine how scientific knowledge is produced or which tools scientists prefer to use.

Higher Level Form and function: globular and fibrous proteins

Globular proteins

Globular proteins take up a roughly spherical shape and are mostly highly soluble in water. Examples include enzymes such as lysozyme and catalase, and hormones such as insulin. Insulin is a very small protein, which allows it to move quickly through the blood, and its shape is recognized by specific receptors on target cell surfaces. Because it is difficult to make such a small protein fold into a stable structure directly, the cell instead synthesizes a longer protein chain that folds correctly, after which the extra piece is removed, leaving the two small chains of the mature hormone, further stabilized by three disulfide bridges.

Figure B1.2.24: Insulin, a globular proteinInsulin is first produced as preproinsulin, a single polypeptide of 102 amino acid residues, by ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) of beta cells in the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas. A signal peptide part of the molecule directs it into the lumen of the RER. Post-translational modification converts preproinsulin into proinsulin, which is stabilized by disulfide bonds and takes shape in the lumen of the RER. Proinsulin is transported from the RER to the Golgi apparatus in vesicles, where further enzyme modification removes the central C chain, leaving mature insulin — the A chain and B chain held together by disulfide bonds — which takes up its tertiary structure as a more or less globular protein.

Fibrous proteins

Some proteins take up a tertiary structure that is a long, much-coiled chain with a long, narrow shape; these are called fibrous proteins, and they are often insoluble. Examples include collagen (a component of bone and tendons) and keratin (found in hair, horn and nails). The collagen molecule consists of three polypeptide chains, each shaped as a helix and wound together as a triple helix, forming a stiff cable strengthened by numerous hydrogen bonds. The ends of individual collagen molecules are staggered so there are no weak points along collagen fibres, giving the whole structure high tensile strength — making the protein well suited to providing structural support in skin, tendons and cartilage.

Figure B1.2.23: Photomicrograph of collagenA photomicrograph of the fibrous protein collagen shows numerous triple helices bound together side by side into thick, ropelike fibres.
Globular vs fibrous proteinsGlobular proteins are compact, roughly spherical, and generally highly soluble in water; their functions (such as catalysis in enzymes, or oxygen transport and hormone signalling) usually depend on a precisely shaped active site or binding site formed by their folded tertiary structure. Fibrous proteins are long, narrow and much-coiled, and are often insoluble; their function is typically structural — providing mechanical strength and support, as in collagen (bone, tendon, skin) and keratin (hair, horn, nails) — rather than depending on a single reactive site.

Phospholipid bilayers: the basis of cell membranes

A plasma membrane is a structure common to all cells. It encompasses and maintains the integrity of the cell, holding its contents together, and it is also the barrier across which all substances entering or leaving the cell must cross. The lipid of membranes is predominantly phospholipid, together with other lipid types.

A phospholipid molecule has a hydrophilic head, which attracts water, and a hydrophobic tail, which repels water. When added to the surface of a water layer, phospholipid molecules arrange themselves into a monolayer, with their hydrophilic heads dissolved in the water and their hydrophobic tails sticking outwards, away from the water. When phospholipid molecules are instead mixed into water, they spontaneously arrange themselves into a bilayer, in which the hydrophobic tails on the inside are attracted to each other and shielded from water, while the hydrophilic heads face outward into the surrounding water on both surfaces.

Figure B2.1.1: The response of phospholipid molecules in waterOn the left, phospholipid molecules added to the surface of water form a single monolayer, heads down in the water and tails pointing up into the air. On the right, phospholipid molecules mixed into water form a double-layered bilayer, with hydrophilic heads facing outward on both surfaces (in contact with water) and hydrophobic tails facing inward, away from water, on both sides of the bilayer.

Monolayers can be extensive and cover a water surface, whereas bilayers need to be continuous, and so they spontaneously form closed structures called vesicles.

Lipid bilayers as barriers

The lipid bilayer is about 4–10 nm in thickness, thin enough to encompass a prokaryotic cell of 0.1–5 micrometres in diameter or a eukaryotic cell of 10–100 micrometres in diameter. Within the bilayer, attractions between the hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails on the inside, and between the hydrophilic glycerol/phosphate heads and the surrounding water on the outside, make a stable, strong barrier. The hydrophobic hydrocarbon chains that form the core of the membrane have low permeability to large and hydrophilic molecules, including ions and polar molecules. Membranes therefore function as effective barriers between aqueous solutions — for example, between the cytoplasm and the fluid outside a cell.

Molecules such as water, respiratory gases (oxygen and carbon dioxide), nutrients (e.g. glucose), essential ions and excretory products continually move into or out of cells; cells may also secrete substances such as hormones and enzymes, or receive growth substances and hormones. Plants secrete the chemicals that make up their walls through their cell membranes and assemble the wall outside the membrane, while certain mammalian cells secrete structural proteins, such as collagen, that are assembled outside the cell.

Simple diffusion across membranes

DiffusionThe free passage of molecules (and atoms and ions) from a region of their higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

Atoms, molecules and ions of liquids and gases undergo continuous random movements, which result in the even, homogeneous distribution of the components of a gas mixture or the atoms, molecules and ions in a solution — so that a small random sample of a solution has the same composition as the whole solution. “Membrane diffusion” describes the movement of molecules through the phospholipid bilayer of membranes. Oxygen and carbon dioxide, being very small molecules, are examples of substances that undergo simple diffusion across membranes: oxygen diffuses into cells from a higher concentration in blood plasma, through tissue fluid, into cells where it is used in aerobic respiration, while carbon dioxide travels in the opposite direction.

Where a concentration gradient exists in a gas or liquid, random molecular movements carry molecules from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration until the particles become evenly dispersed; the energy for this process comes from the kinetic energy store of the molecules themselves.

Figure B2.1.4: Diffusion in a liquidA three-step sequence shows a crystal of potassium permanganate (potassium manganate(VII), KMnO4) placed in distilled water. As the crystal dissolves, its potassium and permanganate ions are dispersed through the water by random molecular movements. Over time, the ions become evenly distributed throughout the water; random movements continue, but with no further net movement in any particular direction.

Diffusion across the cell membrane occurs where the plasma membrane is fully permeable to the solute; the lipid bilayer itself is permeable to non-polar substances, including steroids, and to oxygen and carbon dioxide in solution, all of which diffuse quickly by this route. The term “diffusion”, when applied to movement across membranes, specifically refers to movement across the phospholipid part of the membrane (as distinct from facilitated diffusion through proteins).

Integral and peripheral proteins in membranes

Membrane proteins have diverse structures, locations and functions, falling into two main categories: integral and peripheral proteins.

Peripheral proteinA protein that is attached to the surface of the bilayer — either the outer surface (facing outside the cell) or the inner surface (facing the cytoplasm). Peripheral proteins may shuttle between integral proteins on the membrane surface, act as scaffold proteins to help hold the cell’s shape, or act as receptors for extracellular signals.

Proteins embedded in one or both of the lipid layers of the membrane are described as integral proteins. Integral membrane proteins may act as channels for the transport of metabolites, as enzymes or carriers, or as receptors or antigens.

Osmosis and the role of aquaporins

OsmosisThe diffusion of free water molecules from a region where they are more concentrated (low solute concentration) to a region where they are less concentrated (high solute concentration), across a partially permeable membrane.

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion. Since water makes up 70–90% of living cells, and cell membranes are partially permeable, osmosis is very important in biology — too much or too little water in the blood creates osmotic effects that can be damaging, causing cells either to burst (too much water) or shrink (too little water).

Dissolved substances attract a shell of polar water molecules around them through hydrogen bonding, a process called hydration; this greatly reduces the tendency of both the solute and its surrounding water molecules to move randomly. The more concentrated a solution, the more water molecules are held nearly stationary in this way, whereas in pure water all water molecules are free to move about randomly.

Figure B2.1.6: The process of osmosisA bag made from dialysis tubing (a partially permeable membrane), containing a concentrated sucrose solution, is lowered into distilled water. Water molecules, free to move about, diffuse through the tubing, but the slow-moving sucrose molecules, each surrounded by attached water molecules, cannot escape into the surrounding water. As a result there is a net flow of water into the bag, and the volume of the bag increases as it becomes stretched with water.

When a solution is separated from water (or a more dilute solution) by a water-permeable membrane, free water molecules diffuse across it, but the dissolved solute molecules hardly move at all, producing a net flow of water from the more dilute solution into the more concentrated solution. Osmosis can therefore be defined as the net movement of water molecules from a region of high water concentration to a region of lower water concentration, across a selectively permeable membrane — or equivalently, as the passive movement of water molecules from a region of lower solute concentration to a region of higher solute concentration.

Common mistakeOsmosis is the movement of water molecules, not simply “particles”, from a region of lower solute concentration to a region of higher solute concentration across a partially permeable membrane. Because the membrane is effectively impermeable to solutes (which are too large or too highly charged to cross the phospholipid bilayer), any change in a cell’s mass is due to water moving by osmosis, not to solute movement.

There are protein-lined pores in the plasma membrane, called aquaporins, that allow water to pass through readily. Water also diffuses across the plasma membrane through tiny, transient spaces between phospholipid molecules, especially where the membrane contains phospholipids with unsaturated hydrocarbon tails, since these tails are spaced more widely apart.

Facilitated diffusion via channel proteins

Facilitated diffusionThe movement of particles from a region of higher to lower concentration through integral proteins (carrier or channel proteins); movement is passive.

Particles that cannot diffuse across the plasma membrane through the phospholipid bilayer directly are helped across by integral channel proteins (made of globular protein) that span the membrane and form pores large enough to allow diffusion. Channel proteins provide hydrophilic channels for polar and charged molecules, such as carbohydrates, amino acids and ions, to pass through; these pores close again once the substance is no longer present.

Figure B2.1.8: Channel proteins in the plasma membraneA channel protein spanning the plasma membrane is shown providing a passage for a specific substance to move through the bilayer; each type of channel allows only one specific substance to pass.
Figure B2.1.9: Facilitated diffusionA globular protein embedded in the lipid bilayer is shown forming a temporary pore. When substance X is present at a higher concentration outside the membrane, its presence triggers the pore to open, and substance X diffuses through the specific pore from its region of high concentration to its region of low concentration; when substance X is no longer present, the pore closes again.

The channel proteins’ structure makes membranes selectively permeable, allowing specific ions to diffuse through only when the relevant channels are open. As with all diffusion, the energy for facilitated diffusion comes from the kinetic energy store of the moving molecules — energy from metabolism (ATP) is not required. Important examples of facilitated diffusion include the movement of ADP into mitochondria and the exit of ATP from mitochondria.

Channel proteinA pore in the membrane that allows specific charged particles (e.g. ions) and polar substances (i.e. all hydrophilic substances) to diffuse through the membrane into or out of the cell.

Active transport via pump proteins

Active transportMovement of particles from a lower to a higher concentration, using energy from ATP created during respiration; movement is through carrier proteins.

Many substances required by cells must be absorbed from a dilute external concentration into cells that already contain a higher concentration of that substance — uptake against a concentration gradient, which cannot occur by diffusion and instead requires an input of energy. In active transport, metabolic energy stored as ATP is transferred to drive the transport of molecules and ions across membranes; pump proteins (and some carrier proteins) use this energy to move specific particles across membranes against their concentration gradient.

Figure B2.1.10: Pump proteins in the plasma membraneA pump protein spanning the membrane is shown using energy released from the reaction ATP → ADP + Pi to move one (or two) specific substances selectively across the membrane, against their concentration gradient.

Active transport is a feature of most living cells, seen for example in absorption in the gut, active uptake of ions by plant roots, urine formation in kidney tubules, and impulse propagation in nerve fibres. Active transport has three characteristic features that distinguish it from diffusion:

  1. It occurs against a concentration gradient: valuable molecules and ions (such as nitrate ions in plant cells, or calcium ions in muscle fibres) are actively absorbed into cells even when their concentration outside the cell is lower than inside.
  2. Active uptake is highly selective: most membrane pumps are specific to particular molecules or ions, so a substance is only transported if the corresponding pump molecule is present — for example, an animal cell offered potassium chloride is more likely to absorb the K+ ions it needs than the Cl− ions, while a plant cell offered sodium nitrate is more likely to absorb the NO3− ions it needs than the Na+ ions.
  3. Active transport involves pump molecules: pump molecules span the lipid bilayer, pick up specific molecules or ions on one side, and release them on the other side; this movement requires a reaction with ATP that transfers metabolic energy to the process.
Figure B2.1.11: Active transport of a single substanceA four-step cycle is shown for a carrier protein in the lipid bilayer. (1) A molecule or ion to be transported into the cell binds to the carrier protein. (2) The carrier protein is activated by reacting with ATP. (3) The reaction causes a change in the shape and position of the carrier protein, moving the bound molecule across the membrane. (4) ADP and Pi are released from the carrier protein, the molecule or ion is released on the other side, and the carrier protein reverts to its original, receptive shape ready to repeat the cycle.

Some pump proteins transport a single type of molecule or ion in one direction; others transport two substances (such as Na+ and K+) in opposite directions (as in the sodium–potassium pump); occasionally, two substances such as Na+ and glucose are transported together in the same direction.

Selectivity in membrane permeability

Permeability by simple diffusion is not selective and depends only on the size and hydrophilic or hydrophobic properties of the particles concerned. In contrast, facilitated diffusion and active transport allow membranes to be selectively permeable. Large polar molecules or charged molecules cannot pass through the hydrophobic interior of the membrane, whereas small non-polar molecules can; for many substances, movement across the phospholipid bilayer can therefore only be controlled by altering the concentration gradient across the membrane — for example, oxygen movement into the lungs can be controlled by adjusting the breathing rate, since the membrane itself is not selectively permeable to oxygen.

Substances moved across the membrane by transmembrane proteins, however, can be selectively transported — such as the ions that control the generation of electrical impulses in nerve cells. Such selective transport is essential for processes including the movement of minerals into plant roots and the absorption of nutrients in the animal intestine.

Colorimetry is a technique often used to investigate how conditions such as temperature affect membrane permeability. A colorimeter passes light of a set colour (wavelength) through a sample and records the light absorbed; absorbance is linearly related to solute concentration, while transmittance (the percentage of light that passes through) is related to it exponentially. In one classic investigation, the pigment betalain, contained within beetroot cell vacuoles, leaks out through the plasma membrane and tonoplast as heat or extreme pH damages the phospholipid bilayer, so measuring the absorbance of green light (betalain’s complementary colour) in the surrounding solution at different temperatures gives a measure of how much membrane damage has occurred.

The solution absorbs some of this light, and the remaining transmitted light reaches a light detector, with the result shown on a digital display as an absorbance reading.

Glycoproteins, glycolipids and the fluid mosaic model

The carbohydrate molecules of the membrane are relatively short-chain polysaccharides that occur only on the outer surface of the plasma membrane. Some are attached to proteins (forming glycoproteins) and some to lipids (forming glycolipids); together they are known as the glycocalyx. The glycocalyx functions in cell–cell recognition and in binding cells into tissues (cell adhesion); it is highly hydrophilic and attracts large amounts of water to the cell’s surface, helping the cell interact with its watery environment and obtain dissolved substances.

The plasma membrane is made almost entirely of protein and lipid, together with a small, variable amount of carbohydrate. This overall view of membrane structure is known as the fluid mosaic model: “fluid” because the lipid and protein components are constantly in motion, and “mosaic” because the proteins are scattered about within the bilayer in a patchwork pattern.

Integral proteins are embedded within the bilayer, including a channel protein with a central pore and a protein that traverses the membrane and is exposed at both surfaces; a peripheral protein is attached only to the surface of the bilayer. Proteins have several roles depending on their location and structure: transport, receptors, anchorage, cell recognition, intercellular joining and enzymatic activity. Carbohydrates on the outer surface are involved in cell recognition and cell adhesion.
Fluid mosaic modelThe accepted view of the structure of the plasma membrane, comprising a phospholipid bilayer with proteins embedded but free to move about.

Understanding of the plasma membrane’s composition and structure built up from evidence gathered over many decades, drawing on cytology, cell biochemistry and cell physiology. Early observations — that cell contents flow out when the cell surface is ruptured, that water-soluble compounds enter cells less readily than lipid-soluble ones, and that phospholipids spontaneously form a stable bilayer in water — supported the idea of a lipid membrane barrier. In the 1930s (revised in 1954), James Danielli and Hugh Davson proposed that membranes consist of a phospholipid bilayer evenly coated on both surfaces with a layer of globular protein, with pores present in places; early electron micrographs, which showed two dark lines with a lighter band in between, appeared to support this model.

Figure B2.1.15: TEM of the cell surface membrane of a red blood cellA transmission electron micrograph (magnification ×700,000) of a red blood cell membrane in cross-section shows two dark parallel lines with a lighter band between them — the appearance that was originally interpreted as supporting the Davson–Danielli model of a lipid bilayer sandwiched between two continuous protein layers.

Later evidence led Singer and Nicolson to propose the fluid mosaic model in 1972. Attempts to extract protein from plasma membranes showed that while some proteins occurred on the external surface and were easily extracted, others were buried within or across the lipid bilayer and were much harder to extract. Freeze-etching studies, in which a membrane is split open along its mid-line, confirmed that some proteins are indeed buried within or across the lipid bilayer.

Figure B2.1.16: Plasma membrane structure — evidence from freeze-etchingPart (a) shows a cell membrane in cross-section as a lipid bilayer with protein molecules and attached polysaccharides. Part (b) shows an electron micrograph of a freeze-etched cell membrane, split along the line shown in part (a), revealing the inner, fractured face of the bilayer studded with protein particles embedded within the membrane, rather than lying only on its surface — evidence against the Davson–Danielli model of continuous surface protein layers.

Further evidence came from “tagging” experiments, in which specific membrane components were labelled with marker chemicals (typically fluorescent dyes) and shown to be continually on the move within the membrane, confirming that the membrane is strong but fluid. It was also found that lipid bilayers contain the unusual lipid cholesterol, which disturbs the close packing of phospholipids and whose quantity varies with ambient temperature, and that carbohydrate molecules on the outer surface form complexes with membrane proteins (glycoproteins) and lipids (glycolipids).

Higher Level Fatty acid composition, cholesterol and membrane fluidity

Membranes contain a mixture of phospholipids. Some have saturated fatty acid tails (no double bonds between carbon atoms), and some have unsaturated fatty acid tails (with double bonds). Unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points because their kinked hydrocarbon tails do not pack together as closely as saturated tails do, so lipid bilayers rich in unsaturated fatty acids are more fluid and flexible at the temperatures a cell experiences. Saturated fatty acids have higher melting points and make membranes stronger (less fluid) at higher temperatures. If membrane temperature falls, the membrane becomes less fluid, and at some point may solidify; membranes must remain sufficiently fluid for many of their proteins to move about and function correctly.

Some organisms vary the balance of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids (and the amount of cholesterol) in their membranes as ambient temperature changes, in order to maintain a properly functioning membrane even in the cold. For example, in the lake sturgeon (Acipenser fulvescens), a North American freshwater fish that experiences long overwintering periods, a decrease in temperature from 16°C to 1°C causes both mono- and polyunsaturated fatty acids in membrane phospholipids to increase significantly while saturated fatty acids decrease, helping the fish survive in cold conditions.

Homeoviscous adaptationThe adaptation of cell membrane lipid composition to maintain adequate membrane fluidity. As temperature decreases, phospholipid fatty acid composition is expected to become more unsaturated to maintain homeoviscosity, with the reverse occurring in warmer conditions.

Cholesterol is an essential lipid component of all animal cell plasma membranes, in addition to phospholipids. It is a steroid, with a hydrophilic hydroxyl (−OH) group at one end and a hydrophobic hydrocarbon chain at the other end of its carbon ring structure.

Figure B2.1.17: Molecular structure of cholesterolCholesterol is drawn with a ring-shaped steroid core, a hydrophilic hydroxyl (−OH) group at one end (the polar, hydrophilic part of the molecule), and a non-polar hydrocarbon tail at the other end (the hydrophobic part of the molecule).
Figure B2.1.18: The interaction between cholesterol and the phospholipid bilayerA cholesterol molecule is shown positioned within the phospholipid bilayer: its polar hydroxyl group is attracted to, and sits alongside, the phosphate heads of the phospholipids near the outer surface, while its non-polar hydrocarbon tail and carbon rings sit alongside, and are attracted to, the hydrophobic fatty acid tails of the phospholipids in the interior of the membrane.

By disturbing the close packing of phospholipids, cholesterol increases membrane flexibility; it acts as a modulator of membrane fluidity, stabilizing membranes at higher temperatures while preventing stiffening at lower temperatures. In mammalian membranes, cholesterol overall reduces fluidity and reduces permeability of the membrane to some solutes. At low temperatures, cholesterol maintains fluidity by forcing phospholipids apart and maintaining distance between them, sustaining movement of membrane components; at higher temperatures, bonds between cholesterol and phospholipids maintain the structural integrity of the membrane, preventing it from becoming too fluid or disintegrating. The amount of cholesterol present in a membrane varies with the ambient temperatures a cell experiences.

Higher Level Membrane fluidity, vesicle formation and cytosis

Bulk transport across the plasma membrane occurs through the movement of vesicles of solid or liquid matter, in processes known generally as cytosis. Uptake of matter into a cell is called endocytosis, and export of matter out of a cell is called exocytosis. The strength and flexibility of the fluid mosaic membrane makes this activity possible, and energy from metabolism (ATP) is required.

Key termsEndocytosis: formation of vesicles as the plasma membrane pinches inward, taking material into the cell. Exocytosis: vesicles fuse with the membrane and material is exported out of the cell.
Figure B2.1.19: Transport by endocytosis and exocytosisA cell is shown with its nucleus and cytoplasm. On one side, the plasma membrane is shown pulling inward at the point where a vesicle forms, enclosing either solid matter (phagocytosis, “cell eating”) or liquid (pinocytosis) to bring it into the cell as endocytosis. On the other side, a vesicle from within the cytoplasm is shown fusing with the plasma membrane and releasing its contents outside the cell by exocytosis.

When solid matter is taken in by phagocytosis, part of the plasma membrane at the point where the vesicle forms is pulled inward while the surrounding membrane and cytoplasm bulge outward, enclosing the solid matter in a small vesicle. Vesicles are also used to transport materials within cells, for example between the rough endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus, and on to the plasma membrane. In the human body, phagocytic cells called macrophages engulf and dispose of the debris of damaged or dying cells by endocytosis — around 2 × 1011 red blood cells are broken down and disposed of by macrophages every 24 hours. White blood cells that produce antibodies secrete them by exocytosis, and neurons release neurotransmitter chemicals, such as acetylcholine, into the synaptic cleft by exocytosis.

Common mistakeDo not confuse membrane fluidity with membrane permeability — fluidity refers to how freely the components of the membrane can move and reorganize, while permeability refers to how readily particular substances can cross the membrane.

Higher Level Gated ion channels in neurons

A nerve impulse is transmitted along the axon of a nerve cell by a momentary reversal in electrical potential difference across the axon membrane, brought about by rapid movements of sodium and potassium ions through facilitated diffusion via pores called gated ion channels. These are channel proteins that span the membrane and have a central channel that can open and close; each channel is exclusively permeable to one type of ion.

Neurotransmitter-gated ion channels

Synapses are small gaps between neurons. Because an action potential cannot cross a synapse directly, the signal must be transduced from an electrical signal into a chemical signal — a neurotransmitter, such as acetylcholine (ACh) — that can diffuse across the gap. The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor protein, found in the central and peripheral nervous system, muscle and many other tissues, contains binding sites for acetylcholine and is ionotropic: when ACh binds, positively charged ions (mainly sodium) can flow through the transmembrane protein channel. Because these channels can be closed (“gated”) and are opened by the neurotransmitter ACh, they are known as neurotransmitter-gated ion channels.

Neurotransmitter-gated ion channelA channel protein that temporarily opens when a specific neurotransmitter binds to it.

Voltage-gated channels

Potassium and sodium channels in nerve cell membranes are voltage-gated channels: they open or close depending on whether a certain threshold membrane potential has been reached. When a stimulus is received, sodium channels open and sodium ions enter the axon, depolarizing its interior so that the membrane potential shifts from about −70 mV to about +40 mV, initiating the nerve impulse (action potential). Almost immediately afterwards, potassium channels open and sodium channels close, allowing potassium ions to exit the axon down their electrochemical gradient. As the interior of the axon becomes less positive again, the potassium channel is first closed mechanically by a “ball and chain” device — a flexible strand of amino acid residues (the “chain”) attached to a globular protein (the “ball”) — before the channel itself returns fully to its closed state once the axon interior is more positive outside than inside again.

Higher Level Sodium–potassium pumps as exchange transporters

Sodium–potassium pumps are globular proteins that span the axon membrane of nerve cells and are an example of exchange transporters. When preparing an axon for the next nerve impulse, this pump actively transports potassium (K+) ions into the axon and sodium (Na+) ions out of the axon, using energy transferred from ATP. Over time, this activity concentrates sodium and potassium ions on opposite sides of the membrane, playing an important role in generating membrane potentials. The pump works in a repeating cycle:

  • With the interior surface of the pump open to the inside of the axon, three sodium ions load onto specific binding sites.
  • The globular protein reacts with ATP, attaching a phosphate group to the pump; this triggers the pump to close to the interior and open to the exterior.
  • The three sodium ions are released outside the axon, and simultaneously two potassium ions load onto specific binding sites from outside.
  • With the potassium ions loaded, the phosphate group detaches, triggering the pump protein to reverse shape, reopening to the interior and releasing the potassium ions inside the axon.
  • The cycle then repeats.

Higher Level Sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters: indirect active transport

Sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters are a family of glucose transporters found in the epithelium of the small intestine and the proximal tubule of kidney nephrons, where they play an important role in glucose absorption. These cotransporter proteins move glucose into the cell while simultaneously moving two sodium ions in the same direction, for each glucose molecule transported.

Active transport of sodium at the base of the cell, using sodium–potassium pumps, removes sodium from the cell and so builds up a sodium concentration gradient, allowing sodium to re-enter through the cotransporter protein down its own concentration gradient at the microvillus surface. As sodium moves down its gradient through the cotransporter, glucose is drawn into the cell through the same protein, even though this moves glucose against its own concentration gradient. Because glucose is not directly powered by ATP but is instead moved in conjunction with the active transport of sodium ions elsewhere in the cell, this overall process is known as indirect active transport. Once inside the cell, glucose then leaves via facilitated diffusion at the base of the cell, through a separate glucose transporter protein, on its way into the blood.

At the microvillus surface, a sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter protein has a glucose-binding site and Na+ binding sites; two Na+ ions and one glucose molecule enter the cell together through this protein, with sodium moving down its concentration gradient and glucose moving against its own gradient (indirect active transport). Higher Level Adhesion of cells to form tissues

Cells in animal tissues are held together by cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs) — proteins embedded in the surface membranes of cells — which is essential for maintaining tissue structure and function. Binding between CAMs causes cells to adhere to one another, forming a structure called a cell junction. Some CAMs bind cells to one another or to other CAMs; other types bind cells to the extracellular matrix (ECM), the non-cellular scaffolding present within all tissues and organs (for example, collagen fibres and bone mineral make up the ECM of bone tissue).

Key termsCell-adhesion molecule (CAM): a protein located on the cell surface involved in binding cells with other cells or the extracellular matrix. Extracellular matrix (ECM): a large network of proteins and other molecules that surround, support and give structure to cells and tissues.
Figure B2.1.24: Types of CAMs and cell junctions in epithelial cellsA layer of epithelial cells is shown, with microvilli on their upper surface and an extracellular matrix beneath them. Between adjacent cells, several types of cell–cell junction are shown, including tight junctions, anchoring junctions and gap junctions. Cell junctions can be divided into two overall types: cell–cell junctions (usually involving cadherin proteins) and cell–extracellular matrix junctions (usually involving integrin proteins). Both cadherins and integrins are transmembrane proteins. Junctions can also be classified by function: anchoring junctions strengthen contact between cells; tight junctions seal gaps between cells and act as an impermeable or semipermeable barrier controlling the movement of membrane transport proteins between the apical and basal layers of epithelia; gap junctions link the cytoplasm of adjacent cells, allowing transport of molecules between them; and signal-relaying junctions include synapses.

Flowering plants contain relatively few CAMs; instead, plant cells are held together rigidly by extensive interlocking of the cell walls of adjacent cells. The cytoplasm of adjacent cells is often connected by functionally similar but structurally different “bridges”: gap junctions in animals and plasmodesmata in plants. These structures allow cells to exchange small molecules, including nutrients and chemical signals, coordinating the function of cells within a tissue.

Organelles as discrete, specialized subunits

Organelles are discrete (separate) subunits within cells, including nuclei, chloroplasts, mitochondria, vesicles, ribosomes and the plasma membrane. The only organelles common to both eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells are the cell membrane and ribosomes. The cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm are not considered organelles.

Top tipMost organelles are surrounded by membrane and are described as “membrane-bound”, including the rough endoplasmic reticulum, smooth endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and the double membrane surrounding the nucleus. Eukaryotic cells contain membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotic cells do not.

New experimental techniques often bring breakthroughs in scientific knowledge; the study of individual organelle function became possible only after the invention of the ultracentrifuge and the development of cell fractionation methods for using it. Centrifugation serves two basic purposes in cell biology: as a preparative technique to separate one type of material from others, and as an analytical technique to measure physical properties (such as molar mass, density, shape and binding ability) of macromolecules.

How cell fractionation worksA centrifuge spins fluids at very high speed, subjecting suspended particles to centrifugal forces as great as 1,000,000 times the force of gravity, which can sediment particles with molar masses as small as 10,000 g/mol; modern ultracentrifuges reach speeds of 150,000 revolutions per minute or higher. More-dense particles settle at the bottom of the tube while less-dense particles remain higher up. Cell fractionation of animal cell homogenates into their various organelles was first achieved in the 1940s: at low speeds, nuclei fall to the bottom of the tube; at a slightly higher speed, mitochondria can be collected; and at even higher speeds and longer run times, ribosomes can be collected. These techniques let biologists study biological processes, free from the complex side reactions of a living cell, using purified cell-free systems — for example, the mechanism of protein synthesis (translation) was discovered using a cell homogenate able to translate mRNA into protein, and ultracentrifugation was also central to the Meselson–Stahl experiment on DNA replication.
Common mistakeCentrifugation does not separate organelles based on their size, but on their relative density.

Theodor Svedberg, the Swedish chemist who designed the first ultracentrifuge in 1925, studied haemoglobin and found that a centrifuged sample revealed a single, sharp band with a molar mass of 68,000 g/mol, strongly supporting the theory that proteins are true macromolecules rather than mixtures. Svedberg also gave his name to the non-SI Svedberg unit (S), which measures the time taken for a particle of a given size to settle at the bottom of a solution, measured at exactly 10−13 seconds; a particle’s mass, density and shape all affect its S value. This is how ribosomes are differentiated by their sedimentation rate: the 70S ribosome found in chloroplasts, mitochondria and prokaryotes has a sedimentation rate of 70 × 10−13 seconds.

Advantages of separating the nucleus and cytoplasm

The nuclear membrane keeps DNA separate from the rest of the cell, protecting it from the many chemical reactions occurring in the cytoplasm, and it also allows gene transcription and translation to be kept separate in space and time. Many eukaryotic genes contain non-coding sequences: the sections of a gene that carry meaningful, protein-coding information are called exons, while the non-coding sequences that interrupt them are called introns. While genes split in this way are common in higher plants and animals, some eukaryotic genes contain no introns at all.

When a gene containing exons and introns is transcribed, the resulting mRNA at first contains the sequence of introns and exons exactly as they occur in the DNA. If this unmodified mRNA were translated directly, it would cause serious problems during protein synthesis. Instead, an enzyme-catalysed reaction called post-transcriptional modification removes the introns as soon as the mRNA is formed — a process called RNA splicing — leaving a shortened, “mature” mRNA that passes out of the nucleus into the cytoplasm to be translated at the ribosomes. This is an advantage for eukaryotic cells because variants of a protein can be produced from a single gene through different patterns of splicing. In prokaryotes, there is no nuclear membrane separating DNA from the cytoplasm, so mRNA may meet ribosomes immediately; prokaryotic genes therefore do not have introns, and prokaryotic cells lack the enzyme machinery needed for splicing.

Like other cell membranes, the nuclear membrane is a phospholipid bilayer, permeable only to small, non-polar molecules; other molecules cannot diffuse through it directly. Lamins, intermediate protein filaments that are a major component of the cytoskeleton, are important in nuclear membrane function.

The nuclear pores are tiny, about 100 nm in diameter, but so numerous that they make up about one-third of the nuclear membrane’s surface area — suggesting just how important communication between the nucleus and cytoplasm is. Unlike the mechanisms that transport proteins into most other organelles (where proteins must unfold to cross the membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts), nuclear pores can transport proteins in their fully folded conformation, and can transport ribosomal components as already-assembled particles. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is assembled with ribosomal proteins in the nucleolus, and the resulting ribosomal subunits are then transported into the cytoplasm; export of RNA through nuclear pore complexes is an active process that requires energy, since proteins made in the cytoplasm are essential for gene expression to proceed efficiently.

Advantages of compartmentalization in the cytoplasm

Each organelle performs a different function, carrying out a specific biological process and a distinct set of chemical reactions; at the most basic level, biochemical reactions within organelles are what sustain life. Organelles separate the biochemical reactions needed to support the cell’s function, so that each fulfils a different role while all cooperate together. Compartmentalization — the division of the cell interior into separate, discrete areas — allows the correct concentration of metabolites and appropriate enzymes to be maintained for specific metabolic processes, so that otherwise incompatible biochemical processes can be kept separate.

Top tipCompartmentalization means the reactions of life can be controlled without interfering with each other — similar to the way different operations in a factory are carried out in different areas (for example, engine assembly in one area, vehicle-frame assembly in another).

Lysosomes are tiny spherical vesicles bound by a single membrane, containing digestive (hydrolytic) enzymes that are only active in the lysosome’s acidic interior. Because the rest of the cell is neutral to slightly alkaline, this pH-dependence protects the cell from self-digestion if a lysosome should leak or rupture — a clear example of how compartmentalization by lysosomes allows a function (controlled digestion) that would not otherwise be possible or safe within the wider cell interior.

Phagocytosis is a process used by specific cells, such as phagocytic white blood cells, to capture and ingest foreign particles such as bacteria, then digest and destroy them. Phagocytosis proceeds through recognition of the foreign particle by receptors and its ingestion into a phagocytic vacuole; fusion of the phagocytic vacuole with a lysosome to create a phagolysosome; and finally destruction of the ingested particle. The temporary phagocytic vacuole, followed by the phagolysosome, provides a contained space within the cell where antimicrobial activity can occur at low pH without disrupting the cell’s other metabolic processes.

Higher Level Adaptations of the mitochondrion for aerobic respiration

Respiration is a key life process occurring in all cells of all organisms: the breakdown of glucose to release the energy in its bonds, converting this energy to ATP while releasing carbon dioxide and water. Respiration begins in the cytoplasm in an anaerobic process called glycolysis, producing pyruvate, which diffuses into the mitochondria where aerobic respiration takes place.

Mitochondria are formed from two membranes, an inner and an outer membrane. The outer membrane contains transport proteins that move pyruvate into the mitochondrion, where carbon is removed from pyruvate as carbon dioxide (decarboxylation) and hydrogen is also removed (oxidation), splitting into protons (H+) and electrons. The electrons pass down a sequence of proteins in the inner membrane, the electron transport chain (ETC), releasing energy that is used to pump protons into the intermembrane space between the two mitochondrial membranes, where they accumulate quickly and create an electrochemical gradient. As well as the ETC, the inner membrane contains ATP synthase, a transmembrane multi-protein assembly through which accumulated protons pass, generating ATP. The inner membrane is highly folded into structures called cristae, which increase the surface area available for the ETC and ATP synthase, increasing ATP production.

The Krebs cycle — the sequence of enzyme-controlled stages in which hydrogen and carbon atoms are removed from derivatives of glucose — occurs in the matrix of the mitochondria, which contains the appropriate enzymes and pH for this to happen. In this way, mitochondria achieve compartmentalization of the enzymes and substrates of the Krebs cycle within the matrix, and of the ETC and ATP synthase within the highly folded inner membrane.

Higher Level Adaptations of the chloroplast for photosynthesis

Just as mitochondria are the site of the Krebs cycle and respiratory ATP formation, chloroplasts are the organelles where the reactions of photosynthesis occur. Chloroplasts, found in green plants, contain photosynthetic pigments along with the enzymes and electron-transport proteins needed for reducing carbon dioxide to glucose and for ATP formation, using light energy.

A chloroplast has a double membrane around it, plus a third inner membrane that folds extensively to form a system of branching membranes called thylakoids. Thylakoid membranes are organized into flat, compact, circular piles called grana (singular, granum), almost like stacks of coins; between the grana are loosely arranged tubular membranes, called lamellae, suspended in a watery stroma.

Chlorophyll, the photosynthetic pigment that absorbs light energy, exists in the grana, where chlorophyll molecules are grouped into structures called photosystems, held in the thylakoid membranes by proteins. The large surface area of the thylakoid membranes optimizes the chloroplast’s ability to harvest light energy: light excites electrons, which pass down an electron transport chain, pumping protons into the thylakoids, where they build up quickly because of the small volume of fluid inside. Protons then flow down their electrochemical gradient through ATP synthase proteins, from inside the thylakoids into the stroma, generating ATP.

This ATP is used in a biochemical process occurring in the stroma, the Calvin cycle, which involves reduction reactions and ultimately produces glucose; the stroma contains the appropriate enzymes and optimum pH for the Calvin cycle. Compartmentalization within chloroplasts therefore separates the light-harvesting activity in the thylakoids (the light-dependent reactions) from the enzymes and substrates of the Calvin cycle in the stroma (the light-independent reactions).

Higher Level Functional benefits of the nucleus double membrane

The nucleus is the largest organelle in a eukaryotic cell, typically 10–20 micrometres in diameter, and is surrounded by two membranes known together as the nuclear envelope. The outer membrane is continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), which makes it easy for the nucleus to obtain proteins made in the ER without needing energy-requiring endocytosis. The double membrane of the nuclear envelope is really one membrane folded to form a double structure, continuous across the inner and outer surfaces of the envelope, which allows the nuclear envelope to connect directly with the ER.

Figure B2.2.6: The nuclear membrane and its connection to the ERThe nuclear envelope is shown as two membranes (an outer surface and an inner surface) enclosing chromatin and a nucleolus within the nucleus, with nuclear pores passing through both membranes. The outer membrane of the nuclear envelope is shown continuous with the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum, which encloses the ER lumen and carries ribosomes on its outer (cytoplasmic) surface.

The nuclear pores are very small, about 100 nm in diameter, but so numerous that they make up about one-third of the nuclear membrane’s surface area, enabling the rapid movement of molecules such as mRNA out of the nucleus and proteins, ATP and some hormones into the nucleus. The inner membrane contains the DNA and nucleolus, and is lined by the nuclear lamina, a meshwork of protein filaments (lamins) that extends into the nucleus and provides structural support; the lamina is present in animal cells but not in plant or fungal cells. The double membrane therefore allows compartmentalization of the nucleus’s functions.

Common mistakeStudents sometimes draw the nuclear membrane as a single line — this is incorrect. The double membrane needs to be drawn clearly, with nuclear pores shown passing through it.

During both mitosis and meiosis, the nuclear envelope dissociates into small vesicles early in cell division. This allows the chromosomes to come into contact with the cytoplasm and the spindle apparatus, which is central to manipulating chromosomes during division.

Higher Level Free ribosomes and the rough endoplasmic reticulum

Many ribosomes occur freely in the cytoplasm, and are the sites of synthesis for proteins that are retained in the cell to fulfil roles there. It is common for several ribosomes to move along the same messenger RNA molecule at once; this structure of mRNA, ribosomes and their growing protein chains is called a polysome, allowing many copies of a particular polypeptide to be produced simultaneously.

Other ribosomes are bound to the membranes of the endoplasmic reticulum, forming the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER). These bound ribosomes synthesize proteins that enter the lumen of the RER for vesicular transport within the cell, for subsequent secretion from the cell, or for packaging into lysosomes.

Top tipFree ribosomes produce proteins for use within the cell, while ribosomes attached to the RER produce proteins destined for export from the cell, or for movement to the Golgi apparatus for further modification.

Higher Level Structure and function of the Golgi apparatus

The Golgi apparatus is involved in processing and secreting proteins. It consists of a stack-like collection of flattened membranous sacs called cisternae, which vary in number, shape and organization between different cell types. On one side of the stack, vesicles arriving from the RER fuse with the Golgi membranes; on the opposite side of the stack, new vesicles form from swellings at the margins that pinch off, carrying proteins for transport within the cell or for secretion.

Proteins enter the Golgi apparatus on the side facing the RER and exit on the side facing the plasma membrane, being transferred from one cisterna to the next along the way. Each cisterna contains different enzymes that catalyse different types of protein modification, including the addition or removal of sugars (glycosylation), the addition of sulfate groups (sulfation) and the addition of phosphate groups (phosphorylation). Some of these Golgi-mediated modifications act as signals that direct proteins to their final destination within the cell, including the lysosome and the plasma membrane.

Higher Level Structure and function of cell vesicles

Membranes must be able to separate and fuse together so that cells and organelles can bring in, transport and release molecules. Many cells take up molecules through receptor-mediated endocytosis: a protein (or protein complex) first binds to a receptor on the cell surface, after which a specialized protein causes the membrane at that point to invaginate (tuck inward). One such specialized protein is clathrin, which polymerizes into a lattice network around the growing membrane bud to form a clathrin-coated pit; the invaginated membrane eventually breaks off and fuses to form a vesicle, budding from either the Golgi body or the plasma membrane.

Some hormones, transport proteins and antibodies use receptor-mediated endocytosis to enter a cell; this pathway is also exploited by viruses and toxins to gain entry into cells. The reverse process — the fusion of a vesicle with a membrane — is a critical step in the release of neurotransmitters from a neuron into the synaptic cleft.
