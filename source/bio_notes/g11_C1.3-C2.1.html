The Transformation of Light Energy to Chemical Energy in Photosynthesis

Green plants and other photosynthetic organisms use the energy of sunlight to produce glucose from the inorganic raw materials carbon dioxide and water, by a process called photosynthesis. The waste product is oxygen. Photosynthesis occurs in plant cells that contain the organelle called the chloroplast, including many of the cells of the leaves of green plants. Here, the energy of light is trapped by the green pigment chlorophyll and becomes chemical energy in molecules such as glucose and ATP. This supplies most of the chemical energy needed for life processes in ecosystems.

Key terms Photosynthesis: the production of glucose from carbon dioxide and water, in the presence of chlorophyll and enzymes, using light energy, producing oxygen as a waste product.
Chlorophyll: the main photosynthetic pigment of green plants, occurring in the grana (thylakoid) membranes of the chloroplasts; also found in cyanobacteria and in the chloroplasts of algae.

Glucose formed in photosynthesis may temporarily be stored as starch, but sooner or later much of it is used in metabolism. Plants synthesize other carbohydrates, together with lipids, proteins, growth factors and all other metabolites they require. For this, they additionally need certain mineral ions, absorbed from the soil solution.

The wider significance of photosynthesis

The importance of photosynthesis to the green plant is that it provides the energy-rich sugar molecules from which the plant builds its other organic molecules. However, the advent of oxygen generation by photolysis (the splitting of water molecules using light energy) had immense consequences for living organisms and geological processes on Earth:

1. Photosynthesis maintains the composition of the atmosphere. The quantity of carbon dioxide removed by plants and other photosynthetic organisms each day is almost equal to that added to the air from respiration and from the burning of fossil fuels (the carbon cycle). Photosynthesis is also the only natural process that releases oxygen into the atmosphere. All the oxygen present in the air (about 21%) is a waste product of photosynthesis, and this is the source of oxygen for aerobic respiration.

2. Some of the oxygen originating from green plants is converted into ozone in the upper atmosphere by the action of ultraviolet (UV) light from the Sun. Ozone occurs naturally as a layer in the stratosphere, protecting terrestrial life by significantly reducing the quantity of UV reaching the Earth’s surface. UV light is very harmful to living things because it is absorbed by the organic bases of nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), causing them to be modified. The conversion of oxygen to ozone and the maintenance of the high-level ozone layer have therefore been important in the evolution of terrestrial life (UV light does not penetrate water, so it cannot reach aquatic organisms).

Common mistake Do not forget to say that light is the original source of energy for photosynthesis, not simply “the Sun”. Also, it is incorrect to say that only plants photosynthesize and only animals respire — plant cells contain mitochondria and are continually respiring, even while photosynthesizing.

What is light?

Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation produced by the Sun. Visible light makes up only part of the total electromagnetic radiation reaching the Earth. When visible “white” light is dispersed through a prism, a continuous spectrum of light is seen — a rainbow of colours from red to violet. Different colours have different wavelengths and different energies.

Not all colours of the spectrum present in white light are absorbed equally by chlorophyll and other pigments used in photosynthesis (accessory pigments). Some are transmitted or reflected rather than absorbed. Biological pigments are coloured compounds produced by metabolism (enzyme-controlled reactions inside cells). Chlorophyll is a biological pigment responsible for the green colour of photosynthetic organisms; the colour seen is the light that is not absorbed but is instead reflected. Examples of naturally occurring coloured photosynthetic compounds include the anthocyanins, carotenoids (e.g. carotene) and the porphyrins (e.g. chlorophyll).

Key terms Pigment: a coloured compound produced by metabolism.
Accessory pigments: light-absorbing compounds that trap light energy and channel it to chlorophyll a, the primary pigment, which initiates the reactions of photosynthesis.
Photolysis: the splitting of water molecules using light energy.

From Carbon Dioxide to Glucose, and Oxygen as a By-product

Photosynthesis is a set of many reactions occurring in chloroplasts in the light, conveniently divided into two main steps.

Step 1: Light energy is used to split water (photolysis). This releases the waste product of photosynthesis, oxygen, and allows the hydrogen atoms to be retained on hydrogen-acceptor molecules, one requirement of Step 2. At the same time, ATP is generated from ADP and phosphate, also using energy from light.

Step 2: Glucose is built up from carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide is “fixed” to make organic molecules. Both the energy of ATP and hydrogen atoms from the reduced hydrogen-acceptor molecules are required. The hydrogen acceptors carry the hydrogens to a series of reactions that result in the formation of glucose.

Figure C1.3.2: Two steps of photosynthesis A flow diagram showing light energy splitting water (2H2O → O2 + 4H) to release oxygen and hydrogen; the hydrogen is carried by a hydrogen acceptor (converted from its oxidized form to its reduced, H-carrying form), while ATP is generated from ADP and Pi using light energy. Carbon dioxide then combines with the hydrogen (carried by the reduced hydrogen acceptor) and uses the ATP to build up glucose in the chloroplast, some of which may be temporarily stored as starch.

Oxygen as a by-product in plants, algae and cyanobacteria

The oxygen produced by photosynthesis comes from the splitting of water. The early Earth had no free oxygen, so the evolution of organisms that could release oxygen was critical in the development of life on Earth. It is not only plants that photosynthesize: algae and cyanobacteria also contain photosynthetic pigments and produce glucose and oxygen.

Cyanobacteria were the first organisms on Earth to produce oxygen, around 2.8 billion years ago. Cyanobacteria have accessory pigments called phycobilins and transfer energy from these to chlorophyll a. Around 1.5 billion years ago, red and then green algae evolved. Red algae have the same pigments as cyanobacteria (chlorophyll a and phycobilins) and derive most of their colouring from a red phycobilin called phycoerythrin. Green algae have chloroplasts containing chlorophyll a and b, giving a bright green colour, plus the accessory pigments β-carotene (red-orange) and xanthophylls (yellow); unlike cyanobacteria and red algae, green algae do not contain phycobilin pigments.

Separation and Identification of Photosynthetic Pigments by Chromatography

Chromatography is used to separate, identify and quantify the component pigments (or dyes) in a mixture, and is ideal for separating biologically active molecules since only very small amounts may be available. Plant chlorophyll consists of a mixture of pigments; some plant pigments are soluble in water, but chlorophyll is not. Chlorophyll can be extracted in an organic solvent such as propanone (acetone), and the photosynthetic pigments in plant leaves can then be identified using chromatography.

Key terms Chromatography: a technique used to separate the components of a mixture; it involves letting soluble substances spread across filter paper (or through a powder).
Chromatogram: the pattern formed on an adsorbent medium showing the result of separating the components of a mixture by chromatography.
Figure C1.3.4: Steps in the extraction of plant pigments A six-step procedure: (1) fresh leaves are dipped into boiling water to kill cells and make cell membranes fully permeable; (2) leaves are finely cut and ground in a mortar with washed, dried sand as an abrasive; (3) about 20 cm³ of propanone (acetone) is added as solvent and the mixture ground again to produce a concentrated chlorophyll extract; (4) the solution is decanted into two centrifuge tubes, balanced for mass; (5) the tubes are centrifuged to remove suspended organic matter such as wall fragments and starch grains; (6) the extracted pigment solution is combined and stored cold and in the dark, since chlorophylls are unstable once removed from the chloroplast and bleach in direct sunlight. Suitable green plants for chlorophyll extraction include spinach, bougainvillea, hibiscus, green grass, and (in Asia) the leaf vegetables kai lan or kang kong.

Paper and thin-layer chromatography

Chromatograms are typically run on adsorbent paper (paper chromatography), powdered solid (column chromatography), or a thin film of dried solid (thin-layer chromatography, TLC).

Figure C1.3.5: Preparing and running a chromatogram Shows paper chromatography: a drop of concentrated pigment solution is loaded onto the origin of a strip of chromatography paper and dried, repeating several times to concentrate the spot; the paper is suspended (via glass rods and a bung) with its lower edge in solvent inside a sealed chromatography tank saturated with solvent vapour; as the solvent rises up the paper by capillary action it carries the pigments with it at different rates, separating them into bands (from the top: carotene, xanthophyll, chlorophyll a — blue-green, chlorophyll b — green); the point the solvent reaches when the run is stopped is the solvent front. It also shows thin-layer chromatography, a much quicker method using a plastic TLC plate pre-coated with silica gel, run inside a similar flat-bottomed chromatography tank with a close-fitting bung and a shallow layer of solvent; the method is otherwise similar to paper chromatography. The figure also shows how the Rf value is calculated from the distance moved by the solvent and the distance moved by the substance, measured from the origin.
Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent
Figure C1.3.6: The principles of paper chromatography Shows a mixture of two pigments loaded at the same starting point on a strip of paper, with solvent moving up the paper. A slow-moving pigment is more attracted to (has greater affinity for) the paper (the stationary phase) and moves only a short distance, while a fast-moving pigment is more attracted to the solvent (the mobile phase) and travels further up the paper in the same time.

In paper chromatography, the stationary phase is a liquid adsorbed onto the surface of the paper: the paper’s pores adsorb and form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, and this water can be displaced by other liquids to give different stationary phases. Pigments more soluble in the solvent than in the water of the stationary phase move rapidly up the paper; those more soluble in the water are not carried as far. TLC uses a stationary phase of silica or alumina particles bonded to a thin layer of glass or plastic, separating pigments by how strongly they adsorb to the stationary phase versus dissolve in the mobile phase — an equilibrium known as partitioning. The greater a pigment’s affinity for the stationary phase, the more slowly it moves.

Rf value The distance a particular substance moves up the chromatogram relative to its solvent front is constant for a given solute in a given solvent — this is the Rf value (retardation factor). It is calculated as the distance travelled by the compound divided by the distance travelled by the solvent front, both measured from the origin. Rf values can identify components of a mixture by comparison with tables of known values, but they depend on the compound, solvent, temperature and other factors such as the nature of the TLC plate.

The Rf value is calculated by dividing the distance moved by the pigment (3.54 cm) by the distance moved by the solvent (7.9 cm).

Absorption and Action Spectra

Light consists of a roughly equal mixture of all the visible wavelengths — violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange and red (colour names and their boundaries are arbitrary, and colours merge into each other). An absorption spectrum shows how much of each wavelength a pigment absorbs.

Key terms Absorption spectrum: a graph showing the relative absorbance of different wavelengths of light by a pigment.
Action spectrum: the range of wavelengths of light within which a process like photosynthesis takes place.
Figure C1.3.8: The absorbance spectrum of chlorophyll A graph of absorbance (y-axis, 0.0–0.5+) against wavelength in nm (x-axis, about 400–700 nm, labelled with the colours violet, blue, green, yellow, orange, red). The curve shows two large absorbance peaks, one in the violet/blue region and one in the red region, with a deep trough (low absorbance) in the green region — showing that chlorophyll absorbs blue and red light most strongly and green light least.

Within a pigment molecule, absorption of light excites electrons. Accessory pigments transfer excited electrons to chlorophyll a, leading to a series of reactions that ultimately produce glucose; in this way light energy is transformed into chemical energy.

Figure C1.3.9: Absorption spectra for substances A and B Two graphs of absorbance against wavelength (300–800 nm) for two unidentified coloured substances, A and B, each showing a distinct absorbance peak at a different part of the visible spectrum; the wavelength(s) at which each substance absorbs strongly can be used to deduce which colour of light each substance absorbs, and therefore its likely apparent colour (the colour it reflects/transmits).

Similarities and differences between absorption and action spectra

The action spectrum of chlorophyll is discovered by projecting light of different wavelengths, in turn, for a unit of time, onto aquatic green pondweed, in apparatus that allows the rate of photosynthesis to be measured (the gas evolved is largely oxygen, and the volume produced per unit time is a measure of the rate of photosynthesis).

Figure C1.3.10: Absorption and action spectra of chlorophyll pigments Two aligned graphs sharing the same wavelength axis (400–700 nm, violet to red). The upper graph (b) is the action spectrum — the amount of photosynthesis occurring at each wavelength — showing peaks in the blue and red regions. The lower graph (a) is the absorption spectrum, measured with a spectrometer, showing separate curves for chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and the carotenoids, each absorbing most strongly in the blue and red regions. The close match between the two graphs shows that the wavelengths of light absorbed by photosynthetic pigments (largely red and blue) are very similar to the wavelengths that drive photosynthesis.
Top tip Unless colours are shown directly on an absorption spectrum, both wavelengths and colours of light should be included on the horizontal axis of absorption spectra graphs, so that each pigment can easily be related to the colour of light it absorbs.

Investigating the Effects of Limiting Factors on the Rate of Photosynthesis

A limiting factor for photosynthesis is an environmental factor that, when in short supply, affects the rate of photosynthesis. Hypotheses can be suggested for the effects of carbon dioxide concentration, light intensity and temperature, and then tested by experimentation. You should be able to identify the dependent and independent variables in such experiments.

Measuring the rate of photosynthesis

An illuminated, freshly cut shoot of pondweed, when inverted, produces a vigorous stream of gas bubbles from the base, showing active photosynthesis, while dissolved carbon dioxide is removed from the water. Suitable plants include Elodea, Myriophyllum and Cabomba. The rate of photosynthesis can be estimated using: a microburette to measure the volume of oxygen given off in the light; an oxygen sensor probe connected to a data logger; or a pH meter connected to a data logger (uptake of carbon dioxide from the water causes the pH to rise).

A small gas bubble produced by the plant collects and is drawn into a capillary tube connected to a microburette via a syringe and a heat trap; the length of the gas column collected over a measured time (e.g. 30 minutes, timed with a stop clock) is converted to a volume, giving the rate of oxygen output.

The effect of temperature

Figure C1.3.14: The effect of temperature on photosynthesis A sketch graph of rate of photosynthesis (arbitrary units, y-axis) against temperature in °C (x-axis), with an entirely different shape from the CO2 and light intensity graphs: at relatively low temperatures, the rate increases more and more steeply as temperature rises; at an optimum temperature the rate is highest; above the optimum, the rate falls off steeply as temperature rises further.

This shape is explained by enzyme collision theory. As temperature increases, the enzymes involved in photosynthesis (and their substrates) have increased kinetic energy, causing more collisions between enzymes and substrates, more enzyme–substrate complexes, and an increased rate of reaction — up to an optimum temperature. Above the optimum, the enzymes involved in fixing carbon dioxide (rubisco, at HL) cannot work effectively and the rate falls sharply.

Common mistakes Refer to the “concentration” of carbon dioxide, not its “amount”, “level” or “quantity”. Also, it is incorrect to say that the rate of photosynthesis falls at higher temperatures because of enzyme denaturation — the rate reduction occurs at much lower temperatures than those at which denaturation would occur. The problem at higher temperatures is an enzyme (rubisco) failing to fix carbon dioxide effectively, not the enzyme being destroyed.

Carbon Dioxide Enrichment Experiments

Carbon dioxide enrichment experiments are a means of predicting future rates of photosynthesis and plant growth. They include enclosed greenhouse experiments and free-air carbon dioxide enrichment (FACE) experiments.

Enclosed greenhouse experiments manipulate variables that affect photosynthesis (such as sunlight, wavelength of light and temperature) using greenhouses or polytunnels to control other variables. The plants used need to be small enough to fit inside, so data gathered do not directly relate to changes that would be seen in natural ecosystems.

FACE experiments pump carbon dioxide into the air to raise its local concentration in forests, grasslands and agricultural fields. Compared with enclosed greenhouse experiments, FACE experiments occur in natural ecosystems and can investigate the effects of carbon dioxide enrichment on large producers such as trees; however, unlike greenhouses, other variables (such as rainfall and sunlight) cannot be controlled and must instead be monitored. Duke University’s FACE project in North Carolina, USA, begun in 1996, is the oldest forest FACE project; it was used to study the response of a temperate coniferous forest to atmospheric carbon dioxide levels of at least 550 parts per million (over 1.25 times higher than levels at the time), monitoring tree growth and productivity.

Figure C1.3.15: A Duke University FACE plot A photograph of one of the circular FACE experimental plots set up by Duke University in a forest, showing the ring of tall vertical pipes used to release carbon dioxide around a group of trees to provide elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration under otherwise natural field conditions.

With global atmospheric carbon dioxide levels continuing to rise, predicting the effects this will have on biological systems is urgent.

Higher Level The Light-Dependent and Light-Independent Reactions: Introduction

Photosynthesis is a complex set of reactions in illuminated chloroplasts, established by biochemical investigation to fall naturally into two interconnected stages.

Light-dependent vs light-independent reactions Light-dependent reactions: use light energy to split water (photolysis). Hydrogen is removed and retained by the photosynthesis-specific hydrogen acceptor NADP+ (very similar to the respiration coenzyme NAD+, but carrying an additional phosphate group). ATP is generated from ADP and phosphate, also using light energy — a process called photophosphorylation. Oxygen is given off as a waste product. This stage occurs in the grana of the chloroplast.

Light-independent reactions: synthesize glucose using carbon dioxide. The products of the light-dependent reactions (ATP and reduced NADPH + H+) are used in glucose production. This stage occurs in the stroma of the chloroplast; it requires a continuous supply of the products of the light-dependent reactions but does not directly involve light energy (hence its name), even though photosynthesis as a whole is powered by light.
Figure C1.3.16: The two sets of reactions of photosynthesis, inputs and outputs A diagram divided into the grana (top, light-dependent reactions) and stroma (bottom, light-independent reactions). In the grana, light energy enters and splits water (H2O) into oxygen (½O2, released) and hydrogen; NADP+ and ADP + Pi enter and are converted to NADPH + H+ and ATP, which pass down to the stroma. In the stroma, carbon dioxide (CO2) enters and, using the NADPH + H+ and ATP from the light-dependent reactions, is reduced to carbohydrate (CH2O); NADP+ and ADP + Pi are regenerated and recycled back up to the grana.
Key terms Light-dependent reactions: part of photosynthesis occurring in the grana of chloroplasts and requiring light; water is split and ATP and NADPH are generated.
Photophosphorylation: the formation of ATP using light energy in the light-dependent step of photosynthesis.
Light-independent reactions: part of photosynthesis occurring in the stroma of chloroplasts; uses the products of the light-dependent step to reduce (fix) carbon dioxide to glucose.

Higher Level Photosystems and the Generation of Excited Electrons

Photosystems are arrays of pigment molecules (with associated proteins) that can generate and emit excited electrons. Photosystems are always located in membranes and occur in cyanobacteria and in the chloroplasts of photosynthetic eukaryotes. Chlorophyll molecules do not occur haphazardly in the grana; they are grouped together in photosystems held in the thylakoid membranes.

Figure C1.3.17: Generalized structure of photosystems A diagram of the thylakoid membrane showing many pigment molecules (chlorophyll and accessory pigments) arranged around a reaction centre of chlorophyll a. Light is absorbed by the surrounding pigment molecules and the energy is funnelled inward to the reaction centre, which absorbs energy at either 700 nm (photosystem I) or 680 nm (photosystem II). Excited electrons are released from the reaction centre and are replaced by low-energy (ground-state) electrons.

In each photosystem, several hundred chlorophyll molecules, plus accessory pigments (carotene and xanthophylls) and proteins (light-harvesting complexes, LHCs) are arranged. All these pigment molecules harvest light energy of slightly different wavelengths and funnel the energy to a single chlorophyll molecule, the reaction centre, which is then photoactivated. There are two types of photosystem in the thylakoid membranes, identified by the wavelength absorbed by the reaction centre chlorophyll: Photosystem I has a reaction centre activated by light of 700 nm (referred to as P700); Photosystem II has a reaction centre activated by light of 680 nm (referred to as P680).

Key terms Light-harvesting complex (LHC): an array of protein and chlorophyll molecules embedded in the thylakoid membrane, which transfer light energy to one chlorophyll a molecule at the reaction centre of a photosystem.
Photosystem I: a chlorophyll–protein complex that uses light energy to release excited electrons, replacing each lost electron with one in the ground state (received from photosystem II).
Photosystem II: a membrane super-complex of proteins and several hundred chlorophyll molecules, plus accessory pigments, that carries out the initial reaction of photosynthesis; light excites electrons, which pass down an electron transport chain and are replaced by splitting water to release protons and electrons.
Ground-state electrons: the energy level normally occupied by an electron; the state of lowest energy.
High-energy (excited) electrons: electrons whose energy level has been raised by chlorophyll absorbing light energy (photons).

When light energy reaches a reaction centre, ground-state electrons are raised to an excited state, releasing high-energy electrons that bring about the biochemical changes of the light-dependent reactions. The spaces vacated in the reaction centres are continuously refilled by ground-state electrons.

Advantages of the structured array of pigments

The structured array of different pigment and accessory pigment types enables light energy to excite electrons in a controlled way and direct them along electron transport chains. The variety of pigments allows plants to harvest light from a range of wavelengths, increasing the flow of electrons to the reaction centre. The two photosystems work in tandem, receiving light energy, releasing excited electrons and replacing each lost electron with one in the ground state (photosystem II electrons come from splitting water; photosystem I electrons come from photosystem II). A single molecule of chlorophyll or any other pigment alone could not perform any part of photosynthesis; a series of different molecules is needed to harvest a range of wavelengths and pass the resulting excited electrons on to the reaction centre.

Figure C1.3.18: Transfer of electrons in a photosystem A diagram of the thylakoid membrane showing pigment molecules and a light-harvesting complex absorbing light and funnelling energy to a special pair of chlorophyll a molecules at the reaction centre. The excited electron (e−) released from the reaction centre passes to a primary electron acceptor located near the thylakoid lumen side of the membrane, beginning its journey along the electron transport chain toward the stroma side.

Higher Level Photolysis of Water and ATP Production by Chemiosmosis

After receiving light energy, excited electrons from photosystem II are accepted and passed along a chain of electron carriers. As a result, the excitation level of the electrons falls back to the ground state and they come to fill the vacancies in the reaction centre of photosystem I — electrons have been transferred from photosystem II to photosystem I.

Meanwhile, the vacancies in the reaction centres of photosystem II are filled by ground-state electrons from water molecules: photosystem II is a catalyst that splits the water molecule (photolysis), releasing hydrogen ions, oxygen atoms and ground-state electrons.

2H2O → 4H+ + 4e− + O2 (photolysis at photosystem II)

From the split water molecule, oxygen atoms combine to form molecular oxygen, the waste product of photosynthesis; the hydrogen ions are used in the reduction of NADP+.

Top tips Photosystem II is called “II” because it was discovered after photosystem I; the numbering does not represent the order of electron flow, which begins at photosystem II. Protons is an alternative term for hydrogen ions and the terms can be used interchangeably.

ATP production by chemiosmosis in thylakoids

In the grana, ATP synthesis is coupled to electron transport via the movement of protons by chemiosmosis, as in mitochondria. Hydrogen ions trapped within the thylakoid space activate the rotary mechanism of ATP synthase enzymes as they move down their electrochemical gradient, synthesizing ATP from ADP and Pi.

As excited electrons from photosystem II pass along the electron transport chain, some of the energy pumps hydrogen ions (protons) from the chloroplast’s stroma into the thylakoid space, where they accumulate and cause the pH to drop. This creates a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane that sustains ATP synthesis: protons pass down their electrochemical gradient through ATP synthase (ATPase), synthesizing ATP.

Figure C1.3.19: Chemiosmosis occurs in both mitochondria and chloroplasts Two parallel diagrams. In the mitochondrion, an electron transport chain in the inner membrane pumps H+ from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating a higher H+ concentration there; H+ then diffuses back through ATP synthase into the matrix, producing ATP from ADP + Pi. In the chloroplast, the equivalent process occurs across the thylakoid membrane: H+ is pumped from the stroma into the thylakoid space (higher H+ concentration), then diffuses back out through ATP synthase into the stroma, producing ATP from ADP + Pi. In both cases, the arrangement is analogous: an electron transport chain builds a proton gradient across a membrane, and ATP synthase uses the gradient to make ATP.

Higher Level Reduction of NADP by Photosystem I: Cyclic and Non-cyclic Photophosphorylation

The excited electrons from photosystem I are passed, two at a time, to the electron acceptor NADP+, which, with the addition of a hydrogen ion from the stroma, is reduced to form NADPH + H+. Because the pathway of electrons from photosystem II to photosystem I is linear, ending with acceptance by NADP+, this is described as non-cyclic photophosphorylation.

Alternatively, the electrons released by photosystem I can be transferred back to P700 (photosystem I) instead of moving on to NADP+. This return of electrons from an acceptor to P700 results in the formation of ATP. Electrons cycle repeatedly through photosystem I and the first part of the electron transport chain but do not pass through photosystem II, so NADPH is not formed and water is not needed to replenish the electron supply. Because the electrons move cyclically, this ATP-producing process is known as cyclic photophosphorylation.

Key terms Non-cyclic photophosphorylation: process used to produce NADPH in addition to ATP; requires the presence of water.
Cyclic photophosphorylation: process that produces a steady supply of ATP in the presence of sunlight.
Figure C1.3.20: Cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation A diagram showing photosystem II (activated by 680 nm light) and photosystem I (activated by 700 nm light) linked by an electron transport chain. In non-cyclic photophosphorylation (the normal pathway): electrons flow from water (2H2O → 4H+ + O2, photolysis), via photosystem II and electron carriers, to photosystem I and then to NADP+, forming reduced NADP; as the excited electrons from photosystem II lose energy along the carrier chain, protons are pumped into the thylakoid space, and the resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis via chemiosmosis through ATP synthase. In cyclic photophosphorylation, which occurs when reduced NADP accumulates in the stroma (e.g. when CO2 concentration is low and the light-independent stage is blocked): excited electrons from photosystem I are captured by electron acceptors that normally serve photosystem II, and are returned (pumping protons along the way) to reoccupy their original space in photosystem I; the proton gradient between thylakoid space and stroma is maintained and ATP formation continues, without NADP being reduced and without oxygen being released.

Thylakoids as systems for performing the light-dependent reactions

ATP and reduced NADP do not normally accumulate, as they are immediately used to fix carbon dioxide in the surrounding stroma; ADP and NADP+ then diffuse back into the grana for reuse in the light-dependent reactions. This sequence repeats at very high speed throughout every second of daylight.

Figure C1.3.21: The light-dependent reactions A detailed diagram of part of a chloroplast showing a granum (thylakoid membrane stack) bathed in stroma. In the thylakoid membrane, photosystem II absorbs light, splits water (H2O → 2H+ + 2e− + ½O2) at the thylakoid-space side, and releases two electrons in an excited state; these pass via electron-carrier molecules along the membrane, and the energy released as they travel pumps hydrogen ions from the stroma into the thylakoid space. Ground-state electrons arriving at photosystem I are re-excited by light and, together with further electron-carrier molecules, pass to reduce NADP+ (using H+ from the stroma) to reduced NADP on the stromal side. The high concentration of hydrogen ions built up inside the thylakoid space (compared with the stroma) flows back out through channels in ATP synthase (ATPase) embedded in the membrane, and this flow catalyses the synthesis of ATP from ADP + Pi on the stromal side, where both reduced NADP and ATP are required for carbon dioxide fixation. Higher Level The Calvin Cycle: Carbon Fixation and the Synthesis of Triose Phosphate

In the light-independent reactions, carbon dioxide is converted to carbohydrate in a cyclical pathway called the Calvin cycle, which requires the products of the light-dependent reactions.

Key terms Calvin cycle: a cycle of reactions in the stroma of the chloroplast, also known as the light-independent reactions, in which carbon dioxide is converted to carbohydrate.
Rubisco (RuBisCo): ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase, an enzyme involved in the first major step of carbon fixation; it is the central enzyme of photosynthesis.

Carbon fixation

Carbon dioxide is combined with an acceptor molecule, ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, a 5-carbon sugar), in the presence of the enzyme rubisco, in the stroma. Rubisco is probably the most abundant enzyme on Earth and makes up the bulk of all the protein in a green plant — high concentrations are needed in the stroma because it works relatively slowly and is not effective at low carbon dioxide concentrations. After RuBP and carbon dioxide combine (fixation), the unstable 6-carbon product immediately splits into two molecules of the 3-carbon compound glycerate 3-phosphate — the first product of carbon fixation.

Common mistake Glycerate 3-phosphate is sometimes abbreviated to “GP”; this is discouraged as it is ambiguous, so use the chemical’s full name.

Synthesis of triose phosphate

Glycerate 3-phosphate is immediately reduced to the 3-carbon sugar phosphate, triose phosphate, using reduced NADP and ATP: reduced NADP supplies the hydrogen for the reduction, and ATP supplies the necessary energy. This is the reduction step of the cycle.

Figure C1.3.22: The path of carbon in photosynthesis — the Calvin cycle A cyclic diagram with three named steps. In fixation, carbon dioxide combines with the acceptor molecule ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, a 5-carbon sugar phosphate) to form an unstable 6-carbon sugar, which splits immediately into two molecules of the 3-carbon compound glycerate 3-phosphate. In reduction, glycerate 3-phosphate combines with hydrogen from NADPH (using energy from ATP, which becomes ADP) to form triose phosphate (a 3-carbon sugar), releasing water as a by-product; this step receives input (NADPH and ATP) from the light-dependent reactions. In regeneration, most of the triose phosphate is converted back into RuBP (using ATP) so the cycle can continue, while in product synthesis the remaining triose phosphate is used directly in respiration or converted into glucose and the other products of photosynthesis, such as lipids, amino acids and carboxylic acids.

Regeneration of RuBP

Some triose phosphate is metabolized to regenerate the acceptor molecule RuBP (the regeneration-of-acceptor step): five molecules of triose phosphate are converted to three molecules of RuBP, allowing the Calvin cycle to continue. The remaining triose phosphate can be used to synthesize other products, including glucose. Because each turn of the cycle generates two molecules of triose phosphate, three turns are needed to produce six molecules of triose phosphate, allowing five to be converted back to RuBP and one to be used to form other molecules such as glucose.

Figure C1.3.23: Summary of the light-independent reactions A cyclic diagram set within the stroma of a chloroplast (with the granum shown alongside as the source of ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reactions). Carbon dioxide combines with ribulose bisphosphate, catalysed by rubisco, to form glycerate 3-phosphate; this is reduced (using ATP and reduced NADP, releasing ADP + Pi and NADP+, which return to the granum) to form triose phosphate. Most triose phosphate undergoes regeneration of acceptor (using further ATP) back to ribulose bisphosphate; the remainder undergoes product synthesis into sugars, starch, lipids and amino acids, which are translocated to the rest of the plant.
Top tips You are expected to know the following details of the Calvin cycle: the names of the substrates RuBP and CO2, the enzyme rubisco, and the product glycerate 3-phosphate. You do not need to know the details of the individual reactions of the cycle, but should understand that five out of every six molecules of triose phosphate produced are converted back to RuBP (i.e. five-sixths, if glucose is the product) so that the cycle can continue.

Higher Level Synthesis of Carbohydrates, Amino Acids and Other Carbon Compounds

The importance of photosynthesis to the green plant is that it provides the energy-rich glucose molecules from which the plant builds its other organic molecules; the metabolism of the green plant is sustained by the products of photosynthesis. Synthesis of carbohydrates, amino acids and other carbon compounds uses the products of the Calvin cycle together with mineral nutrients absorbed from the soil. For example, triose phosphate can be further metabolized to produce carbohydrates such as sugars, sugar phosphates and starch, and later lipids, amino acids (e.g. alanine) and organic acids (e.g. malate) — the product-synthesis step.

Figure C1.3.24: The product-synthesis steps of photosynthesis A metabolic map linking the Calvin cycle (carbon dioxide, the acceptor molecule RuBP, and the 3-carbon phosphorylated sugars glycerate 3-phosphate and triose phosphate) to phosphorylated 6-carbon sugars, which lead on one side to glucose and then to carbohydrates such as starch, cellulose and sucrose, and, via glycerol and fatty acids, to lipids. On another branch, triose phosphate is converted to pyruvate and then to acetyl coenzyme A, feeding into the Krebs cycle’s organic acids (respiration pathway), including α-ketoglutarate. Nitrate (NO3−) and ammonia (NH3) taken up from the soil combine with α-ketoglutarate to form glutamate, which via transamination gives rise to all other amino acids, and hence proteins — showing how the products of photosynthesis and mineral ion uptake together supply the essential metabolites of the plant.

Higher Level Interdependence of the Light-Dependent and Light-Independent Reactions

The light-dependent reactions provide the NADPH and ATP that supply the electrons and energy needed to produce carbohydrates in the light-independent reactions; these reactions happen at the same time, and one cannot function without the other. A lack of light therefore stops the light-independent reactions too, because NADPH and ATP are no longer produced by the light-dependent reactions.

Cyanobacteria, algae and higher plants require carbon dioxide in solution, in the form of hydrogen carbonate ions (HCO3−), for the optimal function of photosystem II. Carbon dioxide, in this ionic form, has a regulating function in the splitting of water: hydrogen carbonate acts as an acceptor for the protons produced when water is split at photosystem II. Carbon dioxide therefore has a dual role — acting as a terminal electron acceptor (via reduction to glucose) at the end of the photosynthetic pathway, and as a proton acceptor (as HCO3−) at the very start of the light-dependent reactions. Removal of hydrogen carbonate slows electron transfer through the electron acceptors associated with photosystem II, because hydrogen carbonate is bound to proteins in photosystem II needed for electron transport chain activity. Depletion of carbon dioxide (and hence hydrogen carbonate) therefore causes both cessation of carbon dioxide fixation and a strong decrease in the activity of photosystem II — illustrating the tight interdependence of the two stages.

Receptors and the Basics of Chemical Signalling

Cells in a multicellular organism (and even bacteria) must respond to environmental stimuli and communicate with each other to coordinate their activities. This is done by specific chemical signalling molecules known as ligands, which include proteins, small peptides, amino acids, nucleotides, steroids, amines and even dissolved gases such as nitrogen monoxide (nitric oxide, NO). Chemical signalling ensures important activities occur in the correct cells, at the right time and in coordination with other cells — usually involving a change in gene transcription, often affecting several genes simultaneously but not necessarily all to the same extent.

Key terms Ligand: general term for a molecule that binds to a specific site on a protein.
Chemical signalling: the release of chemicals (ligands) that bind to a specific molecule which delivers a signal within the cell or to another cell.
Receptor protein: protein that recognizes and binds with a specific chemical signal molecule on the outside of the plasma membrane.
Signal transduction: the conversion of an impulse or stimulus from one physical or chemical form to another; in cell biology, the process by which a cell responds to an extracellular signal.

In most cases the ligand cannot cross the cell membrane and must bind with a receptor protein; receptor proteins are unique to specific ligands, and this specificity allows a ligand to produce responses in specific target cells. Ligands can activate many different target cells simultaneously, allowing regulation and control of the cellular response. The binding process transmits a signal across the membrane and generates a second messenger inside the cell, which can cause further changes such as activation of an enzyme, often via phosphorylation (addition of phosphate groups). In some cases, the messenger enters the nucleus (via nuclear pores) and changes transcription.

Common mistake Do not confuse “receptor protein” with the term “receptor”. Receptors are specialized cells that receive stimuli and transduce this signal into an electrical impulse. Receptor proteins are molecules that bind with ligands to convey messages into a cell, stimulating specific metabolic reactions.
Figure C2.1.1: Outline of cellular chemical signalling (signal transduction) A simple diagram showing a signalling molecule (ligand) approaching a receptor protein embedded in the cell membrane. Attachment of the ligand to its complementary receptor protein generates a signal (message) that is then transmitted inside the cell.

A chemical signalling pathway has one or more critical functions: to relay (“pass on”) the signal and spread it through the cell; to amplify the signal received (via a second messenger), making it stronger so a small number of ligands produces a large intracellular response (one ligand can trigger many signal transduction pathways simultaneously); to detect and integrate signals from more than one intracellular pathway (combining two or more signals into one) before relaying it onward; and to distribute the signal to more than one effector protein, creating branches and a complex response.

Primary transduction is when the “message” converts from being extracellular to intracellular. A scaffold protein holds several proteins close together so the reaction proceeds faster. The same signal molecule can trigger different cellular responses in two different cells, because differentiated cell types activate different sets of genes and so contain different collections of proteins.

Figure C2.1.2: Intracellular signalling proteins can relay, amplify, integrate and distribute the incoming signal A diagram showing a ligand binding an extracellular receptor protein, which activates an amplifier and transducer protein inside the cytoplasm, generating a second messenger. This activates relay proteins and a messenger protein, which pass the signal on, sometimes via an adaptor protein and an anchoring protein linked to the cytoskeleton. A scaffold protein holds several of these proteins together as a functional complex. An integrator protein combines signals from more than one pathway, and a modulator protein fine-tunes the response. Ultimately a target protein is activated in the cytoplasm, or the signal passes through the nuclear envelope to a target gene, where gene transcription (DNA to RNA) is altered.
Key terms Second messenger: small intracellular ligand (signalling molecule) generated or released inside a cell in response to an extracellular signal.
Effector protein: a protein that causes a change inside a cell during signalling.
Scaffold protein: a protein that binds two or more other proteins, organizing binding partners into a functional unit to enhance signalling efficiency.

Cell Signalling by Bacteria in Quorum Sensing

Bacteria were once considered non-social organisms with little interaction, but many strains communicate with each other by emitting, detecting and responding to ligands. One well-studied method, quorum sensing, allows bacteria to switch on genes together in response to increases in the density of cells in the population. Bacteria produce and release signal molecules that diffuse outwards, allowing the population to communicate and coordinate behaviour once a certain population size is reached.

Key term Quorum sensing: the ability of some bacteria to monitor cell density and adjust their gene expression accordingly.
Figure C2.1.3: Bacterial quorum sensing A diagram contrasting a low cell density of bacteria, where a low concentration of secreted signal molecules diffuses away and individual behaviours result, with a high cell density, where the accumulated signal molecules reach a threshold concentration and trigger coordinated group behaviours across the population.

Quorum-sensing signalling molecules are called autoinducers, detected by specific proteins inside the cell or in the bacterial cell membrane. When an autoinducer binds its receptor, it activates or represses transcription of target genes, often including genes for autoinducer synthesis itself. At low population density, diffusion keeps autoinducer concentration very low; as the population increases, the concentration reaches a threshold, gene expression occurs, and more autoinducer is synthesized — a positive feedback loop for autoinducer production.

A well-known example is the bioluminescent marine bacterium Vibrio fischeri, which produces the light-emitting enzyme luciferase once it reaches a critical population density in the light organ of its host, the Hawaiian bobtail squid. It is of little benefit to V. fischeri to produce luciferase alone as a single cell, and bacteria do not emit light outside the host. Bioluminescence is energy intensive, so the bacteria benefit from being housed within the squid, where they obtain nutrients; the squid benefits because the light produced attracts prey or provides camouflage. This is an example of mutualism.

Figure C2.1.4: The Hawaiian bobtail squid A photograph of the Hawaiian bobtail squid showing the blue-green bioluminescence produced within its light organ by colonies of the symbiotic bacterium Vibrio fischeri once they reach a critical population density.

Signalling Chemicals in Animals: Functional Categories

In animals, many ligands may be classified as hormones, neurotransmitters, cytokines or calcium ions.

Key terms Hormone: extracellular signal molecule that is secreted and transported via the bloodstream (in animals) or the sap (in plants) to target tissues, where it causes a specific effect.
Neurotransmitter: chemical released at the presynaptic membrane of an axon when an action potential arrives, which transmits the action potential across the synapse.
Cytokines: small signalling molecules (usually a protein or glycoprotein) made and secreted by cells that act on neighbouring cells to alter their behaviour.

Hormones are secreted by endocrine glands and carried through the circulatory system to act on distant target cells; they act over a longer time, affecting cell metabolism and other functions such as sexual reproduction. Neurotransmitters carry signals between neurons, or from neurons to other target cells such as muscle cells; their release is triggered by the arrival of an action potential at the end of a neuron. Cytokines form a family of relatively small, secreted proteins that control many aspects of growth and differentiation of specific cell types; some, such as α-interferon, are produced and secreted by many cell types after viral infection.

Calcium ions (Ca2+ (aq)) are one of the few inorganic ligands: many ligands in animals, including neurotransmitters, growth factors and some hormones, act via signal transduction pathways that increase cytoplasmic Ca2+ (aq) concentration. Increasing cytoplasmic Ca2+ causes responses including muscle cell contraction, secretion of substances (e.g. the neurotransmitter acetylcholine) and cell division. Calcium ions can function as a second messenger because their cytoplasmic concentration is normally much lower than their concentration outside the cell.

Chemical Diversity of Hormones and Neurotransmitters

A wide range of extracellular molecules and ions can act as ligands for receptor proteins, including metal ions, low molar mass compounds (e.g. amino acids and their derivatives), steroids, peptides and proteins.

Hormones

There are three main classes of hormone: proteins/peptides (e.g. insulin), amines (amino acid derivatives, e.g. epinephrine) and steroids (e.g. cholesterol-derived hormones such as oestradiol and testosterone). Hormones can be small, non-polar, hydrophobic molecules that diffuse through the cell membrane to reach receptors in the nucleus or cytoplasm (e.g. progesterone, testosterone and thyroid hormones), or water-soluble molecules that bind receptors on the plasma membrane (e.g. protein hormones such as insulin and glucagon, or small charged molecules such as histamine and epinephrine).

Top tip Some neurotransmitters can also act as hormones — for example, epinephrine (adrenaline) functions both as a neurotransmitter and as a hormone produced by the adrenal gland, signalling glycogen breakdown in muscle cells.

Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are a chemically diverse group, including simple amines (e.g. dopamine), amino acids (e.g. gamma-aminobutyric acid, GABA), polypeptides (e.g. endorphins) and acetylcholine (an amino acid derivative). Although some neurons release only one kind of neurotransmitter, most make and release two or more at any given time, allowing a cell to exert several influences simultaneously. In the brain and central nervous system, the gas nitric oxide (NO) also functions as a neurotransmitter, or as an agent that influences neurotransmitters. Nitric oxide is synthesized from the amino acid arginine, diffuses rapidly across the plasma membrane into nearby target cells, and acts only locally because it is quickly converted to other products by reacting with oxygen and water outside cells; it causes smooth muscle in blood vessels to relax, dilating the vessel and increasing blood flow.

Localized and Distant Effects of Signalling Molecules

Cells in multicellular organisms communicate via ligands targeted for cells that may be adjacent (local signalling) or not adjacent (long-distance signalling).

Local chemical signalling

Local signalling involves direct or close contact between cells. Synaptic signalling occurs across chemical synapses and involves neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine and norepinephrine; the gap between the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes is very small (20–40 nm), so neurotransmitters only need to transfer the signal over a tiny distance. While the immediate effect is localized, the overall effect is widespread, since the signal enables an electrical impulse to be established in the postsynaptic neuron and the message is transferred on through the body. On reaching the postsynaptic membrane, acetylcholine binds neurotransmitter receptors (ligand-gated sodium channels), opening ion channels and causing an influx of Na+ (aq), which generates a new impulse. Other examples of local signalling include gap junctions in animals and plasmodesmata in plants, and cell–cell recognition via membrane-bound molecules on the cell surface.

Key term Synaptic signalling: a type of cell–cell communication that occurs across chemical synapses in the nervous system.

Long-distance chemical signalling

For long-distance signalling, hormones are secreted into a transport system (blood plasma in animals, sap in plants) to act on distant target cells. In animals with a closed circulatory system, hormones travel in the blood throughout the body to receptors far from their source, specific to receptor proteins in the plasma membrane or within the cell, triggering a cascade of reactions that alters the target cell’s metabolism. Specialized cells release hormones (e.g. insulin and glucagon) into blood vessels to reach target cells elsewhere — known as endocrine signalling. Plant hormones (e.g. auxin) sometimes travel in the vascular tissue, but often reach their targets by moving through cells or by diffusion through the air as a gas.

Differences Between Transmembrane and Intracellular Receptors

Receptor proteins can be in the plasma membrane (transmembrane) or within the cytoplasm (intracellular). The properties of a ligand determine the receptor type used: non-polar, lipid-soluble ligands can diffuse through the phospholipid bilayer and access receptors within the cell; polar, charged, water-soluble ligands (such as peptide hormones like insulin and glucagon) are hydrophilic and cannot pass through the bilayer, so they must bind receptors on the cell surface.

Figure C2.1.5: Lipid-insoluble vs lipid-soluble ligand signalling Two side-by-side pathways to the same target cell. (a) A lipid-insoluble ligand binds a receptor protein on the outside of the plasma membrane, triggering the production of secondary messengers inside the cell, which activate an enzyme or other protein, producing a product without the ligand entering the cell. (b) A lipid-soluble ligand diffuses directly through the plasma membrane and binds a receptor protein inside the cell, forming a ligand–receptor complex that enters the nucleus, binds DNA and directs mRNA and protein synthesis.
Common mistake It is incorrect to say that all receptor proteins are in the plasma membrane. Cell-surface receptors respond to charged, polar ligands; lipid-soluble ligands, such as steroid hormones, pass through the phospholipid bilayer, so their receptors are located inside the cell.

Transmembrane receptor proteins

Transmembrane receptor proteins have three regions: a ligand-binding region, a hydrophobic region extending through the membrane, and an intracellular region that transmits the signal into the cell. Integral proteins, including receptor proteins, have hydrophobic amino acid regions helping them embed in membranes, with hydrophilic regions towards the outside where the phosphate heads of phospholipids are located; hydrophilic ligands bind to these hydrophilic regions. In most cases, ligand binding changes the receptor’s shape, relaying the signal across the membrane to the cytoplasm — the process of cell transduction. There are many different plasma membrane receptors, classifiable into a small number of structural classes.

Intracellular receptor proteins

Many lipid-soluble ligands diffuse across the plasma membrane and into the nucleus, bind DNA and initiate specific protein synthesis, which then initiates further reactions. Steroid hormones such as oestradiol, progesterone and testosterone are examples. Steroid receptors are not membrane proteins but are normally soluble proteins in the cytoplasm; since steroid hormones are lipid soluble, they diffuse across the plasma membrane into the cytoplasm. If the cell is a target cell, the hormone binds a receptor molecule (in the cytoplasm or nucleus); if the receptor is cytoplasmic, it moves to the nucleus by simple diffusion once activated, and acts as a transcription factor, binding specific regions of DNA. Depending on the hormone’s mode of action, transcription of a gene may be switched on or off; if activated, new RNA leaves the nucleus and directs formation of new proteins (often enzymes) at ribosomes, bringing about a structural or functional change in the cell.

The hormone–receptor complex moves into the nucleus and activates a gene, which yields mRNA; the mRNA passes to ribosomes in the cytoplasm and is translated into an activated enzyme, which brings about an alteration in the structure/function of the cell.
Key terms Transmembrane receptor protein: cell signalling receptor protein located in the membrane.
Intracellular receptor protein: cell signalling receptor protein located inside the cell.
Nuclear receptor: eukaryotic protein that, on binding a signal molecule, enters the nucleus and regulates transcription.

Initiation of Signal Transduction Pathways by Receptors

For a cell to respond to a signal, a ligand must first be recognized by a specific receptor molecule and the signal transmitted into the cell’s interior before a cellular response can occur. Cell signalling can be divided into three stages.

1. Ligand–receptor interaction

The ligand is complementary in shape to a binding site on the receptor and binds to it with high specificity, activating the receptor.

2. Signal transduction

This often requires a sequence of changes in a series of different molecules in a multistep pathway; these molecules are often called relay molecules. Signal transduction occurs mainly via two mechanisms: protein phosphorylation in a phosphorylation signal cascade, and release of second messengers (e.g. cyclic AMP). Such pathways allow signal amplification. The activated receptor activates one relay molecule, which activates another, and so on, until the molecule (usually a protein) that produces the final cellular response is activated. Many relay molecules are protein kinases, which often act on other protein kinases, producing a phosphorylation cascade; relay molecules are usually activated when phosphorylated and deactivated when dephosphorylated, acting as a molecular switch turning metabolic activities on and off.

Key terms Signal cascade: a series of linked reactions, often including phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, that carries information within a cell, often amplifying an initial signal.
Phosphorylation cascade: a sequence of events where one protein kinase phosphorylates another, causing a sequence of events leading to phosphorylation of many protein kinases; as the signal is carried onward it is amplified and can sometimes spread to other signalling pathways.

3. Cellular response

The signal transduction pathway leads to a specific cellular response, regulating one or more cellular activities, which may occur in the nucleus or cytoplasm. Depending on the cell and ligand, responses may include: regulation of protein activity (e.g. opening/closing an ion channel, changing membrane permeability); regulation of protein synthesis by activating or deactivating gene expression; regulation of enzyme activity; rearrangement of the cytoskeleton; or cell death (e.g. apoptosis). After the response has occurred, the signal is terminated; a cell’s ability to receive new ligands depends on the reversibility of the changes produced by previous signals.

Key term Apoptosis: a form of programmed cell death that allows cells that are unneeded or unwanted to be eliminated from an adult or developing organism.

Transmembrane Receptors for Neurotransmitters and Changes to Membrane Potential

The ligand-gated sodium ion channel is composed of five transmembrane protein subunits that combine to form an aqueous pore across the lipid bilayer, lined by five transmembrane α-helices, one from each subunit. There are two acetylcholine-binding sites on this receptor.

Figure C2.1.7: The open and closed conformations of the acetylcholine receptor (ligand-gated sodium channels) Two states of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor embedded in the membrane are shown: in the closed state, the central pore is blocked and no ions pass; when acetylcholine (ACh) binds both binding sites, the channel changes conformation so the hydrophobic side chains move apart, opening a gate that allows sodium ions (Na+) to flow through the pore down their electrochemical and concentration gradient.

When acetylcholine released by a motor neuron binds both acetylcholine-binding sites, the channel opens, allowing an influx of sodium ions. Top tip Neurons releasing acetylcholine are described as cholinergic neurons, while those releasing norepinephrine are described as adrenergic neurons.

Transmembrane Receptors that Activate G Protein

GTP is an energy-rich nucleotide like ATP, composed of ribose and three phosphate groups but with guanine rather than adenine as the base. G proteins are membrane-bound GTP-binding proteins, usually activated by the binding of a hormone or other ligand to a transmembrane receptor. They respond to extracellular signals from hormones and neurotransmitters and trigger intracellular signalling cascades that regulate bodily functions. G proteins are important in vision, smell and taste, coupling the primary stimuli (light waves, odorants or flavourants) to ion channels; changes in ion flow through these channels produce a nerve impulse that travels from the sensory organ to the brain.

Key terms GTP: guanosine triphosphate, with a role in cell signalling.
G protein: a membrane-bound GTP-binding protein, usually activated by the binding of a hormone or other ligand to a transmembrane receptor.
G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR): a cell-surface receptor that associates with a G protein.
GTPase: an enzyme that hydrolyzes GTP into GDP and Pi.

The G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) is closely associated with a G protein, which binds guanosine triphosphate (GTP) or guanosine diphosphate (GDP). GPCRs have a common structure of seven hydrophobic α-helices spanning the plasma membrane; hydrophobic interactions with the membrane lipid bilayer allow the receptor to remain embedded within it. Signal transduction from GPCRs is controlled by G proteins that bind mainly to the third and largest cytoplasmic loop of the receptor polypeptide.

Figure C2.1.8: A G protein-coupled receptor A diagram of a GPCR polypeptide chain threading back and forth through the plasma membrane seven times as seven transmembrane α-helices, connected by alternating extracellular and intracellular loops, with the amino terminus (NH2) outside the cell and the carboxyl terminus (COOH) inside the cytoplasm.
Mechanism of GPCR action The GPCR is inactive when not bound to a ligand, and the G protein is inactive when bound to GDP. When a ligand binds the extracellular side of the GPCR, the receptor is activated and changes conformation, which activates the G protein, causing it to exchange bound GDP for GTP. The activated G protein dissociates from the receptor and binds to an enzyme or other membrane-bound effector protein, activating it; the activated effector protein generates a second messenger, and (depending on the pathway) G-protein subunits or second messengers can also modulate ion channels directly, triggering signal transduction leading to a cellular response. Once the signal molecule is no longer present, GTP is hydrolyzed back to GDP by the GTPase activity of the G protein, so the G protein dissociates from the enzyme and returns to its inactive form, switching the signal off.
Figure C2.1.9: Mechanism of action of G protein-coupled receptors A cyclic diagram: an inactive GPCR bound to GDP-loaded G protein is activated when a ligand binds; GDP is dislodged and replaced by GTP, activating the G protein, which dissociates and interacts with a membrane-bound effector protein and/or an ion channel to generate second messengers or alter ion flow (ions shown moving through an ion channel). The GTPase activity of the G protein then hydrolyzes GTP back to GDP, returning the system to its inactive state.
Top tip GPCRs are found only in eukaryotes, not prokaryotes. There are nearly 1000 different GPCRs, varying in their binding sites for different signal molecules and in the different G proteins they interact with inside the cell.

Mechanism of Action of Epinephrine (Adrenaline) Receptors

The adrenal glands release epinephrine (adrenaline), which circulates in the bloodstream and binds to a class of GPCRs known as adrenergic receptors, present on many cell types. Epinephrine prepares the body for vigorous activity, increasing heart and breathing rate so more oxygen and glucose can be delivered to muscle cells for respiration. Because epinephrine is a vasoconstrictor, co-administering it with a local anaesthetic reduces blood supply at the injection site, keeping the anaesthetic at its target for longer.

Epinephrine acts as a peptide-type hormone: its receptor activates a cascade mediated by a second messenger inside the cell. Second messengers are small, non-protein, water-soluble molecules or ions that relay signals from cell-surface receptors to target molecules in the cytoplasm or nucleus; being small and water soluble, they diffuse readily throughout the cell, greatly amplifying signal strength. The most common second messengers are cyclic AMP and calcium ions, Ca2+ (aq); they participate in pathways initiated by both G protein-coupled receptors and receptor tyrosine kinases.

Key term Cyclic AMP (cAMP): small intracellular signalling molecule generated from ATP in response to hormonal stimulation of cell-surface receptors.
Figure C2.1.10: The cell signalling pathway involving epinephrine, stimulating glycogen breakdown in liver cells Epinephrine, arriving via the blood supply to the liver, binds a receptor on the cell surface membrane without entering the cell. This activates a G protein, which activates the membrane enzyme adenylyl cyclase, converting ATP into the second messenger cyclic AMP (cAMP). Cyclic AMP activates an existing (inactive) protein into an activated enzyme, which forms part of an activated enzyme system that converts glycogen to glucose inside the liver cell, altering the cell’s structure/function; cAMP is eventually inactivated, switching the response off.
The epinephrine → cAMP → glycogen breakdown cascade An enzyme embedded in the cell surface membrane, adenylyl cyclase (adenylate cyclase), when activated by the G protein, catalyses the conversion of ATP into many cAMP molecules, rapidly amplifying the signal. cAMP does not last long, as the enzyme phosphodiesterase converts it to AMP, terminating the signal. In detail: the hormone activates a GPCR, which activates a G protein (GS) that activates adenylyl cyclase to boost cAMP production; the rise in cAMP activates PKA (cAMP-dependent protein kinase), which phosphorylates and activates phosphorylase kinase, which in turn activates glycogen phosphorylase, the enzyme that breaks down glycogen. In skeletal muscle, epinephrine increases intracellular cAMP, activating PKA, which both activates the enzyme promoting glycogen breakdown and inhibits the enzyme that catalyses glycogen synthesis, maximizing the glucose available as a respiratory substrate for muscular activity. Epinephrine also stimulates adipose cells to break down fat into fatty acids, which can also be used to generate ATP by respiration.
Key terms Adenylyl cyclase / adenylate cyclase: enzyme that catalyses the formation of cAMP from ATP.
Phosphodiesterase: enzyme that catalyses the breaking of a phosphodiester bond in an oligonucleotide.
Kinase: enzyme that catalyses the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP to a specific amino acid side chain on a target protein.
Phosphorylase: enzyme that breaks down glucose-based polysaccharides (e.g. glycogen) to glucose 1-phosphate.

The second and third stages of signal transduction critically amplify the hormone signal: from one activated receptor molecule, around 10 000 (10&sup4;) molecules of cAMP are formed; the resulting cAMP activates approximately 10&sup6; molecules of active phosphorylase enzyme, which in turn trigger the formation of perhaps 10&sup8; molecules of glucose 1-phosphate.

Figure C2.1.11: Cascade of reactions triggered by the binding of epinephrine to a target cell A linear cascade beginning with cAMP, which converts an inactive kinase enzyme into an active kinase enzyme; the active kinase enzyme converts an inactive phosphorylase enzyme into an active phosphorylase enzyme; the active phosphorylase enzyme then converts glycogen into glucose 1-phosphate, illustrating how each step amplifies the number of molecules affected by the original signal.
Top tip Because the reactions in the epinephrine signalling cascade do not involve changes in gene transcription or new protein synthesis, they occur rapidly (unlike the slower, transcription-based responses of intracellular steroid receptors).

Transmembrane Receptors with Tyrosine Kinase Activity

Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) are a major class of receptors with enzymatic activity. One RTK complex may activate ten or more different signal transduction pathways and cellular responses simultaneously, helping the cell regulate and coordinate several activities at once.

Key terms Tyrosine kinase: an enzyme that catalyses the transfer of phosphate groups from ATP to tyrosine (amino acid) residues on a substrate protein, activating it.
Dimer: a molecule composed of two structurally similar subunits.
Dimerisation: the formation of a dimer from two monomers.
Autophosphorylation: the phosphorylation of a kinase by itself.
Relay protein: a protein that passes the signal to the next member of the signalling pathway.

An RTK is a single polypeptide chain with a single transmembrane α-helix, an extracellular ligand-binding site, and an intracellular tail that functions as tyrosine kinase, containing many tyrosine residues. Before ligand binding, RTKs exist as individual monomers. Ligand binding to the extracellular binding sites causes two RTK proteins to associate in the membrane, forming a dimer; dimerisation brings a suitable substrate up to the kinase active site in the intracellular tails, and tyrosine kinase adds a phosphate group from ATP to tyrosine residues on the tail of the other RTK protein, by autophosphorylation. The activated RTK triggers the assembly of specific relay proteins on its tails, activating them; each activated protein triggers a signal transduction pathway leading to a cellular response.

Figure C2.1.12: Activation of an RTK stimulates assembly of an intracellular signalling complex (a) Before ligand binding, an RTK exists as a single, inactive monomer spanning the membrane ("OFF"). (b) When ligands bind the extracellular portions of two RTK monomers, the receptors dimerize; the tyrosine kinase domains in the intracellular tails then phosphorylate (P) multiple tyrosine residues on each other's tails by autophosphorylation, switching the complex "ON" and creating docking sites that assemble an intracellular signalling complex.

Insulin signalling

Insulin is an extracellular messenger molecule (hormone) informing cells that glucose levels are high. Cells expressing insulin receptors (an RTK), such as liver cells, respond by increasing glucose uptake, increasing glycogen and triglyceride synthesis, and/or decreasing gluconeogenesis.

Key term Gluconeogenesis: set of enzyme-catalysed reactions by which glucose is synthesized from small organic molecules such as pyruvate, lactate or amino acids.

Insulin uses RTK signalling to allow cells to take up glucose: activated relay proteins cause vesicles embedded with glucose transporter proteins to move to the cell surface membrane, where the vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane, inserting the transporter proteins and increasing glucose uptake into muscle cells. Many glycogen synthase molecules are also activated, catalysing the synthesis of glycogen from glucose (glycogenesis), so a single insulin molecule binding a receptor leads to synthesis of large amounts of glycogen. The signal is terminated when insulin is released from the receptor, the tyrosine residues are dephosphorylated by protein phosphatases (which inactivate protein kinases by dephosphorylation), and the two halves of the dimer separate.

Figure C2.1.13: Insulin–RTK signalling pathway A numbered sequence of events: (1) insulin activates RTK receptors on the cell surface; (2) relay proteins are activated and travel to vesicles within the cell; (3) these vesicles contain glucose transporter proteins known as GLUT4 (glucose transporter type 4); (4) the GLUT4-containing vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane; (5) the GLUT4 proteins become incorporated into the membrane, where they transport glucose into the cell.
Key term Glycogenesis: the enzymatic formation or synthesis of glycogen.

Intracellular Receptors that Affect Gene Expression

Steroid (and thyroid) hormones are extracellular chemical signal molecules that can pass through the plasma membrane. Testosterone, a steroid hormone secreted by cells of the testes, travels through the blood and enters cells throughout the body, but only cells containing testosterone receptor molecules will respond. In the cytoplasm of target cells, the hormone binds and activates the receptor; with the hormone attached, the activated ligand–receptor complex enters the nucleus and acts as a transcription factor, binding a regulatory sequence in the DNA and promoting transcription of specific genes — in this case, giving rise to male secondary sexual characteristics. Not all genes are transcribed in the genome: specific genes can be activated (a process called gene expression) to produce proteins required by the cell or organism.

Top tip Intracellular receptors and a very similar signalling pathway operate for the female steroid hormones oestradiol and progesterone.

Effects of the Hormones Oestradiol and Progesterone on Target Cells

In the ovary, each ovum (egg) is surrounded by a protective, nourishing structure called the ovarian follicle, which also secretes the steroid hormones oestradiol and progesterone. Oestradiol is synthesized mainly in the ovary, but also in the placenta, testis and possibly the adrenal cortex, and controls the development and maintenance of female sex characteristics. Its production is controlled by hormones from the hypothalamus (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) acting on the pituitary gland, causing release of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), which are involved in the menstrual cycle. Target cells are located in the pituitary, the lining of the uterus, the breasts and bone marrow; the interaction between the hormone and these tissues leads to the development of female secondary sexual characteristics at puberty.

Once a month, one egg is released from a follicle in ovulation; after ovulation, the follicle becomes the corpus luteum. Progesterone is produced primarily by the corpus luteum (also by the placenta) and prepares the endometrium (inner lining of the uterus) for implantation of a fertilized ovum. If implantation fails, the corpus luteum degenerates and progesterone production stops; if implantation occurs, the corpus luteum continues secreting progesterone (under the influence of LH and prolactin) for several months of pregnancy, after which the placenta takes over. During pregnancy, progesterone maintains the endometrium and prevents further release of ova from the ovary.

Key terms Ovarian follicle: a fluid-filled spherical sac that contains and nourishes an immature egg (oocyte).
Oestradiol: a steroid hormone; a sex hormone of female mammals.
Progesterone: hormone released by the corpus luteum that stimulates the uterus to prepare for pregnancy.

Regulation of Cell Signalling Pathways by Positive and Negative Feedback

The steps in a chemical signalling pathway may be controlled by feedback regulation. In positive feedback, changes increase and amplify conditions further away from the starting point (away from equilibrium). In negative feedback, the feedback loop leads to a return to the initial conditions.

Key terms Feedback: when part of the output from a system returns as an input, affecting subsequent outputs.
Positive feedback: feedback that increases change; it promotes deviation away from an equilibrium.
Negative feedback: feedback that tends to counteract any deviation from equilibrium and promotes stability.
Figure C2.1.14: Positive and negative feedback within an intracellular chemical signalling pathway Two simple signalling pathways producing an output, Y, from an input, X. In signalling pathway 1 (positive feedback), Y feeds back to further increase X (shown with a plus sign), amplifying the response. In signalling pathway 2 (negative feedback), Y feeds back to decrease X (shown with a minus sign), counteracting and stabilizing the response.
Top tip Positive feedback can generate all-or-none, switch-like responses, whereas negative feedback can generate responses that oscillate on and off; more typically, negative feedback acts after a time delay to shut down a pathway once it has produced its effect.

Fruit ripening as an example of positive feedback

Plants make extensive use of transmembrane receptors, especially enzyme-coupled receptors, in processes controlling growth, development and disease resistance; plant cells do not use RTKs, cAMP or steroid hormone-type nuclear receptors. One well-studied plant signalling system controls the response of cells to ethylene (ethene, C2H4), a gaseous hormone that controls seed germination and fruit ripening. Ethylene receptors function as enzyme-coupled receptors, and it is the empty receptor that is active: in the absence of ethylene, the empty receptor activates a protein kinase that switches off ethylene-responsive genes in the nucleus; when ethylene is present, the receptor and kinase become inactive, and the ethylene-responsive genes are transcribed — as ripening proceeds and more ethylene is produced, this reinforces further ripening, an example of positive feedback.

Control of blood sugar as an example of negative feedback

The use of insulin to control blood sugar is an example of a negative feedback loop. Insulin causes cells to absorb glucose, which is stored as glycogen; this lowers blood sugar concentration, so insulin secretion by the pancreas is reduced once blood sugar has returned to normal.
