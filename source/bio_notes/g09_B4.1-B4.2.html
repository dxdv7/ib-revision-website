Habitats, Populations, Communities and Ecosystems

The smallest biological unit an ecologist tends to study is the species — a group of organisms that can potentially interbreed to produce fertile offspring. Life is organized in a hierarchy: individuals of the same species form a population; populations of different species living together and interacting form a community; and a community interacting with its abiotic (non-living) environment forms an ecosystem.

Key terms Habitat: the place in which a community, species, population or organism lives.
Microhabitat: a small-scale habitat that differs in abiotic and biotic factors from the surrounding, more extensive habitat (e.g. the crevices in tree bark, which have their own conditions distinct from the wider woodland).
Niche: a complete description of a species’ ecology — where, when and how it lives (covered in full in the next sub-topic).
Environment: the external surroundings that act on an organism, population or community, influencing survival and development.

Communities form the biotic part of an ecosystem, while abiotic components (rocks, water, light, air) form the non-living part. Within any ecosystem, organisms occupy a particular habitat: for example, within a woodland the tree canopy is the habitat of some insects and birds, while other organisms live in the soil. A description of a species’ habitat includes both geographical/physical location and ecosystem type — for example, the habitat of an orangutan is the rainforests of South East Asia (Borneo and Sumatra), and it is largely arboreal (living and foraging above ground in tree canopies).

Common confusion Students often use “species”, “population” and “community” interchangeably, but each has a precise meaning: a species is a group capable of interbreeding to produce fertile offspring; a population is a group of individuals of the same species living in the same area; a community is all the populations of different species living together and interacting.

Adaptations of Organisms to the Abiotic Environment of their Habitat

Marram grass: adaptation to sand dunes

Marram grass (Ammophila arenaria) grows in coastal sand dune areas of Europe and western Asia. It is a xerophyte — a plant adapted to survive with little available water. Xerophytic adaptations are found not only in hot deserts but also in cold regions (water frozen and unavailable) and windy, exposed places such as sand dunes, since wind and heat both increase water loss by evaporation or by steepening the water-concentration gradient between leaf tissue and air.

Figure B4.1.1: Marram grass on sand dunes A photograph of marram grass growing in a coastal sand dune habitat, illustrating the exposed, windy, well-drained environment in which the species survives.
How marram grass reduces water loss Marram grass has a rolled leaf rather than the flat leaf typical of most grasses. Rolling the leaf places the stomata on the inside of the roll, so that:
• water vapour that diffuses out through the stomata cannot easily escape, so the enclosed air becomes saturated with water vapour;
• the stomata are shielded from wind, which would otherwise strip away humid air and increase the water-concentration gradient;
• the reduced water-concentration gradient between the mesophyll air space and the enclosed air lowers the rate of evaporation;
• the inner epidermis is folded and hairy, trapping water vapour and further limiting air movement past the stomata;
• the outer epidermis has a thick cuticle and thick-walled cells, preventing water loss across the outer leaf surface.
Figure B4.1.2: Cross-section of a marram grass leaf a) A light micrograph of a transverse section through a rolled marram grass blade. b) A magnified section showing the xerophytic adaptations: an outer epidermis with a thick cuticle and thick cell walls; an inner, folded and hairy epidermis enclosing a humid air space; stomata sunk on the inside of the roll; hairs projecting into the enclosed air space; and a central vascular bundle.

Mangrove trees: adaptation to mangrove swamps

Mangrove swamps occur in tropical coastal areas with average rainfall generally over 1500 mm per year and constant insolation (the amount of solar radiation received by a surface in a given time) throughout the year. Mangrove trees grow in saline, oxygen-deficient soil, which limits productivity compared with other tropical ecosystems (although productivity is still high). Mangroves provide food, habitats and nursery sites for many aquatic species.

Mangrove root adaptations • Aerial roots allow mangroves to absorb oxygen directly from the air rather than from waterlogged, anoxic soil, enabling survival even when the forest floods with salt water.
• Stilt-like roots also stabilize the trees where the ground itself is unstable.
• Some genera (Avicennia, Laguncularia, Sonneratia) produce specialized roots called pneumatophores, which emerge vertically from the ground and act like snorkels when the forest floods. Their surface is covered in pores called lenticels where oxygen absorption occurs; the lenticels contain hydrophobic substances so that, when submerged, water cannot flood into the root.
Figure B4.1.3 & B4.1.4: Mangrove aerial roots and pneumatophores Figure B4.1.3 shows the arching aerial (stilt) roots of a mangrove tree in Thailand, raising the trunk above the waterlogged, unstable substrate. Figure B4.1.4 shows a dense field of pneumatophores — thin, vertical, snorkel-like roots — projecting up from the mud in the Sundarbans, India.

Mangrove trees tolerate a wide range of soil salinities, from 60–65 parts per thousand (ppt) NaCl, where red mangroves (Rhizophora stylosa) occur, up to 90 ppt, where black (Avicennia germinans) and white (Laguncularia racemosa) mangroves are found. Two strategies deal with salt:

Salt-excluders vs salt-excreters Salt-excluding species (e.g. red mangrove): root membranes prevent salt from entering the plant while still allowing water through.
Salt-excreting species (e.g. black and white mangroves): salt that does enter the plant is actively excreted through glands on the leaves.
These adaptations give mangrove trees a competitive advantage over other plant species, which lack tolerance of such saline conditions.

Abiotic Variables Affecting Species Distribution

Key terms Limiting factor: any variable that slows life processes or stops a population from growing (this applies whether the factor is deficient or in excess).
Biotic factor: a living part of an ecosystem (part of the community) that can influence an organism or ecosystem, e.g. competition or predation.
Abiotic factor: a non-living, physical factor that can influence an organism or ecosystem, e.g. temperature, salinity, pH, oxygen, carbon dioxide, light, hydrostatic pressure, water current, wind velocity, substratum type, rainfall and humidity.

Plants and animals share some limiting factors (often biotic, e.g. competition) but differ in others (often abiotic). Limiting factors in plants include light, water, nutrients, carbon dioxide and temperature; in animals they include food, space, mates and water.

The concept of tolerance holds that there are environmental levels beyond which a population cannot survive, and an optimum range within which a species can thrive. Adaptations give a species its particular range of tolerance.

Zones of Stress and Limits of Tolerance

The law of tolerance was developed by American zoologist Victor Ernest Shelford in 1911. It states that an organism or population has minimum, maximum and optimum environmental factor levels that determine its survival success. This model simplifies complex real-world systems into a diagram, usually shown as a bell-shaped curve, even though real species distributions may in fact be skewed towards one preferred area of tolerance rather than being perfectly symmetrical.

Key terms Limits of tolerance: the upper (critical maximum) and lower (critical minimum) limits to the range of a particular environmental factor within which an organism can survive.
Range of tolerance: the range between the critical minimal and critical maximum limits of an environmental factor affecting an organism.
Figure B4.1.6: Zones of stress and limits of tolerance A bell-shaped graph with an environmental gradient (e.g. temperature, low to high) on the x-axis and population size on the y-axis. The central portion is the optimal range, where conditions favour maximum fitness, growth, abundance and survival. On either side are zones of stress, where fewer individuals occur, survival is lower and reproduction is not possible. Beyond the lower and upper limits of tolerance lie the zones of intolerance, where organisms are absent altogether.

Tolerance ranges are not fixed: they can change with the season, with changing environmental conditions, or with the life stage of the organism. For some species one factor dominates in regulating distribution and abundance, but generally many factors interact.

Case study: thermal limits of the Pompeii worm

The Pompeii worm (Alvinella pompejana) lives at deep-sea hydrothermal vents (up to 13 cm long, pale grey, with red tentacle-like head gills), colonizing black smoker chimneys that expel superheated, metal-rich fluid. It is one of the most heat-tolerant animals known, living in self-made protective tubes at depths of around 2500 m and pressures of 25 MPa.

Figure B4.1.7: The Pompeii worm (Alvinella pompejana) A photograph of the pale-grey tube-dwelling worm with red, tentacle-like gills projecting from its head, at a deep-sea hydrothermal vent.

Researchers removed worms from vents using a pressurized isobaric sampling device and transported them to surface aquaria maintained at the same pressure. Worms were exposed to three thermal regimes: a constant 20°C, and two 2-hour heat exposures (42°C and 55°C) followed by a 3-hour recovery at 20°C. Survival, cell (coelomocyte) mortality, and expression of the heat-shock stress gene hsp70 (activated by environmental stresses such as heat) were measured.

Figure B4.1.8: Results of the Pompeii worm heat-tolerance experiment Three bar charts comparing the 20°C, 42°C and 55°C treatments. a) Percentage animal survival: high at 20°C and 42°C, zero at 55°C. b) Percentage coelomocyte (circulating cell) mortality: relatively high at 20°C, lowest at 42°C, and highest at 55°C. c) Mean normalized hsp70 gene expression: low at 20°C and 42°C, and markedly elevated at 55°C.

At 55°C, worms initially ventilated their tubes normally, but after 10 minutes left their tubes and crawled on the colony surface — highly abnormal behaviour for this normally sedentary species, indicating severe disturbance. By the end of the experiment all worms at 55°C were dead, with serious tissue and cell damage, and significantly elevated hsp70 transcripts confirming that 55°C exposure is harmful. At 42°C, survival was highest at both organism and cell level, with no observable tissue damage, and only a mild hsp70 response. Surprisingly, at 20°C, cell mortality and stress-gene expression were both significantly higher, and survivorship lower, than at 42°C — showing that 20°C actually caused more physiological damage than 42°C.

These findings confirm A. pompejana is a thermophilic extremophile (organisms that thrive at temperatures of roughly 45–122°C). The experiment gave the first direct evidence that the worm cannot withstand prolonged exposure to 50–55°C, and that its thermal optimum lies below 55°C and above 20°C. Recent research shows that a layer of bacteria on the worm’s back provides additional insulation from extreme heat, meaning the worm alone tolerates up to 55°C but can tolerate higher temperatures with bacterial help.

Case study: limits of tolerance of crop plants to salt

Key terms Halophyte: a plant that can grow in soil or water of high salinity, via contact through roots or salt spray (e.g. in saline semi-deserts, mangrove swamps, salt marshes and seashores). Halophytes make up perhaps under 0.5% of all plant species.
Glycophyte: a plant that is not salt tolerant and is damaged fairly easily by high salinity; most plant species are glycophytes.

High soil salt concentrations make it harder for roots to extract water (due to osmotic effects) and can also be directly toxic within the plant. A study compared the salt tolerance (shoot dry matter after at least 3 weeks of NaCl exposure, relative to no-NaCl controls) of cereal crops (rice, durum wheat, barley) and wild-growing plants (tall wheatgrass, arabidopsis, alfalfa, saltbush).

Figure B4.1.9: Salinity tolerance of crop and wild plant species A line graph plotting salinity tolerance (increase in dry matter, as % of control) against NaCl concentration (0–600 mM) for seven species. Saltbush and tall wheatgrass maintain the highest relative growth across the salinity range, showing the greatest tolerance; rice and alfalfa decline earliest and most steeply, showing the least tolerance; durum wheat, barley and arabidopsis fall between these extremes.

More than 800 million hectares of land worldwide (over 6% of Earth’s land area) are affected by salt, including 20% of irrigated land, which produces a third of the world’s food. Understanding wild and crop plant responses to salinity can help reduce salinity stress on agriculture — for example, crossing tall wheatgrass with its domesticated relative wheat can transfer salt-tolerance traits.

Range of Tolerance of a Limiting Factor: Correlating Distribution with an Abiotic Variable

Rocky shores are ideal for studying optimum zones of tolerance and critical limits because abiotic factors vary dramatically over a small spatial scale between the high and low tide lines. Transects are used to study the distribution of organisms along such environmental gradients; quadrats are used to sample plants, algae and other immobile organisms along a transect.

Types of transect Line transect: a tape measure is laid out in the direction of the gradient (e.g. at 90° to the sea on a beach), and all organisms touching the tape are recorded; many line transects are needed for valid quantitative data.
Belt transect: a band of chosen width (usually 0.5–1 m) is sampled along the gradient, giving a larger sample.
Continuous transect: the whole transect is sampled.
Interrupted transect: samples are taken only at points of equal distance along the gradient. Horizontal distances are used where there is no visible vertical change (e.g. a shingle ridge succession); vertical distances are used where there is a climb or descent (e.g. a rocky shore).
Figure B4.1.10: Line transects on a rocky shore A photograph of researchers using line transects (tape measures laid perpendicular to the sea) to sample an environmental gradient down a rocky shore.
Figure B4.1.11: Profile and belt transect analysis of a rocky shore community An upper profile diagram (obtained by surveying with poles and a levelling device) showing drop in height (cm) against distance along the transect (m), dividing the shore into high water, midshore and low water zones. Below it, a kite diagram plots the abundance (rare, occasional, frequent, abundant, dominant) of named plant species (black lichen, channelled wrack, spiral wrack, knotted wrack, black wrack, serrated wrack, oar weed) and animal species (nerite winkle, rough winkle, smooth winkle, edible winkle, dog whelk, barnacle, acorn barnacle, common limpet) at each sampling station, with kite width representing abundance. Species such as black lichen and nerite winkle, which tolerate high desiccation, dominate near the high-water line, while oar weed and other seaweeds requiring near-constant submersion dominate near the low-water line.

Most of the seashore is periodically submerged, and tidal changes affect the highest point on the shore submerged at any time in a month; almost all shore species are of marine origin. The higher an organism occurs on the shore, the longer its daily exposure to air and the less frequent its submersion. Exposure — bringing the risk of desiccation and wider temperature extremes than occur underwater — is the key abiotic factor correlated with the distribution of organisms along a rocky-shore transect.

Conditions Required for Coral Reef Formation

Coral reefs are a marine ecosystem found between the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (23.5° N and S of the equator), where seas are warm and sunlight is strong year-round. Many reef-building corals have a narrow temperature tolerance, preferring water between 23°C and 29°C.

Key term Symbiotic: a close and long-term biological interaction between two different biological organisms.

Reef-building corals live in symbiosis with a microscopic unicellular alga called zooxanthellae. Because zooxanthellae need light to photosynthesize, corals can grow only in relatively shallow water — most reef-building corals occur in less than 25 m of seawater. Temperature is also a limiting factor for algal survival: corals live at the uppermost boundary of their temperature tolerance, so even a 1°C rise in sea surface temperature can stress the zooxanthellae, causing them to leave the coral (“bleaching”). Because coral tissue itself is largely transparent and the calcium carbonate skeleton beneath is white, large-scale zooxanthellae loss makes the coral appear white. Bleached corals begin to starve, since they no longer receive glucose from their algal partner, and most cannot survive long without their zooxanthellae — although if conditions return to normal quickly enough, corals can regain zooxanthellae and recover.

Ocean acidification and coral reefs Rising atmospheric CO₂ from fossil fuel combustion increases dissolved CO₂ in the oceans. Dissolved CO₂ reacts with water to form hydrogencarbonate and hydrogen ions, lowering seawater pH (ocean acidification). Because coral skeletons are made of basic calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), a decrease in pH reduces calcification rates and can dissolve existing reef structure, since calcium carbonate reacts with hydrogen ions to form Ca2+ (aq). Note: carbon dioxide is not itself acidic — it becomes acidic once it reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which dissociates to release H+ ions. Sea-level rise from global warming can also reduce coral reef extent, since greater water depth means less light reaches the coral, lowering the rate of zooxanthellae photosynthesis.

Overall, the factors that must be in delicate balance for coral reef growth are: water depth, pH, salinity, clarity and temperature.

Abiotic Factors as Determinants of Terrestrial Biome Distribution

Insolation, temperature and precipitation affect the type of ecosystem that develops in a given area. Temperature affects the rate of enzyme reactions (and hence photosynthesis, decomposition and transpiration); precipitation and insolation both affect rates of photosynthesis and primary productivity. Other climatic elements affecting ecosystems include air pressure, humidity, cloudiness and wind.

Key terms Climograph: a graphical model showing the relationship between temperature, precipitation and ecosystem type, first developed by plant ecologist R. H. Whittaker.
Biome: groups of ecosystems with similar abiotic conditions and communities, defined by their climate and dominant plant species.

Because temperature and rainfall are among the main limiting factors for plant growth, and vegetation underpins communities in all geographic areas, these two abiotic factors can be used to model and predict the geographical distribution of ecosystem types.

Figure B4.1.12: Whittaker’s climograph A scatter/zone plot with mean annual precipitation (cm, x-axis, 0–450) against mean annual temperature (°C, y-axis, roughly −15 to 25). Distinct zones on the graph correspond to named biomes: tropical rainforest (high temperature, very high precipitation), tropical deciduous forest, desert shrubland and thorn scrub (low precipitation across a range of temperatures), savannah and thorn woodland, temperate rainforest, temperate deciduous forest, temperate grassland, sclerophyllous woodland, boreal forest, tundra and arctic-alpine zones (low temperature). A dashed line marks a zone where factors other than rainfall and temperature (e.g. soil type, fire, grazing, seasonal drought) strongly influence which ecosystem actually develops — for example, in savannah areas grassland forms on well-drained sandy soil while forest forms on water-retaining clay soil.

To read the climograph: locate the mean annual precipitation on the x-axis and the mean annual temperature on the y-axis, then find where perpendicular lines from these two points intersect — that zone gives the biome. For example, 300 cm precipitation and 25°C temperature corresponds to tropical rainforest. Note that ecosystems in reality grade into one another rather than having sharp edges (e.g. tropical ecosystems grade from highly productive rainforest through to unproductive desert), even though the climograph shows apparently distinct boundaries.

Biomes as Groups of Ecosystems Shaped by Convergent Evolution

Biomes are groups of ecosystems with similar communities, arising from similar abiotic conditions and convergent evolution (unrelated organisms independently evolving similar features in response to similar environmental pressures). Rainfall, insolation and temperature together determine productivity — the rate of storage of energy in plant biomass through photosynthesis — in each biome. Higher temperatures speed up the enzyme reactions driving photosynthesis, although excessive heat can denature enzymes.

Five inset climate graphs (temperature line and precipitation bars by month) for representative locations show contrasting climates: Manaus (tropical rainforest — warm and wet year-round), Bulawayo (savannah — warm with a marked wet/dry season), Oxford (temperate — mild, moderate year-round rainfall), Kazachye and Fairbanks (both high-latitude — cold winters, short cool summers, low precipitation).

Tropical rainforest

Tropical rainforests have the highest productivity of any biome, found between the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn where rainfall is high (over 2500 mm/year), insolation is constant year-round, and temperatures are warm (typically 26°C). High productivity supports a complex vertical structure, from ground level to a canopy, with emergent trees up to 50 m tall and lower layers of shrubs and vines. Because rainforests lie within a band close to the equator, they experience little seasonal variation in sunlight or temperature (although the monsoon period can reduce insolation), giving an all-year growing season.

Figure B4.1.14: Tropical rainforest, Thailand A photograph of dense, multi-layered tropical rainforest vegetation.

Temperate forests

Temperate ecosystems range from deciduous and evergreen forest to grassland, found largely between 40° and 60° N and S of the equator. Although highly productive for part of the year, temperate forests have overall lower productivity than rainforests because of seasonal variation in sunlight, rainfall and temperature (cold winters, warm summers), resulting in less stored chemical energy. At these latitudes, sufficient rainfall (500–1500 mm/year) supports forest rather than grassland.

Figure B4.1.15: Temperate forest, UK A photograph of a temperate deciduous forest.

Taiga (northern coniferous forest)

Taiga is dominated by coniferous forest (mostly pines), found between 50° and 70° N, near the Arctic Circle. The climate is extremely cold and harsh, with low precipitation and a short growing season; long severe winters can last up to 6 months, mean annual temperatures range from a few degrees above freezing down to −10°C, and summers (50–100 frost-free days) are short. Snow cover reflects incoming solar radiation, increasing cooling. Taiga is the world’s largest land biome, covering around 27% of Earth’s land surface, spanning most of Canada, Alaska and parts of the northern USA, most of Sweden, Finland, much of Russia (including two-thirds of Siberia), large areas of Norway and Estonia, Iceland, and parts of northern Kazakhstan, Mongolia and Japan (Hokkaido).

Figure B4.1.16: Taiga forest, Canada A photograph of a dense coniferous (pine) forest typical of the taiga biome.

Grasslands

Grasslands are found on every continent except Antarctica, covering about 16% of Earth’s surface. They develop where precipitation is insufficient to support forest but too great for deserts to form — the Great Plains and Russian Steppes are temperate grasslands, while the East African savannahs are tropical grasslands. Grasses show wide diversity but low productivity. Away from the sea, grasslands have wildly fluctuating temperatures, which can limit survival; mixing of cold polar air with warmer winds increases precipitation relative to polar and desert regions, and rainfall is roughly balanced by evaporation. Grasses grow beneath the surface and can remain dormant through cold winters until the ground warms.

Figure B4.1.17: The Serengeti grasslands, Tanzania A photograph of open, tropical (savannah) grassland with scattered trees.

Tundra

Tundra occurs at high northern latitudes (Arctic tundra), with small amounts also in ice-free parts of Antarctica and at high altitude on mountains at lower latitudes (alpine tundra). Water is locked up as ice for much of the year and unavailable to plants, reducing productivity; low temperatures also slow the enzyme-driven reactions of photosynthesis, respiration and decomposition, making this a highly stressful, low-productivity environment where only mosses, lichens and low scrubs/grasses typically survive. Low insolation (from high latitude and short day length) further limits productivity. Soil may be permanently frozen (permafrost), limiting nutrient availability, and even in summer permafrost lies only centimetres below the surface, preventing tree growth. Winter temperatures can fall to −50°C; but in summer, near-continuous daylight raises insolation and temperature, driving a burst of plant growth.

Figure B4.1.18: Alaskan tundra A photograph of open, low-lying tundra vegetation (mosses, lichens, low shrubs) with no trees.

Hot deserts

Hot deserts have high average temperatures and very low rainfall, and are found in bands at approximately 30° N and S, covering 20–30% of Earth’s land surface, where dry air descends having already lost its water vapour over the tropics. The Sahara Desert (about 9.2 million km²) is the world’s largest hot desert, only slightly smaller than the USA (9.8 million km²). Hot deserts have high daytime temperatures (typically around 45–49°C in the early afternoon) and low precipitation (typically under 250 mm/year), often unevenly distributed; the lack of water limits photosynthesis and productivity. Night-time temperatures under clear skies can fall to 10°C or even 0°C, so organisms must also cope with large daily temperature fluctuations.

Figure B4.1.19: Location of hot deserts worldwide A world map marking the major hot deserts within roughly 30° N and S of the equator, including the Sahara, Namib, Kalahari, Arabian, Thar, Taklamakan, Gobi, Dasht-e-Lut, Turkestan, Somali, Sonoran, Mojave, Atacama, Sechura and Australian deserts.
Common mistake Deserts are simply areas where water is generally inaccessible and vegetation sparse — not necessarily hot. Antarctica is classified as a (cold) desert, since its water is locked up as ice; it is incorrect to say that all deserts are hot.

Adaptations to Life in Hot Deserts and Tropical Rainforest

Hot desert survival strategies

Three strategies for surviving desert extremes Expire: parent organisms die when conditions are too extreme, but leave behind tough seeds or eggs that survive.
Evade: avoid extreme conditions by shifting activity from day to night, or from above to below ground (e.g. kangaroo rats sleeping in burrows during the hot day).
Endure: adaptations that allow survival despite the extreme conditions (e.g. the cactus). Fatty deposits stored by animals can be used as a respiratory substrate, releasing metabolic water; plants (succulents, e.g. cacti) store water in roots, stems and/or leaves.

Camels

Camels tolerate hot, dry days and cold nights, wind-blown sand and water scarcity. They can tolerate a body temperature up to 42°C. Thick fur on top of the body provides shade, while thin fur elsewhere allows easy heat loss; long necks and legs increase surface area for heat loss. Large, flat feet spread body mass over sand, reducing pressure and aiding movement. Camels lose very little water through urination or sweating, and fat stored in the hump can be converted to metabolic water via respiration. Slit-like, closable nostrils and two rows of eyelashes keep sand out of the nose and eyes.

Cacti

Cacti have no leaves, so photosynthesis occurs in the stem, reducing surface area for water loss; their rounded, spherical shape further reduces surface area-to-volume ratio, limiting transpiration. A thick, waxy cuticle prevents water loss across the surface. Large volumes of water are stored in collapsible water-storage cells in the stem (and sometimes roots). Needles (modified leaves) deter mammals from eating the water-rich flesh. Shallow, wide-spread roots rapidly absorb any precipitation before it evaporates, while deep roots reach water sources below the surface.

Figure B4.1.20: Cacti growing in the Andes, South America A photograph of tall, columnar cacti in an arid mountain landscape.

Scorpions

Scorpions are nocturnal arthropods that live underground during the day to avoid heat. They are opportunistic ambush predators, waiting for prey to approach rather than expending energy chasing it, and use a venomous sting to kill prey. Their exoskeleton provides protection and prevents water loss, and low food and water requirements suit them to desert life. Their eight legs detect the surface vibrations caused by moving prey, allowing them to judge its direction and distance.

Figure B4.1.21: Granulated thick-tailed scorpion, Kalahari Desert A photograph of a large, dark-coloured scorpion on desert sand.

Kangaroo rats

Dipodomys species live in hot, dry deserts, hiding in burrows by day and surviving without ever drinking liquid water. They excrete extremely concentrated urine and produce no sweat, minimizing water loss through excretion and evaporation.

Figure B4.1.22: Kangaroo rat A photograph of a small, long-legged, long-tailed desert rodent.

Tropical rainforest adaptations

The complex, multi-layered structure of the rainforest, from ground level to canopy (with emergent trees above the canopy and shrubs/vines below), creates a wide range of conditions and intense competition, driving diverse adaptations.

Pitcher plants

Carnivorous pitcher plants (Nepenthes species) grow in the rainforest canopy and in nutrient-poor soils. They photosynthesize for glucose but lack access to other nutrients (e.g. nitrogen for amino acids) from their environment, so they catch and digest insects to obtain these. The pitcher is a modified leaf folded into a cup-shaped trap; digestive enzymes are secreted inside, and downward-pointing spines prevent escape. Larger species such as Nepenthes rajah instead collect nutrients from the faeces of small mammals (e.g. tree shrews), which are attracted to a sweet secretion and use the pitcher as a toilet — a mutualistic relationship.

Figure B4.1.23: Pitcher plants in the rainforest canopy A photograph of cup-shaped pitcher traps hanging among rainforest canopy vegetation.

Flying lizards

Draco species (up to about 20 cm long) glide between trees rather than walking along the dangerous, predator-rich forest floor, saving energy and evading ground predators. Folds of skin lie flat against the body but stretch taut between extended ribs to form gliding “wings.”

Figure B4.1.24: Draco lizard gliding, Thailand A photograph of a lizard mid-glide with its rib-supported skin membranes extended like wings.

Gibbons

Hylobates species (lesser apes of South East Asian rainforests) have elongated forearms, hook-like fingers and highly mobile shoulder joints adapted for brachiation — swinging from tree to tree — and rarely, if ever, descend to the forest floor.

Figure B4.1.25: A Lar gibbon swinging between trees A photograph of a gibbon brachiating, gripping one branch with elongated arms while reaching for the next.

Orchid mantis

The orchid mantis (Hymenopus coronatus) is a mimicry adaptation: its four legs resemble flower petals, so it resembles an orchid flower, attracting pollinators that it then captures and kills with its forelimbs. The young (first instar), however, are dark orange with black legs and head, resembling the unopened buds of a flowering tree rather than an open orchid.

Figure B4.1.26: Adult and young orchid mantis a) An adult orchid mantis sitting on an orchid flower, its petal-like legs blending with the real petals. b) A young (first instar) orchid mantis, dark orange with a black head and legs, resembling an unopened flower bud rather than an open flower.

Mimicry of this kind is an effective survival adaptation used by many rainforest species, including beetles, caterpillars, moths, lizards, snakes and frogs.

The Ecological Niche

Communities consist of many interacting species, each playing a unique role because of its unique combination of spatial habitat and interactions with other species. This complete description of a species’ place within a community is its niche.

Key term Niche: the role played by a species in its community, including its abiotic requirements and tolerances, and its interactions with other organisms.

A species’ niche depends not only on where it lives (habitat) but also on what it does — its courtship displays, grooming, alertness to prey, activity patterns, interactions with other species, how it obtains food, and so on. No two species can share exactly the same niche, since the niche completely defines the species’ role in the ecosystem; if two species temporarily occupy overlapping niches, this drives evolutionary change or competitive exclusion.

Figure B4.2.1: Different niches of the cormorant and the shag A diagram comparing the cormorant (Phalacrocorax carbo) and the shag (Gulosus aristotelis), two similar seabirds that nest on similar coastal cliffs and rocks. Bar data show the percentage of different prey types taken by each species: the cormorant’s diet is dominated by bottom-feeding prey such as flatfish, while the shag’s diet is dominated by surface-swimming prey such as sand eels and herring, with shrimps/prawns making up a small proportion for both. The cormorant feeds close to shore on seabed fish; the shag nests on narrower cliff ledges and feeds further out to sea, capturing fish from the upper water layers.

Although the cormorant and shag appear to share the same habitat (coastal cliffs), their different diet and feeding behaviour mean they occupy different niches and so avoid competing with each other.

Obligate Aerobes, Obligate Anaerobes and Facultative Anaerobes

Earliest life on Earth evolved without atmospheric oxygen and so carried out only anaerobic respiration; all niches were then anaerobic. Once photosynthetic organisms began producing oxygen, atmospheric oxygen concentration rose, allowing prokaryotes to evolve into new, aerobic niches, thereby avoiding competition with existing anaerobic species. As oxygen levels rose, anaerobic species retreated into low- or no-oxygen environments, such as the intestines and stomachs of mammals.

Key terms Obligate aerobes: organisms that can only respire aerobically (e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis).
Obligate anaerobes: organisms that only respire in the absence of oxygen; they are poisoned by oxygen because they lack defence mechanisms to protect their enzymes from oxidants (e.g. methane-producing archaea).
Facultative anaerobes: organisms that normally respire aerobically but can switch to anaerobic respiration when oxygen is absent (e.g. Escherichia coli, which lives in animal intestines but can also survive in water, food, soil or contaminated surfaces).
Figure B4.2.2: Classification of micro-organisms by respiration A flow diagram starting from glucose, through glycolysis to pyruvate. Pyruvate can proceed via the Krebs cycle plus terminal oxidation/oxidative phosphorylation (using oxygen) to yield ATP, carbon dioxide and water — this is aerobic respiration. Alternatively, pyruvate can undergo anaerobic pathways: alcoholic fermentation (yielding ethanol and CO₂, using a reduced hydrogen acceptor, NAD) or lactic acid fermentation (yielding lactic acid). Micro-organisms that only respire aerobically are obligate aerobes; those that only respire anaerobically are obligate anaerobes; those that can use either pathway depending on conditions are facultative anaerobes.
Top tip The tolerance of obligate anaerobes, facultative anaerobes and obligate aerobes to their environment is determined specifically by the presence or absence of oxygen gas.

Modes of Nutrition

As well as respiration type, a species’ mode of nutrition also shapes its niche, allowing species in the same habitat to be distinguished by how they feed. Micro-organisms (and other organisms) are broadly either autotrophic (making their own organic molecules using an external energy source) or heterotrophic (relying on ready-made complex organic food).

Common mistake Energy cannot be created or destroyed, so it is incorrect to say autotrophs “make their own energy.” Autotrophs convert energy from one form (sunlight, or chemical energy in inorganic reactions) into another (chemical energy in food) — they produce their own food, not their own energy.

Photosynthesis: photoautotrophs

Plants contain pigments such as chlorophyll to absorb sunlight and convert it into chemical energy (photosynthesis), together with the metabolic machinery to reduce carbon dioxide and produce glucose and oxygen. Most green plants are entirely autotrophic and, because they use sunlight, are called photoautotrophs; this places them at the start of most food chains. Algae (a group of protists) and photosynthetic bacteria are also autotrophic. Cyanobacteria (aquatic photosynthetic bacteria) were the first photosynthetic organisms on Earth, evolving around 3.7 billion years ago and dramatically changing the atmosphere.

Key term Photoautotroph: an organism that uses light energy to generate ATP and to produce glucose from inorganic substances.

Holozoic nutrition in animals

Unlike green plants, animals (and most other organisms) rely on existing nutrients obtained by digestion, then absorption into cells and tissues for assimilation; animal nutrition therefore depends, directly or indirectly, on plant nutrition. In ecology, animals are known as consumers, and animal nutrition is described as heterotrophic.

Key terms Heterotrophic: using carbon compounds obtained from other organisms to synthesize required carbon compounds. A heterotroph obtains organic molecules from other organisms.
Holozoic: nutrition in consumers where food is ingested, digested internally, absorbed and assimilated. A consumer ingests other organic matter that is living or recently killed.
Herbivore: an animal that feeds holozoically, exclusively on plants (a primary consumer).
Carnivore: a flesh-eating organism. Carnivores feeding on primary consumers are secondary consumers; those feeding on secondary consumers are tertiary consumers, and so on.
Top tip Holozoic nutrition (“feeding like an animal”) is just one form of heterotrophic nutrition. Autotrophs use inorganic molecules in their nutrition; heterotrophs use organic molecules. All heterotrophs are ultimately dependent on autotrophs, which manufacture their own food.

Mixotrophic nutrition in some protists

Some organisms carry out both autotrophic and heterotrophic nutrition, and are called mixotrophs. Examples include pitcher plants (autotrophic by photosynthesis, but also carnivorous), marine flatworms (e.g. Convoluta) with algae living symbiotically (endosymbiosis) in their tissues, and many corals that live in symbiosis with algae. Euglena is a well-known freshwater protist that is both autotrophic and heterotrophic.

Figure B4.2.3: Euglena, an autotrophic and heterotrophic protist A false-colour micrograph of Euglena (magnification ×40,000) showing chloroplasts, where photosynthesis (autotrophic nutrition) occurs; a light-sensitive “eyespot” that detects the light source for photosynthesis; and food vacuoles, into which bacteria are taken by phagocytosis and digested by hydrolytic lysosomal enzymes (heterotrophic nutrition). The plasma membrane has a ridged appearance, supported by an underlying system of microtubules.
Obligate vs facultative mixotrophs Obligate mixotrophs always carry out both forms of nutrition (e.g. Euglena).
Facultative mixotrophs sometimes carry out both, but can also function as purely autotrophic or purely heterotrophic (e.g. the phytoplankton Cryptomonas, which takes up dissolved organic carbon, or under inorganic nutrient stress uses dissolved amino acids or other organic nitrogen sources). The uptake of dissolved organic material is known as osmotrophy.

Saprotrophic nutrition in some fungi and bacteria

Key term Saprotroph: an organism that lives on or in dead organic matter, secreting digestive enzymes into it and absorbing the products of digestion (external digestion). Fungi and bacteria with this mode of nutrition are called decomposers.

Feeding by saprotrophs releases inorganic nutrients from dead organic matter — including carbon dioxide, water, ammonia, amines, and ions such as nitrates and phosphates — which are then absorbed and reused by green plants.

Saprotroph vs detritivore A detritivore ingests dead organic matter and digests it internally — this is holozoic nutrition, and detritivores are not saprotrophs. A saprotroph (decomposer) instead secretes enzymes and digests dead matter externally, then absorbs the products — saprotrophic nutrition. It is incorrect to describe a saprotroph simply as “an organism that feeds on dead organic matter”, since this would also describe detritivores; the defining feature of a saprotroph is external digestion. It is also incorrect to describe saprotrophic feeding as “recycling energy”, since the energy obtained is ultimately released as heat during respiration, not recycled.

Archaea include a diverse range of photosynthetic, chemosynthetic and heterotrophic organisms:

Key terms Chemosynthesis: inorganic molecules are oxidized to release energy, which is used to synthesize glucose.
Chemoautotrophs: organisms that use energy from chemical reactions (oxidation of inorganic compounds) to make glucose.

Some archaea (e.g. halobacteria) use sunlight as an energy source without oxygen-generating photosynthesis: light-activated ion pumps generate ion gradients across the plasma membrane, and the potential energy of these gradients is transferred to ATP by ATP synthase. Other archaea are autotrophic without using sunlight: they are chemoautotrophs, making glucose using energy from chemical reactions. Some, living at great ocean depths with no light for photosynthesis, are hydrogen-dependent, using molecular hydrogen as an energy source. Chemosynthetic archaea also occur in the stomachs and intestines of some mammals. Methanogens (methane-producing archaea) release energy for ATP synthesis by using electrons from hydrogen gas to produce methane and other organic compounds; they occur in mammal guts and at the ocean floor, where they can create large methane bubbles beneath the seabed. This shows it is incorrect to say all food energy ultimately comes from the Sun — some is generated by chemoautotrophs.

Top tip: photosynthesis vs chemosynthesis Both processes use energy to synthesize glucose, but the energy source differs: photosynthesis (e.g. in plants) uses light energy to excite electrons; chemosynthesis (e.g. in some extremophile archaea/bacteria) uses energy from oxidative chemical reactions involving inorganic compounds.

Heterotrophic archaea include marine species that feed on lignin from woody plants washed out to sea. Lignin, found in vascular plant cell walls, protects plants from pathogens and strengthens the cell wall, and is naturally resistant to degradation by most microbes. By digesting lignin, archaea help recycle plant remains, contributing to the carbon cycle.

Relationship Between Dentition and Diet in the Hominidae

Humans belong to the family Hominidae, one of the two families within the ape superfamily Hominoidea (the other being the Hylobatidae, the gibbons). Hominidae includes the great apes: orangutans (Pongo), gorillas (Gorilla), chimpanzees and bonobos (Pan), and humans (Homo).

Figure B4.2.4: The evolutionary tree of the Hominidae A phylogenetic tree showing the branching relationships among gibbon species (Hylobates lar, H. agilis, H. moloch, Nomascus concolor), the orangutan (Pongo pygmaeus), the gorilla (Gorilla gorilla), the bonobo and chimpanzee (Pan paniscus, Pan troglodytes), and the hominins, including Australopithecus africanus, Paranthropus robustus, Homo erectus and Homo sapiens.

Species within the Hominidae have varied diets. Gorillas are mainly herbivores, feeding on large amounts of vegetation daily; large masseter muscles (connecting skull and jaw, producing a side-to-side grinding motion) and temporal muscles (pulling the jaw up for biting) are adapted for this diet. Gorillas have a large temporal area and a sagittal crest (a bony ridge along the midline of the skull top) providing attachment for the large temporal muscle, reflecting the need to forcibly bite fibrous plant material. Chimpanzees are primarily frugivores (fruit-eaters), eating vegetation mainly when fruit is scarce, and occasionally eat meat (e.g. from monkeys), making their diet omnivorous; their masseter and temporal muscles are correspondingly less developed than in gorillas.

Tooth types and their function Incisors: slicing food.
Canines: tearing food.
Premolars and molars: grinding food.
Figure B4.2.5: Comparing the dentition of gorillas and humans A side-by-side comparison of gorilla and human skulls/jaws. The gorilla skull shows large, prominent canines and incisors (similar to chimpanzees), large premolars and molars for grinding tough vegetation, and a sagittal crest. The human skull shows relatively small, narrow, vertical incisors, short and blunt canines almost level with other teeth, small molars (with wisdom teeth sometimes impacted due to jaw shortening), and flatter premolars/molars with low, rounded cusps, along with a larger cranial capacity and more V-shaped jaw than the gorilla.

Humans and chimpanzees shared a common ancestor roughly 4 million years ago; one evolutionary branch led to modern humans, and a side branch led to the australopithecines, now extinct. Paranthropus robustus had a gorilla-like skull, including a sagittal crest, reflecting a diet of tough vegetation. Its species name refers to its robust skull (large teeth, strongly built jaws), in contrast to the lighter, more slender (“gracile”) skull of humans. P. robustus had megadont (“large teeth”) cheek teeth with thick enamel, and large molars and premolars for powerful chewing, suited to tough vegetation.

Around 7 million years ago, human ancestors had jaws and teeth similar to modern chimpanzees: relatively large incisors that projected outward, and very long, pointed canines (larger in males, linked to male–male fighting for access to females), both used to grasp and bite food. In modern humans, by contrast, incisors are small, narrow and vertical; canines are short and blunt, almost level with other teeth; molars are small, with wisdom teeth sometimes impacted due to a shortened jaw; and premolars/molars are relatively flat with low, rounded cusps. Evolution towards a larger cranial capacity, smaller teeth and a more V-shaped jaw in humans reflects a shift away from tough vegetation towards a more omnivorous diet.

Homo floresiensis was a very small hominid (just over a metre tall, with a very small brain), thought to be a descendant of Homo erectus that underwent island dwarfism (isolated species lacking predators and with limited resources evolving to become smaller). Remains were found in Liang Bua cave, Flores, Indonesia (2003); tooth wear suggests a tough, fibrous (plant-based) diet requiring powerful chewing, alongside some evidence of meat eating, with large premolars and a robust mandible similar to australopithecines, but reduced molar size and a cranium more similar to later Homo species, suggesting a reduction in forceful biting.

Adaptations of Herbivores for Feeding on Plants, and of Plants for Resisting Herbivory

Key term Herbivory: feeding on plants.

Herbivore feeding adaptations

Aphids have modified piercing mouthparts called stylets, which secrete the enzyme pectinase to digest pectin (the polysaccharide that sticks plant cell walls together), allowing the stylet to slide between cell walls into the phloem and access sucrose.

Figure B4.2.6: A black aphid feeding by stylet A scanning electron micrograph of a black aphid with its needle-like stylet mouthpart inserted into a leaf to reach the phloem.

Other insects (grasshoppers, locusts, cockroaches, most wasps, beetles, termites, and caterpillars — the larval form of moths and butterflies) have chewing mouthparts for biting, removing and masticating leaf sections. Some animals have adaptations for detoxifying plant toxins: the proboscis monkey (Nasalis larvatus, forests of northern Borneo) has bacteria in its extended intestines that neutralize leaf toxins and digest cellulose cell walls.

Figure B4.2.7: Proboscis monkey feeding on leaves A photograph of a proboscis monkey (with its characteristic large nose) eating leaves in a Bornean rainforest.

Plant adaptations to resist herbivory

Cacti use spines to prevent predators accessing their water stores. Stinging nettles (Urtica dioica) have long, thin, hollow hairs over the stem and leaf undersides, containing methanoic (formic) acid and histamine, causing a painful stinging/burning sensation when touched.

Figure B4.2.8: Stinging hairs on a nettle stem A close-up photograph of fine, needle-like hollow hairs covering the stem of a stinging nettle.

Other plants produce toxic secondary compounds in seeds and leaves:

Toxic plant defences Deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna): contains atropine and scopolamine in leaves, stems, berries and roots, causing paralysis of involuntary muscles (including the heart) by competitively blocking acetylcholine (a neurotransmitter) at its receptors.
Oleander (Nerium oleander): contains toxic cardiac glycosides (oleandrin and nerine); ingestion can cause diarrhoea, vomiting, an erratic pulse, seizures, coma and death.
Cassava (Manihot esculenta) roots: produce precursors of hydrogen cyanide, converted to cyanide on ingestion, which blocks the respiratory (metabolic) pathway and can kill. Cassava is widely eaten by humans (native to South America; also grown in Nigeria, Thailand and Indonesia) but must first be detoxified by cutting into small pieces, soaking and boiling before further preparation.

Adaptations of Predators for Finding, Catching and Killing Prey, and of Prey for Resisting Predation

Key terms Predation: a biological interaction where one organism, the predator, kills and eats another, its prey.
Predator: an organism that catches and kills other animals to eat.
Prey: an organism hunted and eaten by a predator.

Physical adaptations

Eye position: predators tend to have eyes at the front of the head, giving binocular vision and better depth perception for judging distance to prey (e.g. the barn owl); prey tend to have eyes on the sides of the head, giving a wide field of view to detect predators approaching from any direction (e.g. red deer).

Figure B4.2.10: Predator vs prey eye position a) A barn owl in flight, with both eyes positioned at the front of the head for binocular vision. b) A red deer, with eyes on the sides of the head giving a wide field of view to detect approaching predators.

Specialized sense organs: predatory snakes (e.g. rattlesnakes) use their tongue to collect chemicals from prey and a specialized Jacobson’s organ in the mouth to detect them; birds of prey (e.g. peregrine falcon) have very acute vision; nocturnal bats (e.g. pipistrelle) use echolocation, detecting reflected sound with large, sensitive ears.

Speed: benefits both predators (catching prey) and prey (escaping). The peregrine falcon, the fastest diving bird known, has been recorded exceeding 380 km/h, reaching prey before it can respond.

Figure B4.2.11: A peregrine falcon in flight A photograph of the falcon in a fast, streamlined diving posture.

Mechanical defences: hard shells (tortoises, turtles) or spines (hedgehogs) physically prevent predators from eating prey, or cause pain, discouraging attack.

Figure B4.2.12: A European hedgehog A photograph of a hedgehog with its dense coat of sharp spines covering its back.

Camouflage: an organism evolves to resemble its background, reducing visibility to predators, e.g. chameleons (family Chamaeleonidae, found in Africa, Madagascar, southern Europe and southern Asia).

Figure B4.2.13: Fischer’s chameleon, Tanzania A photograph of a chameleon whose skin colouring closely matches its leafy background.

Mimicry: coral snakes (highly venomous, with the second-strongest venom of any snake after the black mamba) have warning black, red and white stripes. Non-venomous king snakes mimic this colour pattern, tricking predators into avoiding them without the metabolic cost of producing venom.

Figure B4.2.14: Mimicry — coral snake and king snake a) A coral snake with black, red and white banded colouration, signalling venomous toxicity. b) A California mountain king snake, non-venomous but closely mimicking the coral snake’s colour pattern.

Chemical adaptations

Toxicity and aposematic (warning) colouration: the cinnabar moth caterpillar has distinctive black and yellow stripes, a colour combination widely used in the animal kingdom to signal toxicity; it absorbs toxins from the ragwort it feeds on, so predators that try to eat it learn to avoid the pattern in future (aposematic colouration). Poison dart frogs (Central/South American rainforests) secrete skin poison that can paralyze or kill predators, obtained from the ants and termites they eat, advertised by bright warning colouration.

Figures B4.2.15 & B4.2.16: Aposematic colouration examples Figure B4.2.15 shows black-and-yellow striped cinnabar moth caterpillars feeding on yellow ragwort flowers. Figure B4.2.16 shows a bright red-striped poison dart frog, its vivid colouration warning predators of its toxicity.

Chemical defences: bombardier beetles (Brachinus) eject a hot, noxious spray from the abdomen tip, produced by a reaction between hydroquinone and hydrogen peroxide stored in two separate abdominal chambers. Skunks spray a pungent liquid containing volatile sulfurous chemicals when threatened, which sticks to a predator’s fur/skin and deters future approach; before spraying, a skunk first faces away, arches its back, raises its tail and stamps its feet while hissing, and its bold black-and-white stripes act as a warning — illustrating how behavioural, chemical and physical/visual adaptations reinforce one another.

Figure B4.2.17: A bombardier beetle A photograph of the beetle, with its abdomen tip raised, positioned to spray its defensive chemical mixture.

Behavioural adaptations

Stealth predators such as the Sumatran pit viper remain motionless, camouflaged, waiting for prey to come within striking range rather than actively chasing it. Many animals (e.g. woodlice, millipedes) roll into a tight ball when threatened, a behaviour that only works in combination with a tough protective exoskeleton (illustrating that adaptations often combine behavioural and physical elements). Not all predators are animals: some plants are carnivorous, e.g. the Venus fly trap and pitcher plants.

Figures B4.2.18 & B4.2.19: Behavioural adaptations Figure B4.2.18 shows a Sumatran pit viper coiled and motionless, camouflaged against its background, waiting to ambush prey. Figure B4.2.19 shows a pill millipede rolled into a tight defensive ball, exposing only its hard armoured exterior.

Adaptations of Plant Form for Harvesting Light

In all ecosystems, resources are limited and competition results; competition is especially severe in species-rich tropical rainforests, where light is one of the main limiting factors. The forest is divided into strata: emergent trees rise above the canopy; canopy trees form a dense layer high above the ground; several metres below is the understory of smaller plants; and at the bottom is the ground layer.

Canopy trees have a competitive advantage, gaining first access to sunlight (all frequencies), maximizing photosynthesis and allowing large size. The understory receives less light, and the ground layer receives only around 1% of the light reaching the canopy.

Strategies for reaching light without a tall trunk Lianas: woody vines with leaves and flowers in the canopy but roots on the ground; rather than building a strong trunk, they use the support of trees to climb towards sunlight, beginning life on the forest floor and sending shoots upward.
Epiphytes: plants (e.g. bird’s nest fern, bromeliads) that grow on tree branches, their seeds deposited there by resting birds or mammals; they gain the light/warmth advantages of canopy height without the cost of growing a long stem, and their water-trapping structure creates microhabitats for animals such as frogs.
Strangler epiphytes (e.g. strangler fig): send roots down to the forest floor to draw up nutrients and water, eventually forming a thick network of stems/roots around the host tree that kills it, giving the strangler exclusive access to the resources the host previously used.
Figures B4.2.20 & B4.2.21: Epiphytes and strangler figs Figure B4.2.20 shows a bromeliad growing on a branch high in the rainforest canopy. Figure B4.2.21 shows a strangler fig in Queensland, Australia, its network of aerial roots and stems enveloping the trunk of its (now dead or dying) host tree.

Shade-tolerant shrubs and herbs on the forest floor receive only a small, filtered proportion of canopy light (the canopy absorbs red and blue wavelengths, leaving mostly green light at ground level), so understory plants often contain different photosynthetic pigments, giving them different leaf colours (e.g. red rather than green), and often have much larger leaves to capture more of the limited light. Few plants flower in the low-light understory, since flowers are hard for pollinators to see; those that do flower tend to have very brightly coloured and/or strongly scented flowers to attract pollinators despite the poor light.

Fundamental and Realized Niches

Early ecologists Joseph Grinnell and Charles Elton defined niche simply as the “job” a species carries out in the environment — a concept that allows for “vacant” niches, roles not currently filled but that could be. In the 1950s, zoologist G. Evelyn Hutchinson redefined niche to include the interactions between organism and environment; in this species-centred view, there can be no truly “empty” niches, since niche is defined by the interaction between the species and both its abiotic and biotic environment.

Hutchinson recognized a difference between the area a species could theoretically occupy and the area it actually occupies. The abiotic environment sets physical limits, via the species’ range of tolerance to different environmental factors, but in practice dispersal ability and interactions with other species (competition, predation, mutualism, etc., operating strongly at smaller geographic scales, while abiotic factors like climate dominate at larger scales) further restrict where a species actually lives.

Key terms Fundamental niche: the potential extent of a species, based on its adaptations and tolerance limits — where an organism could live.
Realized niche: the actual extent of a species when in competition with other species — where an organism does live.
Figure B4.2.23: The fundamental and realized niche A two-dimensional graph plotting two environmental variables (e.g. temperature on one axis, salinity on the other). A large outer region represents the fundamental niche — the full range of conditions the species could tolerate in the absence of competitors. A smaller region nested within it represents the realized niche — the reduced range the species actually occupies once interactions with other species (e.g. competition) are taken into account.

Competitive Exclusion and the Uniqueness of Ecological Niches

When resources are limiting, populations compete to survive. Intraspecific competition occurs within a species; interspecific competition occurs between different species, and exists whenever the niches of different species overlap — the greater the niche overlap, the greater the degree of interspecific competition.

Figure B4.2.24: Niche overlap and interspecific competition A graph plotting the proportion of individuals of species A, B and C against body size. The distributions (niches) of species A and species B overlap substantially, indicating strong interspecific competition between them; species C’s distribution barely overlaps with either, indicating little competition with A or B.

If two species share the same resource in the same place at the same time, the dominant (superior) competitor will outcompete the other; the inferior competitor either dies out locally or moves away to avoid competition — this is competitive exclusion. The principle was first proposed by Russian biologist G. F. Gause in 1934, based on laboratory culture experiments with Paramecium protozoa.

Figure B4.2.25: Gause’s Paramecium competitive exclusion experiment Population growth curves (numbers of Paramecium per 0.5 cm³ of culture solution, plotted against time in days) for P. caudatum (a relatively large, slow-growing species) and P. aurelia (a smaller, fast-growing species). When cultured separately, both species grow well and reach a stable population size. When cultured together in a mixed culture (sharing the same food source, plankton, via a feeding current generated by oral-groove cilia leading to the gullet/cytopharynx, where food vacuoles form, receive digestive enzymes in acid then alkaline phases, and release digested products into the cytoplasm, with waste later expelled), P. caudatum is competitively excluded and its population declines to extinction, while the superior competitor P. aurelia persists.

Competitive exclusion may be the evolutionary pressure that drives closely related species living together in the same habitat to evolve clearly separate niches over time — for example, it likely accounts for the distinct feeding niches that have evolved between the cormorant and the shag.
