Natural Selection as the Mechanism Driving Evolutionary Change

Evolution is the development of life from its earliest beginnings to the diversity of organisms known today, both living and extinct. Evidence for evolution comes from several sources: fossils, base sequences in DNA or RNA, amino acid sequences in proteins, homologous structures, and the selective breeding of domesticated animals and crop plants.

Natural selectionThe process where organisms better adapted to their environment survive and produce more offspring than competitors; the mechanism through which evolution occurs.

Natural selection operates continuously and over billions of years, producing the biodiversity of life on Earth, but it can also happen over much more rapid timescales, for example the evolution of viruses and antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

The theory of evolution by natural selection was developed by Charles Darwin (1809–82), a careful observer and naturalist. After attempting to train as a doctor and then a clergyman, Darwin became the unpaid naturalist on a five-year Admiralty expedition to the southern hemisphere aboard HMS Beagle. During his lifetime the age of the Earth was increasingly recognized as millions, not thousands, of years old. His observations of living and fossilized species made him question the idea that species were fixed and unchanging. Darwin developed the idea of evolution by natural selection during and after this voyage, recording early ideas in private notebooks.

Darwin remained anxious about how the idea of evolution would be received and delayed publishing until he received a letter from another biologist and traveller, Alfred Russel Wallace, who had independently arrived at the same theory while working in South East Asia. Nature of science — paradigm shiftsA paradigm shift is a significant, fundamental change in how the world is viewed, occurring when scientific research contradicts previously held assumptions. Darwin’s (and Wallace’s) theory of natural selection replaced the previously accepted explanations for the origin of species and is a key example of a paradigm shift in science. A new theory replaces an old one when it is better supported by data and gives a better explanation of phenomena — for example, because it provides a more useful framework for problem-solving (the ‘pragmatic’ theory of truth), fits more consistently with other accepted theories (the ‘coherence’ theory of truth), or more accurately reflects an independent external reality (the ‘correspondence’ theory of truth). Calling evolution by natural selection a scientific ‘theory’ reflects the highly grounded, well-evidenced nature of the idea, not that it is mere speculation.

Roles of Mutation and Sexual Reproduction in Generating Variation

Natural selection can only occur if there is variation among members of the same species. Modern genetics has shown that genetic variation arises in two main ways.

Variation from sexual reproduction

Genetic variation arises during both meiosis (gamete formation) and fertilization, through:

  • random assortment of paternal and maternal homologous chromosomes during meiosis;
  • crossing over of segments of individual maternal and paternal homologous chromosomes, producing new combinations of genes on the chromosomes of the haploid gametes;
  • random fusion of male and female gametes at fertilization.

These processes occur every time meiosis and fertilization take place, producing new combinations of existing characteristics. These new combinations may favour some individuals during their lifetime, affecting their survival and their opportunities to reproduce; if so, that individual’s reproductive success passes on certain alleles to the next generation in greater proportions than other alleles.

Variation from mutation

Mutations are changes in DNA and give rise to entirely new alleles. Mutations occurring in the ovaries, testes (or anthers/embryo sacs of flowering plants) are called germ line mutations; because they occur in cells that give rise to gametes, they may be passed to offspring. Mutations occurring in body cells of multicellular organisms are somatic mutations; these are only passed to the immediate descendants of those cells and disappear when the organism dies. Characteristics acquired during an individual’s lifetime are therefore not heritable through mutation of body cells.

Common mistakeWhen explaining the role of sexual reproduction in evolution, it is not enough to state that it ‘produces variation’ — how the variation arises must also be explained (random assortment, crossing over, random fusion of gametes). The role of mutation should not be brought into an explanation of sexual reproduction, because mutation is not part of the process of sexual reproduction.

In a stable population, on average a breeding pair gives rise to only a single breeding pair of offspring — the rest are casualties of the ‘struggle’, and many organisms die before they can reproduce. Population size is naturally limited by environmental factors such as space, light and the availability of food; these factors set the carrying capacity of a species’ population. Because the environment can only support a certain number of organisms, the number of individuals in a species remains more or less constant over time. Overproduction of offspring and the resulting competition for resources are therefore factors that promote natural selection.

Abiotic Factors as Selection Pressures

Abiotic factors are the non-living components of an ecosystem. They can act as density-independent factors affecting the survival of individuals in a population and causing population numbers to fluctuate; an example of a density-independent factor is high or low temperature.

Common mistakeIt is incorrect to describe species as ‘adapting by developing new characteristics according to the environment’. Instead, different environments impose different selective pressures on isolated populations of the same species, leading to different adaptations arising through natural selection acting on existing heritable variation.

Differences Between Individuals as the Basis for Natural Selection

When genetic variation arises, favourable characteristics are expressed in the phenotypes of some offspring; these offspring may be better able to survive and reproduce in a particular environment, while others are less able to compete successfully. Natural selection therefore operates to determine which individuals survive and which genes are perpetuated in future generations, resulting in offspring with favourable characteristics. This process is sometimes summarized (loosely) as ‘survival of the fittest’, although these were not Darwin’s own original words.

FitnessA term referring to the survival value and reproductive potential of a genotype — an organism’s ability to pass its genetic material to its offspring, i.e. its reproductive success. ‘Fittest’ is understood in a particular environmental context, for example the fittest wildebeest may be those with the most acute senses, quickest reflexes and strongest leg muscles for escaping predators.
Intraspecific vs. interspecific competitionNatural selection results mainly from intraspecific competition — competition between individuals of the same species — rather than interspecific competition (competition between individuals of different species). Individuals usually interact with more members of their own species than of other species, and individuals of the same species share the same niche, competing for exactly the same resources and experiencing the same biotic and abiotic interactions. Any mutation giving a selective advantage improves an individual’s chance of surviving intraspecific competition.
Common mistakeRefer to ‘competition between individuals within a species’, not ‘competition between species’. Refer to fitter individuals within a population surviving and passing on adaptive alleles, not ‘survival of the fittest species’.

When the environment changes, some individuals may be at a disadvantage. Natural selection then operates and causes changes to gene pools: individuals possessing a particular allele, or combination of alleles, may be more likely to survive, breed and pass on their alleles than other, less well-adapted individuals — a process also called differential mortality.

Requirement that traits are heritable for evolutionary change to occur

Genes are the heritable unit through which evolution occurs. Mutation and the remixing of alleles during sexual reproduction produce new arrangements of the genetic code that are passed down through generations. Characteristics acquired during an individual’s life due to environmental factors are not encoded in the base sequence of genes, so they are not heritable.

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck put forward an earlier explanation for change in species over time, proposing that physical changes occurring in an individual during its lifetime could be inherited by its offspring (the inheritance of acquired traits). This theory influenced evolutionary thought during the early nineteenth century but was subsequently replaced by Darwin’s theory of evolution by natural selection, which is compatible with modern genetics.

Nature of science — falsificationAll scientific theories are tentative and can be falsified. The evidence Darwin gathered on the Galápagos Islands falsified Lamarck’s theory of the inheritance of acquired characteristics. Unlike Lamarck’s theory, the theory of evolution by natural selection allows prediction and explanation, which is why the scientific community regards it as the best and fullest explanation of the available evidence.

Higher Level In mammals, not all imprinted genes are stripped of their epigenetic tags between generations, so some changes caused by environmental factors can still be heritable even though they do not alter the base sequence of genes.

Natural selection in action: the peppered moth (Biston betularia)

During the Industrial Revolution in Britain, air pollution (sulfur dioxide gas and soot) blackened lichen- and moss-covered brickwork and tree trunks in industrial areas. The proportion of dark (melanic) forms of some 80 species of moth increased in these habitats — a phenomenon called industrial melanism. The dark form of the peppered moth followed this trend, becoming common in industrialized areas but remaining rare in unpolluted countryside, where the pale, speckled form was far more common. In sooty areas the melanic form was effectively camouflaged from predation by insectivorous birds and so became the dominant form.

In both data sets, the melanic form predominates in the polluted habitat and the pale form predominates in the unpolluted habitat, providing evidence of selective predation acting against whichever form is less well camouflaged in a given habitat.

Sexual Selection as a Selection Pressure in Animal Species

Sexual dimorphismDifferences in appearance between males and females of the same species, such as in colour, shape, size and structure.
Sexual selectionSelection arising through preference by one sex for certain characteristics in individuals of the other sex.

Birds of paradise, found in the rainforests of Papua New Guinea, show how alleles that confer an advantage in phenotype can lead to survival and the passing on of adaptive alleles. Differences in physical appearance and courtship display have led to the evolution of numerous species within the same forest: males have bright, colourful feathers used to attract females, and different species have different dancing displays. Such differences can act as an isolating mechanism (males and females of diverging populations no longer attracted to each other) and can also result in sexual dimorphism.

Figure D4.1.4: A male Raggiana bird of paradise displaying to a femaleThe photograph shows a brightly coloured male Raggiana bird of paradise (Paradisaea raggiana) performing a courtship display, with an unshowy, plain female nearby — illustrating sexual dimorphism between the sexes of this species.

In most bird of paradise species, males are brightly coloured and perform elaborate courtship displays, while females have plain grey and brown plumage. This difference is the result of sexual selection, producing physical and behavioural traits (usually in the male) that signal overall fitness; females choose the most impressive and attractive males based on their displays. Bright feathers and energetic dances signal fitness, so males with the most impressive plumage and display are more likely to attract a mate; over time, the genes for these traits become more prominent in the species.

Top tipNatural selection results from competition for resources; sexual selection results from competition for mates. Sexual selection enhances mating success, but does not necessarily adapt individuals to their environment — unlike natural selection.

Darwin regarded sexual selection as his ‘second great insight’, defining it as depending on ‘the advantage which certain individuals have over other individuals of the same sex and species solely in respect of reproduction’. He distinguished it from natural selection by noting that sexual selection ‘depends not on the struggle for existence, but on the struggle between males for possession of females’.

Peacocks (the males of peafowl species) have bright, iridescent tail feathers with eye-spot patterning, presented during courtship displays to females (peahens). Sexual selection has caused these tail feathers to become increasingly long and elaborately patterned over time, even though the tail reduces manoeuvrability and powers of flight, making the bird more conspicuous and vulnerable to predation.

Figure D4.1.5: Peacock (Pavo cristatus) displaying to a femaleA photograph of a male peacock with its long, iridescent, eye-spot-patterned tail feathers fanned out in a courtship display towards a female (peahen).
Natural selection vs. sexual selectionNatural selection produces individuals well adapted to their environment. Sexual selection does not necessarily do this — it enhances the chance of breeding success, even where the resulting trait (such as the peacock’s tail) reduces survival chances. Because variation in mating success can amplify selection and maintain new genetic variation, sexual selection can act as a stronger evolutionary force than natural selection, leading to rapid evolutionary change.
Testing sexual selection: Møller’s barn swallow experimentIn the barn swallow (Hirundo rustica), the outermost tail feather is about 16% longer in the male than the female. Researcher Anders Møller tested whether females prefer males with longer tails by experimentally shortening the tails of some males (cutting off feathers with scissors) and lengthening the tails of others (gluing on the cut feathers with fast-drying superglue). He then measured how long it took different males to find a mate. Males with experimentally lengthened tails mated faster than those with shortened tails, showing higher reproductive success — direct evidence that females prefer longer-tailed males.

Modelling of Sexual and Natural Selection Based on Experimental Control of Selection Pressures

Guppies (Poecilia reticulata), native to mountain forest streams of north-east Venezuela, Margarita, Trinidad and Tobago, show complex variation in colouration and patterning and sexual dimorphism (brightly coloured males, drab females).

Figure D4.1.6: Guppies (Poecilia reticulata)A photograph showing several guppies with a wide variety of colours and spot patterns on their bodies and fins.

Scientist John Endler found in field work that guppy colour patterning varied with predation pressure, and designed lab and field experiments to investigate the effects of natural and sexual selection on guppy evolution. Guppies derive much of their camouflage from spots that resemble the gravel of their native streams; some streams have coarser, pebbly gravel and others finer, sandier gravel.

Three hypotheses were tested: (1) substrate type affects survival and so affects spot brightness when predators are present; (2) spot brightness increases when predation is low or absent, due to sexual selection; (3) high spot brightness attracts predators, so brightness decreases as predation increases.

Endler’s laboratory experiment
  1. Ten ponds were set up in a greenhouse resembling the guppies’ tropical environment: five with coarse gravel and five with fine gravel.
  2. Guppies were randomly assigned to the ponds and allowed to breed for six months with no predation.
  3. Three predation levels were then introduced: two ponds (one coarse, one fine gravel) had no predation; four ponds (two coarse, two fine) received the dangerous predator, the pike cichlid (Crenicichla alta); four ponds (two coarse, two fine) received the weak predator, the killifish (Rivulus hartii). The unequal numbers of dangerous versus weak predators reflected predator densities in the wild.
  4. After five months (and again after a further nine months), spots on all guppies were counted and measured.
Results: before the experiment, guppies had shown a wide range of spot size and number. During the six predator-free months, spot number increased, presumably through selection by females. Once predators were introduced, mean spot number decreased in ponds with the dangerous predator, while it continued to increase in ponds with no or weak predation. With either weak or strong predation, coarse gravel favoured larger spots and fine gravel favoured smaller spots (spot size mimicking gravel size); with no predation the reverse occurred — fine gravel favoured large spots and coarse gravel favoured small spots, since being more conspicuous against the background helped attract females when predation risk was absent.
Endler’s field experimentEndler transferred dull-coloured male guppies from a stream with a dangerous predator (Crenicichla alta) to a stream with only a weak predator (Rivulus hartii), and left them for 15 guppy generations (two years). On returning, males in the stream with the weak predator had evolved more colourful patterning, attributed to sexual selection acting in the absence of strong predation.
Figure D4.1.7: Guppy experimental data — spots per fishBar charts show mean numbers of black, red, yellow, blue and iridescent spots, and total spots, per fish after 15 generations, for guppies from ponds with no predation (K), weak predation from the killifish Rivulus hartii (R), and strong predation from the pike cichlid Crenicichla alta (C). Shaded bars represent guppies from the greenhouse experiment and unshaded bars represent guppies from the field experiment; small vertical lines show two standard errors. In both data sets, spot numbers are generally highest with no predation, intermediate with weak predation, and lowest with strong predation, showing a trade-off between the reproductive advantage of bright colouration (via sexual selection) and its cost in increased visibility to predators (via natural selection).

Endler’s experiments show that guppy evolution is a dynamic balance of natural and sexual selection: natural selection favours guppies that avoid predation (less conspicuous colouration where predators are common), while sexual selection favours brighter, more ornamented males that are more likely to mate. In areas of high predation, brightly coloured males are less likely to survive regardless of any reproductive advantage.

Higher Level Concept of the Gene Pool

Population genetics is the study of genes in populations (breeding groups). The total of all the genes and their different alleles present in a population makes up a gene pool. A sample of the genes of the gene pool forms the genomes of individuals in the next generation, and so on from generation to generation.

Gene poolAll the genes (and their alleles) present in a breeding population.

When a breeding group is large and all individuals have an equal opportunity of contributing gametes, random mating maintains the original proportions of alleles in the population — allele frequency does not change. This can be demonstrated by setting two alleles of a gene, A and a, at arbitrary frequencies (0.8 and 0.2) in an infinitely large gene pool and following the genotype frequencies produced by random mating over one cycle.

Figure D4.1.8: The fate of genes in a gene poolA gene pool contains alleles A and a at frequencies 0.8 and 0.2. Both male and female gametes therefore carry A and a in the same proportions (0.8 A : 0.2 a). Random fertilization combines these gametes to give genotype frequencies among the offspring of 0.64 AA (0.8 × 0.8), 0.32 Aa (2 × 0.8 × 0.2, from the two ways of forming a heterozygote), and 0.04 aa (0.2 × 0.2). Adding the alleles contributed by each genotype back together (0.64 AA + half of 0.32 Aa = 0.8 A; half of 0.32 Aa + 0.04 aa = 0.2 a) shows that the allele frequencies in the new generation are unchanged from the previous generation (still 0.8 A : 0.2 a). The diagram demonstrates that, in the absence of any ‘disturbing factors’, allele frequencies never change however many mating cycles occur — when there is no change in a gene pool, the population is not evolving.

Higher Level Allele frequencies of geographically isolated populations

The composition of a gene pool can change due to a range of ‘disturbing factors’ that alter the proportions of some alleles, so that allele frequencies change from generation to generation. For example, some alleles may increase in frequency because of the advantage they give to individuals that carry them, allowing those individuals to produce more offspring. Detecting a change in the proportions of particular alleles in a gene pool can therefore reveal evolution occurring before a new species is observed. Allele frequencies are likely to differ substantially between geographically isolated populations and their original source population, owing to natural selection, mutation and random genetic drift acting independently in each isolated group.

Genetic databases allow researchers to browse, share and compare allele frequency data. The NCBI Short Genetic Variation database (dbSNP) catalogues short variations (including single-nucleotide polymorphisms, SNPs) in human nucleotide sequences; each variant is identified by a unique reference SNP cluster ID (rsID). Frequency data for a given rsID are listed for major studies, broken down by subpopulation where available, using the same 0–1 notation as the Hardy–Weinberg equation (for example, a global allele frequency of 0.983727 for one allele and 0.016273 for an alternative allele at the same locus). The Allele Frequency Net database similarly stores frequencies for immune-system genes such as the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) system, broken down by geographic population.

Top tipNeo-Darwinism is a modified version of Darwinism that explains the origin of species on a genetic basis. Darwinism describes favourable, inheritable phenotypic variations as the driving force of speciation, whereas neo-Darwinism states that it is specifically inheritable genetic variations that drive speciation.

Higher Level Changes in Allele Frequency as a Consequence of Natural Selection

Darwin developed his theory without any knowledge of DNA or Mendelian genetics, yet it still explains the evolution of life on Earth. Since Darwin’s time, scientists have integrated genetics with natural selection — an approach known as neo-Darwinism — allowing evolution to be explained at the molecular level: changes in allele frequency in the gene pool are a consequence of natural selection between individuals according to differences in their heritable traits. Evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins has written influentially about this gene-centred view of evolution in works such as The Selfish Gene and The Blind Watchmaker.

Higher Level Differences between directional, disruptive and stabilizing selection

Natural selection changes the composition of gene pools in different ways depending on which phenotypes are favoured. Three modes of selection are recognized.

Figure D4.1.10: Three modes of selection operating on phenotypic variationThree pairs of ‘before selection’ and ‘after selection’ graphs (numbers of individuals plotted against degree of variance of a trait) illustrate directional, stabilizing and disruptive selection. In directional selection, the whole distribution shifts towards one extreme after selection. In stabilizing selection, the spread of the distribution narrows around the existing average after selection, as both extremes are selected against. In disruptive selection, the middle of the distribution is reduced after selection, leaving two peaks at the extremes. Directional selection is illustrated by multiple antibiotic resistance in bacteria; stabilizing selection is illustrated by human birth weight and infant mortality; disruptive selection is illustrated by feather colour in young male lazuli buntings.
Directional, stabilizing and disruptive selectionDirectional selection occurs when changing environmental conditions make the majority phenotype less well suited to the environment, so an alternative phenotype gains a selective advantage and the population shifts towards it over time — e.g. the spread of antibiotic resistance in bacteria. Stabilizing selection occurs when there is an increase in frequency of the average phenotype: it maintains a favourable characteristic and eliminates harmful variants and abnormalities at both extremes — e.g. human birth weight, where infant mortality is higher at both very low and very high birth weights. Disruptive selection occurs when environmental conditions favour phenotypes at both extremes of a range over the intermediate phenotype, which is at a fitness disadvantage — e.g. feather colour in yearling male lazuli buntings.

Stabilizing selection in detail: data on human birth records from a London hospital, 1935–46, show that infant mortality was higher for babies born much lighter or much heavier than an optimum, intermediate birth weight; babies of intermediate weight had the lowest mortality. Birth weight is influenced both by environmental factors (e.g. maternal nutrition, smoking) and by inheritance (about 50%), so higher mortality at both extremes of birth weight tends to eliminate the genes associated with very low and very high birth weight from the gene pool.

Figure D4.1.11: Birth weight and infant mortality, a case of stabilizing selectionA histogram shows the percentage of a population of London infants (born 1935–46) at each birth weight, forming a roughly bell-shaped (continuous variation) distribution; a broken line superimposed on the same axes shows mortality rate (%) at each birth weight, which is high at very low and very high birth weights and lowest at an intermediate, ‘optimum’ birth weight. The central tendency of the continuous birth-weight data can be expressed as the mode (the most frequent value), the median (the middle value when data are ranked in order) or the mean (the sum of values divided by the number of values).

Directional selection in detail: in a large bacterial population, some individuals may carry a gene for resistance to a particular antibiotic. A resistant bacterium has no selective advantage in the absence of the antibiotic and must compete for resources with non-resistant bacteria. When the antibiotic is present, however, most non-resistant bacteria are killed, and the resistant bacteria that remain create the future population — all now carrying the resistance gene. The genome of the population has been changed abruptly by directional selection.

Disruptive selection in detail: in yearling (one year old or less) male lazuli buntings (Passerina amoena), a North American bird, feather colour ranges continuously from dull brown to bright blue (as bright as fully mature adult males), because full adult colouration takes a year or two to develop. A 2008 study scored colouration on a scale from 14 (extremely dull) to 36 (extremely bright).

Figure D4.1.12: Colourful male lazuli bunting with brown femaleA photograph shows an adult male lazuli bunting with bright blue, orange and white plumage, alongside a female with plain brown plumage.

The dullest and brightest yearling males generally obtained better territories than yearlings with intermediate colouration, and males in better territories paired with females more often, producing a bimodal (two-peaked) pattern of pairing success. Adult males appeared to treat dull, brown-coloured yearlings as non-threatening (perhaps because they resemble females) and brightly coloured yearlings as too conspicuous a threat to challenge, while intermediate males were more often attacked by adult males and failed to establish good territories or attract mates — disruptive selection favouring the colour extremes.

Higher Level Hardy–Weinberg Equation and Calculations of Allele or Genotype Frequencies

The total of the alleles of the genes in the reproductive cells of a population’s individuals makes up its gene pool. When the gene pool of a population remains more or less unchanged, that population is not evolving; if the gene pool is changing, evolution may be occurring. Change or constancy in a gene pool can be detected using a mathematical formula called the Hardy–Weinberg equation.

p + q = 1

where p = frequency of the dominant allele and q = frequency of the recessive allele. Because it is not possible to distinguish homozygous dominant individuals from heterozygotes by phenotype alone, the Hardy–Weinberg equation extends this to genotype frequencies:

p² + 2pq + q² = 1

where p² = frequency of dominant homozygous individuals, 2pq = frequency of heterozygous individuals, and q² = frequency of homozygous recessive individuals (the three terms sum to the whole population).

Worked example: PTC tastingIn humans, the ability to taste the chemical phenylthiocarbamide (PTC) is conferred by the dominant allele T. Both homozygous dominants (TT) and heterozygotes (Tt) are ‘tasters’; the recessive homozygotes (tt) are ‘non-tasters’.

Because non-tasters are homozygous recessive, their frequency equals q²: q² = 0.35. Taking the square root, q = 0.59. So the frequency of the non-tasting allele (t) in this population was 0.59.

Top tipAllele frequency ranges from 0 to 1, and the frequencies of the two alleles of a gene must add up to 1 — for example, if the frequency of an allele for blue eyes is 0.25, the frequency of the allele for brown eyes must be 0.75. Remember p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1; if one genotype frequency is known, the allele frequencies (and hence the other genotype frequencies) can be calculated from these equations.

Higher Level Hardy–Weinberg conditions that must be maintained for genetic equilibrium

If the genotype frequencies observed in a population do not fit the Hardy–Weinberg equation, this indicates that one or more of the conditions required for genetic equilibrium is not being met — for example, mating may be non-random, or survival rates may vary between genotypes (i.e. natural selection is occurring). The Hardy–Weinberg model assumes: a large population size (so chance alone does not alter allele frequencies, i.e. no genetic drift); random mating with respect to the gene concerned; no mutation creating new alleles; no migration of individuals (and their alleles) into or out of the population; and no natural selection acting on the alleles concerned. When the gene pool of a population remains more or less unchanged and these conditions hold, the population is not evolving; if the gene pool changes, evolution may be occurring.

Higher Level Artificial Selection by Deliberate Choice of Traits

Artificial selection is carried out in crop plants and domesticated animals by deliberately choosing individuals with desirable traits for breeding.

Natural selection vs. artificial selectionIn natural selection, the environment (including other organisms) selects which individuals survive and reproduce, without deliberate human choice — for example, the evolution of antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an unintended consequence of human antibiotic use, so it is due to natural, not artificial, selection. In artificial selection, humans deliberately choose which individual organisms breed, based on desirable traits, to produce future generations with more of those traits.

Stability as a Property of Natural Ecosystems

Ecosystems are open systems supported by continual inputs of energy and matter. Sunlight, or energy from chemical reactions, allows producers to synthesize glucose, supporting the whole food chain; inputs of matter, such as organisms entering the ecosystem or inorganic material like carbon dioxide, provide the elements from which biological molecules (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins) are built. Given an ongoing supply of energy and matter, ecosystems are sustainable.

StabilityThe ability to maintain or support systems and processes continuously over time.

Some ecosystems on Earth have persisted for millions of years — for example, the rainforests of South East Asia are estimated to be at least 70 million years old. Steady conditions, with high rainfall and sunlight throughout the year combined with warm temperatures, have ensured the stability of these ecosystems, at least until the advent of humanity.

Requirements for Stability in Ecosystems

For an ecosystem to remain stable, it requires a continual supply of energy, nutrient recycling, a diversity of organisms, and a climate that stays within tolerance levels for the species living there. Energy input and the recycling of chemical elements within an ecosystem both contribute to its stability.

Genetic diversity refers to the range of genetic material present in a gene pool or population of a species (or, more broadly, across all species in an area). Ecosystems that are complex, supported by a large biomass of producers, provide an increased number of niches, which increases species and genetic diversity and results in greater stability. Ecosystems containing species with small population sizes tend to have low genetic diversity, leaving those species more prone to disease.

Biome distribution is determined by climatic variables — temperature, precipitation and insolation — that must remain within tolerance levels for the plant species found there; the type of stable ecosystem that emerges in an area is predictable from its climate. If conditions move outside tolerance levels, the survival of the ecosystem is threatened, as seen in the case of the Amazon rainforest.

Deforestation of the Amazon Rainforest as an Example of a Possible Tipping Point in Ecosystem Stability

Tipping pointA critical threshold when even a small change can have dramatic effects and cause a disproportionately large response in the overall system.

Tipping points occur when there is a dramatic change in ecological state away from stability, representing points beyond which irreversible change or damage occurs. Equilibrium refers to a state of balance among the components of a system. Positive feedback loops tend to amplify changes and drive a system towards a tipping point, where a new equilibrium is adopted.

Such changes are driven by human population growth and associated factors: resource consumption, habitat transformation and fragmentation, energy production and consumption, and climate change. Most projected tipping points are linked to climate change; increases in carbon dioxide concentration above a certain value (450 ppm) would raise global mean temperature and cause melting of ice sheets and permafrost.

Deforestation of the Amazon rainforest

The Amazon rainforest biome spans nine South American countries (Brazil, Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname and French Guiana), covers 6 million km², and contains half of the planet’s remaining tropical forest. Economic, ecological and climatic systems in Amazonia may be interacting in ways that could move the forest beyond a tipping point. Burning and deforestation to create grazing land or housing, to provide timber, and illegal mining, all cause loss of large areas of rainforest; remaining areas can become fragmented into habitat ‘islands’, and continued burning and the establishment of grassland prevents rainforest re-establishing.

A large area of rainforest is needed to generate atmospheric water vapour through continuous transpiration, with consequent cooling, air flows and rainfall; there is scientific uncertainty over the minimum forest area needed to sustain these processes. Climate change reinforces these effects by increasing air temperatures, dry season severity and the frequency of extreme weather events. One 2008 projection suggested that by 2030, 24% of the Amazon rainforest would be damaged by drought or logging and 31% of its closed-canopy forest would be deforested; more recent estimates (a 2022 WWF briefing) suggest 40% of the Amazon could be lost by 2050, with more frequent fires potentially releasing an additional 20% of forest carbon to the atmosphere — some 15–26 billion tonnes of carbon released in under 30 years as a result of large-scale forest dieback.

percentage change = (change in forest cover ÷ initial forest cover) × 100
where change in forest cover = final forest cover − initial forest cover. For example, estimated natural forest cover in the Brazilian Amazon was 3 399 308 km² in 2017 and 3 390 835 km² in 2018, giving a change of −8473 km², so the yearly percentage change = (−8473 ÷ 3 399 308) × 100 = −0.25% (a negative sign represents a loss). A tipping point may be reached at 20–25% deforestation.

MesocosmAn enclosed environment that allows a small part of a natural environment to be observed under controlled conditions.

The stability of an ecosystem may change when an external ‘disturbing’ factor disrupts the natural balance. This can be investigated in natural habitats or in mesocosms — enclosed, contained experimental areas in which variables other than the independent and dependent variable can be kept constant to ensure a fair test. Sealed glass vessels can be used, allowing light in and heat out while preventing entry or exit of matter; both aquatic and terrestrial systems can be modelled this way.

Role of Keystone Species in the Stability of Ecosystems

In architecture, a keystone is the central stone at the apex of an arch; the keystone supports the whole structure, and without it the arch collapses.

Figure D4.2.7: An arch from the Temple of Hadrian, Ephesus, TurkeyA photograph of a stone archway shows the wedge-shaped keystone positioned at the very top-centre of the arch, locking the surrounding stones in place.
Keystone speciesA species which has a disproportionately large effect on community structure relative to its abundance.

In communities, certain species are vital to the continuing function of the ecosystem; without them, the community undergoes a fundamental shift and effectively ‘collapses’. Just as an architectural keystone is not under the greatest structural pressure, a keystone species may make up only a small part of the ecosystem’s total biomass or productivity, yet still play a fundamental role in the community, so that removing it risks ecosystem collapse.

Figure D4.2.8: An agouti (Dasyprocta sp.) — a keystone speciesA photograph of an agouti, a large rodent of tropical Central and South America, shown on the forest floor where it forages for and buries seeds, including Brazil nuts.

The agouti of tropical South and Central America is a keystone species: it feeds on the nuts of the Brazil nut tree (Bertholletia excelsa), a hardwood found from eastern Peru and northern Bolivia across the Brazilian Amazon, with some specimens among the oldest and tallest (up to 50 m) trees in the Amazon. The agouti is the only animal with teeth strong enough to open the tree’s tough seed pods. It buries many nuts around the forest floor for times of scarcity, but does not recover all of them; the buried, uneaten nuts germinate into new trees. Without the agouti, the Brazil nut tree could not distribute its seeds and would eventually die out; without the tree, other dependent species (such as harpy eagles, which nest in it) would also be affected. Brazil nuts are also an important food and cosmetic-oil resource and a significant source of income for local communities.

Assessing Sustainability of Resource Harvesting from Natural Ecosystems

SustainabilityThe responsible maintenance of ecological systems so that there is no reduction of conditions for future generations, ensuring the long-term viability of a system.

Renewable resources continue to exist despite being consumed, or replenish themselves naturally over time even as they are used. Sustainability depends on the rate of harvesting being lower than the rate of replacement; if harvesting exceeds the rate of replenishment (for example, forests harvested faster than they can regrow), resource use is unsustainable.

Terrestrial plant species

Trees are a renewable resource provided they are replanted at the same rate as they are harvested. Finland promotes multi-functional forest management: over 70% of the country is forested, most of it (61%) privately owned, and management balances economic, social and environmental aspects. The Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) is one of Finland’s four main logged tree species. Sustainability of harvesting can be assessed by surveying soil disturbance and forest structure in logged versus unlogged sites; to achieve sustainable harvesting, the volume of replanting needs to exceed the volume of timber removed, prioritizing the long-term life of the forest over short-term profit. Because forests are an important carbon store, sustainable forestry requires that the volume of timber removed be replaced by new growth.

Figure D4.2.9: Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris) plantation and harvestA photograph shows rows of Scots pine trees in a managed plantation, alongside evidence of timber harvesting, illustrating a forestry system managed for sustained yield.

Marine fish

Fish are an important food source for many human populations, and most commercially exploited fish populations are marine species shared among several harvesting nations, making overfishing a global issue. Overfishing of Atlantic (North Sea) cod (Gadus morhua) led to a collapse of fish stocks.

Figure D4.2.10: Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua)A photograph of an Atlantic cod, a commercially important marine fish species whose stocks have historically collapsed under sustained overfishing pressure.

If fish populations are persistently overfished, stocks become rapidly depleted to the point where they collapse and can no longer support a commercial fishery. The extent to which a species should be fished ought to be based on population dynamics that consider its reproductive and growth rates; population size and age structure can inform sustainable fishing practices.

Maximum sustainable yield (MSY)The maximum average catch that a fish stock can sustain over a long period, corresponding to the optimum balance between the stock’s reproductive and growth rates and the losses due to harvesting and natural mortality. Harvesting at MSY typically requires much lower fishing rates than occur in many real fisheries.

A growing fish population has relatively more younger fish, while a declining population has relatively more older fish; such age-structure data, together with population size, can indicate whether current fishing practices are sustainable. Calculating MSY requires good knowledge of the relationship between stock size and the number of juveniles produced each year, which is difficult to establish because of natural year-to-year variation in juvenile production; it usually requires a long series of data (at least 20 years). Reliable methods exist to approximate MSY, but they depend on cooperation from the fishing community, since estimates rely on accurate measurement of age structure; operators who discard restricted fish at sea before returning to port create skewed data that can lead to miscalculation of stock size.

Factors Affecting the Sustainability of Agriculture

Humans have practised agriculture for around 10 000 years, and it now provides the majority of food for human societies. Various physical factors affect agriculture, including precipitation (type, frequency, intensity, amount), temperature (length of growing season, whether the ground freezes, temperature range), soil (fertility, nutrient status, structure, texture, depth), pests (vermin, locusts, disease), and location (slope gradient, relief, altitude, aspect). Environmental impacts — pollution, habitat loss, reduced biodiversity and soil erosion — determine whether agriculture is sustainable.

Soil erosionThe process that occurs when the impact of water or wind detaches and removes soil particles, causing the soil to deteriorate.

When forests or other ecosystems are cleared for agriculture, exposed soil is prone to erosion. Fertile soil can be considered a non-renewable resource because, once depleted, it can take a long time to restore — in some cases it may never recover, reducing the land available per person for food production.

Figure D4.2.11: Soil erosion on a farm after heavy rainA photograph shows rills and channels cut into bare, exposed farmland soil by the flow of rainwater, with sediment visibly carried away.

Exposed soil can also undergo leaching — the washing away of soluble mineral nutrients (e.g. nitrates, needed for amino acid synthesis) by rainwater, reducing plant growth and protein synthesis. Fertilizers such as nitrates and phosphates are added to soils to increase productivity, but over-application can lead to eutrophication.

AgrochemicalA chemical used in agriculture, such as a pesticide or a fertilizer.

Pesticides kill organisms that eat crops (e.g. insects) or compete with them (e.g. weeds). Targeted, biodegradable pesticides minimize environmental damage, but non-biodegradable pesticides can seriously damage food chains through biomagnification, in which toxins accumulate in the tissues of consumers at higher trophic levels.

Carbon footprintThe amount of carbon dioxide (and also methane) released into the atmosphere as a result of the activities of a particular individual, organization or community.

Transporting agricultural produce long distances uses fossil-fuel energy, adding carbon dioxide to the atmosphere and increasing a crop’s carbon footprint; demand for unseasonal foods (such as strawberries in Europe in winter) has increased global food transport and its associated carbon footprint. Growing and consuming crops locally and seasonally reduces the environmental impact of agriculture.

Eutrophication of Aquatic and Marine Ecosystems Due to Leaching

EutrophicationThe natural or artificial enrichment of a body of water, particularly with respect to nitrates and phosphates; there are both natural and human causes.

Natural causes of eutrophication include nutrients from decomposing biomass and run-off from surrounding land. Human causes include run-off of fertilizers or manure from agricultural land, domestic waste water containing phosphates from detergents, untreated sewage, and leaching of mineral nutrients from agricultural land into rivers (which can also reach and affect the sea).

Figure D4.2.12: Eutrophication in the Ijssel River, NetherlandsA photograph shows water coloured green by a dense algal bloom, illustrating the visible effect of nutrient enrichment in a river ecosystem.
The chain of events leading to eutrophication
  1. Nutrients (from leaching, run-off, fertilizer, sewage, etc.) enter the water body, increasing nutrient availability.
  2. Algal biomass increases rapidly, using the added nutrients (an algal bloom).
  3. Algal growth reduces light penetration, causing underwater (submerged) plants to die.
  4. Dead algae and plants add to dead organic matter (DOM), which bacteria feed on and decompose, and bacteria populations increase.
  5. Increased bacterial respiration raises biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), lowering the water’s dissolved oxygen content (hypoxia).
  6. Oxygen-dependent organisms, such as fish, die from lack of oxygen; the fish community becomes dominated by tolerant, surface-dwelling coarse fish (e.g. roach and rudd).

Further effects of eutrophication include: net primary productivity is usually higher than in unpolluted water, often shown by extensive algal or bacterial blooms; the diversity of primary producers decreases as dominant species change; food chain length decreases because algae lock up nutrients and block sunlight from reaching the riverbed; macrophytes (submerged aquatic plants) disappear as light penetration falls; early algal blooms give way to toxic cyanobacteria (blue-green algae); and fish populations decline as oxygen availability falls.

Common mistakeExplanations of anoxic water following eutrophication often omit the role of bacteria. It is the bacteria feeding on dead plant material that remove oxygen from the water, causing the low-oxygen conditions that then kill animals in the ecosystem — algae themselves do not directly deplete oxygen in this way.
Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)A measure of the amount of dissolved oxygen required to break down the organic material in a given volume of water through aerobic biological activity.

BOD rises in water polluted with organic matter, such as sewage, or nutrient-enriched by fertilizers, because aerobic organisms (e.g. bacteria) that feed on the pollutant increase in number and respire faster, using more oxygen. BOD is therefore an indirect method of assessing water pollution: it is affected by the number of aerobic organisms present and their rate of respiration.

Measuring BOD directly
  1. Take a water sample of measured volume.
  2. Measure the initial oxygen level with an oxygen probe.
  3. Place the sample in the dark at 20°C for five days (darkness prevents photosynthesis, which would otherwise release oxygen and give an artificially low BOD reading).
  4. After five days, remeasure the oxygen level.
  5. BOD is the difference between the two oxygen measurements.

Biomagnification of Pollutants in Natural Ecosystems

Non-biodegradable toxins such as DDT or mercury can affect food chains, especially top predators. Increased concentrations occur because consumers feed on many organisms and so accumulate the toxins present in all of them; organisms higher up a food chain live longer and so have more time to accumulate toxin, putting top carnivores especially at risk of poisoning.

Wild plants and animals have evolved alongside their predators and parasites and mostly live in balance with them; cultivated crops and herds, artificially selected for high productivity, typically have limited resistance to local parasites and predators, and monocultures are especially prone to attack. Pesticides control such harmful organisms but have caused environmental problems.

PesticideA chemical used to control organisms that are a danger to crops or herds.

Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) was a revolutionary insecticide, causing rapid death of insects even at low concentrations. DDT is fat-soluble and is selectively retained in fatty tissues rather than being excreted, because it is not recognized as a toxin by the body.

BioaccumulationThe build-up of non-biodegradable or slowly biodegradable chemicals in the body.
BiomagnificationThe process by which chemical substances become concentrated in the tissues of organisms at higher trophic levels.
Common mistakeDo not confuse bioaccumulation with biomagnification. Bioaccumulation is the build-up of toxin within the biomass (usually lipids) of a single organism. Biomagnification is the increasing concentration of toxin per gram of biomass as the chemical passes along successive stages of a food chain.

At each stage of a food chain, DDT becomes more concentrated, so that a top predator can accumulate several thousand times the concentration found in a primary producer. In vertebrates such as fish and birds, DDT concentrations sometimes reached toxic levels, with devastating consequences for top carnivores.

Figure D4.2.13: Biomagnification of DDTA pyramid of biomass for a food chain (producer/plankton → primary consumer/herbivorous invertebrate → secondary consumer/carnivorous fish → tertiary consumer/bird) shows typical DDT concentrations increasing at each successive trophic level: 0.0025 ppm in plankton, 1 ppm in herbivorous invertebrates, 5 ppm in carnivorous fish, and 124 ppm in the top predator bird. Accompanying text explains that run-off from agricultural land carried a very dilute solution of DDT into water, DDT entered the cells of phytoplankton, herbivorous invertebrates accumulated DDT by eating phytoplankton, carnivorous fish accumulated more DDT by eating many herbivores, and top predator birds accumulated the highest concentration by eating many fish.

Although DDT is not a nerve poison in birds and mammals, in breeding birds it inhibits deposition of calcium in the eggshell, causing affected birds to lay thin-shelled eggs that crack easily; this led to a rapid decline in numbers of birds of prey in areas where DDT had become widely used. When this harmful effect was discovered, DDT’s chemical ‘stability’ was renamed ‘persistence’, and once the wider effects of organochlorine pesticides on wildlife were recognized, developed countries banned it.

Effects of Microplastic and Macroplastic Pollution of the Oceans

MacroplasticRelatively large, easily visible plastic debris found especially in the marine environment (typically more than 5 mm in size), such as bottles, plastic bags, rubbish and other materials that have not degraded.
MicroplasticExtremely small pieces of plastic debris, less than 5 mm in size, resulting from the breakdown of consumer products and industrial waste.

Plastics are increasingly used in disposable consumer products and persist in the environment because they are non-biodegradable. The physical and chemical breakdown of macroplastic into microplastic creates additional problems.

Figure D4.2.15: Plastic garbage contamination of the sea and beachesA photograph shows a beach and coastal water littered with macroplastic debris, such as bottles and bags, washed up from the ocean.
Figure D4.2.16: Sources and fate of marine macroplastics and microplasticsA flow diagram shows plastic entering the ocean via rivers and wind, ships, and physical processes, as macroplastic (greater than 5 mm) and microplastic (less than 5 mm). Floating plastics encounter persistent organic pollutants (POPs, chemicals resistant to environmental degradation) at the nutrient- and contaminant-rich surface microlayer of the water and interact with algae there; plastics absorb and concentrate chemicals dissolved in seawater, and chemical additives leach from the plastic and equilibrate into the water. UV exposure degrades macroplastic into smaller microplastic particles with greater surface area for absorbing POPs; weathering, biofouling and sinking carry plastic and its associated chemicals down to sediments, forming a sink with low bioavailability to microbes, though wave action can re-suspend chemicals from sediment-bound plastic. Organisms take up POPs and plastic additives through ingestion of plastic and leaching of additives into their tissues, leading to bioaccumulation of POPs and transfer of contaminants along the food chain.

Macroplastics can be mistaken for food by marine predators, and evidence shows that degrading marine plastics release non-biodegradable contaminants that bioaccumulate in food chains, putting top predators at extra risk through biomagnification. Studies of Southern fur seals have found plastic particles from night-feeding myctophids (lanternfish) in their faeces (scats), since the seals eat the fish that had themselves ingested plastic.

Case studies

The Laysan albatross (Phoebastria immutabilis) ranges across the North Pacific, with 99.7% of the population breeding on the north-western Hawaiian Islands. On Midway Atoll, in the North Pacific gyre, large volumes of plastic wash ashore. Albatrosses catch food (squid and other seafood) by skimming the ocean surface with their beaks, and in doing so accidentally scoop up floating plastic, which they then feed to their chicks; unlike adults, chicks cannot regurgitate swallowed plastic, so it can fill their stomachs and potentially cause starvation.

Plastic garbage harms and kills an estimated 100 000 sea turtles and other marine animals each year. Sea turtles mistake floating plastic bags for jellyfish (a favourite food) and eat them; if the plastic becomes lodged in the mouth, the turtle may have trouble feeding, eventually leading to starvation.

Restoration of Natural Processes in Ecosystems by Rewilding

Rewilding aims to restore ecosystems and reverse declines in biodiversity by allowing wildlife and natural processes to reclaim areas no longer under human management. Rewilding reintroduces lost animal species, such as top (apex) predators that have a significant effect on the food web and trophic structure, and keystone species that are essential for ecosystem function. Establishing connectivity of habitats over large areas, using habitat corridors, allows movement of animals between habitat fragments and gives larger animals the space they need to feed and breed. Agriculture and other resource harvesting are no longer permitted, and natural ecosystems are allowed to recover through ecological management.

The Hinewai Reserve, on Banks Peninsula, New Zealand, is an ecological restoration project. Parts of the reserve were cleared, first by Polynesian settlers (from about 700 years ago) and then by European settlers (from around 1850), who completed the clearance. The 1250-hectare reserve is managed to allow natural regeneration of native vegetation and wildlife through minimal intervention, letting succession proceed towards ecosystems (nearly all forest) similar to those present before clearance; alien species such as exotic trees and vines are removed, allowing endemic flora and fauna to re-establish.

Figure D4.2.17: Otanerito Bay from Hinewai Reserve, Banks Peninsula, New ZealandA landscape photograph shows regenerating native forest covering hillsides above a bay, illustrating natural vegetation recovery within the Hinewai Reserve following the removal of grazing and other human land uses.

Higher Level Ecological Succession and Its Causes

SuccessionThe orderly process of change over time in a community.

All ecosystems develop over time, from colonization of a pristine area to the maturation of a stable, final community. Succession can be triggered by changes in both the abiotic environment and biotic factors: changes in the community of organisms frequently alter the physical environment, allowing another community to become established and, through competition, eventually replace the former one. Later communities in such a sequence (or sere) are often, but not inevitably, more complex than those appearing earlier.

Pioneer communityThe first stage of an ecological succession, containing hardy species able to withstand difficult conditions.
Climax communityA community of organisms that is more or less stable and in equilibrium with natural environmental conditions such as climate; it is the end point of ecological succession.

Two examples of succession from pioneer to climax community are: in freshwater, aquatic plants (e.g. water lilies) → reeds → low woodland species (e.g. willow); and in an abandoned quarry, mosses and lichens → grasses and herbs → shrubs (e.g. birch) → woodland.

Higher Level Changes Occurring During Primary Succession

The formation of an ecosystem in an environment devoid of vegetation and lacking soil (e.g. bare rock) is called a xerosere. In a xerosere: pioneer species (e.g. lichens, mosses, bacteria) arrive first; as pioneers die, soil begins to form; new plant species that need soil arrive and displace the pioneers; growth of these plants changes environmental factors such as light, wind and moisture; growth of roots retains soil and increases the nutrients and water it holds; nitrogen-fixing plants arrive, adding nitrates to the soil; soil depth increases further, allowing shrubs and other taller plants to establish; animal species arrive as the plant species they depend on become established; and eventually a climax community is established.

Figure D4.2.18: A primary succession on dry land — a xerosereThe diagram shows the colonization of new land (e.g. deposited in a river delta, exposed by a retreating glacier, or formed from wind-blown sand or cooled volcanic lava). Higher Level Changes in energy, matter and complexity through succession

Productivity changes through succession: in pioneer communities, gross productivity is low (initial conditions, low density of producers) and the proportion of energy lost to community respiration is also low, so net primary productivity (NPP) is high and the system grows, accumulating biomass. As biomass increases, gross primary productivity rises further while sunlight remains available to all plants (little shading yet). In later stages, an increased consumer community and higher respiration reduce NPP, so biomass accumulation slows; in climax communities gross productivity may be high, but this is balanced by community respiration, so overall NPP may be lower than earlier in the succession.

Common mistakeIt is not enough to say that ‘productivity increases through a succession’. Gross primary productivity (GPP) increases, but net primary productivity (NPP) decreases in later stages as community respiration increases.

The size of plants and the complexity of food webs both increase through a succession. Nutrients initially come from outside the developing community in the pioneer stages, but as biomass, soil depth and complexity increase, nutrient cycling within the ecosystem becomes increasingly important. Greater ecosystem complexity provides a greater variety of niches, so species diversity increases through the succession. Nutrient availability changes from poor initial stores to gradual accumulation as biomass develops and soil fertility improves.

A secondary succession occurs in an environment where an ecosystem has been disrupted or destroyed by a disturbance that reduces the population of the original inhabitants, but where soil already exists (unlike in primary succession) — for example, recolonization of woodland after a fire.

Higher Level Cyclical Succession in Ecosystems

In some ecosystems there is a cycle of communities rather than a single, unchanging climax community. Wood pasture, for example, cycles from pasture to scrub to woodland and back to pasture again.

Wood pasture is tree-land on which farm animals or deer are systematically grazed. It was originally formed and maintained by large wild herbivores; following the extinction of many large mammal species, livestock and deer now act as the grazers that maintain it. Grazing prevents mass regeneration of trees that would otherwise develop into high forest, but the reduced competition for light still allows enough tree regeneration to replace the tree population gradually over time. Wood pastures are valued mosaic habitats, especially for their veteran and ancient trees and the plants and animals they support; grazing animals, together with nectar-providing flowers and open grassland or heathland ground vegetation, are fundamental to the ecosystem’s existence.

Higher Level Climax Communities and Arrested Succession

Given specific environmental conditions, ecological succession tends to lead towards a particular type of climax community, determined by climatic and edaphic (soil) factors, although human influence can prevent this from developing.

Climax communities are more stable than earlier seral (successional) stages because: they contain more complex food webs, so the loss of one species can be compensated for by another; negative feedback mechanisms lead to a steady-state equilibrium; each successional stage helps create a deeper, more nutrient-rich soil, allowing larger plants to grow; a climax community is more productive, supporting more consumers and decomposers; and increased biomass provides an increased number of niches, increasing species and genetic diversity and so increasing stability further.

Arrested succession (plagioclimax)An area where human activity has prevented the ecosystem from developing a climax community.

Disturbance can stop succession before the climax community is reached — this is called arrested succession or plagioclimax. Human activity can decrease productivity by removing primary producers; reduce habitat diversity and niches, threatening specialized species; worsen abiotic conditions, creating harsher conditions that fewer species can tolerate; cause species extinction, shortening food webs; and produce a less complex, less stable community overall. Such activities divert succession towards an alternative stable state, so that the original climax community is never reached.

Arrested succession: grazing by farm livestock

Large parts of the UK were once covered by deciduous woodland, with relatively little heather except in the north. From the Middle Ages (1300–1500) onwards, forests were cleared for timber, fuel, housing, shipbuilding (especially using oak) and agricultural land; soil quality deteriorated as a result, and heather came to dominate the plant community. Sheep grazing, and burning associated with it, has since prevented woodland regrowth by destroying young tree saplings.

Figure D4.2.21: Interrupting a succession — grazing prevents a climax community from forming and maintains heather moorlandA photograph of open heather moorland illustrates a plagioclimax community maintained by continued sheep grazing, which prevents progression to woodland (the natural climax vegetation for the area).

Controlled burning of heather, carried out roughly every 15 years before the heather matures, also prevents re-establishment of deciduous woodland: if heather were allowed to mature, other plants could colonize the area, but instead the ash from burning adds to soil fertility and the resulting new heather growth increases the productivity of the moorland ecosystem.

Arrested succession: drainage of wetlands

Wetlands include diverse ecosystems worldwide, such as deltas and estuaries, mudflats, floodplains and peat bogs, on which humans and wildlife have relied for thousands of years. According to the Wildfowl and Wetlands Trust, approximately 87% of the world’s wetlands were lost over the last 300 years as they were drained for housing and industry. In many areas, wetland soils have been drained for agriculture (for example, over 1 million acres in South Florida drained for sugarcane, vegetables and other crops); elsewhere, wetlands are drained specifically so that peat can be extracted.

Figure D4.2.22: Drainage network for a peat extraction field, next to a natural peat bog wetlandAn aerial photograph shows a grid of straight drainage channels cut into a peat extraction site, contrasting sharply with the irregular, undrained texture of adjacent natural peat bog wetland, illustrating how artificial drainage diverts succession away from the original wetland climax.
Top tipVarious factors — fire, agriculture, grazing pressure or resource use such as deforestation — can divert the progression of succession towards an alternative stable state by modifying the ecosystem. Whether this diversion is permanent or temporary depends on the resilience of the ecosystem.
