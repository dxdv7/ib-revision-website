Production of Gametes as the Means of Inheritance

Meiosis produces haploid gametes, and this process is essential in maintaining a constant chromosome number across generations. At fertilization, haploid gametes from a male and a female parent fuse, restoring the diploid number of chromosomes. This pattern of inheritance, involving the alternation of a diploid phase and a haploid phase, is common to all eukaryotes with a sexual life cycle.

Figure D3.2.1: Meiosis and the diploid life cycle The diagram shows a cycle: an adult organism (2n, diploid) undergoes meiosis to form gametes — sperm (n) and egg (n), both haploid. Fertilization fuses sperm and egg to form a zygote (2n, diploid). The zygote then undergoes mitosis (and development) to produce the adult (2n) once again, completing the cycle. The diagram distinguishes the diploid phase (adult and zygote) from the haploid phase (gametes).
Autosomes and sex chromosomes There are two types of chromosomes. Autosomal chromosomes are the numbered chromosomes in a karyogram, ranged from smallest to largest, and an autosomal gene is a gene located on one of these non-sex chromosomes. Sex chromosomes contain genes that determine the sex of the offspring. In diploid cells, chromosomes exist as homologous pairs, so a diploid cell has two copies of each autosomal gene.

Methods for Conducting Genetic Crosses in Flowering Plants

Gregor Mendel, a monk working in a monastery at Brno (Moravia), made the first discoveries of the fundamental laws of heredity by cross-breeding garden pea plants (Pisum sp.) — over 30,000 plants over eight years — contrasting characteristics such as rounded versus wrinkled seed pods, yellow versus green peas, and tall versus dwarf stems.

Figure D3.2.2: Gregor Mendel A portrait of Gregor Mendel (1822–84), founder of modern genetics, shown alongside an illustration of the contrasting features he studied in the garden pea: dwarf versus tall plants, round versus wrinkled seeds, and green versus yellow cotyledons (seed leaves).
Figure D3.2.3: Mendel’s peas Historical artwork showing pea pods from Mendel’s experiments. Parental generations with either yellow peas (A) or green peas (B) produced offspring that were all yellow (C). When these yellow F1 offspring were bred together, the resulting F2 offspring were a mixture of yellow and green peas (D), in a ratio of 3 yellow : 1 green.

Before Mendel, scientists believed that inheritance worked by ‘blending’, like mixing paints of different colours — for example, a tall and a short parent would be expected to produce children of medium height. This idea worked poorly for characteristics such as eye colour, where a blue-eyed and brown-eyed parent do not produce all medium-brown-eyed children.

Why Mendel succeeded Mendel succeeded where others had failed because: his experiments were carefully planned and used large samples; he recorded numbers of each type of plant but expressed results as ratios; contrasting pea characteristics are easily recognized; by chance, each characteristic he studied was controlled by a single gene rather than many genes (unlike most human characteristics); the pairs of characters he studied happened to be controlled by genes on separate chromosomes; and he applied mathematics to interpret his results.

The Monohybrid Cross

Key terms A parental generation (P) is the first set of parents in a genetic cross, whose genotypes are used to predict the genotypes of the offspring (F1 generation). A monohybrid cross is a cross involving one pair of contrasting characters exhibited by homozygous parents. The F1 generation (first filial generation) arises from crossing the P generation; when F1 individuals are selfed or sibling-crossed, they produce the F2 generation.

Mendel began with plants that always ‘bred true’ when self-fertilized — tall plants produced only tall progeny, dwarf plants produced only dwarf progeny. Pollen contains the male gametes and the ovary contains the female gametes, so pollination is required for a cross; peas can self-pollinate because both male and female gametes are produced on the same plant.

Figure D3.2.4: The steps of Mendel’s monohybrid cross Pure-breeding tall plants are crossed with pure-breeding dwarf plants (self-pollination prevented at this stage). Many seeds are produced (150–250 typically harvested); when sown and grown (about 10–15 plants), all are tall — this is the F1 generation. The F1 plants are allowed to flower and self-pollinate. Mendel recorded 1064 F2 seeds, which when grown showed a ratio of 3 tall : 1 dwarf (Mendel actually recorded 787 tall : 277 dwarf, a ratio of 2.84 : 1).

Mendel argued that because the dwarf characteristic disappeared in the F1 generation and reappeared in the F2, a factor controlling dwarfness must remain intact between generations without expressing itself in the presence of the factor for tallness. Tallness is therefore dominant and dwarfness recessive. There must be two independent factors for height, one from each parent, and a gamete contains only one of these factors.

Dominant and recessive alleles A dominant allele has the same effect on the phenotype whether present in the homozygous or heterozygous state. A recessive allele has an effect on the phenotype only when present in the homozygous state. A Punnett grid is a diagram used to show and calculate all the combinations and frequencies of genotypes and phenotypes among the offspring of a genetic cross.
Figure D3.2.5: Genetic diagram of Mendel’s monohybrid cross Homozygous tall (TT) is crossed with homozygous dwarf (tt). Each parent produces one type of gamete by meiosis (T or t). The F1 offspring are all Tt (heterozygous tall), since T is dominant to t. When the F1 are selfed, each heterozygous parent produces two kinds of gametes (1/2 T, 1/2 t) because alleles on homologous chromosomes segregate to opposite poles of the spindle during meiosis (the Law of Segregation). The Punnett grid combining these gametes gives F2 genotypes in the ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, and phenotypes in the ratio 3 tall : 1 dwarf. The fractions on the grid represent the probabilities that particular gametes and zygotes will occur, and these ratios are only reliably seen with large numbers of offspring.

Genotype, Phenotype and Dominance

Genotype Genotype is the combination of alleles inherited by an organism (present in every cell). A genotype in which the two alleles of a gene are the same is homozygous for that gene (e.g. the P-generation tall and dwarf pea plants). If the two alleles differ, the organism is heterozygous for that gene (e.g. the F1 tall progeny, Tt).
Phenotype Phenotype is the observable traits of an organism resulting from genotype and environmental factors — in Mendel’s monohybrid cross, plant height was the phenotype. Phenotype may be determined by genotype alone (e.g. eye colour, blood type), by environment alone (e.g. a suntan), or by an interaction of genotype and environment (e.g. height and weight, explored through monozygotic twin studies).

Organisms may share a phenotype but differ in genotype: TT and Tt plants are both tall because both carry a dominant allele, and can only be distinguished by the offspring they produce. Crossing a plant of unknown genotype but dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive plant is called a test cross: if all offspring show the dominant phenotype, the tested plant is homozygous (TT); if approximately half show the recessive phenotype, the tested plant is heterozygous (Tt).

Figure D3.2.6: Mendel’s test cross A heterozygous tall plant (Tt) is crossed with a homozygous dwarf plant (tt). Tt produces gametes 1/2 T and 1/2 t; tt produces only t gametes. The Punnett grid gives offspring genotypes 1/2 Tt : 1/2 tt, so phenotypes are 50% tall : 50% dwarf. This demonstrates that the tested tall plant is heterozygous (Tt), not homozygous (TT), since a homozygous TT parent would have produced all-tall offspring.

Dominant vs recessive phenotypes An organism showing a recessive characteristic must be homozygous for the recessive allele (e.g. tt). An organism showing the dominant characteristic may be homozygous dominant (TT) or heterozygous (Tt) — these two genotypes look identical (the same phenotype). A recessive characteristic can only appear in the homozygous condition. It is incorrect to assume that dominant alleles are always advantageous or produce the most common phenotype: Huntington’s disease and six fingers (polydactyly) are both caused by dominant alleles but are not common or advantageous.

Phenotypic Plasticity

Phenotypic plasticity Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity to develop traits suited to the environment experienced by an organism, by varying patterns of gene expression. It is not due to a change in genotype, and changes in traits may be reversible during an individual’s lifetime — for example, the effects of exercise and diet on human morphology and physiology.

Phenylketonuria as an Example of a Recessive Human Disease

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a rare inherited disorder caused by a mutation in the PAH gene on chromosome 12, which codes for phenylalanine hydroxylase, the enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine to tyrosine. Without this enzyme, phenylalanine accumulates in the body.

Phenylketonuria (PKU) A recessive genetic condition caused by mutation in an autosomal gene that codes for the enzyme needed to convert phenylalanine to tyrosine. Untreated infants develop unusually light eye, skin and hair colour (high phenylalanine interferes with melanin production), and disruptions to neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine, which can cause seizures, abnormal muscle movements and, without treatment, severe brain damage. Treatment is a phenylalanine-restricted diet begun in early infancy.
Carrier A carrier is an individual with one copy of a recessive allele that causes a genetic disease in individuals homozygous for that allele. Carriers do not show the disease in their phenotype because the working allele masks the faulty one, but they may pass the faulty allele to offspring. Do not confuse ‘carrier’ (one recessive allele, unaffected) with ‘affected’ (two recessive alleles, shows the disease).

Single-Nucleotide Polymorphisms, Multiple Alleles and ABO Blood Groups

A single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) is a difference in a single nucleotide (for example, replacing guanine with adenine at one position), which can create an alternative version of a gene — an allele. Many different alleles of a gene can exist in a gene pool, depending on how many SNPs have occurred and whether these affect the gene. Since only one allele can occupy each chromosome, an individual inherits only two of the possibly many alleles present in the gene pool (one from each parent).

Most genes discussed so far have just two alleles, but most genes in reality have multiple alleles. With multiple alleles, a single capital letter represents the locus, and individual alleles are shown as an additional letter in superscript. The ABO blood group system in humans is a classic example: human blood belongs to one of four groups, A, B, AB or O.

Incomplete Dominance and Codominance

In certain monohybrid crosses, the standard 3:1 ratio is not obtained because the heterozygote does not resemble either homozygous parent exactly.

Codominance vs incomplete dominance In codominance, both alleles are expressed in the phenotype, equally and separately (e.g. the AB blood type, where both IA and IB antigens appear on red blood cells). In incomplete dominance, the dominant allele does not completely mask the effects of the recessive allele, and heterozygotes show an intermediate, blended phenotype that differs from both homozygous phenotypes (a new, third phenotype). A cross between two heterozygotes gives a 1:2:1 phenotype ratio rather than 3:1.

Mirabilis jalapa (the four o’clock flower) has pure-breeding red-flowered and white-flowered varieties. Crossing them gives F1 plants with pink flowers. Selfing the pink F1 gives F2 offspring in the ratio 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. Pink colouration occurs because both a red and a white pigment system are expressed together in the heterozygote.

Sex Determination and Sex-Linked Inheritance

In a karyogram, the final (23rd) pair of human chromosomes is not numbered but labelled X and Y — the sex chromosomes, which determine the sex of the individual. All other chromosomes (pairs 1–22) are autosomes. The X chromosome is longer than the Y and carries far more genes.

All egg cells carry an X chromosome, but sperm are of two types: 50% carry an X chromosome and 50% carry a Y chromosome. Fertilization by an X-carrying sperm produces a female (XX); fertilization by a Y-carrying sperm produces a male (XY). The sex of human (and other mammalian) offspring is therefore determined by the male parent, and equal numbers of male and female offspring are expected over time.

Figure D3.2.10: X and Y chromosomes and the determination of sex A human male (XY) produces gametes by meiosis: 1/2 carry X, 1/2 carry Y. A human female (XX) produces gametes that are all X. The Punnett grid combining these gives offspring genotypes 1/4 XX : 1/4 XY : 1/4 XX : 1/4 XY, i.e. a 50% female (XX) : 50% male (XY) phenotype ratio.

Male and female embryos develop identically at first. In the seventh week of pregnancy, a cascade of developmental events is triggered if a Y chromosome is present. The Y chromosome carries the prime male-determining gene, coding for testis-determining factor (TDF), a molecular switch that, on reaching the embryonic gonad tissue, initiates production of testosterone. This hormone inhibits development of female genitalia and causes the embryonic genital tissue to form testes, scrotum and penis. In the absence of a Y chromosome, the gonad tissue forms an ovary and female reproductive structures develop under the influence of ovarian hormones.

Common mistake The condition for determining a female is the absence of a Y chromosome, not the presence of an X chromosome (since all individuals, male or female, have at least one X chromosome).

Haemophilia as an Example of a Sex-Linked Genetic Disorder

Sex linkage Sex linkage is a special case of linkage occurring when a gene is located on a sex chromosome, usually the X chromosome. Because the X chromosome is much longer than the Y, most X-linked alleles have no corresponding allele on the Y. The region of the X chromosome without a corresponding Y sequence is called the non-homologous region.
Figure D3.2.11: Comparing an X and Y chromosome The X and Y chromosomes are shown side by side. A short region at one end is homologous between them (does not carry sex-determining genes). The much larger remaining regions of both chromosomes are non-homologous — these carry sex-determining genes and other genes unique to each chromosome.

In a male (XY), any allele on the non-homologous part of the X is expressed in the phenotype, even if recessive, because there is no second allele on the Y to mask it. In a female (XX), a recessive allele on one X may be masked by a dominant allele on the other X. A human female can therefore be homozygous or heterozygous for sex-linked characteristics, while a male has only one allele. A heterozygous female carrying a recessive allele without showing its effect is a carrier. Examples of X-linked recessive conditions include red–green colour blindness and haemophilia.

Haemophilia is a rare condition in which the blood does not clot normally, due to failure to produce adequate amounts of specific blood-clotting proteins. There are two forms, haemophilia A and haemophilia B, treated today by administering the missing clotting factor. Because the genes controlling these clotting proteins are on the X chromosome, haemophilia is largely a disease of males: a single X chromosome carrying the recessive allele (XhY) causes the disease. A female would need to be homozygous (XhXh) to be affected, which is usually fatal before birth. A female with one recessive allele (XHXh) is a carrier with normal clotting.

Pedigree Charts to Deduce Patterns of Inheritance

Since experimental crosses cannot ethically be performed on humans, patterns of inheritance are investigated instead by researching family pedigrees, using appropriate historical records.

Analysing Pedigree Charts for Dominant and Recessive Patterns

A characteristic due to a dominant allele tends to occur in one or more members of the family in every generation. A recessive characteristic is seen infrequently, often skipping generations altogether.

Figure D3.2.14: Pedigree chart of a family with albinism Albinism is a rare recessive condition (individual has a block in melanin synthesis, resulting in white hair, very light skin and pink eyes). The chart spans four generations (I–IV). In generation I, one individual has albinism (genotype pp). In generation II, all offspring of this individual must be carriers (Pp), having received the recessive allele (p) from the affected parent. Albinism does not reappear until generation IV, skipping two generations altogether: in generation III, one individual (labelled as a carrier) and her partner (also a carrier, marked with an X) must both be carriers since they produce a son with albinism in generation IV. People with albinism must be homozygous recessive (pp); people with normal pigmentation may be homozygous dominant (PP) or carriers (Pp).
Figure D3.2.15: Pedigree chart of a family with brachydactyly Brachydactyly (very short fingers, shown via an X-ray of normal-length hand bones compared with a drawing of a brachydactylous hand) is caused by a dominant mutant allele, so it shows a pattern of dominant monohybrid inheritance, appearing in every generation. In generation I, the parents are inferred to be a normal male (nn) and a heterozygous brachydactylous female (Nn), because the ratio of affected to unaffected offspring resembles a test cross (roughly 1:1). In each subsequent generation (II, III, IV), about half of the offspring are brachydactylous (Nn or NN) and half are normal (nn).

Many societies prohibit marriage between close relatives partly for genetic reasons: closely related individuals share a smaller pool of near ancestors, so their offspring are more likely to inherit two copies of the same harmful recessive allele (increasing the chance of homozygous recessive genetic disorders) compared with unrelated parents.

Continuous Variation, Polygenic Inheritance and Statistics

Variation may be continuous or discontinuous. Discontinuous (discrete) variation, such as ABO blood type, shows clear-cut categories with no intermediate forms. Most characteristics of organisms, however, are controlled by more than one gene. Polygenic inheritance is the inheritance of phenotypic characters (such as height and eye colour) that are determined by the collective effects of several different genes, often (though not always) located on different chromosomes. Each gene has a small individual effect, but their combined effect produces continuous variation among offspring.

Multiple genes vs multiple alleles ‘Multiple alleles’ refers to several different versions of the same gene (e.g. the three ABO alleles). ‘Multiple genes’ (polygenic inheritance) refers to several different genes that together determine one characteristic. Do not confuse the two.

Human skin colour depends on the amount of melanin produced in the skin, controlled by three, four or more separately inherited genes, producing an almost continuous range of skin colour from very pale to very dark.

Figure D3.2.16: Polygenic inheritance of human skin colour (two-gene model) To illustrate the principle simply, only two independent genes (A/a and B/b) are shown, though skin colour is really controlled by three or more genes. Parents of genotype AABB (darkest) and aabb (lightest) are crossed to give an F1 generation that is entirely AaBb (intermediate). When the F1 are crossed together, the F2 generation shows a wide range of genotypes and a spread of phenotypes from very light to very dark, with most offspring showing intermediate shades — illustrating how several genes acting together can generate continuous variation rather than a small number of discrete classes.

Both human height and skin colour may also be influenced by environmental factors, in addition to the genes involved.

Box-and-Whisker Plots

For data that are not normally distributed, the appropriate descriptive statistics are medians and quartiles, displayed using a box-and-whisker plot. Quartiles divide an ordered distribution into four equal parts: the median of the ‘lower half’ of the data gives the first quartile, and the median of the ‘upper half’ gives the third quartile.

Figure D3.2.17: Frequency distributions of normal and skewed data Two frequency-distribution curves are compared. A normal distribution is a symmetrical bell-shaped curve where the mode, median and mean coincide at the central peak. A skewed distribution is asymmetrical: values decrease in frequency more rapidly on one side of the peak than the other, so the mode, median and mean fall at different points — the difference between the mean and mode is a measure of the skewness of the data.
Constructing a box-and-whisker plot The ticks at the top and bottom of the vertical lines (‘whiskers’) show the highest and lowest values in the data set. The top of the box shows the upper (third) quartile, the bottom of the box shows the lower (first) quartile, and the horizontal line inside the box shows the median. The box itself spans the interquartile range (IQR) — from the first to the third quartile — showing the spread of the middle 50% of the data. A data point is an outlier if it lies more than 1.5 × IQR above the third quartile or below the first quartile.

Limits of the Law of Independent Assortment The 9:3:3:1 (and 1:1:1:1) ratios only apply if the genes involved are on different chromosomes, or are far enough apart on the same chromosome for the recombination rate to reach 50%. If genes are located close together on the same chromosome, these ratios are not obtained — this is the phenomenon of gene linkage, described below.

Loci of Human Genes and Their Polypeptide Products

Each gene occupies a specific position, or locus (plural loci), on a chromosome, so each eukaryotic chromosome is a linear series of genes. The gene for a particular characteristic is always found at the same locus on a particular chromosome. Online genome databases (such as NCBI Gene) can be used to look up the locus of a specific human gene and its polypeptide product, and to find pairs of genes that are either on different chromosomes or in close proximity on the same chromosome.

Autosomal Gene Linkage

William Bateson and Reginald Punnett crossed pure-breeding sweet pea plants with purple flowers and long pollen grains against plants with red flowers and round pollen grains. All F1 plants were purple-flowered with long pollen (both dominant traits). When the F1 were self-crossed, the F2 offspring mostly resembled the parental phenotypes, but a small number of new (recombinant) combinations also appeared — a strong deviation from the 9:3:3:1 ratio expected for unlinked genes.

Gene linkage vs sex linkage ‘Gene linkage’ refers to two or more genes located together on the same (typically autosomal) chromosome. ‘Sex linkage’ refers specifically to genes located on a sex chromosome (usually the X). Do not confuse the two terms.

Recombinants in Crosses Involving Two Linked or Unlinked Genes

Recombinant A recombinant is a chromosome (or the gamete/offspring genotype it produces) in which the genetic information has been rearranged compared with the parental combinations, typically as a result of crossing over (chiasma formation) between linked gene loci during meiosis.
Figure D3.2.22: Chiasmata and the origin of recombinants The diagram compares two outcomes of meiosis in a heterozygote for two linked genes (alleles E/e for pollen length and F/f for flower colour, carried on the same chromosome pair). If a chiasma forms between the two gene loci, crossing over exchanges alleles between homologous chromatids, producing new recombinant gamete types (Ef and eF) in addition to the parental types (EF and ef). If the chiasma forms elsewhere on the chromosome (not between the two loci), the parental combinations of alleles remain intact, and only parental-type gametes (EF and ef) are produced. Since a chiasma between the two loci is a relatively rare event, most F2 offspring are of the parental type, with recombinants forming the minority.

Drosophila and the Work of Thomas Morgan

Thomas Morgan selected the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster in 1908 as an experimental organism, work for which he won the Nobel Prize in 1933. His experiments showed that non-Mendelian ratios are commonly (but not always) obtained in Drosophila crosses; established the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance (that Mendel’s ‘factors’ are linear sequences of genes on chromosomes); discovered sex chromosomes and sex linkage; and demonstrated crossing over resulting from chiasmata during meiosis. Drosophila is useful experimentally because it has only four pairs of chromosomes, a generation time of about 10 days at 25°C, produces hundreds of offspring per female, and is easily cultured and handled in the laboratory.

Use of a Chi-Squared Test on Data from Dihybrid Crosses

A statistical test allows differences between observed and expected results to be evaluated to see whether they are likely to be due to chance (sampling variation) or represent a genuine (‘treatment’) effect. All statistical tests examine a null hypothesis (typically, that there is no real difference between observed and expected results); the alternative hypothesis proposes that a real difference or association exists. A result is statistically significant if there is less than a 5% probability (p = 0.05) that it occurred by chance alone.

The expected ratio from a dihybrid cross is 9:3:3:1, but observed offspring numbers in real experiments rarely match this exactly, since the exact ratio requires entirely random fertilization, equal survival chances for all offspring, and very large numbers of offspring. Deviations can arise from chance events such as unequal fertilization success, differential mortality, or unequal transmission of pollen or gametes.

How to interpret a chi-squared result 1. Count the number of phenotype categories and subtract 1 to find the degrees of freedom (df); with 4 categories, df = 3. 2. Read along the row for that df in the χ2 distribution table to find the critical value at p = 0.05. 3. If the calculated χ2 is smaller than the critical value, the difference between observed and expected results is not significant (i.e. likely due to chance) — the data conform to a Mendelian ratio. 4. If the calculated χ2 is larger than the critical value, the difference is statistically significant, and the experimental hypothesis (e.g. that the genes are unlinked) must be reconsidered. In the worked example, χ2 = 0.47 with df = 3 lies well below the critical value of 7.81 (p = 0.05), so the deviation from the expected 9:3:3:1 ratio is not significant and is attributed to chance.

Homeostasis and Negative Feedback

Living organisms face changing, sometimes hostile, environments. An animal that can maintain a constant internal environment, allowing it to continue normal activities regardless of external conditions, is called a regulator. Mammals and birds, for example, maintain a high and almost constant body temperature across a wide range of external temperatures, keeping their enzymes, muscles and nervous system working efficiently, and giving them greater freedom to choose where to live.

Homeostasis Homeostasis is the maintenance of the internal environment of an organism — the ability to maintain a constant internal environment (‘staying the same’). The internal environment consists of the blood and the tissue fluid that circulates among cells, delivering nutrients and removing waste. Mammals regulate body temperature, blood pH, blood glucose concentration and blood osmotic concentration at constant levels or within narrow limits, despite fluctuations in the external environment.
Figure D3.3.1: Homeostasis in mammals A summary diagram lists several examples of homeostatic variables regulated by negative feedback in mammals: body temperature, blood glucose level, blood water content, blood pressure in arteries, concentration of respiratory gases in blood (pCO2, pO2), concentration of essential ions, heart rate, and blood pH.
Common mistake Homeostasis refers to control of the internal environment. It should not be confused with a behavioural response to an external stimulus, such as withdrawing a hand from a hot object.

Negative Feedback Loops in Homeostasis

Negative feedback Negative feedback is a type of control in which conditions are brought back to a set value as soon as a deviation from that value is detected. Negative feedback loops are used in homeostasis (rather than positive feedback) because they act to restore the original set point.
Negative vs positive feedback Negative feedback returns a homeostatic variable to its set point whether the variable has moved above or below it (e.g. body temperature above or below the optimum). Positive feedback moves conditions further away from the set point (equilibrium) and so does not restore original conditions — it amplifies a change rather than reversing it.
Figure D3.3.2: The mechanism of negative feedback A generalized negative feedback control loop is shown as a cycle: an input (a change to the system) is detected by a receptor, which measures the level of the variable. This information passes to a coordinator, where the normal (set) level of the variable is defined, information from the receptor is compared with that set value, and commands are sent to an effector. The effector brings about a second change to the system, in the opposite direction to the original input, producing an output in which the condition is restored to its set value. A feedback loop then establishes that the change has been corrected and causes the effector to switch off.

In mammals, body temperature, blood glucose level, and the water/ion content of blood and tissue fluid (osmoregulation) are all regulated by negative feedback. Detectors are specialized cells in the brain or other organs (such as the pancreas); effectors are organs such as the skin, liver and kidneys. Information travels between detector cells and effector organs via nerves, hormones, or both.

Regulation of Blood Glucose

Blood glucose transport to all cells is a key function of the circulation. Normal human blood glucose is about 90 mg per 100 cm3 of blood, but this can vary — falling to as low as 70 mg after prolonged fasting or exercise, or rising to 150 mg after a carbohydrate-rich meal.

Respiration is continuous in all living cells, requiring a regular glucose supply. Most cells (including muscle) hold glycogen reserves that can be rapidly converted to glucose, but these reserves can run out quickly; the brain, notably, has no glycogen store and depends entirely on glucose as its respiratory substrate.

Hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia Hypoglycaemia is the condition when blood sugar levels are too low; if blood glucose falls below 60 mg per 100 cm3 and is not reversed, fainting, convulsions and coma can follow. Hyperglycaemia is the condition when blood sugar levels are too high; a high concentration of blood solute lowers the water potential of the plasma, drawing water from cells and tissue fluid by osmosis into the blood, ultimately causing dehydration as the kidneys excrete the excess water and blood pressure becomes difficult to maintain.
Top tip The prefix ‘hypo-’ means a factor is too low; the prefix ‘hyper-’ means a factor is too high. Hence hypoglycaemia (blood sugar too low) and hyperglycaemia (blood sugar too high).

Mechanism for Regulation of Blood Glucose

After digestion, glucose is absorbed across the epithelial cells of the intestinal villi into the hepatic portal vein, reaching the liver first. If glucose level is too high, the liver withdraws glucose from the blood and stores it as glycogen. Even so, blood circulating around the body after a meal is raised in glucose. This excess is detected at the pancreas, in patches of endocrine tissue called the islets of Langerhans — hormone-secreting glands with a rich capillary supply but no ducts, so their hormones are released directly into the blood.

Figure D3.3.3: Islet of Langerhans in the pancreas A transverse section of the pancreatic gland shows a duct carrying pancreatic (digestive) juice to the duodenum, surrounded by cells of the pancreatic gland that secrete this juice. Embedded among these are patches of tissue called islets of Langerhans, richly supplied by a blood capillary network, containing two cell types: alpha (α) cells and beta (β) cells.
Key terms Alpha (α) cells are glucagon-secreting cells of the islets of Langerhans. Beta (β) cells are insulin-secreting cells of the islets of Langerhans.

A raised blood glucose level stimulates β cells to secrete insulin. Insulin causes uptake of glucose into cells throughout the body (especially the liver and skeletal muscle), increases the use of glucose (rather than fat) in respiration, and triggers conversion of glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis) and to fatty acids and fats for storage. As blood glucose returns to normal, insulin secretion stops and circulating insulin is removed by the kidney tubules.

When blood glucose falls below normal, α cells secrete glucagon. Glucagon activates enzymes that convert glycogen to glucose (glycogenolysis) and amino acids to glucose (gluconeogenesis), and reduces the rate of respiration of glucose. As blood glucose returns to normal, glucagon secretion ceases and it too is removed by the kidney tubules.

Glucagon A hormone made in the pancreas (by α cells) that promotes the breakdown of glycogen to glucose in the liver and muscle cells.
Figure D3.3.4: Glucose regulation by negative feedback The diagram shows a normal blood glucose level (about 90 mg/100 cm3) at the centre, with two opposing arms. A rise in blood glucose (after a meal) is detected in the islets of Langerhans, triggering insulin secretion by β cells; insulin causes the liver to convert glucose to glycogen and increases fat synthesis and cell respiration in liver, muscle (glucose to glycogen) and other tissues, restoring normal glucose level, after which insulin secretion stops (negative feedback) and insulin is excreted by the kidneys. Common mistakes The β cells of the pancreatic islets monitor blood glucose concentration directly — the hypothalamus is not involved in this monitoring. Also, glucagon is a hormone, not an enzyme: it is correct to say glucagon causes cells to break down glycogen, not that glucagon itself breaks down glycogen into glucose. Do not confuse the words ‘glucagon’ (a hormone) and ‘glycogen’ (a storage carbohydrate) — check spelling carefully.

Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes

Diabetes is a failure to regulate blood glucose levels correctly, resulting in erratic and generally permanently raised blood glucose, with glucose regularly excreted in the urine. Untreated, it carries increased risk of circulatory disorders, renal failure, blindness, strokes or heart attacks.

Type 1 vs type 2 diabetes Type 1 diabetes results from a failure of insulin production by the β cells (destroyed by the body’s own immune system — an autoimmune disease). Type 2 diabetes results from a failure of the insulin receptor proteins on target cell membranes (the β cells continue to function, but the body stops responding normally to insulin).

Thermoregulation as an Example of Negative Feedback

Thermoregulation is the regulation of body temperature, involving control of both heat loss across the body surface and heat production within the body — an example of negative feedback control. White adipose tissue insulates the body, reducing heat loss; brown adipose tissue can instead release heat when temperature falls. Thyroxin, an iodine-containing hormone from the thyroid gland, also contributes: the hypothalamus secretes thyrotropin-releasing hormone, which stimulates the pituitary to secrete thyroid-stimulating hormone, which stimulates thyroxin production. Thyroxin increases oxygen consumption and basal metabolic rate, so variations in its secretion help control body temperature. Temperature changes are detected by peripheral thermoreceptors in the skin and in the hypothalamus.

Physiological vs behavioural thermoregulation Physiological controls generate and regulate heat production within the body from metabolic reactions; an animal using this strategy is an endotherm (‘inside heat’), such as mammals and birds. Behavioural controls involve modifying behaviour — e.g. birds spreading feathers and opening wings to allow cooling airflow, or mammals seeking cooler areas of habitat when overheating.

Thermoregulation Mechanisms in Humans

Human core body temperature is held just below 37°C, varying only between about 35.5 and 37.0°C over 24 hours in health. Heat is lost by convection, radiation, conduction and evaporation. The regulatory control centre is the hypothalamus, which receives impulses from temperature receptors in the skin and brain and sends signals to effectors (skin, liver, muscles) to correct deviations from the set point.

Vasoconstriction and vasodilation Vasoconstriction is the narrowing of blood vessels by small muscles in their walls, restricting blood flow. Vasodilation is the widening of blood vessels by small muscles in their walls, increasing blood flow. Arterioles supplying the skin capillaries constrict or dilate to regulate heat loss from the skin.
Responses to increased temperature Arterioles supplying skin capillaries dilate (vasodilation), increasing blood flow to the skin and increasing radiative heat loss. Muscles attached to skin hairs relax, so hairs lie flat, allowing more heat to radiate away. Sweat glands are activated, producing more sweat, which evaporates and cools the skin.
Responses to decreased temperature Arterioles supplying skin capillaries constrict (vasoconstriction), decreasing blood flow to the skin and reducing radiative heat loss. Muscles attached to skin hairs contract, causing hairs to stand erect and trap an insulating layer of air. Sweat glands reduce sweat production. Skeletal muscles contract and relax rapidly, causing shivering, which generates heat. Metabolic activity in the liver increases, generating more heat.

In mammals, brown adipose tissue maintains body temperature in newborns by metabolizing triglycerides in mitochondria to release heat directly rather than producing ATP — a process called uncoupled respiration, in which the mitochondria are ‘uncoupled’ from their usual ATP-generating function. Newborns have a large surface area relative to their body size and so lose heat rapidly; uncoupled respiration in brown fat helps them generate enough heat to maintain body temperature.

Higher Level The Kidney: Osmoregulation and Excretion

Metabolism produces by-products, some of which would be toxic if allowed to accumulate. Excretion is the removal from the body of the waste products of cell metabolism, a characteristic activity of all living things; metabolites present in excessive concentration are also excreted. In mammals, excretion is central to homeostasis. Closely associated with excretion is osmoregulation: the maintenance of a proper balance of water and dissolved substances in the organism. The kidney maintains the osmotic concentration of tissue fluid and blood plasma.

Key terms Osmotic concentration is a measure of solute concentration, defined as the number of osmoles (osmol) of solute per litre. If water uptake into animal cells is excessive, hydrostatic pressure can stretch the plasma membrane to bursting; if water loss is excessive, cells shrink and die, since water makes up about 90% of cell volume.

The Human Urinary System

The kidneys regulate the body’s internal environment by continuously adjusting blood composition. Waste products are transported by blood to the kidneys and excreted as urine. Ion concentrations (e.g. Na+, Cl−) and water content are also regulated here — another example of homeostasis by negative feedback.

Role of the Glomerulus, Bowman’s Capsule and Proximal Convoluted Tubule

Human kidneys produce about 1.0–1.5 L of urine per day, containing roughly 40–50 g of solutes (mainly about 30 g of urea and up to 15 g of sodium chloride).

Step 1: Ultrafiltration in the Renal Capsule

In the glomerulus, much of the water and many small solutes (useful ions, glucose, amino acids, urea) are forced out of the capillaries into the lumen of the Bowman’s capsule, forming the glomerular filtrate. This process, called ultrafiltration, is powered by blood (hydrostatic) pressure, which is kept high because the afferent arteriole (into the glomerulus) is wider than the efferent arteriole (leading away).

The capillary wall has pores through which water and small solutes pass; blood cells and plasma proteins are retained inside the capillary. Outside the capillary wall is a basement membrane, described as the ‘final filter’. The inner wall of the Bowman’s capsule is formed of specialized cells called podocytes, each with foot-like extensions that wrap around the capillaries, leaving filtration slits between them. Filtrate passes between (not through) the podocytes into the lumen of the renal capsule, then on into the proximal convoluted tubule. Role of the Loop of Henle

The loop of Henle enables the kidney to conserve water. Since urea must be excreted in solution, some water loss is unavoidable, yet water can be a scarce resource for terrestrial organisms.

Figure D3.3.11: The functioning loop of Henle The loop of Henle is shown with a descending limb and an ascending limb, running alongside the vasa recta (a parallel capillary blood supply) within the tissue of the medulla. In the ascending limb, the walls are permeable to ions (Na+, Cl−) but impermeable to water: in the upper, thick-walled part, Na+ and Cl− are actively transported out into the surrounding interstitial fluid; in the lower, thin-walled part, Na+ and Cl− diffuse out passively. This creates a gradient of increasing ion concentration across the medulla, from less concentrated near the cortex to most concentrated at the tips of the medullary pyramid (where the collecting ducts run). The descending limb, by contrast, is fully permeable to water but has very low solute permeability: as the filtrate flows down, water passes out into the interstitial fluid by osmosis, following the rising external salt concentration, so that the fluid at the hairpin bend of the loop is the saltiest. The vasa recta delivers oxygen to the metabolically active tubule cells and also absorbs the water leaving the descending limb and the collecting ducts.
How the loop of Henle creates a concentration gradient At every level, the salt concentration in the descending limb is slightly higher than in the adjacent ascending limb. As filtrate flows through the loop, this small difference is repeatedly reinforced (multiplied) along its length, so that a steep, sustained osmotic gradient builds up across the medulla — from less concentrated near the cortex to highly concentrated at the tip of the medulla. This hypertonic environment around the collecting ducts is what allows water to be drawn out of the collecting ducts later, when required.

Osmoregulation by Water Reabsorption in the Collecting Ducts

Osmoregulation is part of homeostasis, regulated by negative feedback. The composition of the blood is continuously monitored by osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus (and by sensory receptors elsewhere in the body). This information allows the hypothalamus to control the pituitary gland, whose posterior lobe stores and releases antidiuretic hormone (ADH). Nerve impulses from the hypothalamus trigger ADH release into the blood; its target is the wall of the collecting ducts of the kidney tubules.

When blood water content is low, ADH is secreted; when blood water content is high, little or no ADH is secreted. The plasma membranes of collecting-duct cells contain channel proteins called aquaporins, normally stored in vesicle membranes inside the cell. When ADH levels rise, aquaporins move from these vesicles into the cell membrane. When ADH binds to receptors on the collecting-duct membrane, the resulting protein channels open, allowing much water to diffuse out into the medulla (and little to diffuse back in), so only a small volume of concentrated urine is produced. The liver continually removes and inactivates ADH, so its regulatory effect depends on continued fresh secretion. When ADH is absent, the channels close, little water is reabsorbed, and a large volume of dilute urine is produced.

When a person has taken in little water, sweated heavily, or eaten salty food, the hypothalamus detects this and directs the posterior pituitary to secrete ADH: with ADH present, the collecting duct walls become permeable, water diffuses out into the medulla (which has a high solute concentration), and a small quantity of concentrated urine is formed. When a person has drunk a lot of water, the hypothalamus detects this and stops ADH secretion: with ADH absent, the collecting duct walls are less permeable, water stays in the collecting duct, and a large volume of dilute urine is formed.
Figure D3.3.13: Homeostasis by osmoregulation in the kidneys — a summary A negative feedback loop diagram shows two branches from a central, normally concentrated blood plasma. On one side: too little water (or too much salt) raises osmotic potential, stimulating both thirst receptors and hypothalamic osmoreceptors; this triggers the feeling of thirst (leading to water being drunk) and causes the pituitary to secrete more ADH, making collecting duct walls more permeable so the medulla absorbs more water from the collecting ducts, producing a small volume of concentrated urine, and returning blood plasma to its normal concentration. On the other side: too much water (or too little salt) lowers osmotic potential; thirst and osmoreceptors are not stimulated, no feeling of thirst arises, and the pituitary secretes less ADH, making collecting duct walls less permeable so the medulla absorbs less water, producing a large volume of dilute urine, again returning blood plasma to normal. ADH present in the plasma is continually excreted/inactivated via the kidneys and liver.
Top tip When water intake exceeds the body’s needs, the urine produced is copious and dilute (as noticed after drinking a lot of water). When water intake is very low, or after heavy sweating or a very salty meal, a small volume of concentrated urine is formed instead.

Differences in Composition of Blood Plasma, Glomerular Filtrate and Urine

Urine composition varies with diet (salt and protein intake), water intake, activity level, environmental conditions, water loss by other routes (e.g. sweat), and state of health. Blood composition, by contrast, is held nearly constant by homeostatic mechanisms, particularly at the kidney tubules, and the composition of the filtrate entering the renal capsule is also remarkably constant — a result of ultrafiltration and the fact that most plasma proteins are too large to be filtered.

Changes in Blood Supply to Organs in Response to Changes in Activity

Blood supplies nutrients and oxygen and removes waste; metabolically active tissues, such as exercising muscle, require increased blood flow to support increased respiration. Vasoconstriction and vasodilation of arterioles control the distribution of blood flow around the body according to activity level.

During sleep, total brain blood flow changes little overall, but regional differences occur — for example, blood flow to the hypothalamus and brainstem may be significantly higher during REM sleep than during wakefulness. Blood flow to most skeletal muscle groups, and particularly the diaphragm, is lower during sleep than during wakefulness.

During vigorous exercise, blood is redirected away from organs not directly involved (such as the gut) and towards skeletal muscles. A similar redistribution occurs during the fight-or-flight response, when epinephrine (adrenaline) from the adrenal gland constricts arterioles supplying many organs while dilating those supplying skeletal muscle. During wakeful rest, less blood is needed in the muscles; after a meal, blood flow to the gut increases to aid digestion and absorption. Renal blood flow is greater when lying down than standing, and is reduced by prolonged vigorous exercise (which constricts renal arterioles and diverts blood elsewhere); overall, blood flow to the kidneys must remain fairly constant to maintain osmoregulation and waste excretion.
