Introduction to Cells and Cell Theory

The cellThe cell is the basic structural unit of all living organisms – it is the smallest part of an organism that can be said to be alive. Cells carry out the essential processes of life and are considered self-contained units of structure and function.

Cells are extremely small – most are only visible as distinct structures under a microscope, although a few cell types are just large enough to be seen with the naked eye. Observations of cells were first reported over 300 years ago, following the early development of microscopes.

Unicellular and multicellular organisms

Some organisms are made of a single cell and are known as unicellular. There are vast numbers of different unicellular organisms in the living world, many with very long evolutionary histories. One group of unicellular organisms belongs to the kingdom Protoctista (organisms in this kingdom are called protists, e.g. Chlamydomonas and Amoeba); another group is the bacteria (e.g. Escherichia coli). Other organisms are made of many cells and are known as multicellular organisms, such as mammals and flowering plants.

Cell theory

Cell theoryAll organisms are composed of one or more cells. Cell theory includes the idea that cells are the unit of structure and function in living organisms. The cell theory states that:
  • cells can only arise from pre-existing cells
  • living organisms are composed of cells, which are the smallest unit of life
  • organisms consisting of only one cell carry out all functions of life in that cell; cells perform life functions at some point in their existence.
Although most organisms conform to cell theory, there are exceptions (see the “Atypical cell structure” section below).

Many biologists contributed to the development of the cell theory. This concept evolved gradually in western Europe during the nineteenth century because of the steadily accelerating pace of developments in microscopy and biochemistry.

Nature of science: induction and deduction in cell theory

Collecting and analysing biological observations can lead to important conclusions based on inductive reasoning – formulating generalizations from many related specific observations. The claim of cell theory that “all organisms will consist of one or more cells” is derived from inductive reasoning, based on microscopic observations by many biologists across a wide range of organisms. In inductive reasoning, biologists begin with specific observations and measurements, detect patterns or common features, formulate a testable hypothesis, and finally develop a theory.

A related process, deductive reasoning, is used to test theories produced by induction. Deductive reasoning proceeds from the more general to the more specific, ultimately leading biologists to test hypotheses with specific data that either support or falsify the theory.

The philosopher Karl Popper rejected the use of inductive reasoning in science, claiming that for induction to be true, every example of its inference must be true. Biologists have found a small number of cells and organisms that are exceptions to the cell theory. However, cell theory remains an important and unifying concept in biology, necessary for inductive science.

Cell Size and Units of Measurement

Since cells are so small, appropriate units are needed to measure them. The metre (m) is the standard SI unit of length used in science.

1 mm (10−3 m)= 1000 micrometres (µm) (or microns)
1 µm (10−6 m)= 1000 nanometres (nm)

Each subdivision of the metre used to measure cells is one thousandth of the unit above it. The dimensions of cells are usually expressed in micrometres (µm), which is one thousandth (10−3) of a millimetre – a useful comparison to the millimetre markings on a standard ruler.

Bacteria are typically 0.1–2 µm in size, whereas plant and animal cells are often in the range of 50–150 µm or larger. Cell size determines the rate of diffusion of substances across the plasma membrane; by being small, this rate is maximized.

Types of light microscope

Figure A2.2.3: Light microscopyTwo diagrams comparing microscope types. The simple microscope (hand lens) diagram shows that the object being examined should be brought nearer to the lens, not the other way round. The compound microscope diagram is labelled with: eyepiece lens; turret (as it is turned, the objectives click into place – first medium-power, then high-power); objective lenses (×4 low, ×10 medium, ×40 high power); coarse focus (used to focus the low- and medium-power objectives); fine focus (used to focus the high-power objective); stage (where the microscope slide is placed); condenser (focuses light onto the object); iris diaphragm (used to vary the intensity of light reaching the object); and a built-in light source.

In the simple microscope (hand lens), the instrument is held very close to the eye and is mostly used today to observe external structure. In the compound (light) microscope, the objective lens forms an image (in the microscope tube) that is further magnified by the eyepiece lens, producing a greatly enlarged image. Coarse focus is used first to locate and see the specimen under low power; fine focus is then used to resolve the image under higher powers.

Tool 1: Using the light microscope
  1. Select a low-power lens, making sure it clicks into position.
  2. Examine the prepared slide without the microscope, noting the position, colour and rough size of the specimen.
  3. Place the slide on the stage, coverslip uppermost, viewing from the side, and position it with the stage adjustment controls so the specimen is lit up.
  4. Focus using first the coarse and then the fine focusing controls, using both hands to keep the controls working properly. Note: the image will be reversed and upside down when viewed directly. Always move the stage down, away from the objective lens, to avoid moving the slide into the objective lens (which could damage the lens and break the slide).
  5. For higher magnifications, swing in the relevant objective lens carefully, checking there is space for it, and adjust focus using only the fine control. If the object is centred with the ×10 objective, it should remain in view with the ×40 objective.
  6. When finished: turn the objective lens back to ×10 and lower the stage; remove the last slide and return it to its correct place; clean the stage and check the lenses are clean; unplug the cable and store tidily, replacing the dust cover.
Common mistake: microscope careNever force any controls. Never touch glass surfaces with anything other than a clean, dry lens tissue. Do not hold the microscope with one hand – hold the stand above the stage with one hand and rest the base on the other. Do not tilt the microscope; always keep it vertical (or the eyepiece may fall out). Do not touch lens surfaces with fingers. Do not allow solvent to touch a lens. When focusing, always move the stage down, away from the objective lens.

Biological material examined by compound microscopy must be sufficiently transparent for light to pass through. When bulky tissues and parts of organs are examined, thin, largely colourless sections are cut.

Preparing temporary mounts

Cells can be mounted on slides so they can be viewed under a microscope and then disposed of. Living cells are very small and transparent, so dyes or stains are commonly added to introduce sufficient contrast to differentiate structure; stains that are taken up by cells are especially useful.

Tool 1: Observing human cheek cellsTake a smear from the inside lining of the cheek using a fresh, unused cotton bud. Touch the material onto the centre of a microscope slide and add a cover slip. Dispose of the cotton bud safely and hygienically; at the end of the observation, immerse the slide in 1% sodium hypochlorite solution to sterilize it. To observe nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane, irrigate the slide with a drop of methylene blue stain and examine cells at medium and high power. Chloroplasts can similarly be observed in moss leaf cells, or the nucleus, cell wall and vacuole in an onion epidermis cell.

The eyepiece graticule and measuring cell size

A transparent scale, the eyepiece graticule, is mounted in the eyepiece at the focal plane, so that when the object under observation is in focus, so too is the scale. The size of the object can then be recorded in arbitrary graticule units. The graticule scale is then calibrated using a stage micrometer – a tiny, transparent ruler placed on the stage in place of the slide and observed at the same magnification. With the eyepiece and stage micrometer scales superimposed, the true dimensions of the object can be calculated in micrometres.

Step 1 shows a prepared slide of mammalian blood smear on the stage, with a red blood cell viewed against the eyepiece graticule scale (arbitrary units, e.g. reading 1.5 or 15 units). Step 2 shows the stage micrometer (a scale in known units, e.g. 10 µm divisions) placed on the stage in place of the slide and examined at the same magnification, so that the graticule scale and stage micrometer scale can be superimposed and the graticule calibrated. The red blood cell’s diameter, measured in graticule units, is then converted to a µm measurement – in this case, about 8 µm.

Once the size of a cell has been measured, a scale bar can be added to a micrograph or drawing to record the actual size of the structure.

Magnification and Resolution

MagnificationMagnification is the number of times larger an image is than the specimen. The magnification obtained with a compound microscope depends on which lenses are used – e.g. a ×10 eyepiece with a ×10 objective gives ×100 magnification (10 × 10); switching to the ×40 objective with the same eyepiece gives ×400 (10 × 40). There is no absolute limit to magnification – a photographed image can be further enlarged photographically, though this does not add extra detail (see resolution, below).
Magnification = size of image ÷ size of specimen
Worked example: magnification of a plant cellA plant cell of 150 µm diameter, photographed and then printed to show the cell at 15 cm diameter (150,000 µm), gives a magnification of 150,000 ÷ 150 = ×1000. If a further enlargement shows the same cell at 30 cm diameter (300,000 µm), the magnification is 300,000 ÷ 150 = ×2000.
Adding a scale bar to a drawing
  1. Use the stage micrometer and eyepiece graticule to determine the number of micrometres equivalent to one eyepiece graticule division.
  2. Remove the stage micrometer and place the specimen on the stage.
  3. Measure the length of the specimen in graticule units.
  4. Determine the actual length in micrometres by multiplying the number of graticule units by the length represented by one unit (e.g. 20 graticule units × 10 µm per unit = 200 µm).
Ideally, a scale bar should be about 20% of the length of the specimen as drawn. The actual length represented by the scale bar should be a whole number, so measurements may need to be rounded, with ratios then used to work out the correct length to draw the bar (e.g. if a 52.5 µm specimen is represented by a rounded 10 µm scale bar, and the drawing is 96 mm long, then 10/52.5 = x/96, so x = (10 × 96)/52.5 = 18.3 mm – the scale bar is drawn 18.3 mm long and labelled “10 µm”).
Magnification formulae (the “AIM” triangle)Where I = size of image, A = actual size of the object, and M = magnification: M = I / A; A = I / M; I = A × M. Remember to convert units so they are the same for both I and A before calculating (convert mm to µm by ×1000; convert µm to mm by ÷1000) – failing to do this gives results incorrect by a factor of 100, 1000 or even 1,000,000.
Figure A2.2.8: Memory diagram for calculating magnificationA triangle divided into three parts, with I (image size) in the top segment and A (actual size) and M (magnification) in the two bottom segments, used as a memory aid: covering the unknown quantity shows the calculation needed (e.g. covering M leaves I over A, showing M = I/A).

Resolution

ResolutionThe resolution (resolving power) of a microscope is its ability to separate visually small objects that are very close together. If two separate objects cannot be resolved, they are seen as one object – merely enlarging (magnifying) them does not separate them. Resolution is a property of lenses that is quite different from, and more important than, magnification.

Resolution is determined by wavelength: shorter wavelengths give better resolution. For the light microscope, the limit of resolution is about 0.2 µm, so two objects less than 0.2 µm apart are seen as one blurred object, however much the image is magnified.

Figure A2.2.9: Magnification without and with resolutionTwo images of a chloroplast at the same magnification (×6000). Image a) is enlarged from a photomicrograph obtained by light microscopy – it appears blurred, with no internal membrane detail visible despite the magnification. Image b) is a (colourized) transmission electron micrograph at the same magnification, clearly showing internal and external membrane structure. This illustrates that resolution, not magnification, determines how much detail can be seen – the membranes in image a) are too thin to be resolved as separate structures by the light microscope.

Nature of science: quantitative and qualitative observation

Biology is an observation-based science. Observations may include counting, drawing, photographing, video recording or measuring changes in concentration or pH. Quantitative observations involve numerical data (e.g. recorded values for a dependent variable, with units and associated random uncertainty, often digitally recorded and processed). Qualitative observations are descriptive, reinforcing quantitative data by recording the conditions or features relevant to a study. Observations and measurements are the basis for developing new biological hypotheses, and knowledge acquired by the senses is known as empirical data.

Biologists often “enhance” their senses using analytical instruments to study phenomena beyond the direct limits of human senses – for example, the electron microscope for studying cell ultrastructure, NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) spectroscopy for studying protein folding, and ultrasound for studying echolocation in bats, dolphins and porpoises.

Developments in Microscopy

Electron microscopy and cell ultrastructure

Electron microscope (EM)A microscope in which a beam of electrons replaces light, so the powers of magnification and resolution are correspondingly much greater. Ultrastructure is the fine structure of cells, determined by electron microscopy.

The electron microscope uses electrons to form a magnified image in much the same way as an optical microscope uses light. Electrons need to travel through a vacuum, so biological material examined must be dead. Because an electron beam has a much shorter wavelength than light, its resolving power is much greater – for biological material, the limit of resolution of the electron microscope is about 5 nm (compared to about 0.2 µm for light). Only with the electron microscope can the detailed structures of cell organelles be observed.

In the electron microscope, the electron beam is generated by an electron gun and focused by electromagnets rather than glass lenses. Since electrons cannot be seen directly, the beam is focused onto a fluorescent screen for viewing or a photographic plate for permanent recording.

TEM vs SEMIn transmission electron microscopy (TEM), the electron beam is passed through an extremely thin section of fixed biological material; membranes and other structures are stained with heavy metal ions, making them electron-opaque so they appear as dark areas in the image. In scanning electron microscopy (SEM), a narrow electron beam is scanned back and forth across the surface of the specimen, and electrons reflected or emitted from the surface are detected and converted into a three-dimensional image.
Figure A2.2.10: Using the transmission electron microscopeA diagram illustrating that electron microscopes have a greater resolving power than light microscopes, and that their application to biology has established the presence and structure of all the cell organelles.
Figure A2.2.11: Scanning electron micrograph of red blood cellsA colourized SEM image of red blood cells, each about 5.7 µm in diameter, showing their characteristic biconcave disc shape in three dimensions. The original image from the electron microscope is black and white; this version has been artificially coloured.

The nucleus is the largest substructure (organelle) of a cell and can be observed with a light microscope, but most organelles cannot be viewed by light microscopy, and none are large enough for internal detail to be seen this way. Because electron microscopes have much greater resolving power, they reveal structures – such as organelles made from membranes – not visible by light microscopy.

Cryogenic electron microscopy

Cryogenic electron microscopy is a technique involving flash-freezing solutions of proteins or other biomolecules and then exposing them to electrons to produce very high-resolution images of individual molecules, used to reconstruct their three-dimensional shape and thereby reveal their function. It is used to reveal how proteins work, how they malfunction in disease and how to target them with drugs; the most recent instruments can locate individual atoms within a protein. In 2017, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson for developing this technique.

Traditional TEMs keep the specimen under high vacuum with intense electron beams, which are incompatible with biological molecules – the water surrounding the molecules evaporates and the high-energy electrons destroy them. Because cryogenic electron microscopes use frozen samples, less intense electron beams can be used and evaporation of water is no longer a problem. Prior to cryo-EM, scientists relied on X-ray crystallography to view biomolecules – crystallizing molecules, exposing them to X-rays, and reconstructing their shape from the patterns of diffracted X-rays (this technique was used to reveal the structure of DNA).

Freeze fracture (freeze etching)

Freeze etchingPreparation of specimens for electron microscope examination by freezing, fracturing along natural structural lines, and preparing a replica.
The freeze-etching processBiological material is instantly frozen solid in liquid nitrogen (at atmospheric pressure, this liquid is at −196°C), so the water present solidifies instantly without the material changing shape. This solidified tissue is broken up in a vacuum, and the exposed surfaces are allowed to lose some of their ice (the surface is described as “etched”). Finally, a carbon replica (a “mask”) of the exposed surface is made and coated with a heavy metal, such as gold, to strengthen it. This mask is then examined in the electron microscope, producing an image described as being produced by freeze etching.

The right image, a replica of a freeze-etched surface, shows the nuclear membrane with its pores visible across the fractured surface. Both preparation methods give a consistent picture of nucleus structure, supporting confidence that the views of cell structure obtained by electron microscopy are realistic.

Fluorescence microscopy

Fluorescent dyes absorb light at one wavelength and emit it at another, longer wavelength. Some dyes bind specifically to target molecules in cells (e.g. DNA) and reveal their cellular location when examined with a fluorescence microscope. This is similar to an ordinary light microscope, except the illuminating light passes through two sets of filters: the first filters the light before it reaches the specimen, passing only wavelengths that excite the chosen fluorescent dye; the second blocks this light and passes only wavelengths emitted when the dye fluoresces. Dyed objects therefore show up in bright colour on a dark background.

Figure A2.2.14: Fluorescent imagingAn immunofluorescence image of cancer cells, with nuclei shown in blue, cytoplasm in red, and damaged DNA in green – each colour produced by a different fluorescent dye or labelled antibody bound to a different target.
ImmunofluorescenceA technique for determining the location of target molecules or cells by reaction with an antibody labelled with a fluorescent dye. Dyes can be coupled to antibody molecules, which act as highly specific and versatile staining reagents, binding selectively to targeted biomolecules (e.g. proteins or DNA) and showing their distribution in the cell. Such techniques have been used to explain the structure of cell plasma membranes and the mechanism of muscle contraction, and have allowed doctors to diagnose cancer from small tissue samples.

Structures Common to Cells in All Living Organisms

Common features of cellsA cell consists of a nucleus surrounded by cytoplasm, composed mainly of water, contained within a cell membrane made of lipids. The nucleus controls and directs the activities of the cell and contains DNA, the hereditary material of all living organisms. The cytoplasm is the fluid that fills the cell, the medium in which many metabolic reactions occur; water is essential for enzymes to function at their optimum. The plasma membrane (cell surface membrane, made of lipid and protein in a fluid mosaic arrangement) is the barrier controlling entry to and exit from the cytoplasm.

Newly formed cells grow and enlarge. A growing cell can normally divide into two cells; cell division is very often restricted to unspecialized cells before they become modified for a particular task, a process known as differentiation (process by which cells become specialized, as some genes and not others are expressed in a cell’s genome).

Figure A2.2.15: Animal and plant cells with interpretive drawingsPhotomicrographs and interpretive drawings comparing a human cheek cell (×800) and an Elodea (Canadian pondweed, which grows submerged in fresh water) leaf cell (×400), drawn at scale approximately relative to a human (1 m) and pondweed sprig (5 cm). The animal cell drawing is labelled with plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, secretory granules, temporary vacuoles and a centrosome. The plant cell drawing is labelled with a cellulose cell wall, the junction between walls (middle lamella), a pit where the cytoplasm of adjacent cells connects, chloroplasts (containing starch grains), cytoplasm, plasma membrane, nucleus, and a large permanent vacuole surrounded by a membrane.

Animal and plant cells have at least three structures in common: cytoplasm with its nucleus, surrounded by a plasma membrane. In addition, the cytoplasm contains many tiny structures called organelles (a discrete structure within a cell, having a specific function), most of which are common to both animal and plant cells.

Eukaryotes vs prokaryotesCells of plants, animals, fungi and protoctista have a large, obvious nucleus (with some exceptions) and cytoplasm containing many membranous organelles – these are called eukaryotic cells (meaning “true nucleus”). Bacteria contain no true nucleus and their cytoplasm lacks the membranous organelles of eukaryotes – these are called prokaryotic cells (meaning “before the nucleus”). This distinction between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells is a fundamental division, more significant than the differences between plants and animals.

Prokaryote Cell Structure

Bacteria and cyanobacteria (a group of bacteria that can photosynthesize) are prokaryotes. The distinctive features of prokaryotes are:

  • they are exceedingly small – about the size of individual organelles found in eukaryotic cells
  • they contain no true nucleus, but have a single loop of DNA in the cytoplasm, referred to as a nucleoid
  • their cytoplasm does not have the membrane-bound organelles of eukaryotes.
Figure A2.2.16: Generalized structure of a bacteriumA labelled diagram of a generalized bacterium showing: cell wall; cytoplasm; cell membrane; a food store; small (70S) ribosomes; a chromosome (always found); a capsule (a polysaccharide coat providing additional protection); a plasmid; an infolded cell membrane (e.g. associated with photosynthetic pigments, or particular enzymes); a flagellum (of simple structure); and pili (sometimes present).

All bacteria contain: a cell wall made from peptidoglycan (a polymer of polysaccharide and peptide chains); a plasma membrane; cytoplasm; naked DNA (not associated with histone proteins) in a loop; and 70S ribosomes.

Figure A2.2.17: The structure of Escherichia coli, with interpretive drawingAn electron micrograph of E. coli alongside an interpretive drawing labelled with a scale bar, showing: flagella (bring about movement of the bacterium); plasma membrane* (a barrier across which all nutrients and waste products must pass); ribosomes* (site of protein synthesis); a nucleoid* (genetic material: a single loop of naked DNA of about 4000 genes); a mesosome; a cell wall* (protects the cell from rupture caused by osmosis and possible harm from other organisms); a plasmid; a food granule; cytoplasm* (site of the chemical reactions of life); and pili (enable attachment to the surface of other bacteria, allowing conjugation to take place). E. coli is a common bacterium occurring in huge numbers in the lower intestine of humans and other endothermic vertebrates, and is a major component of faeces; it was named by bacteriologist Professor T. Escherich in 1885, and is typically about 1–3 µm in length – about the size of a mitochondrion in a eukaryotic cell.

Ribosomes are organelles where protein synthesis takes place. There are two sizes of ribosome: smaller 70S ribosomes, found in bacteria, and larger 80S ribosomes, found in eukaryotic cells. Unlike eukaryotic chromosomes, which are linear, with many contained in each cell, prokaryotic chromosomes are singular and form a loop within the nucleoid region of the cell.

Key termsOrganelle: a unit of cell substructure. Eukaryote: group of organisms that have a nuclear membrane surrounding their genetic material, i.e. they have a true nucleus. Prokaryote: unicellular organism without a true nucleus; they have a loop of naked DNA.

Eukaryote Cell Structure – the Organelles

The chemicals in the cytoplasm are substances formed and used in the chemical reactions of life. All the reactions of life are known collectively as metabolism (the integrated network of all the biochemical reactions of life), and the chemicals are known as metabolites (a chemical substance involved in metabolism). Cytoplasm and organelles are contained within the plasma membrane, which is the barrier that must be crossed by all metabolites moving between the cytoplasm and the cell’s environment.

Figure A2.2.18: The ultrastructure of the eukaryotic animal and plant cellSide-by-side generalized diagrams of an animal cell and a plant cell, built up from knowledge gained by examining many electron micrographs. Structures common to both are labelled: nucleus (with nuclear envelope, chromatin and a nucleolus), free ribosomes, rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) with ribosomes attached, smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER), a Golgi apparatus, a lysosome, a mitochondrion, and the cell surface (plasma) membrane. The animal cell additionally shows centrioles and small temporary vacuoles formed by intucking of the plasma membrane. The plant cell additionally shows a chloroplast, a cellulose cell wall, and a large permanent vacuole.

The nucleus

The nucleus is the largest organelle in the eukaryotic cell, typically 10–20 µm in diameter, surrounded by a double-layered membrane, the nuclear envelope (a lipid bilayer surrounding the genetic material, containing nuclear pores that control the movement of molecules between the nucleus and cytoplasm). This contains many nuclear pores (protein-lined channels regulating transport between nucleus and cytoplasm), about 100 nm in diameter – these are so numerous that they make up about one-third of the nuclear membrane’s surface area, suggesting communication between nucleus and cytoplasm is important.

The nucleus contains the chromosomes (lengths of DNA carrying specific genes in a linear sequence). These thread-like structures are visible when the nucleus divides; chromosomes are made of DNA bound to histone proteins, which help pack the DNA into condensed structures so it can be moved within the cell during division. At other times, chromosomes appear as a diffuse network called chromatin (nuclear material comprising DNA and histone proteins in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells at interphase; forms into chromosomes during mitosis and meiosis).

Common mistakeA common misconception is to regard a chromosome solely as a condensed structure visible with a light microscope, implying chromosomes are absent unless a cell is dividing. Chromosomes are continuously present in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell; the uncondensed state should be seen as normal, since this form persists longest and is when genes can be transcribed into proteins.

A nucleolus (plural, nucleoli; compact region of nucleus where ribosomes are synthesized) is a tiny, rounded, darkly staining body; one or more may be present in the nucleus. Chromatin, chromosomes and the nucleolus are visible only if stained with certain dyes.

Common mistakeDo not confuse “nucleus” and “nucleolus”. The nucleolus is a sub-organelle located inside the nucleus, whereas the nucleus is itself a membrane-bound organelle of the cell. The nucleolus produces ribosomes; the nucleus contains the genetic material of the cell.

Most cells contain one nucleus, but there are exceptions – both mammalian red blood cells and the sieve tube elements of phloem in flowering plants lack a nucleus, having lost it as they mature (see “Atypical cell structure” below).

Mitochondria

Mitochondria (organelle in eukaryotic cells, site of the Krebs cycle and the electron transport pathway) appear mostly as rod-shaped or cylindrical organelles in electron micrographs, occasionally more variable in shape. They are relatively large, typically 0.5–1.5 µm wide and 3.0–10.0 µm long, found in all cells, usually in very large numbers – very metabolically active cells (e.g. muscle fibres, hormone-secreting cells) contain thousands, and human liver cells can individually contain 2000 mitochondria.

The mitochondrion has a double membrane: the outer membrane is a smooth boundary, while the inner membrane is folded to form cristae (folds in the inner membrane of mitochondria). The interior contains an aqueous solution of metabolites and enzymes called the matrix (the fluid surrounded by the inner membrane, containing enzymes, ribosomes and mitochondrial DNA). The mitochondrion is the site of the aerobic stages of respiration.

An accompanying note explains that many of the enzymes of respiration are housed in the mitochondrion, and this is where the “energy currency” molecule ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is formed.

Ribosomes

Ribosomes are tiny structures, approximately 25 nm in diameter, built of two subunits, with no membranes as part of their structure. Chemically, they consist of protein and the nucleic acid RNA. Ribosomes are found free in the cytoplasm, bound to endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER), and within mitochondria and chloroplasts. The sizes of tiny objects like ribosomes are recorded in Svedberg units (S), a measure of their rate of sedimentation in centrifugation, not their actual physical size. Ribosomes of mitochondria and chloroplasts are slightly smaller (70S) than those in the rest of the cell (80S); prokaryotes also contain 70S ribosomes.

Figure A2.2.20: The ribosomeA diagram of a ribosome showing its two component parts: a small subunit and a large subunit, which together form the complete structure where protein synthesis occurs.

Ribosomes are the sites where proteins are made in cells. Some cell proteins produced by ribosomes have structural roles (e.g. collagen); most cell proteins are enzymes – biological catalysts that cause the reactions of metabolism to occur more quickly under the conditions found within the cytoplasm.

Endoplasmic reticulum

Endoplasmic reticulumA system of branching membranes in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells, formed into sheets, tubes or sacs that are extensively interconnected, and contiguous with (connected to) the nuclear envelope membrane. It exists as rough ER (with ribosomes attached) or smooth ER (without ribosomes).
Figure A2.2.21: Endoplasmic reticulum, rough (RER) and smooth (SER)A diagram showing SER and RER in the cytoplasm, originating from the outer membrane of the nucleus, alongside electron micrographs of RER (with ribosomes visible studding its surface) and SER (with a smooth surface). Roles of the endoplasmic reticulum are annotated: storage of calcium ions in (relaxed) voluntary muscle; vesicles containing enzymes to deactivate toxins; vesicles containing steroid hormones; and vesicles pinched off carrying proteins/enzymes for export from the cell.

Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) has ribosomes attached. At its margin, vesicles form from swellings and pinch off – a vesicle is a small, spherical organelle bounded by a single membrane. These sacs store and transport substances around the cell. For example, RER is the site of synthesis of proteins that are packaged in vesicles and typically discharged from the cell by exocytosis (e.g. digestive enzymes).

Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER) has no ribosomes and is the site of synthesis of substances needed by cells – for example, it is important in manufacturing lipids. In voluntary muscle fibres, a special form of SER stores calcium ions, important in muscle contraction.

Golgi apparatus

Golgi apparatusA stack of flattened membranes in the cytoplasm involved in the processing, modifying and packaging of molecules.

The Golgi apparatus consists of a stack-like collection of flattened membranous sacs. One side of the stack is formed by the fusion of membranes of vesicles from the SER and RER; at the opposite side, vesicles form from swellings at the margins and pinch off. It occurs in all cells but is especially prominent in metabolically active cells (e.g. secretory cells), and is the site of synthesis of specific biochemicals such as hormones and enzymes, which are then packaged into vesicles.

Figure A2.2.22: The Golgi apparatusAn electron micrograph of the Golgi apparatus in section, alongside a labelled diagram showing the stack of flattened, membranous sacs and the point where vesicles are pinched off from the margins.

Lysosomes

LysosomesMembrane-bound vesicles, common in the cytoplasm, containing digestive enzymes.

The Golgi apparatus produces a variety of vesicles; in animal cells these may form lysosomes. Some vesicles develop into non-permanent vacuoles (small vacuoles in animal cells used to temporarily store materials or transport substances), which help sequester waste products. Lysosomes are tiny spherical vesicles bound by a single membrane, containing a concentrated mixture of digestive (hydrolytic) enzymes, produced in the Golgi apparatus or by the RER.

The role of lysosomes in digestionLysosomes are involved in the breakdown of the contents of “food” vacuoles. For example, harmful bacteria invading the body are engulfed into tiny vacuoles by macrophages (special white blood cells, part of the body’s defence system). Foreign matter or food particles taken up into these vacuoles are broken down when lysosomes fuse with the vacuole, and the products of digestion escape into the cytoplasm. Lysosomes also destroy damaged organelles in this way. When an organism dies, the hydrolytic enzymes of lysosomes escape into the cytoplasm and cause self-digestion, known as autolysis. Lysosomes are also involved in apoptosis (“programmed cell death”), which occurs in cells damaged by infection or mutation.
Figure A2.2.23: LysosomesA cycle diagram showing the formation and role of lysosomes: vesicles from the RER fuse to form the flattened membranous sacs of the Golgi apparatus; vesicles of hydrolytic enzymes (lysosomes) are cut off (budded off) from the Golgi apparatus; a food vacuole forms at the cell membrane by phagocytosis; a lysosome fuses with the food vacuole, bringing hydrolytic enzymes into it; digestion occurs and useful products of digestion are absorbed into the cytosol of the cell, while undigested remains are discharged from the cell. A defunct organelle is also shown being broken down by fused lysosomes as part of this cycle.

Plasma membrane

The plasma membrane (cell surface membrane) is an extremely thin structure, about 7 nm thick, consisting of a lipid bilayer in which proteins are embedded. It retains the cytoplasm, forms the barrier across which all substances entering and leaving the cell must pass, and is where the cell is identified by surrounding cells.

Cytoskeleton

CytoskeletonA microscopic network of protein filaments and tubules in the cytoplasm that gives cells shape and is used for internal organization. There are three main types of fibre: microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments.

Microtubules (tiny, hollow protein tubes in cytoplasm, e.g. a component of the spindle) are the thickest cytoskeletal fibres – straight, unbranched, hollow cylindrical fibres about 25 nm in diameter, made of a helically arranged globular protein called tubulin, which is built up and broken down as the microtubule framework is required in different places for different tasks.

Figure A2.2.24: Structure of microtubulesSide and top views of a microtubule, showing it is built from a helical arrangement of soluble tubulin protein subunits, with a growing point at one end where new tubulin subunits are added.

Functions of microtubules: the movement of chromosomes during cell division is achieved by the lengthening and shortening of microtubules; they guide and direct the movement of other cell components within the cytoplasm (motor proteins carry organelles along microtubules to their destination – for example, guiding secretory vesicles from the Golgi apparatus to the plasma membrane); and they provide mechanical support and maintain cell shape, while also being dynamic – able to be quickly dismantled and reassembled elsewhere in the cell, changing cell shape.

Figure A2.2.25: Computer illustration of a microtubule with motor proteinsA diagram showing a vesicle attached via a receptor to a motor protein (powered by ATP hydrolysis), which moves along a microtubule of the cytoskeleton, illustrating how organelles are transported through the cytoplasm.

Microfilaments (the thinnest class of cytoskeletal fibres, made of solid rods of globular protein called actin) are about 7 nm in diameter, made of solid rods of globular actin protein, each filament consisting of a twisted double chain of actin molecules; they are designed to resist tension.

Figure A2.2.26: Structure and assembly of microfilamentsA diagram showing actin microfilaments built from globular monomer subunits assembled into a twisted double chain.

Functions of microfilaments: they are involved in cleavage furrow formation to divide the cell during cell division; they play a part in cell motility, particularly muscle contraction; and they maintain and change cell shape.

Top tip: cytoplasmic streamingIn plant cells, the flow of cytoplasm over actin filaments is important for the distribution of chemical substances within the cell. This cytoplasmic streaming is due to actin–myosin interactions.

Processes of Life in Unicellular Organisms

Unicellular and multicellular organisms share the same functions of life:

  • Homeostasis: maintaining a constant, stable internal environment
  • Metabolism: the complex network of interdependent and interacting chemical reactions occurring in living organisms
  • Nutrition: the process by which organisms take in and/or make use of food/nutrients
  • Movement: moving from one place to another
  • Excretion: elimination of waste created by metabolic processes inside cells
  • Growth: increase in size, mass or number of cells within an organism
  • Response to stimuli (sensitivity): reaction to changes in the environment
  • Reproduction: formation of new individuals by sexual or asexual means.

Differences in Eukaryotic Cell Structure Between Animals, Fungi and Plants

As well as the organelles that occur in all eukaryotic cells, there are structures found only in particular types of cell.

Structures typically found in animal cells (and some protists)

Centrioles: a centriole is a tiny organelle consisting of nine sets of three microtubules each, arranged in a short, hollow cylinder. In animal cells, two centrioles occur at right angles, just outside the nucleus, forming the centrosome (organelle situated near the nucleus in animal cells, involved in the formation of the spindle prior to nuclear division). Before an animal cell divides, the centrioles replicate, and their role is to grow the spindle fibres (structure formed from microtubules, associated with the movement of chromosomes in mitosis and meiosis).

Figure A2.2.28: Electron micrograph of a cell showing two centriolesA colourized electron micrograph showing two centrioles (highlighted in red) positioned at right angles to each other just outside the nucleus, together forming the centrosome.

Cilia and flagella: cilia (singular, cilium; motile, hair-like outgrowth from the surface of certain eukaryotic cells, moving rhythmically to propel objects) and flagella (singular, flagellum; a long, thin structure occurring singly or in groups, used to propel unicellular organisms and to move liquids past anchored cells) are made up of microtubules and are almost structurally identical, except cilia are usually shorter and more numerous than flagella. Both project from the cell surface and can move.

Cilia occur in large numbers on certain cells, such as the ciliated epithelium lining the air tubes (bronchi) of the lungs, where they move mucus across the cell surface (these are the cilia that cigarette smoke destroys over time); they are also found inside the oviduct, helping move the egg (ovum) from the ovary to the uterus, and in some protists (e.g. Euglena and Paramecium). Flagella occur singly, typically on small, motile cells such as sperm, or may occur in pairs.

A cilium is about 10 µm long and 0.2 µm in diameter; a flagellum is about 100 µm long and 0.2 µm in diameter. Both contain a ring of nine microtubule doublets surrounding a central pair of microtubules (the “9 + 2” arrangement), and both have a basal body at the base, identical in structure to a centriole, which anchors the cilium or flagellum to the cell. (Note: flagella of prokaryotes and eukaryotes have a different internal structure – some bacteria have flagella for movement, although structurally different from those in eukaryotes.)

Structures typically found in plant cells (and some protists)

Chloroplasts (organelle that is the site of photosynthesis and contains chlorophyll) are large organelles, typically biconvex in shape, about 4–10 µm long and 2–3 µm wide, occurring in green plants, mostly in mesophyll cells of leaves – a mesophyll cell may be packed with 50 or more chloroplasts.

Figure A2.2.29: The chloroplastAn electron micrograph and diagram of a chloroplast, showing two continuous membranes forming the envelope, and a third system of membranes tucked inside to form branching membranes called lamellae or thylakoids. The thylakoids are arranged in flattened circular piles called grana (resembling a stack of coins), which contain chlorophylls and other pigments. Between the grana, branching membranes are loosely arranged in an aqueous matrix called the stroma, usually containing small starch grains.

Most chloroplasts incorporate several grana, and the number of membranes in each stack is larger in leaves grown at lower light levels. Thylakoids (membrane system of the chloroplast) form grana (singular, granum; stacked discs of membranes within the chloroplast, containing photosynthetic pigments and the site of the light-dependent reaction of photosynthesis). The stroma (colourless fluid contents of the chloroplast; site of the light-independent reaction of photosynthesis) surrounds the grana.

Chloroplasts belong to a larger group of organelles called plastids (organelle containing pigments), found in many plant cells but never in animals. Other plastids include leucoplasts (colourless plastids, in which starch is stored) and chromoplasts (coloured plastids, containing non-photosynthetic pigments such as carotene, occurring in flower petals and root tissue of carrots, and anthocyanin, which shades and protects the photosynthetic apparatus by absorbing excess visible and UV light).

Permanent vacuole: a vacuole (fluid-filled space in the cytoplasm, especially large and permanent in plant cells) is surrounded by a single membrane called the tonoplast (membrane around the plant cell vacuole), which separates the vacuole’s contents from the cytoplasm. Its main functions include maintaining cell turgor pressure and regulating waste. As the vacuole fills with water it pushes the cytoplasm against the cell wall, creating turgor pressure that maintains the rigidity of plant tissue. Ions in the vacuole sap provide the negative osmotic pressure (potential) that draws water into the vacuole.

Chemicals can be permanently stored in the vacuole, such as pigments (e.g. betalains, the red pigment in beetroot), and carbohydrates, proteins and fats are stored in the vacuoles of storage cells in seeds. The vacuole also functions similarly to lysosomes in animal cells, moving waste material there for disposal; the environment inside a vacuole is slightly acidic (about pH 5.0), allowing hydrolase enzymes to break down large molecules, whose breakdown products are retained within the vacuole.

Top tipPlant cells frequently have a large, permanent vacuole present. By contrast, animal cells may have small vacuoles, but these are mostly temporary.

Extracellular components

Cells may secrete material outside the plasma membrane – for example, plant cells have an external wall, while many animal cells secrete glycoproteins.

The cell wall: the plant cell is surrounded by a wall completely external to the cell (it is not an organelle), primarily constructed of cellulose – a polysaccharide and an extremely strong material. Cellulose molecules are very long and arranged in bundles called microfibrils. Cell walls make the boundaries of plant cells easy to see under the microscope, and allow the plant cell to develop high internal pressure due to water uptake without the danger of bursting – a major difference between plant and animal cell water relations.

Common mistakeCell walls are not only found in plant cells – prokaryote cell walls exist as well, and cell walls are also part of fungal cell structure (although the chemicals used in construction differ in each case – peptidoglycan in bacteria, cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi).

Extracellular glycoproteins around animal cells: many animal cells can adhere to one another, a property that enables cells to form compact tissues and organs. Other animal cells occur in simple sheets or layers, attached to a basement membrane below them. This adhesion is brought about by glycoproteins (membrane protein with a carbohydrate chain attached) that cells secrete – large protein molecules to which a small number of sugar molecules bonded together (oligosaccharides) are attached by covalent bonding.

Fungal cells

Fungi are a large group of eukaryotic organisms that obtain their food by absorbing nutrients from their external environment. They cannot photosynthesize, as they lack chloroplasts. Fungal cells have a cell wall made of a different material compared to plants and bacteria – chitin. Most fungi are multicellular, although some, such as yeast, are unicellular.

Atypical Cell Structure in Eukaryotes

Although most organisms conform to the cell theory, there are exceptions. In addition to the familiar unicellular and multicellular organization of living things, there are a few multinucleate organs and organisms that are not divided into separate cells – known as atypical cells. Examples include the mould Mucor (an atypical organism) and striped muscle fibres (an atypical organ, or tissue) in mammals.

MultinucleateA cell that has two or more nuclei. Hypha (plural, hyphae): the tubular filament “plant” body of a fungus, which in certain species is divided by cross walls into either multicellular or unicellular compartments.
Figure A2.2.31: Atypical structure in MucorA diagram of the mould Mucor, whose main body consists of fine, thread-like structures called hyphae, showing a sporangiophore bearing a sporangium, with hyphae and rhizoids anchoring it, and a stolon connecting structures. A close-up diagram of the cell structure at the tip of one hypha is labelled with a cell wall, mitochondrion, vacuole, ribosome, nucleus and cell membrane – illustrating that the hypha is not divided into separate uninucleate cells (it is coenocytic/multinucleate along its length).
Figure A2.2.32: Atypical structure in skeletal muscle fibres of mammalsA diagram of skeletal muscle cut open to show bundles of thousands of muscle fibres attached via a tendon; a single individual muscle fibre (shown at ×300) is highlighted to show that each muscle fibre contains several nuclei – i.e. it is a syncytium, a single large multinucleate cell/tissue rather than a collection of separate uninucleate cells.

A further example of atypical cell structure is the mammalian red blood cell, which has no nucleus. This adaptation allows more room for haemoglobin (the red pigment that carries oxygen in the blood and gives the cell its red colour) and also creates the cell’s biconcave shape, giving a greater surface area for the diffusion of oxygen into and out of the cell.

Phloem tissue transports sugars as sucrose in plants, and consists of sieve tubes and companion cells. The sieve tube elements are another example of atypical cell structure – a mature sieve tube has no nucleus or many other organelles in its cytoplasm.

Figure A2.2.33: The structure of phloem tissueA diagram of phloem tissue showing sieve tubes joined end to end via sieve plates (shown both in section and in face view, perforated by pores), alongside companion cells connected to the sieve tube contents by cytoplasmic connections (plasmodesmata) passing through narrow gaps (pits) in the walls. An accompanying photomicrograph shows a longitudinal section (LS) of phloem at high power (×200), noting that phloem tissue may become crushed and damaged as stem tissue is sectioned for microscopy.

Sieve tubes are narrow, elongated elements connected end to end to form tubes. The end walls (sieve plates) are perforated by pores, and each sieve tube is connected to a companion cell by strands of cytoplasm called plasmodesmata, passing through narrow gaps (pits) in the walls. Companion cells are believed to service and maintain the cytoplasm of the sieve tube, since the sieve tube itself has no nucleus.

Limitations of cell theory
  • Striated muscle cells contain many nuclei, whereas most eukaryotic cells have one nucleus.
  • Red blood cells have no nucleus, whereas most eukaryotic cells have one nucleus.
  • Multicellular fungi, such as Mucor, have multicellular hyphal cells (not divided into discrete uninucleate cells).
  • Sieve tube elements in phloem tissue do not contain a nucleus, and rely on companion cells to provide metabolic support.
  • Viruses have some characteristics of living organisms but are not cells.

Higher Level Origin of Eukaryotic Cells by Endosymbiosis

Prokaryote cells differ from simple microspheres in a number of ways – for example, a circular chromosome of DNA is attached to the plasma membrane, and a cell wall of complex chemistry is secreted outside the membrane barrier. The first prokaryotes could have survived nutritionally on the organic molecules of the pre-biotic “soup”, where an abundance of organic molecules would have required only limited enzymatic machinery for early “digestion” and “respiration”, with greater biochemical sophistication evolving over time.

Present-day prokaryotes appear similar to fossil prokaryotes, some of which are 3500 million years old. By comparison, the earliest eukaryote cells date back only 1000 million years.

Higher Level The endosymbiotic theory

Endosymbiotic theoryTheory that suggests some organelles in eukaryotic cells (e.g. mitochondria and chloroplasts) were once free-living prokaryotic microbes that were incorporated into an ancestral host cell. Proposed by Lynn Margulis in 1967.

Evidence suggests that all eukaryotes evolved from a common unicellular ancestor that had a nucleus and reproduced sexually. The eukaryotic cell may have formed from large prokaryote cells that came to contain their chromosome in a sac of infolded plasma membrane, giving rise to a distinct nucleus. But how did the other membranous organelles originate?

Common mistakeA common error when explaining endosymbiosis is to say that the original engulfing process was carried out by a eukaryote rather than a prokaryote. The theory states that a larger prokaryotic cell engulfed smaller prokaryotic cells, ultimately forming the first eukaryotic cell.
Figure A2.2.38: Origin of the eukaryotic cellA branching evolutionary diagram starting from a prokaryotic cell, in which the nuclear membrane and endoplasmic reticulum form by intucking of the plasma membrane, giving an ancestral eukaryotic cell. This ancestral cell engulfs free-living aerobic bacteria, which are retained (rather than digested) as endosymbionts, forming an ancestral eukaryotic cell with mitochondria. A further branch shows this cell (or a descendant of it) engulfing free-living cyanobacteria (photosynthetic prokaryotes), which are retained as endosymbionts, forming an ancestral eukaryotic plant cell with chloroplasts.
How the organelles formedProkaryotic cells taken up into food vacuoles for digestion may instead have survived as organelles inside the host cell, rather than becoming food items. The double membrane of the mitochondrion results from this process: the outer membrane originates from the food vacuole, and the inner membrane from the engulfed cell itself. The engulfed prokaryotic cells were not destroyed or digested, and became integrated by natural selection into the biochemistry of their “host” cell over time, developing an endosymbiotic (mutualistic) relationship. If the engulfed cell respired aerobically, consuming oxygen, it would have provided the host with energy in the form of ATP, forming a mitochondrion; if it could photosynthesize, it would have formed a chloroplast.
Top tipEndocytosis would have allowed a free-living prokaryote to enter and remain in a host prokaryotic cell. Because the prokaryote made itself useful – for example, providing energy in the case of an aerobic prokaryote – there was a selective advantage in developing a mutualistic relationship rather than digesting the engulfed cell.

Higher Level Nature of science: what makes a theory strong – autogenous vs endosymbiotic theories

A scientific theory not only explains known observations and data, but also allows scientists to make predictions of what they should observe. Scientific theories must be testable; new observations should support the theory, and if they do not, the theory must be modified or rejected. The longer the central features of a theory hold, the more observations and data it predicts, the more tests it passes, and the more it explains, the stronger the theory.

The evolutionary origins of eukaryotes can be grouped into autogenous theories and endosymbiotic theories. Although both involve natural selection, they provide different explanations and novel predictions. In autogenous theories, all the structures and functions of eukaryotes are suggested to have evolved gradually from a single prokaryote ancestor, with infolding of regions of the cell membrane forming internal vesicles that subsequently evolved into the various organelles. However, the presence of DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts cannot be explained by most autogenous theories.

The presence of extra-nuclear DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts was discovered indirectly in the 1960s and 1970s; American evolutionary biologist Lynn Margulis carried out experiments providing indirect evidence for DNA in the cytoplasm of algae (i.e. in the chloroplast). In endosymbiotic theories, certain eukaryotic organelles are believed to have evolved from prokaryotic organisms that entered into symbiosis with an ancestor of eukaryotic cells (the proto-eukaryote).

The most widely accepted and supported variant of endosymbiosis theory hypothesizes that the mitochondrion and chloroplast evolved from bacteria. There is a broad range of biochemical, molecular and cell structural data strongly supporting this, especially for the chloroplast evolving from cyanobacteria. However, debate continues about the exact nature of the bacteria involved in the evolution of the mitochondrion in the proto-eukaryote. Margulis also proposed that the eukaryotic flagellum originated by endosymbiosis, but very little evidence supports this proposal.

The discovery of DNA in mitochondria and chloroplasts was supportive of the endosymbiont hypothesis, but it was the later evolutionary analysis of the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes themselves that provided the strongest evidence for the theory. The DNA sequences of chloroplasts were most similar to those of free-living photosynthetic cyanobacteria (as predicted by their shared photosynthetic properties and pigmentation), while mitochondrial DNA was clearly bacterial but did not initially have a strong connection to a specific type of bacteria. Around the 1980s it became clear that mitochondrial DNA was specifically linked to alphaproteobacteria (a large and diverse taxon of bacteria) and Rickettsiales in particular (non-motile, gram-negative bacteria associated with disease), a result that has stood the test of time.

Higher Level Cell Differentiation and the Development of Specialized Tissues in Multicellular Organisms

Unicellular organisms, although structurally simple, carry out all the functions and activities of life within a single cell – taking in nutrients, respiring, excreting, sensing and responding to internal and external conditions, possibly moving, and eventually dividing or reproducing. By contrast, most multicellular organisms – all animals and plants, the majority of fungi, and seaweeds in the Protoctista – are made of cells, most of which are highly specialized to perform a particular role or function.

Tissue and organTissue: a collection of cells of similar structure and function (e.g. heart muscle tissue of a mammal, or phloem tissue in plants). Organ: a part of an organism, consisting of a collection of different tissues, having a definite form and structure, and performing one or more specialized, coordinated functions (e.g. the heart of a mammal, or the leaf of a plant). So the tissues, organs and organ systems of multicellular organisms consist of specialized cells.
Figure A2.2.40: Tissues of part of the mammalian gutA diagram of a transverse section (TS) of the small intestine at low power, showing several distinct tissues: pavement epithelium (a smooth, strong sheet of cells); smooth muscle (arranged in circular and longitudinal layers, able to contract and relax repeatedly for long periods without fatigue); connective tissue (which binds together all the tissues); a peritoneum (smooth outer lining) with attachment to the body wall; and villi (finger-like projections into the channel of the gut).

Cells in a multicellular organism, though they all contain the same DNA, are differentiated to carry out specialized functions. They express (“switch on”) different sets of genes according to their developmental history and the signals they receive from their environment; changes in gene expression are often triggered by changes in the cell’s environment.

Higher Level Control of cell specialization

The nucleus of each cell controls and directs the activities of the cell, using information held in the nucleic acid DNA. The nucleus contains DNA in thread-like chromosomes, which are linear sequences of genes. A gene can be defined as a specific region of a chromosome capable of determining the development of a specific characteristic of an organism, or as a specific length of the DNA double helix (hundreds or thousands of base pairs long) which codes for a protein.

When a cell is becoming specialized, it is said to be differentiating: some of its genes are being activated and expressed, and these genes determine how the cell develops. The nucleus of each cell contains all the information required to make each type of cell present within the whole organism; only a selected part of that information is needed in any one cell or tissue. How a cell specializes, and which genes are activated, are controlled by the immediate environment of the differentiating cell and the cell’s position in the developing organism.

Common mistakeDifferentiation of cells is often dealt with superficially: if asked about this in a question, you need to refer to some genes being expressed and others suppressed, and not just say that “cells specialize”. The strongest answers explain how cells use genes selectively, and give specific examples of specialized tissue and their functions.

Higher Level Differences between the proteomes of cells within a multicellular organism

ProteomeThe entire set of proteins expressed by a genome, in a cell, tissue or organism at a certain time. Because the genome (the whole of the genetic information of an organism) is unique to each individual, the proteome it expresses is also unique.

The genome is the same in all cells of an organism, because all cells in a multicellular organism are ultimately derived from one original cell by cell division. The genome instructs the expression of proteins; because individual tissues have specific structures relating to their function, different proteins are expressed in each specific tissue cell type. Specific genes are expressed (turned on or off) in different cells according to a required function. As the proteome is all the proteins produced by a cell, the proteome varies with the function, location or environmental conditions of the cell through the process of cell differentiation.

Higher Level The cost of specialization

Division of labourThe carrying out of specialized functions by different types of cell in a multicellular organism.

Specialized cells are efficient at carrying out their particular function, such as transport, support or protection – the resulting differences between cells are due to division of labour. By specialization, increased efficiency is achieved, but at a price: specialized cells are now totally dependent on the activities of other cells. For example, in animals, nerve cells are adapted for the transport of nerve impulses but depend on red blood cells for oxygen, and on heart muscle cells to pump the blood. This modification of cell structure to support differing functions is another reason why no “typical” cell really exists.

Higher Level Evolution of Multicellularity

Many fungi and eukaryotic algae, and all plants and animals, are multicellular, indicating that multicellularity has evolved repeatedly. The broad distribution of multicellular taxa across the eukaryotic tree of life suggests that multicellularity is relatively “easy” to evolve, with few barriers. The various mechanisms needed for multicellularity (e.g. cell adhesion, communication between cells, and differentiation) found across taxa show that there are many ways it can evolve.

Multicellularity has the advantages of allowing larger body size and cell specialization. The frequent occurrence of multicellularity is most probably due to strong selective pressures favouring it, the few genetic changes necessary to enable the change, or a combination of these factors.
