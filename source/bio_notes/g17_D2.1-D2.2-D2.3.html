Generation of new cells by cell division

Cell theory states that all cells arise from pre-existing cells. Multicellular organisms begin life as a single cell (the zygote), which grows and divides repeatedly. Initially, stem cells can become any other cell type, but during embryonic development cell specialization (differentiation) occurs. In all living organisms, a parent (“mother”) cell divides to produce two daughter cells.

Key termsA parent cell is a cell that divides to form daughter cells by mitosis or meiosis. Daughter cells are the cells produced when a cell divides.

There are two types of nuclear division that lead to cell division: one produces genetically identical daughter cells (mitosis) and the other produces genetically varied cells (meiosis).

Cytokinesis

In cell division, both the nucleus and the cytoplasm divide. Division of the cytoplasm is called cytokinesis, and it follows the final stage of nuclear division. During division, organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts are distributed between the two daughter cells — these are the only organelles that can be made only by division of a pre-existing structure, so each daughter cell must receive at least one.

DefinitionCytokinesis: splitting of cytoplasm in a parent cell between daughter cells, following division of the nucleus.
Figure D2.1.1: Cytokinesis – division of the cytoplasmShows an animal cell in which the cytoplasm divides by in-tucking (invagination) of the plasma membrane at the equator of the cell, progressively pinching the cytoplasm into two halves until the two daughter cells separate.

In animal cells, division occurs by in-tucking of the plasma membrane at the equator of the cell, “pinching” the cytoplasm in half. A ring of actin, myosin and other proteins forms a contractile ring beneath the membrane that pinches the cell membrane together to split the cytoplasm.

In plant cells, this cannot happen because of the rigid cellulose cell wall. Instead, the Golgi apparatus forms vesicles containing new cell wall materials. These vesicles collect along the line of the equator of the spindle (a region called the cell plate), where they fuse to form the new plasma membranes and cell walls separating the two daughter cells.

Figure D2.1.2: Cytokinesis in a plant cellShows a plant cell after the nucleus has divided into two daughter nuclei. The endoplasmic reticulum secretes vesicles of wall-forming enzymes and the Golgi apparatus secretes vesicles of wall components; these vesicles collect at the midline (the cell plate, at the equator of the remains of the spindle) and fuse to form new cell membrane and cellulose cell wall layers, ultimately forming two separate daughter cells, each with its own cytoplasm.
Animal vs plant cytokinesisCytokinesis differs between animal and plant cells because plant cells have a cell wall and animal cells do not. Animal cells use a contractile ring of actin and myosin that pinches the cell membrane inward (a cleavage furrow). Plant cells cannot form a cleavage furrow, so instead they build a cell plate at the equator from Golgi-derived vesicles of membrane and wall material, which fuses to divide the old cell into two new ones.

Equal and unequal cytokinesis

When cells divide to form new cells within a body tissue, cytoplasmic division is usually equal, producing two daughter cells of the same size.

In some cases, however, division of the cytoplasm is unequal. In human egg production (oogenesis), one large egg (ovum) is formed along with several small cells that never go on to be fertilized. Oogenesis begins in the ovaries of the foetus before birth, but final development of the oocytes is only completed in adult life. An oocyte is an immature egg cell. When a primary oocyte divides, the cytoplasmic division that follows is unequal, forming a tiny polar body and a larger secondary oocyte. When the secondary oocyte divides, another polar body is formed. The polar bodies degenerate, and their cytoplasm and cellular contents are absorbed by the final ovum, so that one large egg forms rather than four equally small cells. The ovum needs a large cytoplasm to provide nutrients and energy for the growing embryo.

Figure D2.1.3: Unequal division of the cytoplasm in human oogenesisShows a primary oocyte dividing unequally to give a secondary oocyte (large) and a first polar body (small); the secondary oocyte then divides unequally again to give the ovum (large) and a second polar body (small). The polar bodies are shown as small cells that eventually degenerate.

Roles of mitosis and meiosis in eukaryotes

Nuclear division occurs by a very precise process, ensuring correct distribution of chromosomes between daughter cells. Nuclear division must happen before cell division, otherwise one daughter cell would end up with a nucleus and the other would not (an anucleate cell).

There are two types of nuclear division: mitosis and meiosis. Mitosis maintains the chromosome number and genome of cells, whereas meiosis halves the chromosome number and generates genetic diversity.

DefinitionsMitosis: nuclear division in which the daughter nuclei have the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Meiosis: nuclear division producing daughter cells (gametes) containing half the number of chromosomes of the parent cell. Diploid (2n): cells with nuclei containing two sets of chromosomes. Haploid (n): cells with nuclei containing one set of chromosomes.

In mitosis, daughter cells have the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell (typically two of each type — the diploid, 2n, state). Mitosis occurs when an organism grows, when old cells are replaced, and during asexual reproduction. In meiosis, daughter cells contain half the chromosome number of the parent cell (one of each type — the haploid, n, state). Meiosis occurs during sexual reproduction, normally when gametes are formed.

Figure D2.1.4: Mitosis and meiosis – the significant differencesShows a single cell about to divide (with nuclear membrane, nucleolus, and chromatin visible), with chromosomes first appearing as thin threads. One branch shows mitosis (replication): occurs when organisms grow, develop, and repair or replace tissues, forming two daughter cells with nuclei identical to the parent and diploid (2n). The other branch shows meiosis (reduction division): occurs during sexual reproduction (e.g. gamete formation in mammals and plants), forming four cells each with half the chromosome number, haploid (n).
Mitosis vs meiosis (overview)Mitosis produces two genetically identical, diploid daughter cells and occurs in growth, replacement and asexual reproduction. Meiosis produces four genetically varied, haploid daughter cells (gametes) and occurs in sexual reproduction. Mitosis and meiosis only occur in eukaryotes — prokaryotes divide by binary fission instead.

DNA replication as a prerequisite for mitosis and meiosis

DNA replication occurs during interphase, a stage of the cell cycle before nuclear division. Replication produces double-stranded chromosomes: each strand is called a chromatid, and the two sister chromatids are held together by a centromere, a specialized DNA sequence visible as the constricted region of the chromosome. During nuclear division, sister chromatids separate and move into different cells.

DefinitionsInterphase: the period between nuclear divisions when the nucleus controls and directs cell activity and replicates its chromosomes. Chromatid: one of two copies of a chromosome after replication, joined at a centromere. Centromere: constriction of the chromosome that attaches to spindle fibres during nuclear division.
Figure D2.1.5: Changes in chromosomes during interphase and mitosisShows a chromosome consisting of a single chromatid before interphase. During interphase, DNA replication produces a duplicated chromosome made of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. During mitosis, the centromere divides and the sister chromatids separate to become individual daughter chromosomes, one moving into each new nucleus.
Terminology checkRefer to the two parts of a replicated chromosome as sister chromatids while they remain attached at a centromere. Once separated into different cells during nuclear division, they should be called distinct chromosomes.

Condensation and movement of chromosomes

The total length of DNA in all 46 human chromosomes is over 2 m. A typical 5 μm chromosome contains DNA that would be about 50 mm long stretched out — roughly 50 000 μm of DNA packed into 5 μm of chromosome. Most chromosome protein has a support and packaging role, although some proteins are enzymes involved in DNA copying and repair.

DNA in the nucleus is packaged with proteins into a complex called chromatin. The coiled DNA double helix of each chromosome is looped around beads of histone protein, called nucleosomes. Histones are basic (positively charged) proteins rich in amino acids with basic side groups (–NH2), such as lysine and arginine, which allows them to bind the negatively charged DNA. The resulting “beads on a thread” structure (the chromatin fibre) is itself coiled, and these coils loop around a non-histone “scaffold” protein fibre. This whole structure is folded again (supercoiled) into the highly condensed metaphase chromosome.

DefinitionsHistone: protein rich in arginine and lysine that forms the scaffolding of chromosomes and is used in chromosome condensation to form nucleosomes. Nucleosome: the basic unit of eukaryotic chromosome structure, consisting of eight histone molecules wrapped by about 220 base pairs of DNA. Supercoiling: further twisting of the DNA double helix about itself within nucleosomes, forming a tightly coiled structure.
Figure D2.1.6: Histone proteins forming tightly packed nucleosomesShows the DNA double helix looped around a core of eight tightly packed histone molecules, forming a bead-like nucleosome; a chain of these nucleosomes forms tightly packed chromatin. Histone tails protrude from the nucleosome core; acetyl groups (–COCH3) can attach to lysine residues in the histone tails. Acetylation loosens the packing of nucleosomes, giving transcription enzymes access to the DNA, whereas tightly packed (non-acetylated) nucleosomes exclude transcription enzymes.
Figure D2.1.7: The packaging of DNA in the chromosomesAn electron micrograph/diagram showing the hierarchy of DNA packaging: the double helix wraps around histones to form nucleosomes (“beads on a string”), which coil into the chromatin fibre, which loops around a non-histone protein scaffold, which is itself supercoiled to form the highly condensed metaphase chromosome.

Chromosomes are moved during cell division using microtubules and microtubule motor proteins. Microtubules are cytoskeletal fibres that lengthen and shorten by polymerization and depolymerization of the protein tubulin. Motor proteins carry chromosomes along the microtubules to the middle of the cell in readiness for nuclear division.

Phases of mitosis

When cell division occurs, the nucleus divides first. In mitosis, the chromosomes (already present as sister chromatids formed during interphase) are separated and precisely distributed to two daughter nuclei, so that each daughter cell is genetically identical to the parent cell. Mitosis is described here as four phases for convenience, but it is actually a continuous process with no breaks between the phases.

Prophase

Chromosomes become visible as long thin threads, then progressively shorten and thicken by supercoiling. Only near the end of prophase can the two sister chromatids (joined at the centromere) be distinguished. The nucleolus gradually disappears and the nuclear membrane breaks down.

Metaphase

Centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell. Microtubules in the cytoplasm form a spindle radiating out from the centrioles. Microtubules attach to the centromeres of each pair of chromatids, and the chromosomes become arranged at the equator of the spindle. (In plant cells, an identical spindle forms, but without centrioles.)

Anaphase

Centromeres divide, spindle fibres shorten, and the sister chromatids are pulled by their centromeres to opposite poles. Once separated, each chromatid is now referred to as an individual chromosome.

Telophase

A nuclear membrane reforms around each group of chromosomes at opposite ends of the cell. The chromosomes decondense (uncoil), becoming chromatin again, and the nucleolus reforms in each new nucleus. Cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm) follows.

Figure D2.1.8: Mitosis in an animal cellA sequence of six diagrams for a cell containing a single pair of homologous chromosomes (for simplicity): (1) Interphase — nuclear membrane, nucleolus and chromatin visible; centrioles duplicate. (2) Prophase — chromosomes condense and become visible (each already divided into two chromatids joined by a centromere, though this is not immediately visible); the nucleolus disappears; the nuclear membrane breaks down; a spindle of microtubule fibres forms, radiating from a pair of centrioles at each pole. (3) Metaphase — chromatids attach to the spindle at the equatorial plate (3D view shows spindle fibres converging on centrioles at each pole). (4) Anaphase — centromeres divide and chromatids are pulled apart by the microtubules towards opposite poles. (5) Telophase — chromosomes uncoil, the spindle disappears, and the nucleolus and nuclear membrane reappear at each pole. (6) Cytokinesis — the cytoplasm divides, forming two separate daughter cells.
DefinitionsProphase: first stage of nuclear division where chromosomes condense. Metaphase: stage where chromosomes become arranged at the equator of the spindle. Anaphase: stage where chromosomes move to opposite poles. Telophase: stage when the nuclear membrane reforms around daughter nuclear material.

Identification of phases of mitosis

Actively dividing plant tissue, such as root tips, contains many cells undergoing mitosis. This tissue can be isolated, stained with acetic orcein stain, squashed, and examined under a high-power microscope. Interphase nuclei stain red-purple with almost colourless cytoplasm, while the condensed chromosomes of dividing cells are clearly visible, allowing the proportion of cells at each stage of mitosis to be assessed.

Meiosis as a reduction division

Meiosis involves two divisions of the nucleus: meiosis I and meiosis II. The parent cell is diploid, and meiosis produces daughter cells that are haploid. As in mitosis, chromosomes replicate into chromatids during interphase before meiosis begins. Early in meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair up; by the end of meiosis I, homologous chromosomes have separated, but the chromatids that make up each chromosome do not separate until meiosis II. There is no further interphase (and so no further DNA replication) between meiosis I and meiosis II — meiosis consists of two nuclear divisions but only one round of chromosome replication.

Stages of meiosis I

1. Chromosome replication occurs during the preceding interphase, forming sister chromatids.

2. As meiosis begins, the diploid parent cell’s homologous chromosomes (each already consisting of two sister chromatids) pair up closely along their length. Each pair is called a bivalent. The members of the bivalent continue to shorten (condense).

3. During condensation, chromatids frequently break; when non-sister chromatids from homologous chromosomes break and rejoin at exactly corresponding points, a cross-shaped structure called a chiasma forms. This event, called crossing over, exchanges lengths of genes between chromatids and generates genetic variation.

4. A spindle forms. Bivalents attach by their centromeres to spindle fibres at the equatorial plate.

5. Spindle fibres pull the homologous chromosomes of each bivalent apart to opposite poles — the chromosome number is halved — but the sister chromatids of each chromosome remain joined at their centromere.

Stages of meiosis II

Meiosis I ends with two cells, each containing a single set of chromosomes (still made of two sister chromatids). These cells do not enter interphase, but continue smoothly into meiosis II, which occurs at right angles to meiosis I and proceeds exactly like mitosis: centromeres divide and individual sister chromatids move to opposite poles. Following cytokinesis, four haploid cells are produced, each with half the chromosome number of the original parent cell.

Figure D2.1.10: What happens to chromosomes during meiosis?Shows a cell with a single pair of homologous chromosomes (2n) through the stages: during interphase, chromosomes replicate (copying occurs); during meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair up, crossing over occurs (visible as chromosomes separate), and homologous chromosomes separate into different cells, halving the chromosome number (n); during meiosis II, the sister chromatids of each chromosome separate and move into daughter cells; cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm, producing four haploid (n) cells as the final product of meiosis.
DefinitionsBivalent: a pair of duplicated homologous chromosomes, held together by chiasmata during meiosis. Chiasma (plural chiasmata): site of crossing over between homologous chromosomes during meiosis. Crossing over: exchange of genetic material between two homologous chromosomes during meiosis.
Accurate terminology for reduction divisionUse precise vocabulary when describing meiosis: “homologous”, “maternal”, “paternal”, “diploid (2n)”, “haploid (n)” and “random” help describe chromosome movement and how it generates genetic variety during the reduction division.

Germ-line cells, found in the gonads (testes and ovaries), undergo meiosis as part of the life cycle of every sexually reproducing organism. Halving the chromosome number in gametes is essential because fertilization doubles the number again, restoring the diploid state in the zygote. Without this reduction division, chromosome number would double every generation.

Down syndrome and non-disjunction

Very rarely, errors occur in the precisely controlled movement of chromosomes during meiosis. Chromosomes that should separate and move to opposite poles instead move together to the same pole. This malfunction is called non-disjunction, and it results in some gametes with more than, and some with less than, the haploid number of chromosomes.

DefinitionNon-disjunction: the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during meiosis. Non-disjunction can result in either an additional or a missing chromosome — not only an extra one.

People with Down syndrome have an extra chromosome 21, giving a total of 47 chromosomes (a condition called trisomy, where an extra copy of a chromosome is present). Symptoms of Down syndrome are variable but, when severe, include congenital heart and eye defects. The incidence of chromosomal abnormalities increases significantly with maternal age, so pregnant people over 40 are advised to have the foetus’s chromosomes screened. Non-disjunction resulting in trisomy also occurs (less commonly) in chromosomes 18 and 13, and occasionally other pairs.

In meiosis II, the two chromatids of that chromosome fail to separate and both go into the daughter cell that forms the secondary oocyte, producing gametes with an extra chromosome 21 (as well as gametes short of a chromosome). If a gamete with the extra chromosome takes part in fertilization, the zygote has a chromosome mutation (three copies of chromosome 21). Meiosis as a source of variation

The four haploid cells produced by meiosis are genetically different from each other for two main reasons: crossing over and random orientation (independent assortment) of chromosomes.

Crossing over

As chromosomes thicken early in meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair up along their length, forming a bivalent. As the chromosomes continue to shorten and thicken, the individual sister chromatids of each homologue become visible. During this coiling and shortening, breaks frequently occur in non-sister chromatids at corresponding points along their length. The broken ends rejoin almost immediately but, where the repair is between non-sister chromatids, segments of the chromatids are swapped — hence “crossing over”. The point of attachment after crossing over is called a chiasma, which stabilizes the bivalent. Virtually every pair of homologous chromosomes forms at least one chiasma, and having two or more chiasmata in the same bivalent is common. Chiasmata increase genetic variability because they exchange DNA between maternal and paternal chromosomes, producing new combinations of alleles on the chromosomes of the haploid cells eventually formed.

Figure D2.1.12: Formation of chiasmataA five-step sequence: (1) homologous chromosomes begin pairing to form a bivalent as they shorten and thicken by coiling; (2) breakages occur in parallel non-sister chromatids at identical points; (3) rejoining of non-sister chromatids forms chiasmata; (4) the positions of chiasmata become visible later, as the tight pairing of homologous chromosomes ends; (5) when homologous chromosomes move apart in anaphase I, the effect of crossing over becomes fully apparent, with chromatids showing new combinations of maternal and paternal segments.

Random orientation (independent assortment)

The way bivalents line up at the equator of the spindle in meiosis I is entirely random: which chromosome of a given homologous pair goes to which pole is independent of the behaviour of chromosomes in other pairs.

Figure D2.1.13: Genetic variation due to random orientationShows the nucleus of a diploid parent cell with two pairs of homologous chromosomes (four bivalents, carrying allele combinations such as A/a and B/b). In meiosis I, the bivalents move to the equator of the spindle and can line up in either of two alternative random orientations. Depending on orientation, homologous chromosomes separate to the nearest pole in one of two possible ways, so that meiosis II ultimately produces one of two alternative sets of four haploid gametes — in one orientation the gametes are AB and ab, in the other they are Ab and aB. The more bivalents present, the greater the possible variation: with 23 pairs of chromosomes in humans, there are over 8 million (2^23) possible combinations from random orientation alone.
DefinitionRandom orientation: the way chromosomes line up at the equator of the cell during meiosis, with each chromosome of a pair behaving independently of chromosomes in other pairs, so that each gamete gets a different combination of maternal and paternal chromosomes.

Meiosis II also contributes to variation: crossing over in meiosis I has already made sister chromatids non-identical, and during meiosis II these chromatids separate and are randomly distributed to the gametes, so each gamete can have a unique combination of genetic material. Finally, fertilization — the fusion of gametes from two different parents — adds further genetic variation.

Higher Level

Cell proliferation for growth, cell replacement and tissue repair

Both animals and plants grow from a single cell (the zygote) by repeated cell divisions to form an embryo. Proliferation (rapid increase in cell number) then occurs in animal early-stage embryos and in plant meristem tissue.

DefinitionsProliferation: increase in the number of cells as a result of cell growth and cell division. Meristem: a group of cells in plants that retains the ability to divide by mitosis and form new cells and tissues throughout the life of the plant.

Plant meristems

Once a plant has passed the early embryo stage, all further growth occurs at restricted points called meristems. Meristem cells are small, with thin cellulose walls and dense cytoplasm, and mostly lack the large vacuoles typical of mature plant cells. Meristems occur either at terminal (apical) growing points, or laterally.

Apical meristems occur at the tips of stems and roots and are responsible for primary growth: cells produced by division first enlarge rapidly, and this cell enlargement phase is followed by cell differentiation. Lateral meristems form from cambium cells (undifferentiated, indefinitely dividing meristematic tissue) located between the phloem (outer) and xylem (inner) in vascular bundles. Growth of the lateral meristem causes secondary growth — an increase in the girth (and strength) of the stem.

Early-stage animal embryos

Following fertilization, the zygote divides rapidly by mitosis to form a ball of cells. After about six days, a blastocyst has formed, from which the placenta and the main body of the organism develop. At this stage all cells are undifferentiated, with the potential to become any type of cell or tissue.

Figure D2.1.15: Cell proliferation in the early-stage embryo of an animalShows the sequence of rapid mitotic divisions of the zygote, forming first two, then four, then increasing numbers of cells, producing a solid ball of undifferentiated cells that ultimately hollows out to form a blastocyst.

Cell proliferation during routine cell replacement and wound healing

Cells in the body are constantly being worn out and replaced — the average adult human loses about 500 million skin cells per day, and red blood cells at a rate of 2 million per second. Cell replacement is routine for almost all cell types, except those which cannot undergo mitosis (such as mature nerve cells). Stem cells in the basal layer of the skin epidermis remain undifferentiated and continue dividing throughout life; their daughter cells differentiate and move up, leaving the basal layer, while the self-renewing basal cells maintain the process.

Wound healing also involves cell proliferation. After blood clotting, inflammation increases blood flow to the wound, allowing macrophages (white blood cells) and fibroblasts to travel rapidly to the damaged area. Macrophages remove infection, while fibroblasts produce proteins that help close the wound.

Higher Level

Phases of the cell cycle

The cycle of growth and division of cells is the cell cycle, which achieves cell proliferation. It has three main stages: interphase (which includes G1, S and G2 phases), division of the nucleus (mitosis), and division of the cytoplasm and whole cell (cytokinesis).

DefinitionCell cycle: repeating process of an orderly sequence of events where cells arise by division of existing cells, grow, and then divide.
Figure D2.1.17: The stages of the cell cycleShows the cell cycle as a circle divided into G1, S, G2 (together forming interphase), M (mitosis, itself subdivided into prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase) and C (cytokinesis), which then repeats. A graph beneath plots cell volume and quantity of DNA (both in arbitrary units) against time through the cycle: cell volume rises steadily throughout interphase and drops sharply at cytokinesis, while the quantity of DNA stays constant through G1, doubles during S phase, stays constant through G2 and M, then halves at cytokinesis. Labels describe each phase: G1 (first growth phase) — cytoplasm active, new organelles formed, intense biochemical activity; S (synthesis) — chromosomes copied (replicated) into sister chromatids; G2 (second growth phase) — further growth, then preparation for mitosis; M — mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase); C — cytokinesis, division of cytoplasm, two cells formed.

The steps of interphase

During G1 (first growth phase), new organelles are synthesized in the cytoplasm; this is a time of intense biochemical activity and accumulation of energy stores. During S phase (synthesis), each chromosome makes a copy of itself (replicates), forming two identical sister chromatids that remain attached until mitosis. During G2 (second growth phase), biochemical activity and cytoplasmic growth continue, in preparation for mitosis.

Higher Level

Cell growth during interphase

Interphase is always the longest part of the cell cycle, but its length is extremely variable. In fast-growing tissue (a developing human embryo, or the growing point of a young stem) interphase may last about 24 hours or less. In mature, infrequently dividing cells (e.g. liver, bone, kidney and lung cells) it lasts much longer. Some differentiated cells, such as brain cells, rarely or never divide again, remaining permanently in interphase.

Although the nucleus at interphase appears “resting” under a light microscope, it is metabolically very active: proteins and DNA are synthesized, and the numbers of mitochondria and chloroplasts increase by growth and division of these organelles. During interphase, chromosomes are actively involved in protein synthesis — mRNA copies of particular genes are made for use by ribosomes in the cytoplasm, where proteins are assembled in the sequence dictated by the mRNA. The compact chromosomes visible during mitosis become dispersed during interphase and are then called chromatin. Within the chromatin, one or more dark-staining nucleoli (singular nucleolus) are visible; these consist of protein and RNA and are the site of ribosome synthesis, after which the ribosomes migrate into the cytoplasm.

Higher Level

Control of the cell cycle using cyclins

The cell cycle is regulated by a molecular control system with key checkpoints — stop points that must be overridden by a “go-ahead” signal before the cycle can proceed. Three checkpoints are recognized: at G1, at G2, and in M. For example, at the G2 checkpoint, receiving the go-ahead signal allows the cell to proceed to M then C.

The molecular control signals are proteins called kinases and cyclins. Kinases are enzymes that activate or inactivate other proteins; they are present in the cytoplasm at all times, sometimes in an inactive state. Kinases are activated by specific cyclins, so they are called cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). Cyclin concentrations in the cytoplasm rise and fall constantly; as cyclin concentration increases, cyclin combines with CDK to form a complex called mitosis-promoting factor (MPF). As MPF accumulates, it triggers chromosome condensation, fragmentation of the nuclear membrane, and spindle formation — mitosis is switched on. By anaphase, destruction of cyclins begins (though CDKs persist in the cytoplasm). There is a threshold level of a specific cyclin required to pass each checkpoint.

DefinitionCyclin: regulatory protein whose concentration rises and falls at specific times during the eukaryotic cell cycle.

Synthesis of fresh cyclin begins in late S-phase; accumulated cyclin molecules combine with recycled CDK to produce MPF (mitosis-promoting factor); MPF triggers mitosis, with MPF concentration peaking in metaphase; cyclin is then broken down (degraded), leaving free CDK to be recycled for the next cycle.
Higher Level

Consequences of mutations in genes that control the cell cycle

When mutations occur in genes that control the cell cycle, normal cell-cycle controls are lost and cancer can result. Two types of genes are involved in initiating cancer when they mutate.

Proto-oncogenes vs tumour-suppressor genes

Proto-oncogenes encode proteins that stimulate normal cell division. Mutation converts a proto-oncogene into an oncogene, increasing the amount of the protein product or producing a permanently activated protein, causing uncontrolled cell division. Example: the Ras gene codes for a G protein that relays a growth-factor signal to a kinase cascade leading to normal cell division; the Ras oncogene codes for a permanently activated Ras protein that triggers the cascade even without growth factor. This mutation is dominant — only one allele needs to mutate for an effect, because the mutant masks the normal allele.

Tumour-suppressor genes encode proteins that inhibit cell division or promote apoptosis (controlled cell death) when damaged DNA is detected. Mutation causes loss, reduction, or permanent deactivation of the protein product, again resulting in uncontrolled division. Example: the p53 gene codes for a transcription factor that triggers cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis when DNA damage is detected; mutated p53 produces no functional protein, so damaged cells continue dividing. This mutation is recessive — both alleles must be mutated to have an effect, since one normal dominant allele still encodes functional protein.

DefinitionsProto-oncogene: gene that codes for proteins that stimulate the cell cycle and promote cell growth and proliferation. Oncogene: a gene that, when activated, can potentially make a cell cancerous — typically a mutant form of a proto-oncogene. Tumour-suppressor gene: a gene that in normal tissue inhibits cancerous behaviour; loss or inactivation of both copies can cause a cell to behave as a cancer cell.
Remembering the distinctionWhen a proto-oncogene mutates, it becomes a cancer-causing oncogene (gain of function). Tumour-suppressor genes normally prevent cancer; when they mutate (loss of function), they can lead to tumour formation.
Higher Level

Differences between tumours in rates of cell division and growth

Cancer is not a single disease but a group of diseases affecting different tissues, characterized by uncontrolled cell division by mitosis. In more economically developed countries, about one in three people will suffer from cancer at some point in their life and about one in four will die from it; common cancers include lung cancer in men and breast cancer in women, though rates vary greatly worldwide.

In cancer, the rate of cell multiplication greatly exceeds the rate of cell death, forming an irregular mass of cells called a tumour. Benign tumours grow slowly and do not spread to other parts of the body (non-cancerous). Malignant tumours grow rapidly, destroy nearby normal tissue, and spread throughout the body (cancerous).

Figure D2.1.19: Steps in the development of a malignant tumourShows normally dividing cells subjected to prolonged exposure to carcinogens (tobacco smoke, ultraviolet light, asbestos, X-rays). A carcinogen causes a mutation in a cell, which then undergoes repeated rapid mitosis because the cell cycle is no longer inhibited; the resulting abnormal cells do not respond to signals from other cells and are not removed by the immune system. A benign tumour absorbs nutrients and enlarges, possibly compressing surrounding tissue, but does not spread and can be surgically removed. A malignant tumour, however, consists of cells that secrete signals triggering growth of new blood and lymph vessels to supply the tumour; the tumour cells lose their attachments to each other, and malignant cells (with unusual chromosomes and disabled metabolism) may be carried via blood vessels or lymph vessels around the body, setting up secondary growths that impair or obstruct the function of invaded organs. This spread of cancer cells to new locations is called metastasis.
DefinitionsBenign tumour: grows slowly and does not spread to other parts of the body. Malignant tumour: grows rapidly, invades and destroys nearby tissue, and spreads throughout the body. Primary tumour: cancer growing at the site where the abnormal growth first occurred. Secondary tumour: formed when cancerous cells detach from the primary tumour, penetrate blood or lymph vessel walls, and circulate to form tumours elsewhere. Metastasis: movement of cells from a primary tumour to set up secondary tumours.
Tumour vs cancer“Tumour” is not equivalent to “cancer”. Tumours are growths caused by uncontrolled cell division, but benign tumours are not cancerous. Only malignant tumours are cancerous.

Calculating the mitotic index

The mitotic index measures the proportion of cells in a sample that are undergoing mitosis compared to the total number of cells.

Mitotic index = (number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells) × 100

Worked example: in a micrograph with 95 visible cells, of which 10 are undergoing mitosis, the mitotic index = (10 ÷ 95) × 100 = 0.105 × 100 = 10.5%. For accuracy, the mean of three or more samples of 100 cells should be used. The mitotic index is used to distinguish benign from malignant tumours (benign tumours have a lower mitotic index than malignant tumours); a high mitotic index indicates a rapidly dividing cell mass, a possible sign of tumour formation. It can also be used to monitor response to chemotherapy — a falling mitotic index indicates successful treatment.

Gene expression and its effect on the phenotype

Gene expression is the mechanism by which information in genes has effects on the phenotype. Information in the genetic code is transcribed into mRNA, which is translated into proteins at ribosomes; the proteins produced by an organism determine its phenotype. The most common stages of gene expression are transcription, translation, and the function of the protein product (such as an enzyme).

DefinitionPhenotype: observable traits of an organism resulting from genotype and environmental factors. Do not confuse genotype (the genetic makeup of an individual) with phenotype (the outward effect of the genotype on the body).

Gene expression underlies many processes across the course: transcription is the key stage at which a gene’s expression can be switched on or off; non-coding DNA sequences include regulators of gene expression; different patterns of gene expression underlie cell differentiation, often triggered by environmental changes; biochemical gradients affect gene expression in early embryos; intracellular receptors alter gene expression when a signalling chemical binds and the activated receptor binds specific DNA sequences; and auxin regulates gene expression mechanisms controlling plant growth and development.

Regulation of transcription by DNA-binding proteins

Transcription factors are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences to control transcription. Each transcription factor has a DNA-binding site enabling it to bind specific regulatory sequences called enhancer or promoter sequences. Enhancer sequences, when bound by transcription factors, increase transcription of an associated gene; they can be thousands of base pairs away from the gene, usually upstream (before) but sometimes downstream (after) of it. Regulation of transcription is the most common form of gene control, allowing unique expression of each gene in different cell types and at different developmental stages.

Promoter regions occur on DNA just before the start of a gene’s coding sequence. Only once a transcription complex (RNA polymerase enzyme plus transcription factors) has assembled at the promoter can transcription of the gene’s template strand begin. The rate of transcription can be increased or decreased by transcription factors binding to the enhancer site for a gene; when activator proteins bind the enhancer, a new complex forms and contacts the polymerase–transcription factor complex at the promoter, increasing the rate of gene expression.

DefinitionsTranscription factor: a protein that binds to specific DNA sequences to control the transcription of mRNA. Enhancer: regulatory DNA sequences which increase the rate of transcription when activator proteins bind to them.

Control of mRNA degradation as a means of regulating translation

After transcription, mRNA is examined, modified and transported to ribosomes for translation. mRNA is eventually broken down (degraded) by nucleases; in human cells it may persist from minutes to days. The 3′ poly-A tail must be degraded (shortened) before the rest of the mRNA molecule is broken down, and poly-A tail shortening is one of the key steps that initiates degradation once the mRNA is no longer needed for translation. Degradation of mRNA ensures that proteins are synthesized only when needed and that mRNA is removed once its job is done.

Epigenesis

Epigenesis is the development of patterns of differentiation in the cells of a multicellular organism, from an undifferentiated zygote, determined by the genome and by gene expression. Environmental factors can change the activity of genes, and these changes can be passed on to offspring, affecting their development.

DefinitionsEpigenesis: the development of patterns of differentiation in the cells of a multicellular organism. Epigenetics: the study of heritable changes in gene activity that are not caused by changes in DNA base sequences (epi = outside); mechanisms include DNA methylation and histone modification.

Epigenetic modifications change the way cells “read” genes rather than altering the DNA base sequence itself (which would be a mutation). Because DNA base sequences are unchanged by epigenetic modification, the phenotype — but not the genotype — can be altered.

Genome, transcriptome and proteome

The proteome of an organism is all the proteins expressed within a cell, tissue or organism at a given time. The genome (the whole of an organism’s genetic information) is the same in every cell of a multicellular organism, because all cells derive from one original cell by mitotic cell division. The genome instructs the expression of proteins, but because individual tissues have specific structures related to their function, different proteins are expressed in different tissue types (e.g. digestive enzymes in the gut, collagen in bone). No cell expresses all of its genes at once; gene expression determines which genes are switched on or off at a given time, since expressing genes costs energy and it would be wasteful to express unneeded genes.

Because the proteome depends on which genes are expressed, it varies with the function, location and environmental conditions of the cell (via cell differentiation), even though the genome remains the same. The genome is thus stable, while the proteome is dynamic.

The transcriptome is the range of mRNA transcripts produced in a specific cell or tissue type at a particular time, depending on differential gene expression; the transcriptome determines which proteins are synthesized, which in turn depends on which parts of the genome are activated.

Figure D2.2.1: The relationship between genome, transcriptome and proteomeShows the genome (about 22 000 genes) being transcribed, via alternative promoters, alternative splicing and mRNA editing, into a much larger transcriptome (about 200 000 transcripts); the transcriptome is then translated and, via post-translational modifications, produces an even larger proteome (over one million proteins). The diagram illustrates how one gene can give rise to multiple mRNA transcripts and multiple protein products, expanding diversity at each stage.
DefinitionsGenotype: the combination of alleles inherited by an organism. Transcriptome: the range of mRNA transcripts produced in a specific cell or tissue type at a particular time.
Genome vs transcriptome vs proteomeThe genome is stable and identical in every cell of an organism (with the exception that monozygotic twins share an identical genome to each other). The transcriptome actively changes, varying with developmental stage and environmental conditions, because it reflects which genes are being transcribed at a given time. The proteome is even more variable and reflects the final protein output of the cell, shaped by transcription, alternative splicing, mRNA editing and post-translational modification — so every individual (other than monozygotic twins) has a unique proteome.

Methylation of the promoter and histones in nucleosomes

Nucleosomes are stable, but not static, protein–DNA complexes; they can inhibit or facilitate transcription. Methylation is the reversible addition of a methyl group (–CH3) within chromatin, usually to the DNA molecule itself at the promoter region (though sometimes to histone tails). These chemical additions are called epigenetic tags. Enzymes add methyl groups to the base cytosine while the DNA is wrapped around histone proteins. Extensive methylation usually inactivates a gene, repressing transcription of the gene downstream; removing methyl groups can switch the gene back on. Methylation of amino acids in histones can either enable or disable the recruitment of regulatory proteins to the chromatin, activating or repressing transcription.

Figure D2.2.2: Methylation of DNAShows the DNA double helix with a methyl group added to a cytosine base, converting it to 5-methylcytosine (structurally, a methyl group –CH3 is attached at a specific carbon of the cytosine ring). The diagram indicates other sites along the DNA double helix where methylation has occurred, illustrating how methylation marks are distributed at multiple positions in the chromatin.
DefinitionsMethylation: the reversible addition of a methyl group (–CH3) within chromatin. Epigenetic tag: chemical tags (such as methyl or acetyl groups) added directly to DNA or to histone proteins to regulate gene expression, blocking or allowing access to a gene’s “on” switch.

Once a gene has been methylated, this state can persist from one cell division to the next. External conditions adverse to a cell or organism can leave a lasting mark on DNA through extensive methylation, leading to genes being switched off.

Epigenetic inheritance

Epigenetic inheritance is the acquisition of characteristics gained by a parent during their lifetime and passed on to their offspring, brought about by modification of chromatin via epigenetic tags such as DNA methylation or histone modification. Phenotypic changes in a cell or organism can be passed to daughter cells or offspring without any change to the DNA nucleotide sequence. This can occur if epigenetic tags remain in place during mitosis or meiosis; if the tags are removed during cell division, epigenetic inheritance does not take place.

DefinitionEpigenetic inheritance: an inheritance pattern in which a modification to chromatin alters gene expression in an organism; a parent’s experiences, in the form of epigenetic tags, can be passed down to offspring.

Methylation can last a lifetime and can be transmitted to offspring, and sometimes to further generations. A well-documented example: in 1944, wartime hostilities deprived communities in the western Netherlands of food for over six months (the Dutch famine). Babies born to starving pregnant mothers were underweight at birth but, despite being fed normally after the war, grew up to be heavier than average as adults with higher rates of obesity — it seems the parents’ starvation was imprinted onto their children’s DNA, causing the children to develop greater body mass as a survival strategy. This challenges the older view (from Mendel through to Watson and Crick) that genes are unchangeable by external factors, with the environment’s only influence being on natural selection of alleles rather than on gene performance itself; poor diet (for example) now appears able to interfere with gene performance in succeeding generations via inherited epigenetic markers.

Environmental effects on gene expression

Many external factors can affect gene expression, including diet, oxygen levels, humidity, light cycles, drugs, temperature, and exposure to mutagens. One well-studied example is air pollution.

Traffic-related air pollution includes particulate matter (small particles from diesel vehicles that can enter lung tissue and cause damage), ozone (O3), carbon monoxide (CO) and nitrogen oxides (NOx). Air pollution contributes to cardiovascular disease and lung conditions such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Research indicates that air pollution exposure can modify DNA and histone methylation patterns, which in turn can affect inflammation and disease development. Air pollution exposure is correlated with long-term negative respiratory outcomes, including interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) — diseases causing inflammation and scarring/thickening of lung tissues (bronchioles, alveoli, and surrounding capillaries), reducing the rate of oxygen diffusion into the blood and, in the bronchioles, causing asthma. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), a common form of ILD triggered by inhaling organic and inorganic substances (including air pollutants), is influenced by such epigenetic modifications. Exercise and B vitamins have been suggested as ways to reduce the impact of air pollution on methylation patterns and health.

Removal of epigenetic tags from ovum and sperm, and imprinting

Many organisms retain and pass on epigenetic marks from environmental exposure via gametes to the next generation. In humans and other mammals, however, egg and sperm cells undergo extensive reprogramming during development, which removes (“silences”) nearly all epigenetic marks. This reprogramming likely evolved to prevent environmentally caused methylation changes from being transmitted across generations. Any tag that is not removed may affect the development and function of the next generation.

For a small minority of genes (about 1%), epigenetic tags survive this reprogramming and pass unchanged from parent to offspring, through a process called imprinting. Imprinting involves adding epigenetic tags to DNA in sperm and eggs during gametogenesis (gamete formation by meiosis), switching “on” some parts of the DNA and “off” other parts. For most genes, two working copies are inherited (one from each parent), but for imprinted genes only one working copy is inherited, because differential silencing means either the maternal copy or the paternal copy is epigenetically silenced, depending on the gene. In sperm development, maternal tags are silenced; in egg development, paternal tags are silenced.

DefinitionImprinting: process by which only one copy of a gene in an individual (from either mother or father) is expressed, while the other copy is suppressed by epigenetic addition of chemical tags to DNA during gamete formation.

Imprinting in tigons and ligers

Tigons and ligers are lion–tiger hybrids. A male tiger crossed with a female lion produces a tigon, about the same size as or smaller than either parent. A female tiger crossed with a male lion produces a liger, which grows to a much greater size than either parent species.

Figure D2.2.3: Different patterns of epigenetic inheritance in lions and tigers explains different phenotypes in ligers and tigonsShows a female lion (120–180 kg) crossed with a male tiger (160–230 kg) producing a tigon that is the same size as or smaller than its parents, because the maternal (lion) growth gene is switched off (an anti-growth imprint) while the paternal (tiger) growth gene is switched on — but with no unopposed paternal growth signal, growth stays limited. In the reciprocal cross, a female tiger (140–180 kg, with no anti-growth imprinting defence) crossed with a male lion (170–230 kg, contributing a growth-promoting paternal imprint) produces a liger exceeding 400 kg, because the paternal growth gene is switched on and unopposed by any maternal anti-growth imprint.

This difference is explained by differing patterns of genetic imprinting between the two species, thought to have evolved because of their different mating systems. Female lions may mate with multiple males, so a male lion’s genes benefit from encouraging extra growth in his own cubs relative to potential half-siblings in the litter; in response, female lions have evolved anti-growth imprinted genes, since a lioness is equally related to all her cubs and benefits from distributing resources equally to maximize the number that survive. Tigers, in contrast, are typically monogamous per litter (a female tiger mates with one male, who is equally related to all her cubs), so male tigers have not evolved genes that encourage extra growth, and female tigers have not evolved anti-growth imprinting defences. When a female tiger (lacking anti-growth imprinting) is crossed with a male lion (which does contribute growth-encouraging genes), the offspring — the liger — grows unusually large.

Monozygotic twin studies

No two members of a family are genetically identical, except identical twins. Differences between individuals can be due to genetic factors (e.g. blood group), environmental factors (e.g. a suntan), or a combination of both (e.g. height and weight). The “nature or nurture” question asks whether phenotypic variation is due to genetic code (“nature”) or environment (“nurture”). Studies of monozygotic twins are designed to measure the relative contribution of genetics versus environment for a given trait.

DefinitionsMonozygotic twins: identical twins resulting from the fertilization of a single egg by a single sperm, with the fertilized egg then splitting in two; they share the same genome and are always the same sex. Monozygotic: derived from a single ovum, and so genetically identical.
Figure D2.2.4: Comparing monozygotic (identical) and dizygotic (non-identical) twinsShows monozygotic twins developing from a single zygote that splits into two genetically identical embryos sharing one placenta, versus dizygotic twins developing from two separately fertilized eggs, each genetically distinct, each with its own separate placenta.

Dizygotic (non-identical) twins develop from two separately fertilized eggs, each with a different combination of alleles, so DNA fingerprinting can distinguish them — unlike monozygotic twins, which cannot be distinguished by DNA fingerprinting because they are genetically identical.

Twin studies compare twins raised together or apart, measuring specific phenotypic traits and attributing variation to genotype, environment, or both. If separated monozygotic twins show similar characteristics, the trait is probably mainly genetic; if there are significant differences, environment is probably more important. Comparing monozygotic and dizygotic twin results further refines this: if monozygotic twins resemble each other more closely than dizygotic twins do, genetic factors likely play a role; if both types of twins resemble each other closely, shared environment is likely responsible; and because monozygotic twins share all their genes, differences between them must be due to non-shared environmental influences.

Although monozygotic twins share the same genotype, most are not perfectly identical (e.g. in physical features or disease predisposition), which can be explained by epigenetic changes — differences in DNA methylation and histone acetylation that accumulate over the twins’ lifetimes (methylation levels tend to increase with age).

In a 1990 twin study, geneticist Claude Bouchard fed male monozygotic twins an extra 1000 calories a day for three months to examine the importance of genes in body-fat storage and obesity. Every participant gained weight, but weight gain within pairs of twins was much more similar than weight gain between different twin pairs, and twins in each pair tended to gain weight in the same body regions — suggesting that genetic factors strongly influence fat storage patterns.

External factors impacting gene expression

The effect of a hormone

Steroid hormones such as oestradiol, progesterone and testosterone affect gene expression by binding to receptors in the cytoplasm; these receptor complexes act as ligand-gated transcription factors that bind to DNA (with the help of co-activators and co-repressors) to alter DNA transcription.

The effect of lactose in bacteria: the lac operon

In bacteria, gene expression is most commonly controlled at the level of transcription (regulating how much mRNA is made), although translational-level control (regulating the rate of translation of mRNA into protein) also occurs. In prokaryotes, operons are regions of DNA containing a group of closely related genes, consisting of structural genes plus regulatory elements: a promoter, an operator, and a terminator. Structural genes encode the enzymes of a metabolic pathway; the operator regulates whether these structural genes are transcribed.

E. coli, found in the human gut, has access to lactose when we drink milk. The lac operon ensures that E. coli only produces lactose-metabolizing enzymes when lactose is present, avoiding wasted energy and resources. The mechanism involves a repressor molecule (coded by a regulator gene), an operator gene next to the structural gene being regulated, and a promoter.

How the lac operon works

When lactose is absent: no lactose is available to bind the repressor, so the repressor molecule binds to the operator and blocks RNA polymerase from binding to the promoter, preventing transcription of the structural gene for the lactose-metabolizing enzyme (lactase). The gene is turned off.

When lactose is present: lactose binds to the repressor protein, changing its shape and inhibiting its ability to bind the operator. RNA polymerase can then bind to the promoter and transcribe the structural gene; the resulting mRNA passes to ribosomes in the cytoplasm where lactase is synthesized. The gene is turned on. Once all the lactose is metabolized, the repressor is free to bind the operator again, switching the gene back off.

Figure D2.2.5: The function of the lac operon in bacteriaTwo panels are shown. In the absence of lactose: the repressor is bound to the operator, physically obstructing RNA polymerase from binding the promoter, so the gene for lactase is not transcribed. In the presence of lactose: lactose has combined with and inactivated the repressor, so RNA polymerase can bind the promoter and transcribe the structural gene for lactase, whose mRNA passes to ribosomes for lactase synthesis.
DefinitionsOperon: a sequence of adjacent bacterial genes all under the transcriptional control of the same operator. Operator: a DNA region at one end of an operon that acts as the binding site for a repressor protein; when the operator is bound by the repressor, transcription is prevented. Lac operon: an inducible operon including three loci involved in the uptake and breakdown of lactose in E. coli. Repressor: the protein product of a regulator gene that controls transcription of an operon by binding the operator and blocking RNA polymerase. Inducer: a molecule capable of activating transcription of a gene by combining with, and inactivating, a genetic repressor.

The lac operon is an inducible operon: transcription is normally switched off but is turned on by the presence of a small effector molecule called the inducer (lactose). The repressor’s ability to bind the operator depends on its conformation, which is allosterically regulated by the inducer, so the concentration of inducer determines the operon’s activity. Operons are only found in prokaryotes; regulatory mechanisms in eukaryotes are more complex.

The effect of tryptophan in bacteria: the trp operon

E. coli synthesizes the amino acid tryptophan from a precursor through a series of enzyme-catalysed steps. The five coding regions for the tryptophan-synthesizing enzymes are arranged sequentially (starting with trpE) in the trp operon, and when E. coli needs to make its own tryptophan, all the enzymes in the pathway are synthesized together.

The trp operon is a repressible operon: its transcription is normally switched on but can be repressed when a small molecule (tryptophan) binds allosterically to a regulatory protein. The trp repressor is the product of a regulatory gene (trpR), located some distance from the operon it controls and with its own promoter. The trp repressor is synthesized in an inactive form with little affinity for the trp operator; only when tryptophan binds to the repressor does it change to an active form that can bind the operator and inhibit transcription of the structural genes.

Figure D2.2.6: The function of the trp operonTwo panels, each showing the promoter, operator, and the five structural genes (trpE, trpD, trpC, trpB, trpA). In the absence of tryptophan: the repressor (inactive form) does not bind the operator, so RNA polymerase binds the promoter and mRNA synthesis of the five tryptophan-synthesizing genes can proceed. In the presence of tryptophan: tryptophan molecules bind to the repressor, activating it; the active repressor binds the operator and blocks RNA polymerase, so mRNA synthesis is blocked.

Tryptophan acts as a co-repressor (in contrast to lactose, which acts as an inducer in the lac operon) — it cooperates with the repressor protein to switch the operon off. As tryptophan accumulates, more tryptophan molecules bind the trp repressor, which then binds the trp operator and inhibits synthesis of the enzymes of the tryptophan biosynthetic pathway.

Solvation with water as the solvent

Solvation is the interaction of a solvent with the molecules or ions (the solute) that dissolve in it. Water is an ideal solvent because of its polarity: the hydrogen ends of the V-shaped molecule are partially positive, and the oxygen end is partially negative. Water molecules can therefore interact with the positive and negative charges of a solute, forming hydrogen bonds or ion-dipole forces (with ions). Solvent polarity is the most important factor in determining how well a solvent solvates a given solute; polar water molecules orient the appropriately charged part of the molecule towards the solute, stabilizing the system and creating a hydration shell around each solute particle.

DefinitionSolvation: the interaction of a solvent with dissolved molecules or ions.
Figure D2.3.1: Solvation of a salt solute (sodium chloride) in a solvent (water)Shows undissolved sodium chloride crystal (an array of Na+ and Cl− ions) being dissolved by surrounding water molecules through ion-dipole forces: the negative (oxygen) pole of water molecules is attracted to and orients towards each positive sodium ion, while the positive (hydrogen) poles of other water molecules are attracted to and orient towards each negative chloride ion, progressively surrounding and separating the ions from the crystal lattice into solution.

Water movement from less to more concentrated solutions

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion involving only water molecules. Water is small enough to move through the phospholipid bilayer directly, though it also moves through specific pore proteins called aquaporins. Osmosis is the net (overall) passive movement of water molecules from a region of lower solute concentration (more dilute) to a region of higher solute concentration (more concentrated), across a selectively (partially) permeable membrane.

Figure D2.3.2: Water moves by osmosis from a less concentrated to a more concentrated solutionShows a membrane separating a dilute solution (low concentration of solute molecules) from a concentrated solution (high concentration of solute molecules); water molecules are shown moving across the membrane in both directions, but with a net movement of water from the dilute side to the concentrated side.

Depending on the relative solute concentrations either side of a membrane, a solution can be described as hypotonic, hypertonic, or isotonic relative to a cell.

DefinitionsHypotonic: the external solution is less concentrated than the cell cytoplasm, so there is a net inflow of water into the cell by osmosis. Hypertonic: the external solution is more concentrated than the cell cytoplasm, so there is a net outflow of water from the cell by osmosis. Isotonic: the external solution has the same concentration as the cell cytoplasm, so there is no net entry or exit of water by osmosis.
Figure D2.3.3: Flow of water into or out from a cell depends on the osmotic concentrationThree panels, each showing a cell membrane with aquaporins, cytoplasm above the membrane and an external solution below: (a) Hypotonicity – the external solution has fewer solute particles than the cytoplasm, so water moves net into the cell. (b) Isotonicity – solute concentration is equal on both sides, so there is no net water movement. (c) Hypertonicity – the external solution has more solute particles than the cytoplasm, so water moves net out of the cell.
Terminology checkThere is no verb “to osmosis”. Always say that water “moves by osmosis”, never that water “osmosises”. To remember hypotonic vs hypertonic: hypo = low solute concentration (relative to the cell), hyper = high solute concentration (relative to the cell).

Water movement by osmosis into or out of cells

The direction of net water movement can be predicted from whether the solution surrounding a cell is hypotonic or hypertonic. In an isotonic solution, there is no net movement of water because solute concentration is equal on both sides of the membrane; this is described as dynamic equilibrium — water continues to move across the membrane in both directions (the situation is dynamic), but there is no overall (net) change in water concentration on either side (the situation is at equilibrium).

Isotonic solutions still involve water movementIt is incorrect to say there is no movement of water in an isotonic solution — water does move across the membrane, but movement out of the cell is exactly balanced by movement into the cell, so there is no net osmosis.

Changes in plant tissue bathed in hypotonic and hypertonic solutions

When plant tissue is bathed in an isotonic solution, there is no net entry or exit of water and the tissue retains the same dimensions and mass. In a hypertonic solution, the tissue decreases in dimensions and mass (net water loss); in a hypotonic solution, it increases in dimensions and mass (net water gain). This principle underlies a classic experiment to determine the solute (osmotic) concentration of potato tuber tissue: strips or cylinders of potato are bathed in a range of sucrose solutions of known concentration, and changes in length or mass are measured after a fixed time. The concentration of sucrose solution that causes no change in length or mass is isotonic with the potato cell cytoplasm, and thus reveals its osmotic concentration.

Effects of water movement on cells that lack a cell wall

In an animal cell, the absence of a protective cellulose wall creates a serious problem for water relations. A red blood cell placed in pure water or a hypotonic solution will swell and burst (lyse) from the pressure of excess water entering by osmosis. The same cells placed in a hypertonic solution shrink due to net water loss; such shrunken cells are described as crenated, and are less able to move smoothly through capillaries, increasing the risk of blood clots. To avoid these problems, the osmotic concentration of body fluids (blood plasma and tissue fluid) in mammals is very carefully regulated to remain isotonic with the cells (an aspect of osmoregulation).

Figure D2.3.7: Osmosis in animal cellsShows three outcomes for an animal cell (such as a red blood cell) in solutions of different tonicity: in a hypotonic external solution, there is net inflow of water and the cell swells and bursts; in an isotonic external solution, there is no net water movement and the cell keeps its normal shape; in a hypertonic external solution, there is net outflow of water, the cell solution becomes more concentrated, and the cell outline becomes crinkled (crenated).

Osmosis in freshwater aquatic unicellular animals

Many unicellular animals live in freshwater, where the external medium is normally hypotonic to their cell contents, causing a continuous net inflow of water and a danger of the plasma membrane rupturing under internal pressure. The protozoan Amoeba deals with this using a contractile vacuole, a tiny water pump that continuously expels excess water from the cell. If the cytoplasm is temporarily anaesthetized (stopping the contractile vacuole from working), the organism quickly bursts, demonstrating the vacuole’s importance.

Figure D2.3.8: Amoeba – the role of the contractile vacuoleShows an Amoeba cell surrounded by pond water, which is less concentrated than the cytosol of the Amoeba, causing a net inflow of water over the whole cell surface membrane. Inside the cytoplasm, a contractile vacuole collects this excess water and periodically expels it from the cell by a pumping action, preventing the cell from bursting.
DefinitionContractile vacuole: a small vesicle in the cytoplasm of many freshwater protists that expels excess water.

Effects of water movement on cells with a cell wall

Osmosis in a plant cell, protected by a cellulose cell wall, produces different outcomes from those in animal cells. When the external solution is hypotonic to the cell solution, there is net inflow of water, diluting the cell solution; the permanent (central) vacuole swells, pushing its membrane (the tonoplast) against the cytoplasm, which in turn presses against the cell wall, increasing turgor pressure. A cell in this state is described as turgid. The developing pressure (from the stretched but inextensible cell wall) eventually prevents further water uptake; the cell wall protects the delicate cell contents from bursting, though the resulting tissue may be quite rigid.

When the external solution is hypertonic to the cell solution, there is net outflow of water, concentrating the cell solution. As the volume of the cytoplasm and vacuole decreases, the cytoplasm pulls away from parts of the cell wall (though contact may be maintained at points of cytoplasmic connection between cells). The cell becomes soft and less rigid — described as flaccid — and is said to be plasmolysed (plasmo = cytoplasm, lysis = splitting).

Figure D2.3.9: Osmosis in plant cellsShows three panels of a plant cell (with a fully permeable cellulose cell wall, a partially permeable plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and a permanent vacuole bounded by the tonoplast membrane): in a hypotonic external solution, there is net inflow of water, diluting the cell solution, and the cell wall is stretched (this stretching prevents rupture of the plasma membrane); in an isotonic external solution, there is no net water movement; in a hypertonic external solution, there is net outflow of water, the cell solution becomes more concentrated, and the cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall — the cell is plasmolysed.
DefinitionsTurgor pressure: hydrostatic pressure in a cell that pushes the plasma membrane against the cell wall. Turgid: condition where a plant cell’s vacuole is full of water, pushing the cell membrane against the cell wall. Flaccid: a plant cell that has become soft and less rigid because its cytoplasm has shrunk and contracted away from the cell wall through water loss. Plasmolysis: condition in hypertonic solutions where the plant cell membrane pulls away from the cell wall due to reduced cell volume and pressure.

Turgor pressure, made possible by the cell wall, provides support to plant tissues in the absence of a skeleton or shell. Plasmolysed plant tissue can survive for a time under hypertonic conditions, but not indefinitely: as the plasma membrane moves away from the cell wall, solute can enter the space between wall and membrane, and over time this can damage the membrane.

Medical applications of isotonic solutions

An intravenous (IV) drip delivers fluids directly into a patient’s circulatory system, used to treat dehydration (replenishing lost salts and water) and to deliver medicine. A sterile saline solution of 0.9% NaCl is safe to use because it is isotonic with body tissues and blood cells. If a hypotonic solution were introduced instead, it would lower the solute concentration of blood plasma, causing red blood cells to swell and burst (reducing oxygen delivery to cells); a hypertonic (high-salt) solution would cause water loss from red blood cells, making them move less freely through capillaries and increasing the risk of blood clots.

Similarly, donated human organs intended for transplant must be kept in a saline solution that is isotonic with the cells of the tissues and organs, to prevent cell damage from water uptake or loss during transit to the recipient.

Higher Level

Water potential as the potential energy of water per unit volume

It is useful to quantify the tendency of water to move from one place to another, to predict the direction and degree of water movement. Water potential is used to quantify the tendency of water to move from dilute to concentrated solutions by osmosis; it is the potential energy of water per unit volume. Because water movement generates pressure against a membrane, water potential is measured in kilopascals (kPa), and represented by the Greek letter ψw (psi). Because the absolute quantity of potential energy of water cannot be measured directly, water potential values are standardized relative to pure water at standard atmospheric pressure and 20 °C (which is given a water potential of zero).

DefinitionWater potential: the potential energy of water per unit volume, relative to pure water, denoted ψw (psi) and expressed in units of pressure (kPa).
Higher Level

Movement of water from higher to lower water potential

Water with a low solute concentration has a high water potential and therefore high potential energy for movement, whereas water with a high solute concentration has a low water potential and lower potential energy for movement. In a solution, water molecules form hydrogen bonds with solute particles, restricting the water molecules’ freedom of movement and lowering their potential energy — so water containing solute always has a lower water potential than pure water. Water therefore always moves from a region of higher water potential (lower solute concentration, higher potential energy) to a region of lower water potential (higher solute concentration, lower potential energy).

Higher Level

Solute potential and pressure potential

The water potential of a cell has two components: the concentration of solute (solute potential) and the pressure within the cell (pressure potential). Dissolving any solute in pure water lowers the water potential (makes it more negative); the highest possible water potential (zero) is found in pure water.

Figure D2.3.10: Water potential becomes more negative as solute is added to a solutionShows pure water with the highest water potential (0 kPa); a dilute solution of solute with a lower (negative) water potential, e.g. −50 kPa; and a concentrated solution of solute with an even lower (more negative) water potential, e.g. −100 kPa — illustrating that increasing solute concentration makes water potential progressively more negative.
ψw = ψs + ψp

where ψw = water potential, ψs = solute potential, and ψp = pressure potential. Both solute and pressure potentials are measured in kPa (their effect on water potential). Because adding solute always lowers water potential, solute potential values range from zero downwards (always zero or negative). Pressure potentials, resulting from water molecules pushing against the cell membrane and cell wall in plant, fungal and prokaryote cells, are generally positive inside cells; negative pressure potentials can occur inside xylem vessels, where water is drawn up the plant under tension.

Higher Level

Water potential and water movements in plant tissue

As established by the potato tissue experiments, plant cells gain water in a hypotonic solution and lose water in a hypertonic solution by osmosis. The changes in solute and pressure potential differ between these two situations.

In a hypotonic solution

As water moves into the cell, pressure potential increases (more water molecules exert greater pressure against the plasma membrane). The solute concentration inside the cell decreases (relative to the number of water molecules), so solute potential becomes less negative. Overall, the water potential of the cell increases, due to the combination of a decreasingly negative solute potential and an increasingly positive pressure potential.

In a hypertonic solution

As water moves out of the cell, pressure potential decreases (fewer water molecules exert less pressure against the plasma membrane). The solute concentration inside the cell increases (relative to the number of water molecules), so solute potential becomes more negative. Overall, the water potential of the cell decreases, due to the combination of an increasingly negative solute potential and a decreasing pressure potential.
