Water as the Medium for Life

Water covers approximately 71% of the Earth’s surface (97% of this is in oceans, only 3% is fresh water), giving Earth its appearance as a mostly blue planet from space. Evidence from the geological record shows water has existed on Earth for 3.8 billion years — almost as long as the planet itself, which formed an estimated 4.5 billion years ago. The first cells originated in water, where the oceans blocked harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun and allowed early life to evolve. Water remains the medium in which most life processes occur.

Water and living massWater forms a large proportion of living organisms — between 65% and 95% by mass of most multicellular plants and animals (about 80% of a human cell consists of water). Enzymes, the biological molecules that increase the rate of chemical reactions, must be dissolved in water to work: water provides a chemically stable medium for the processes of life.

Despite being taken for granted, water has a number of unusual physical and chemical properties (explored throughout this chapter) that allow life to exist at a huge range of scales, from the smallest bacteria to the tallest tree. Without water, life as we understand it would not exist on Earth.

A brief note on the nature of science: claims that water can retain a “memory” of substances previously dissolved in it (the central idea behind homeopathy) are considered pseudoscientific. Careful replication attempts by independent research groups failed to reproduce the original claims, and the idea of a chemical “memory” contradicts well-established physical chemistry. This illustrates two hallmarks that separate science from pseudoscience: genuine scientific claims must be falsifiable and must be checked by replication, rather than only being supported by evidence that confirms them (a bias known as confirmation bias).

Hydrogen Bonds in Water

A water molecule consists of one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms, sharing pairs of electrons. The molecule is V-shaped (bond angle about 104.5°), not linear. Because the oxygen nucleus draws the shared electrons (negatively charged) away from the hydrogen nuclei (positively charged), the molecule carries an unequal distribution of electrical charge even though it is electrically neutral overall: there is a small negative charge (δ−) on the oxygen atom and small positive charges (δ+) on the hydrogen atoms. This arrangement makes water a polar molecule.

Key definitionsCovalent bond: a bond between atoms in which pairs of electrons are shared. Polar molecule: a molecule with an unequal distribution of electrical charge — one end is slightly positive and the other slightly negative. Hydrogen bond: a weak attractive intermolecular force in which a hydrogen atom in one molecule is attracted to an electronegative atom, such as oxygen, in a different molecule.

Because water molecules are polar, the positively charged hydrogen atoms of one water molecule are attracted to the negatively charged oxygen atoms of nearby water molecules. These attractive intermolecular forces are called hydrogen bonds. They are weak compared with covalent bonds, yet they are strong enough to hold water molecules together and to attract water to charged particles or charged surfaces. Hydrogen bonds account for most of water’s unique properties.

Common mistakeDo not confuse bonding within a water molecule with bonding between water molecules. Covalent bonding (intra-molecular) holds the two hydrogen atoms to the oxygen atom within a single water molecule. Hydrogen bonding (inter-molecular) forms between separate water molecules and is much weaker.

Nature of science – using technology: researchers at Queen’s University Belfast used computer simulations to explore how changes to water’s molecular structure would affect its properties. They found that if the 104.5° bond angle were changed to 90°, or if the hydrogen bonds were made about 15% weaker, the three-dimensional network of hydrogen bonds that gives liquid water its unique properties would be severely disrupted or collapse — illustrating just how finely tuned water’s structure is for sustaining its unusual properties, and how computer modelling lets scientists test such “what if” questions that cannot easily be tested in the laboratory.

Cohesion of Water Molecules

CohesionThe force by which individual molecules of the same type attract and associate (“stick together”). Water molecules continually form and break hydrogen bonds with their neighbours, but at any one moment a large number of molecules are held together by these bonds, giving water unusually strong cohesive properties for a liquid — strong enough to resist “breaking” under tension.
Common mistakeA single hydrogen bond is a weak interaction — hydrogen bonds are not individually strong. It is only because there are enormous numbers of hydrogen bonds in a body of water that they collectively exert large cohesive forces.
Figure A1.1.4: Water drawn up a tree trunk through xylem vesselsA cross-section of a tree trunk showing xylem vessels running as continuous narrow tubes from the roots to the leaves. Labels trace the pathway: water is taken up from the soil by root cells; heat from sunlight causes evaporation of water from the leaves (transpiration); this evaporative loss generates a force that draws the water column up the trunk. Because the water column coheres (does not break) and adheres to the walls of the xylem vessels, and because its viscosity is low, it flows smoothly through the vessels and can be drawn up to great heights without the column breaking or pulling apart.

Related to cohesion is surface tension: a property of the surface of a liquid that allows it to resist an external force, arising from cohesion between water molecules. Water has a very high surface tension — higher than any other liquid except mercury. Molecules on the surface have no water molecules above them, so they experience a net inward force from their neighbours below and to the sides; within the bulk of the liquid, cohesive forces from all directions cancel out and there is no net force. This net inward force causes the surface to contract and resist being stretched or broken, hence “surface tension”. It allows water to form almost perfectly spherical droplets.

Figure A1.1.5: Hydrogen bonding at the surface of waterA depth cross-section of water showing many H2O molecules. Molecules below the surface form hydrogen bonds with neighbours in every direction (up, down and sideways), so the forces cancel and there is no net pull in any one direction. Molecules right at the surface can only form hydrogen bonds with neighbours below and beside them (not above, since there is air rather than water there), so they are pulled more strongly inward and sideways, forming a taut, hexagonally arranged lattice of stronger bonds at the surface. This lattice is what produces water’s unusually high surface tension.
Figure A1.1.6: A pond skater on the water surfacePart (a) shows a pond skater insect standing on top of a body of water, its thin legs resting on the surface without breaking through. Part (b) is a close-up showing that the water surface is visibly depressed (dimpled downward) under each of the insect’s feet, but the surface does not break because the hydrogen bonds between surface water molecules hold it together. The insect’s waxy cuticle stops its body becoming wetted, and its mass is not great enough to overcome the surface tension.

Below the surface, water molecules slide past one another very easily — a property described as low viscosity (viscosity is a measure of a fluid’s resistance to flow). Because of its low viscosity, water flows readily through narrow capillaries, tiny gaps and pores. The rate at which molecules diffuse through a solvent such as water is inversely proportional to the solvent’s viscosity, and viscosity itself falls as temperature rises (the viscosity of water at 25°C is roughly half that at 4°C) — which matters because diffusion rate is critical to many life-sustaining processes.

Adhesion of Water and Capillary Action

AdhesionThe force by which individual molecules cling to surrounding materials and surfaces (as distinct from cohesion, the attraction between water molecules themselves). Materials with an affinity for water are described as hydrophilic (“water-loving”), e.g. hydrogen bonds readily form between such a material and water.
Cohesion vs adhesion – common mistakeThese terms are sometimes wrongly treated as interchangeable. Cohesion (“co” = together) is attraction between water molecules; adhesion (“ad” = toward) is attraction of water to a surface. In xylem transport, cohesion is by far the more significant force — it is what lets the water column resist the tension generated by transpiration. Adhesion becomes significant mainly when air-filled xylem vessels need to refill with sap under positive pressure, which happens rarely (no more than once a year).
Figure A1.1.7: Adhesive and cohesive forces in a xylem vesselA narrow xylem vessel shown in cross-section with water molecules inside. Cohesive forces (hydrogen bonds between neighbouring water molecules, each showing δ+ hydrogens and δ− oxygen) hold the column of water together in the centre of the vessel; adhesive forces attract water molecules at the edge of the column to the polar/charged wall of the xylem vessel, helping to support the water column against gravity.

Soil contains many vertical, thin channels called capillary tubes (channels with a very small internal diameter), in which plant roots are located. Capillary action (or capillarity) is the tendency of a liquid to move up against gravity within such a narrow tube: adhesion between water molecules and the tube wall draws water upward, letting plants draw water up from the water table to their roots when the ground is dry.

Capillary action in plant cell wallsPlant cell walls are made from the fibrous, polar/hydrophilic material cellulose. Fibrous materials can act like wicks, drawing water up into the material by capillary action. Cell walls therefore draw water by capillary action from nearby xylem vessels, keeping water flowing through plant tissue. Cells directly exposed to air — such as the spongy mesophyll cells of a leaf — are kept constantly wetted this way: water evaporates from their moist, blotting-paper-like cell walls and then diffuses out of the leaf through pores (stomata) on the leaf surface, which is what enables water to be continuously transported up through the whole plant.

Water as a Solvent

Hydrogen bonds pull water molecules very close together, because the potential energy of the hydrogen bonds exceeds the kinetic energy of the water molecules up to 100°C at atmospheric pressure — this is why water is liquid across the temperature and pressure ranges found over most of the Earth’s surface, giving a liquid medium with distinctive thermal and solvent properties.

Key definitionsSolute: a dissolved molecule or ion in a solution. Solvent: a liquid in which another substance can be dissolved. Hydrophobic: repelled by water.

Water is a powerful solvent for polar and ionic substances. When an ionic compound such as sodium chloride (Na+ and Cl−) dissolves, every cation (positive ion) and anion (negative ion) becomes surrounded by a shell of orientated water molecules, with the appropriately charged end of each water molecule facing the ion.

Figure A1.1.9: The hydration of sodium and chloride ionsA central Na+ ion is shown surrounded by several water molecules, each orientated with its negative (oxygen, δ−) end pointing toward the positive ion. A separate Cl− ion is shown surrounded by water molecules orientated the opposite way, with their positive (hydrogen, δ+) ends pointing toward the negative ion. This ordered shell of water molecules around each ion is what allows ionic solids to dissolve in water.

A diverse range of hydrophilic molecules dissolve in water: carbon-containing (organic) molecules with ionized groups (e.g. amino acids, which carry a negatively charged carboxyl group, –COO−, and a positively charged amino group, –NH3+), and soluble organic molecules such as sugars, which dissolve because hydrogen bonds form with their slightly charged hydroxyl (–OH) groups. Once dissolved, solute molecules or ions are free to move by diffusion and become more chemically reactive than in the undissolved solid.

Non-polar substances, by contrast, are repelled by water (e.g. oil on the surface of water) and are described as hydrophobic. Some cell molecules depend on being hydrophobic and insoluble to function — for example, the cell membrane is built from phospholipids whose hydrophobic tails form the internal structure of the membrane.

Of the common atmospheric gases, only carbon dioxide (CO2) is particularly soluble in water; oxygen (O2) and nitrogen (N2) are only slightly soluble. CO2 is moderately soluble because a proportion of it reacts chemically to form carbonic acid (H2CO3), which immediately dissociates into hydrogen ions (H+) and hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO3−). Oxygen and nitrogen, being non-polar, do not form hydrogen bonds with water and do not ionise or dissociate, which is why they dissolve poorly. One consequence of oxygen’s poor solubility in water is the evolution of respiratory pigments such as haemoglobin, which greatly increase the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood compared with plain water.

Most enzymes catalyse reactions in aqueous solution: enzymes require a certain level of water within their structure to maintain their shape and stability, and most naturally occurring enzymes cannot form their active shape without being immersed in water. Hydrogen bonds often act as bridges between an enzyme’s binding site and its substrate. Some enzymes, however, are held in a fixed position (e.g. within a cell membrane), which allows reactions to be localized to particular sites in the cell.

Physical Properties of Water and Aquatic Habitats

The physical properties of water — buoyancy, viscosity, thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity — all depend on the hydrogen bonding between water molecules.

Key definitionsBuoyancy: the ability of any fluid to provide a vertical upward force on an object placed in or on it — objects float when their average density is less than that of water, and sink when denser (density = mass per unit volume). Thermal conductivity (k): a measure of how easily heat flows through a material. Specific heat capacity: the amount of energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C.
Common mistakeDo not confuse “heat capacity” with “specific heat capacity”. Heat capacity is the amount of heat needed to change the temperature of a whole body by one degree, and so depends on the mass/volume of that body. Specific heat capacity is the heat energy needed per unit mass, and so is independent of the total mass or volume of the sample.

The importance of these properties for life can be illustrated using two animals that live both in water and in air/on land: the black-throated loon (Gavia arctica), a diving bird that catches fish underwater and breeds around deep freshwater lakes across northern Europe, Alaska and Asia; and the ringed seal (Pusa hispida), which lives in Arctic and sub-arctic regions, spending time both on ice and swimming beneath it.

Specific heat capacity

A relatively large amount of energy is needed to raise water’s temperature, because a great deal of energy is required to break the large number of hydrogen bonds restricting the movement of water molecules. Consequently, aquatic environments (rivers, lakes, seas) change temperature very slowly compared with terrestrial environments, giving them more stable temperatures. Since organisms and their cells are largely composed of water, this same property provides internal “thermal cushioning”, protecting cells and organisms from large temperature fluctuations. Relatively stable sea temperatures let seals feed and live year-round, while air’s lower specific heat capacity means air temperature fluctuates more.

Unusually, water floats when it freezes, because ice is less dense than liquid water (water reaches its maximum density at 4°C) — a consequence of how hydrogen bonds arrange water molecules in ice. The resulting ice platform lets ringed seals live on top of the sea; the seals use their claws to dig breathing holes through the ice so they can live under and on the ice year-round.

Figure A1.1.11: The open lattice structure of iceWater molecules in ice are shown hydrogen-bonded to each other in a rigid, open, tetrahedral lattice arrangement (each molecule bonded to four neighbours in a spaced-out three-dimensional framework), which holds the molecules further apart, on average, than in liquid water — this is why ice is less dense than liquid water and floats.

Thermal conductivity

Water conducts heat about 28 times better than air. By trapping air within its feathers, the black-throated loon creates an insulating layer between its skin and the outside air; the trapped layer also restricts convection currents, helping maintain body temperature. The seal instead relies on a thick layer of blubber (subcutaneous fat) for insulation. Ice also has low thermal conductivity (similar to air), so a surface ice layer traps thermal energy in the water beneath it and insulates it from heat loss to the very cold air above, even in extreme cold.

Buoyancy

Bird anatomy (hollow bones, air trapped in feathers) is adapted to reduce weight for flight, but this makes buoyancy a problem for underwater hunting. The loon compensates with solid bones and by compressing air out of its lungs and feathers before diving, reducing its buoyancy so it can dive successfully. Aquatic diving mammals such as seals store a large amount of subcutaneous fat as blubber, which acts as a buoyancy aid as well as thermal insulation.

Viscosity

Water is far more viscous than air (Table A1.1.1). In the air, the loon’s light feathers move through the low-viscosity air with minimal friction, aiding flight. In water, both animals rely on water’s low viscosity below the surface to move efficiently: the seal uses its flippers (modified forelimbs) to propel itself, while the loon uses large, webbed feet, positioned laterally and toward the rear of its body, to maximize propulsion and reduce turbulent eddies (and so reduce drag) as it swims.

Higher Level Extraplanetary Origin of Water on Earth

The Earth formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago in an environment too hot for water to condense into a liquid, so the Earth’s water must have an extraplanetary origin. Further from the Sun, water vapour can condense directly into ice; it is in these colder regions that water is thought to have first formed. Researchers studying the composition of asteroids, and the meteorites that break off from them, hypothesise that asteroids are the most likely source of Earth’s water. Such asteroids still contain ice and organic material (including amino acids), so could have delivered both water and organic molecules to Earth — both critical for the possible evolution of life.

A group of meteorites called carbonaceous chondrites — among the oldest meteorites in the solar system — can be up to 28% water by composition, similar to ocean water, with the water molecules incorporated into the crystal structures of their minerals. The likely source of water can be assessed by comparing isotopes of hydrogen and their relative proportions.

Figure A1.1.12: Isotopes of hydrogenTwo simple atomic diagrams side by side. Hydrogen-1 (protium): a nucleus with a single proton (P) and an orbiting electron (e), no neutron. Hydrogen-2 (deuterium): a nucleus with one proton (P) and one neutron (N), plus one orbiting electron (e). The two isotopes differ only in the presence of a neutron in the nucleus.

Meteorites with hydrogen isotope ratios (deuterium to protium) that closely match Earth’s seawater could have been the original source of Earth’s oceans. Two 4.5-billion-year-old meteorites containing liquid water, found on Earth, support this hypothesis. When such meteorites heat up (for example on impact with a planet), they release their water as gas, which is then captured by that planet’s gravitational attraction. The Earth’s current deuterium-to-protium ratio also matches ancient eucrite achondrites, a type of meteorite originating from the large asteroid Vesta in the outer asteroid belt (located between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter). Carbonaceous chondrites and eucrite achondrites are therefore both hypothesised to have delivered water to Earth. Once water vapour was present, temperatures on the cooling Earth became low enough for it to condense into liquid water, and gravity was strong enough to retain this water at the surface rather than let it disperse into space.

There are several competing hypotheses for how water first arrived on Earth, including delivery by icy comets and formation beneath the planet’s surface. For this syllabus, only the asteroid-delivery hypothesis needs to be considered in detail.

Higher Level The Search for Extraterrestrial Life and the Presence of Water

Because life as we know it depends on water, any planet where such life could exist must also have liquid water. This requires the planet to orbit at the right distance from its star: too close, and water boils and evaporates; too far, and water freezes solid.

Goldilocks zoneAlso known as the “habitable zone”: the region around a star where it is neither too hot nor too cold for liquid water to exist on the surface of surrounding planets. The name comes from the nineteenth-century British fairy tale Goldilocks and the Three Bears.

Transit spectroscopyA technique used to establish whether a distant planet may contain water. As a planet passes in front of (“transits”) its star, starlight passes through the planet’s atmosphere on its way to Earth-based or space-based telescopes. This light is analysed to see which wavelengths have been absorbed or deflected, revealing which elements and molecules — such as water — are present in the atmosphere. A planet showing evidence of water this way is said to have a “water signature”. Scientists search for exoplanets (planets outside our solar system) that have a water signature, orbit at the right distance from their star (within the Goldilocks zone), and are the right size for life to plausibly exist — for example, Kepler-186f, an Earth-sized planet located within a Goldilocks zone, is considered a strong candidate for hosting life in another solar system.

DNA as the Genetic Material of All Living Organisms

DNA is a nucleic acid. Nucleic acids are the information molecules of cells (and of viruses) found throughout the living world. The genetic code — the information carried within nucleic acids — is universal: it makes sense in all organisms, not just a few species or groups.

Key definitionsNucleic acid: a polynucleotide chain of one of two types, DNA or RNA. Genetic code: the order of bases in DNA (of a chromosome) that determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein. Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA): a form of nucleic acid consisting of two complementary chains of deoxyribonucleotide subunits, containing the bases adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine. Ribonucleic acid (RNA): a form of nucleic acid containing the pentose sugar ribose and the organic bases adenine, guanine, uracil and cytosine.

Living cells contain two types of nucleic acid: DNA and RNA. DNA is the genetic material and occurs in the chromosomes of the nucleus (and also in chloroplasts and mitochondria). While some RNA occurs in the nucleus, most is found in the cytoplasm, particularly in ribosomes.

Some viruses (such as SARS-CoV-2, which causes COVID-19, and the viruses responsible for the common cold, influenza, Dengue fever, hepatitis C, rabies, Ebola, polio, mumps and measles) use RNA rather than DNA as their genetic material. Viruses depend entirely on the cells of living organisms to survive and replicate, so they are not considered living — meaning it remains true to say that DNA is the genetic material of all living organisms.

The presence of genetic material in a structure does not, by itself, indicate life: viruses, usually considered non-living, contain genetic material. In addition, DNA is chemically stable, so it can persist in dead organic matter and in some fossils.

Components of a Nucleotide

A nucleotide consists of three substances joined by covalent chemical bonds: a nitrogenous base (in DNA: cytosine (C), guanine (G), adenine (A) or thymine (T)); a pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA); and a phosphate group. These components combine via an enzyme-controlled condensation reaction — a reaction in which two molecules combine and water is released as a by-product. Since any one of the four bases can be incorporated, there are four different types of nucleotide in DNA.

Key definitionsNucleotide: the phosphate ester of a nucleoside — an organic base combined with a pentose sugar and a phosphate group. Cytosine, guanine, adenine, thymine: the four nitrogenous bases of DNA. Pentose: a 5-carbon monosaccharide sugar. Condensation: formation of larger molecules involving the removal of water from smaller component molecules.

The bases fall into two groups by molecular structure: the purines (adenine and guanine, double-ring structures) and the pyrimidines (cytosine and thymine, single-ring structures).

The Sugar–Phosphate Backbone

Nucleotides combine one at a time, by further condensation reactions, to form large macromolecules called nucleic acids or polynucleotides. Nucleic acids are very long, thread-like (linear) macromolecules, with alternating sugar and phosphate molecules forming the “backbone” of each strand — this part of the molecule is uniform and unvarying. Sugar–phosphate bonding creates a continuous chain of covalently bonded atoms along each strand of DNA (and RNA), forming a strong molecular backbone. Along the strand, each base is attached to a pentose sugar and projects sideways from the backbone; because the bases vary from nucleotide to nucleotide, their sequence carries the coded information held by the nucleic acid.

Bases and the Genetic Code

The information in DNA lies in the sequence of its nitrogenous bases (cytosine, guanine, adenine, thymine), which together make up the genetic code. This sequence dictates the order in which amino acids are assembled to synthesize a protein. The code lies in the sequence of one DNA strand, the coding strand (the other strand is complementary to it), and the coding strand is always read in the same direction by enzymes.

CodonThree consecutive bases in DNA (or RNA) which specify one amino acid. The genetic code is a three-letter or “triplet” code: each sequence of three bases (a codon) stands for one of the 20 amino acids used to build proteins. With a four-letter base “alphabet” (C, G, A, T), there are 64 possible different triplet combinations.

Nucleic acids code for the production of proteins in cells. Proteins make up about two-thirds of the total dry mass of a cell, and differ from carbohydrates and lipids in that they contain nitrogen (and sometimes sulfur) as well as carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.

RNA as a Polymer

RNA molecules are relatively short compared with DNA, typically from a hundred to several thousand nucleotides long, depending on their role. RNA is a polymer — a large organic molecule made of repeating subunits (monomers). In messenger RNA (mRNA), it takes the form of a single strand of polynucleotide in which the sugar monomer is ribose. The bases found in RNA are cytosine, guanine, adenine and uracil (which replaces the thymine of DNA).

Key definitionsPolymer: large organic molecules made of repeating monomer subunits. Uracil: a pyrimidine nitrogenous base found in RNA (not DNA); it pairs with adenine. Messenger RNA (mRNA): single-stranded RNA formed by transcription of the genetic code in the nucleus, which then moves to ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Transfer RNA (tRNA): short lengths of RNA that combine with specific amino acids prior to protein synthesis. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): RNA that forms part of the ribosome, the protein-synthesizing organelle.

The DNA Double Helix

The DNA molecule consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands, paired together and held by hydrogen bonds, twisted into the shape of a double helix. The strands are termed antiparallel because one strand runs from its 5′ carbon to its 3′ carbon while the other runs from its 3′ carbon to its 5′ carbon — i.e. the two strands run in opposite directions.

Double helixTwo interlocking helical polynucleotide strands joined by hydrogen bonds between pairs of purine–pyrimidine bases (A pairs with T, G pairs with C). The helical structure makes a 360° twist after every 10 nucleotides, i.e. every 3.4 nm.

Nature of science – models: a model is a simplified description of a biological system, concept or process, used to describe phenomena that cannot be directly experienced or to simplify complex systems. The first model of DNA’s structure was built in 1953 by Francis Crick and James Watson, who combined experimental evidence from other scientists to deduce the likely structure of the molecule. Like theories, models may be revised as new experimental data emerge. A key piece of evidence was an X-ray diffraction image of a DNA fibre, taken by Raymond Gosling in 1952 at King’s College London under the supervision of Rosalind Franklin; calculations Watson and Crick made from this image gave crucial dimensions for their double-helix model. The 1953 publication of DNA’s structure, and a proposed mechanism for its replication, marked a paradigm shift from classical to molecular genetics, later enabling recombinant DNA technology, genetic engineering, monoclonal antibodies, CRISPR gene editing and genetic fingerprinting — developments that raise their own ethical questions (e.g. germline editing for non-therapeutic “enhancement” reasons, or the potential misuse of genetic data by third parties such as insurers).

Genes and chromosomes

Within a DNA molecule, sections that code for proteins are called genes. A gene is a heritable factor influencing a specific characteristic of an organism (e.g. height in a pea plant, or blood group in humans); “heritable” means the factor passes from parent to offspring during reproduction. Genes are located on chromosomes — a chromosome is a length of DNA carrying specific genes in a linear sequence, and each gene occupies a specific position, or locus (plural loci), on a particular chromosome. A gene may exist in two or more alternative forms, called alleles.

Key definitionsGene: a heritable factor consisting of a length of DNA that codes for a protein. Chromosome: a length of DNA carrying specific genes in a linear sequence. Locus: the particular position of a gene on homologous chromosomes. Allele: different versions of the same gene. Homologous chromosomes: pairs of chromosomes, one from each parent, that carry the same sequence of genes (but not necessarily the same alleles of those genes).

In eukaryotic cells, chromosomes occur in pairs called homologous chromosomes — one member of each pair originally came from each parent. Humans, for example, have 46 chromosomes: 23 from each parent. Homologous chromosomes resemble each other in structure and carry the same sequence of genes (though possibly different alleles at each locus).

Differences Between DNA and RNA

Both DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides, but they differ in structure. One key difference is the pentose sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose, RNA contains ribose.

The RNA nucleotide shows a ribose sugar (with an –OH at the 2′ carbon) attached to a phosphate group and to one of the bases A, C, G or U.
DNA vs RNADNA: double-stranded; pentose sugar is deoxyribose; bases are adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine; generally very long, forms a stable double helix, and is used for long-term storage of genetic information. RNA: single-stranded; pentose sugar is ribose; bases are adenine, uracil, guanine, cytosine (uracil replaces thymine); generally much shorter, and includes mRNA, tRNA and rRNA, each with different, more transient functional roles.

Complementary Base Pairing

Bases pair specifically — adenine (A) with thymine (T), and cytosine (C) with guanine (G) — simply because only these combinations fit correctly together along the helix. When A pairs with T the bases are held by two hydrogen bonds; when C pairs with G they are held by three hydrogen bonds. Only these specific pairs can form hydrogen bonds with each other.

Complementary base pairingDescribes how the nitrogenous bases of nucleic acids align with each other in a specific way — adenine with thymine (or uracil in RNA), cytosine with guanine — held together by hydrogen bonds. Because of this specific pairing, the sequence of bases on one strand of the double helix determines the sequence of bases on the other. This is what allows genetic information to be replicated (copied precisely, during DNA replication) and expressed (used as a template for building proteins).

Conservation of the Genetic Code

The 64 codons of the genetic code have the same meaning — coding for the same amino acids — in nearly all organisms. This supports the idea of a common origin of life on Earth: that the first DNA has sustained an unbroken chain of life from the first cells to every cell alive today, with only very minor variations in the genetic code arising since life originated some 3.5 billion years ago.

MutationA change in the amount or the chemical structure (base sequence) of the DNA of a chromosome.

Over many generations, mutations can change the base sequence of the genome and therefore of mRNA and the resulting order of amino acids. However, many sequences — both protein-coding and non-coding — persist largely unchanged over many generations; these are called conserved sequences, and highly conserved sequences may have particular functional importance (although the function of many non-coding sequences remains unclear). Even where the base sequence of a coding region does change, the amino acid sequence it codes for may not, because most amino acids are specified by more than one possible codon — such changes that do not alter the resulting protein are called synonymous mutations.

Common mistakeDo not equate “genome” or genome size with the total number of genes in an organism. The correct definition of genome is the total amount of DNA (i.e. all the genetic information) of an organism or cell — not simply a count of its genes.

The most highly conserved genes are those found in all organisms, including genes for proteins involved in transcription, translation and ribosome structure. The existence of such universally shared genes indicates that all life is interlinked, descending from a single universal ancestor. Histone proteins, which help package DNA within the nucleus, are similarly highly conserved in sequence and structure across species, again pointing to universal common ancestry.

Higher Level Directionality of RNA and DNA

The DNA double helix has direction, or polarity: along each strand, phosphate groups bridge carbon-3 of one sugar to carbon-5 of the next, so one strand runs 5′ to 3′ while its partner runs 3′ to 5′ — the two strands of DNA are antiparallel. This directionality matters throughout gene expression: during DNA replication (when DNA is copied), during transcription (when the genetic code is copied into mRNA), and during translation (when the mRNA message is read to build a protein).

The coding strand of DNA is the template read during transcription, and it is always read in the 3′ to 5′ direction. The resulting single strand of mRNA is synthesized in the 5′ to 3′ direction by complementary base pairing, and mRNA is itself later translated (read by a ribosome) in the 5′ to 3′ direction to build a polypeptide chain.

Common mistakeDNA’s role is not limited to coding for proteins. While genes code for polypeptides, other regions of DNA regulate gene expression, and some sections code for RNA molecules (such as tRNA) that play their own direct role in building proteins at ribosomes, rather than coding for a protein themselves.

Higher Level Purine-to-Pyrimidine Bonding and Helix Stability

When Watson and Crick built their first physical model of DNA in 1953, they initially used cardboard cut-outs based on incorrect molecular shapes for thymine and guanine, so the model did not fit together correctly — the lengths of the base pairings were wrong. Following a suggestion from the American scientist Jerry Donohue about the correct shapes for the bases, Watson made new cardboard cut-outs and found that the complementary bases now fitted together perfectly: a purine always paired with a pyrimidine (A with T, C with G), each pair held together by hydrogen bonds, and this structure also matched Chargaff’s rules (below).

Figure A1.2.11: Watson and Crick with their demonstration model of DNAA photograph of James Watson and Francis Crick standing beside their large physical demonstration model of the DNA double helix, built from metal plates and rods representing the sugar–phosphate backbones and the paired bases.

Because a purine (double-ring) always pairs with a pyrimidine (single-ring) across the helix, every base pair (A–T or C–G) has the same width, so the DNA helix has the same three-dimensional structure regardless of the underlying base sequence. This purine-to-pyrimidine pairing, and the hydrogen bonding it allows, confers stability on the molecule, making DNA an ideal molecule for storing information in cells.

Nature of science – physical modelling: building physical, scaled models of DNA’s components let Watson and Crick combine known chemical data with X-ray diffraction evidence, trying different arrangements of the model pieces until they found one consistent with all the available data, including Chargaff’s rules. A brief point for reflection: Watson and Crick’s distinctive working method — reinterpreting already-published data and building on the work of others (including Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray diffraction images) — illustrates how scientific progress can rest on a mix of cooperation and competition between different research groups.

Higher Level Nucleosome Structure

In eukaryotic cells, DNA occurs in the chromosomes of the nucleus together with protein — more than 50% of a chromosome’s mass is protein. While some chromosomal proteins are enzymes involved in copying and repairing DNA, most have a support and packaging role.

Packaging is necessary because of the sheer length of DNA relative to the space available: in a human cell, the total DNA of all 46 chromosomes measures over 2 m, yet a typical chromosome only about 5 µm long (1 µm = 1/1000 mm) contains a DNA molecule roughly 5 cm long — meaning about 50 000 µm of DNA is packed into just 5 µm of chromosome.

Key definitionsNucleosome: a sequence of DNA wound around eight histone protein cores — a repeating unit of eukaryotic chromatin. Histone: a protein rich in the amino acids arginine and lysine that forms the scaffolding of chromosomes and is used in chromosome condensation to form nucleosomes.

This phenomenal packaging is achieved by coiling the DNA double helix and looping it around protein “beads” called nucleosomes. Histones, the packaging proteins of the nucleosome, are basic (positively charged) proteins with a high concentration of amino acids bearing extra basic (–NH2) groups, such as lysine and arginine — their positive charge lets them bind strongly to the negatively charged DNA. Eight histone molecules combine to form a single nucleosome “bead”, around which the DNA double helix is wrapped in a double loop.

At times of cell division, the whole beaded thread coils up to form the chromatin fibre, which is coiled again and looped around a “scaffold” of non-histone chromosomal protein, folding (supercoiling) the structure into the much-condensed chromosome seen at cell division. Nucleosomes are therefore key to the safe storage of these enormous lengths of DNA within the nucleus, while also allowing the cell to open up access to selected lengths of DNA (particular genes) during transcription.

Non-histone chromosomal proteinProteins that remain in the chromatin once histone proteins have been removed; they play a key role in regulating gene expression.

Prokaryotes (organisms without a true nucleus, i.e. bacteria) have much smaller genomes and do not require this histone-based packaging, since protein is absent from their circular chromosomes: their DNA is described as “naked”.

Common mistake“Naked” DNA specifically means DNA that is not associated with histone proteins (as in eubacteria). It should not be used to mean DNA that is simply not enclosed within a nuclear membrane, which is true of all bacteria regardless of whether their DNA is “naked” in the histone sense.

Because roughly 50% of a chromosome consists of protein, and because there is more chemical “variety” in proteins than in nucleic acids, scientists once speculated that protein — not DNA — might be the information-carrying substance of the cell. This idea proved incorrect.

Nature of science – the global impact of science: the Hershey–Chase experiment illustrates how a technological development can open up new kinds of experiment. Higher Level Chargaff’s Data on Purine and Pyrimidine Bases

Watson and Crick’s discovery of base pairing rested partly on their interpretation of data published by Erwin Chargaff, who in 1935 had analysed the base composition of DNA from a range of organisms and found consistent patterns whose significance was not immediately obvious even to Chargaff himself.

Chargaff’s rulesTwo consistent patterns emerged from Chargaff’s analysis of DNA from many different organisms: (1) the number of purine bases (adenine + guanine) always equalled the number of pyrimidine bases (cytosine + thymine); and (2) the number of adenine bases always equalled the number of thymine bases, and the number of guanine bases always equalled the number of cytosine bases. This is explained by the fact that only a purine (double-ring) will fit with a pyrimidine (single-ring) between the two sugar–phosphate backbones during base pairing — so in DNA, adenine must always pair with thymine, and cytosine must always pair with guanine.

Nature of science – falsification: Chargaff’s data illustrate how scientific knowledge advances not just by confirming hypotheses but by falsifying them. The Russian-American biochemist Phoebus Levene, who discovered ribose (1909) and deoxyribose (1929), had earlier proposed — incorrectly, as later shown — that nucleic acid had a repeating “tetranucleotide” structure, with the four bases always present in equal amounts. Levene did not recognize that base composition is organism-specific, partly because the analytical techniques of his time could not reliably measure the relative amounts of each base. Only in the late 1940s did Chargaff establish the organism-specificity of nucleic acid composition and the special numerical relationships between bases described above, which falsified the tetranucleotide hypothesis (since a simple four-unit repeating structure could not carry the variable information needed for heredity).

Inductive vs deductive reasoningBiologists use both forms of reasoning. Inductive reasoning (a “bottom-up” approach) starts from specific observations and measurements, identifies a general pattern, and from this develops a hypothesis and general conclusions — but inductive conclusions can never be fully certain, since a single future counter-example could falsify them (the “problem of induction”). Deductive reasoning works the other way: starting from a general principle and applying it to predict a specific case. The philosopher Karl Popper argued that scientists often work creatively and intuitively to generate a hypothesis first, and that science advances mainly through attempts at falsification: a hypothesis that survives repeated attempts to falsify it (by different research groups) is considered a strong hypothesis, but one counter-example is enough to falsify an inductive conclusion. This is exactly what happened to the tetranucleotide hypothesis once Chargaff’s data became available.

Conditions on Early Earth and the Pre-biotic Formation of Carbon Compounds

Around 4.3 billion years ago, much of the Earth’s surface was molten rock, during a period known as the Hadean eon (named after Hades, the Greek god of the underworld, reflecting the hellish conditions of the time). As the Earth cooled, gases released by volcanic activity formed its atmosphere, which included ammonia (NH3), nitrogen, methane, water vapour and much higher levels of carbon dioxide than today.

Key definitionsGreenhouse gas: an atmospheric gas that absorbs and emits infrared radiation, causing heating at Earth’s surface. Greenhouse effect: the process by which greenhouse gases trap outgoing long-wave radiation from Earth, making the planet warmer than it would otherwise be.

Methane and carbon dioxide are both greenhouse gases: they absorb and re-emit infrared radiation from the Earth’s surface, raising surface temperature via the greenhouse effect. Today’s atmosphere has relatively high oxygen levels, essential for sustaining life, and a protective ozone (O3) layer in the stratosphere, formed naturally from the interaction of solar UV radiation with molecular oxygen (O2). The early Earth lacked free oxygen (and therefore ozone), so UV radiation reached the surface largely unfiltered: UV radiation 3.7 billion years ago is estimated to have been 100 times more intense than today. Since UV radiation damages and mutates DNA, today’s lower surface UV levels are part of what allows life to exist on the Earth’s surface now.

Conditions on the early Earth may have allowed a variety of carbon compounds to form spontaneously via chemical processes that do not occur today. If energy was added to the gases of Earth’s early atmosphere, building blocks of life — such as amino acids, peptides, ribose, nucleobases, fatty acids, nucleotides and short oligonucleotides — could have formed. This idea, that high-energy or UV radiation led to the formation of the first biological molecules, is called the “primordial soup” (or “pre-biotic soup”) hypothesis, proposed independently by Alexander Oparin (1924) and JBS Haldane (1929).

High-energy UV light is, however, also destructive to early biochemistry: when it destroys a molecule, the resulting small, highly reactive fragments can go on to recombine into new, larger, high-energy molecules (for example, pyruvic acid — central to key metabolic pathways today — forms larger molecules when dissolved in water and illuminated with UV light). Research shows that the bases found in nucleotides are extremely efficient at reducing the harmful effects of UV radiation, protecting the pentose sugar and phosphate parts of nucleic acids; under strong UV light, RNA was shown to be more likely to form chains than other candidate molecules. It is therefore possible that the intense UV levels on the primordial Earth acted as a selection pressure that actually drove the origin of life forward, rather than simply being an obstacle to it.

Cells as the Smallest Units of Self-sustaining Life

Cells are the fundamental, self-sustaining units of life, reproducing by cell division; all present-day cells are believed to have evolved from a single common ancestral cell that existed around 4 billion years ago. All cells are enclosed by a plasma membrane separating their interior from the environment, and all cells contain DNA, which stores genetic information and is used as a template for synthesizing RNA and proteins.

Simply identifying cellular life processes (respiration, nutrition, reproduction, etc.) does not, by itself, define life or explain how life first appeared. Three broader principles are often used to help define life: living organisms must be able to evolve through natural selection (requiring reproduction and a hereditary system showing genetic variation); life forms are contained and separate from, but in communication with, their surrounding environment (as a cell is); and life forms are chemical and physical “machines” that receive and respond to information. A systems-level view is also useful: living organisms can be seen as complex systems that process information about themselves and their environment, not simply as a large set of biochemical reactions.

Figure A2.1.3: Three factors essential for sustaining lifeA simple three-part diagram with “Genetic material”, “Membrane compartment” and “Metabolic processes” shown as three converging elements that together support “LIFE” at the centre — illustrating that all three features must be present together for a self-sustaining living system to exist.

NASA’s working definition of life is: “life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.” This definition would technically include viruses, but a major difficulty in defining life generally is that we only have one confirmed example — life on Earth — where every organism shares the same basic biochemistry, making it hard to know which properties of Earth life are essential to “life” in general versus properties unique to our particular biochemistry. (Mars, which may have sub-surface pockets of water, is considered the most promising location for life elsewhere in our solar system; some scientists have also speculated about “weird life” elsewhere in the universe based on a liquid other than water, such as ammonia.)

Viruses are regarded as non-living: they are non-cellular, lack organelles, and cannot carry out metabolism (e.g. releasing energy as ATP, or protein synthesis) independently — they can only replicate inside a living host cell, using the host’s own cellular machinery. Viruses do not exhibit homeostasis or directly respond to environmental stimuli in the way cells do, although they can respond to external pressures over evolutionary time (e.g. evading host immunity, adapting to antiviral drugs, or changing host range through mutation, especially in RNA viruses). Viruses do carry hereditary material (DNA or RNA) with genes coding for specific structures, such as capsid coat proteins, and (in retroviruses) reverse transcriptase and integrase enzymes. Viral genomes are prone to rapid mutation, which drives their evolution by natural selection; many viruses can also undergo a form of recombination known as genetic shift.

The Challenge of Explaining the Spontaneous Origin of Cells

Cells are highly complex structures that, today, can only be produced by the division of a pre-existing cell — yet the very first cells must have had non-biological origins. Explaining how the first cells arose from non-living matter is one of the most important unanswered questions in biology.

All cells share three common features1. A stable, partially permeable membrane surrounding the cell’s components. 2. Genetic material that can be passed on when new cells form, and which controls cell function and behaviour. 3. Metabolic processes that generate energy, enabling growth, self-maintenance and reproduction.

For living cells to have formed from non-living materials, several steps would have been required: the synthesis of simple organic molecules such as sugars and amino acids; the assembly of these molecules into polymers; the development of self-replicating molecules, such as nucleic acids; and the retention of these molecules within membranous sacs, so that an internal chemistry different from the surroundings could develop. These elements must then have spontaneously self-assembled into the first cells.

Nature of science – competing hypotheses: two broad approaches are used to study how life originated. The “bottom-up” approach (favoured by chemists, e.g. the Miller–Urey experiment) tries to reconstruct the chemical conditions of the primitive Earth in the laboratory. The “top-down” approach (favoured by biologists) studies modern organisms for surviving relics of ancient metabolic pathways and molecular processes. A key limitation of both approaches is that the exact conditions of the pre-biotic Earth cannot be exactly replicated, and the earliest protocells did not fossilize — meaning hypotheses about life’s origin can be tested only indirectly, by seeing whether laboratory experiments produce outcomes consistent with a given theory’s predictions, not by directly observing the historical event.

Three competing theories for the origin of lifeProtocell-first: a cell-like compartment with a basic metabolism, but lacking a full genetic system, arose spontaneously and was able to grow and divide into similar daughter cells; these protocells evolved adaptively until they eventually acquired a genetic system (likely RNA, later DNA). Predicted evidence: cell-like units that can grow and divide without genetic molecules should be able to be engineered, or seen to arise spontaneously, in the laboratory. Gene-first: a genetic molecule (likely RNA), or a small set of them, arose spontaneously with the capacity to replicate; these replicators evolved adaptively by natural selection, eventually forming variants able to assemble a membrane and begin metabolising. Predicted evidence: complex RNA molecules should be able to form spontaneously under the right conditions, and show collective self-replication and open-ended evolution. Metabolism-first: a self-sustaining system of simple chemical reactions, capable of using nutrients and energy (perhaps adsorbed onto a mineral surface), arose spontaneously and evolved adaptively, eventually evolving both cell-like compartments and genetic systems. Predicted evidence: chemical mixtures supplied with a flux of nutrients/energy in the laboratory should sometimes show autocatalysis, adaptive evolution, and the production of lipids and genetic polymers. Many scientists currently favour the metabolism-first theory, since rapid growth, replication and division (all essential to protocell evolution) require significant amounts of energy, which a working metabolism could supply.

The central hypothesis for the origin of the cell is that organic molecules self-assembled within a vesicle to form the first protocell, roughly 4 billion years ago.

Biologists studying the origin of life focus on four critical processes: the formation of organic molecules (such as amino acids and nucleic acids, especially RNA); the polymerization of these molecules; the formation of membranes; and the development of metabolic pathways for energy transfer. Scientists broadly agree that organic molecules formed first via chemical evolution, but disagree about the order in which metabolism, polymerized molecules and membranes then evolved. For example, the “iron–sulfur world” hypothesis proposes that the first steps of polymerization and protocell formation occurred in a hot, high-pressure iron–sulfur hydrothermal environment deep under the sea; this hypothesis gained support after sub-ocean hydrothermal vents were discovered, though testing it experimentally is hampered by the difficulty of simulating such high pressures and temperatures in the laboratory. Because this is a historical problem for which critical evidence may simply no longer exist, we may never know with certainty exactly how life originated on Earth.

Evidence for the Origin of Carbon Compounds

The molecules of living things are built mainly from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with some nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur (plus small amounts of other elements, including biologically important metal ions). Living cells today build these molecules using enzymes, but for life to have originated from non-living material, the very first step must have been the non-biological synthesis of simple organic molecules.

The Miller–Urey experiment (1953)Stanley Miller and Harold Urey investigated how simple organic molecules might have arisen from the chemicals present on the early, lifeless Earth. They passed strong electric sparks (simulating lightning) through mixtures of gases — methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water vapour, circulated with boiling water and a cooling condenser — for an extended period. They found that amino acids (known components of cell proteins), among other compounds, formed naturally within the apparatus, confirming that organic molecules can be synthesized outside cells, in the absence of oxygen. The experiment has since been repeated with different gas mixtures and other energy sources (particularly ultraviolet radiation); products obtained across these repeats have included amino acids, fatty acids, sugars such as glucose, nucleotide bases, and in some cases simple polymers of these molecules — showing that a wide range of the chemical building blocks of cells could plausibly have formed on the pre-biotic Earth.

The gas mixture then passes through a cooling condenser, where it condenses back to liquid; a stopcock at the bottom of the system allows samples of the resulting liquid to be removed for chemical analysis.

An evaluation of the Miller–Urey experiment: at the time (1953), the early atmosphere was believed to be strongly reducing (hydrogen-rich) and rich in methane, matching the experiment’s gas mixture; current evidence instead suggests methane was in low abundance for most of this period, with carbon present mainly as carbon dioxide (i.e. a more oxygen-rich mixture than Miller and Urey assumed). The experiment also used electrical discharges rather than UV light to supply energy, although subsequent work shows organic molecules such as amino acids and bases can also form when carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water are exposed to ionizing radiation or UV light, as well as electrical discharges. Despite this success, efforts to reproduce pre-biotic chemistry did not succeed in generating nucleotides directly until relatively recently, when nucleotides were synthesized via a route using four simple organic molecules (cyanamide, cyanoacetylene, glycolaldehyde and glyceraldehyde) that readily form under plausible pre-biotic conditions.

Assembling polymers without cells or enzymesForming polymers in the absence of cells and enzymes would have required biologically important monomers — monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids — to become concentrated in “pockets” where further reactions between them were possible. Clays have been shown to promote phosphodiester bond formation by binding and concentrating nucleotides, and microscopic clay layers may have played a similar role in forming the first polyribonucleotides — potentially in water near volcanic lava flows, or at submarine volcanic vents, where the environment is hot, under high pressure, and rich in vented sulfur compounds (e.g. H2S) and other chemicals.
PolymerizationThe process by which relatively small molecules, called monomers, chemically combine to produce a larger molecule called a polymer.

Spontaneous Formation of Vesicles

All cells are bounded by membranes that separate genetic material and metabolic reactants from the external environment; the earliest cells (protocells) were likely bounded by basic membranes of this kind.

Key definitionsAmphipathic: having two different affinities within one molecule — a polar end attracted to water, and a non-polar end repelled by it. Microsphere: a microscopic hollow sphere made from a lipid bilayer. Vesicle: a membrane-bound sac. Protocell: a pre-cellular or cell-like entity, e.g. a lipid droplet with a few molecules inside.

Fatty acids are likely to have formed the first protocell membranes because they are amphipathic. If a small number of lipid molecules are placed in water, they form a monolayer on the water’s surface; with more lipid present, bilayers form. Lengths of these bilayers are likely to have curled up into microspheres or very small vesicles — meaning vesicles form spontaneously (without any external cause or stimulus) by the coalescence of fatty acids into spherical bilayers. Simple microspheres enclosing a portion of the surrounding pre-biotic “soup” of polymers and monomers may have been the forerunners of true cells, since they would begin to develop an internal chemical environment distinct from their surroundings.

Step 2: the lipid molecules assemble into a bilayer sheet, associated with the mineral surface. Step 3: a length of this bilayer curls up and seals itself into a closed, hollow, spherical microsphere, separating an internal aqueous space from the external water.

Modern cell membranes are made from a modified type of lipid, the phospholipids, which have hydrophobic tails and hydrophilic heads. Fatty acids are chemically simpler than phospholipids, so are thought to have formed the first membranes, with membranes evolving from primitive to more modern via a pathway roughly like this: protocells first formed using stable fatty acids (which may have accumulated to significant levels on the early Earth); condensation of fatty acids with glycerol then produced triglycerides, a more stabilizing membrane component; and phosphorylation (addition of a phosphate group) of a triglyceride then formed the simplest phospholipid.

RNA as a Presumed First Genetic Material

For life to evolve from a mixture of polymers and monomers, two special capabilities needed to emerge: a “self-replication” system, and the ability to catalyse chemical change. In modern cells these roles are filled separately, by DNA (genetic code) and by enzymes (typically large, globular proteins) — but neither DNA nor functioning enzymes have been produced in any experiment repeating the Miller–Urey approach to pre-biotic synthesis.

RNA world hypothesisThe hypothesis that the earliest life forms (protocells) may have used RNA alone — without DNA or protein enzymes — for both storing genetic information and catalysing chemical reactions.

The idea arose partly as a by-product of a genetic engineering experiment investigating enzymes that join short lengths of RNA: it was discovered that RNA itself, not just protein, can function as an enzyme. Because mRNA is single-stranded (simpler than double-stranded DNA), hydrogen bonds can form between nucleotides within the same RNA chain, causing it to fold into a unique three-dimensional shape determined by its base sequence — a fold that can confer enzymatic activity, a property needed by the earliest forms of life.

RibozymeAn RNA molecule capable of acting as an enzyme, using its folded three-dimensional shape as an active site. In 1989, Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discovering catalytic RNA.

Central dogmaThe idea that the transfer of genetic information from the DNA of a chromosome, to mRNA, to protein (amino acid sequence), is irreversible — i.e. information flows DNA → RNA → protein, and not in reverse. (Retroviruses such as HIV are a notable exception: their RNA genome is reverse-transcribed into DNA inside a host cell, which then integrates into the host chromosome.)

Because nucleic acids are needed to make proteins, yet proteins are needed to make nucleic acids in modern cells, it is difficult to see how this interdependent system could have evolved directly by natural selection — the RNA world hypothesis proposes a way out of this “chicken and egg” problem: an RNA-only stage existed in protocells before modern DNA- and protein-based cells evolved, with RNA acting as both the genetic material and the catalyst, and DNA only later taking over as the dedicated genetic material once proteins became the dominant catalysts and structural components of cells.

Relics of a possible RNA world persist in modern cells: RNA primers (short RNA sequences) are used to initiate eukaryotic DNA replication, and ribosomal RNA appears to directly catalyse peptide bond formation within the ribosome — arguably the strongest evidence for an RNA world, since it suggests that protein synthesis itself could first have developed in a pre-protein world, using an RNA-based enzyme (an early ribosome) to build proteins from an RNA template. Further supporting evidence comes from the pentose sugars: ribose (found in RNA) is readily formed from methanal (H2CO), one of the principal products of the Miller–Urey experiment, whereas in modern cells deoxyribose (found in DNA) is produced from ribose only via a reaction catalysed by a protein-based enzyme — implying RNA’s chemistry is the more “primitive” of the two. DNA’s other distinguishing features — its stable double helix, and its use of thymine rather than uracil — further increase its chemical stability and ease of enzymatic repair compared with RNA.

Although the RNA world hypothesis does not fully explain how life originated, it represents an important paradigm shift in the scientific study of life’s origins, and has helped guide subsequent experimental research.

Evidence for a Last Universal Common Ancestor

Fossil remains and other evidence (anatomical and biochemical similarities) reveal how species are related — for instance, ape skeletons show that gibbons, gorillas, chimpanzees, orang-utans and humans share a common ancestor and have since adapted to different environments and lifestyles by natural selection.

Common ancestorThe most recent species from which two or more different species have evolved.

DNA comparisons can be used the same way: species with very similar genes are closely related, while species with very different DNA are only distantly related. All life on Earth is related, and ultimately shares a single common ancestor believed to have existed around 4 billion years ago — the evolutionary link between the abiotic (non-living) and biotic (living) phases of Earth’s history. This organism is known as LUCA, the “Last Universal Common Ancestor”: if all life on Earth is drawn as a tree, LUCA sits at its base, with an unbroken line of descent connecting every living thing back to it. All organisms share the same basic biochemistry, the same DNA bases, and much of the same amino acid “alphabet”; the shared genetic code (the rules for translating RNA into protein) used by nearly all organisms is strong evidence for this common ancestry, since multiple different genetic codes would be expected if separate lineages had independently evolved protein synthesis from scratch.

Several of the shortest branches nearest the base of the tree are marked with asterisks and identified as hyperthermophilic species, suggesting the universal ancestor of all living things was itself a hyperthermophile, possibly having “assembled” at deep ocean hydrothermal vents where volcanic gases are discharged into water under high temperature and pressure. Common mistakeLUCA should not be described as a “protocell”. A protocell refers to a much simpler, pre-cellular or cell-like entity (e.g. a lipid droplet containing a few molecules) — something far less elaborate than LUCA, which is thought to have resembled an ancestral prokaryote.

Researchers analysing DNA from modern bacteria (eubacteria) and archaea (a separate group of extremophile prokaryotes that live in environments such as deep ocean vents, geysers, salt pans, acidic conditions and polar regions) searched for genes shared between them, as an indicator of genes inherited from a common ancestor. Searching DNA databanks covering roughly six million genes across 2000 sequenced modern microbial genomes, researchers identified 355 gene families that were widespread across bacteria and archaea — likely inherited from LUCA. Care was taken to exclude genes that could have spread between species by lateral (horizontal) transfer, such as bacterial conjugation (direct transfer of genetic material between two bacteria), rather than genuinely by vertical descent from a common ancestor. Genomic analysis of these shared gene families was then used to predict LUCA’s likely structure and function, since DNA codes for proteins and proteins determine an organism’s structure and function. It is likely that other early life forms existed alongside LUCA but went extinct through competition for shared resources, leaving LUCA as the sole ancestor of all life alive today; descendants of LUCA are also likely to have out-competed other species over subsequent evolutionary history, further shaping today’s tree of life.

Estimating the Dates of the First Living Cells and LUCA

LUCA may have existed around 4 billion years ago, roughly 560 million years after Earth itself formed. Several independent approaches let scientists estimate such dates for early life.

Fossils provide direct evidence of the history of life, though fossilization is an extremely rare, chance event: predators, scavengers and bacterial decomposition normally break down dead organisms before fossilization can occur, and of the relatively few fossils that do form, most remain buried, undiscovered, overlooked, or are accidentally destroyed. Nevertheless, many fossils have been found and new ones continue to be discovered. If a fossil, or the surrounding rock, can be accurately dated using radiometric dating (measuring the amounts of naturally occurring radioactive substances, e.g. the ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12, or of potassium-40 to argon-40), it provides good evidence about the history of life; older rocks are expected to contain more ancient groups of organisms, provided conditions allowed fossilization and the fossils were not subsequently destroyed (e.g. by the heat and pressure of metamorphic rock formation, which destroys fossils).

The molecular clockGenomic analysis offers a further technique for dating ancient organisms. DNA accumulates mutations over time; by estimating the average rate at which such mutations occur, and extrapolating this rate backward through time, scientists can estimate when two lineages last shared a common ancestor. The amino acid composition of proteins can be used similarly, since changes to the genetic code alter protein composition and structure. If such biochemical changes occur at a roughly constant rate, they can be used as a “molecular clock”: by comparing specific genes shared between early and present-day life, and applying the estimated rate of biochemical change, scientists can estimate both when those genes last shared a common ancestor and, ultimately, a date for the earliest life on Earth.

Evidence for the Evolution of LUCA Near Hydrothermal Vents

Because LUCA probably evolved in the environment of deep-sea hydrothermal vents, rocks formed from ancient seafloor hydrothermal vent precipitates are the most likely places to find fossils of the earliest life. Scientists have found fossilized evidence of bacteria in ancient seafloor hydrothermal vent precipitates from the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt in Quebec, Canada: thin, hundredth-of-a-millimetre rock slices examined under a light microscope revealed structures resembling those produced by modern hydrothermal-vent bacteria. These fossils have been dated to at least 3.77 billion years old (possibly up to 4.28 billion years old), making them the oldest fossil remains found to date and strong evidence for some of the very first cells to exist on Earth.

Figure A2.1.10: Haematite tubes found in NuvvuagittuqA microscope image showing extremely fine, thread-like tubular structures (roughly half the width of a human hair) made of haematite, the mineral form of iron(iii) oxide, embedded in ancient rock at least 3.77 billion years old — identified as the fossilized remains of ancient filamentous, iron-oxidizing bacteria similar to microbes found at modern hydrothermal vents.

The Nuvvuagittuq rocks also contain carbonate and carbonaceous material, further supporting evidence of past oxidation and biological activity. Iron-oxidizing microbial communities — which extract iron atoms from water and remove electrons from them for metabolic energy transfer — are still found today around widespread ocean-floor hydrothermal vents, and it is likely the ancient Nuvvuagittuq bacteria had a broadly similar biochemistry, even though the deep-sea hydrothermal vent environment as a whole offers many possible routes to energy generation via chemosynthetic pathways.

Key definitionsChemosynthesis: the oxidation of inorganic molecules to release energy, which is then used to synthesize glucose. Extremophile: an organism that lives in conditions of extreme temperature, acidity, alkalinity, salinity, pressure or chemical concentration. Autotrophic: able to synthesize glucose from simple inorganic substances using an external energy source.

Because protein synthesis is the most energy-intensive activity of a cell (using around 75% of a typical cell’s ATP), the fact that LUCA’s conserved genes are dominated by proteins associated with ribosomes shows that LUCA actively released and used energy — though these particular conserved genes alone do not reveal LUCA’s specific metabolism, so other lines of evidence are needed. The 355 shared gene/protein families identified from genomic analysis (see previous section) are not evenly distributed across all organisms, which lets scientists infer LUCA’s likely physiology. For example, LUCA is inferred to have carried a gene for “reverse gyrase”, an enzyme that helps stabilize DNA structure and is today found only in extremophiles living in high-temperature environments, including hydrothermal vents. Taken together, the properties of LUCA’s inferred protein set indicate that LUCA was: anaerobic (survived without oxygen); CO2-fixing (converted carbon dioxide into glucose); H2-dependent (used molecular hydrogen, rather than sunlight, as an energy source); N2-fixing (converted nitrogen gas into ammonia, for subsequent amino acid synthesis); and thermophilic (able to survive at very high temperatures, up to 122°C). Modern microbes with broadly similar physiology include Clostridia (anaerobic bacteria found in soil and in the intestines of humans and other animals) and methanogens (anaerobic bacteria that produce methane as a waste product).

Figure A2.1.11: A hydrothermal ventA photograph of an active deep-sea hydrothermal vent structure on the ocean floor, with dark, mineral-laden, superheated water plumes rising from the vent opening into the surrounding cold, high-pressure seawater — an environment rich in hydrogen, carbon dioxide, iron and dissolved sulfides and metals, of the kind in which LUCA is believed to have evolved.

Overall, the genes inferred to have been passed down from LUCA describe an autotrophic extremophile that likely lived at hydrothermal vents, where seawater and magma meet on the ocean floor, in a geochemically active environment rich in hydrogen, carbon dioxide and iron — among the same toxic plumes of sulfides and metals where similar prokaryotic organisms still live today. Combined with the fossil evidence from Nuvvuagittuq, many researchers now consider deep-sea hydrothermal vents the most likely site for the origin of life on Earth.
