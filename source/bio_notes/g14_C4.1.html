Populations

Members of a species may be reproductively isolated in separate populations. The members of a population have the chance to interbreed, assuming the species reproduces sexually. Reproductive isolation is used to distinguish one population of a species from another.

Key termPopulation: interacting groups of organisms of the same species living in an area.
Figure C4.1.1: A population of zebraA photograph of a herd of zebra standing together, illustrating a single population — many individuals of one species (Equus quagga) occupying the same habitat at the same time, with the potential to interbreed.

Estimation of population size by random sampling

To study populations, an ecologist must be able to sample them. In ecological investigations, study sites are too large and complex to examine every organism, so samples are taken that represent the full site. Population growth is affected by abiotic and biotic factors, and the increase in a population follows a pattern that can be modelled.

Random sampling avoids bias — without it, an investigator might be tempted to choose areas of unusually high biodiversity that do not represent the whole study site. If two or more species show a genuine association in a habitat (rather than a purely random distribution), reliable data on their distribution, obtained by random sampling, are needed to detect it. Soils rich in calcium ions, for example, typically support different plant populations from dry acidic soils.

Key terms Sample: a subset of a whole population or habitat used to estimate the values that might have been obtained if every individual or response was measured.
Random sampling: a method of choosing a sample from a population without any bias.
Sampling error: statistical errors that arise when a sample does not represent the whole population — the difference between the real values of the population and the values derived from the sample.

Top tip! Random sampling ensures every individual in the community has an equal chance of being selected, giving a representative sample. It is appropriate when the study area is homogeneous, but not when sampling along a transect where there is an environmental gradient and a change in species composition.

Random, stratified and systematic sampling

Sampling strategies Random sampling is used when the same habitat type is found throughout the whole study area — sample points are chosen using random numbers so every location has an equal chance of selection.
Stratified sampling is used in areas containing two or more different habitat types; it takes into account the proportional area of each habitat type and samples each one accordingly.
Systematic sampling is used where the study area includes an environmental gradient. Transect positions are located at random, but sampling along the transect itself is done at fixed distances (e.g. every 5 m) so that the whole gradient is covered.
Key terms Stratified sampling: used in areas that contain two or more different habitat types; the technique accounts for the proportional area of different habitat types and samples each one accordingly.
Systematic sampling: used where the study area includes an environmental gradient, sampling systematically along it using a transect.

A random number generator (a calculator function, a website such as random.org, or a smartphone app) helps ensure sampling is free from bias. Alternatively, numbers can be drawn from a bag.

Random error and systematic error

Random sampling always involves error Measuring a sample rather than an entire population inevitably introduces random errors, caused by unknown or unpredictable differences, which lead to imprecision and uncertainty. Repeating and averaging measurements reduces random error, but it can never be fully corrected — it is always present and relates to the precision of repeated measurements.
Systematic errors result from flaws in the investigation design or procedure that shift all measurements in the same direction, leading to inaccuracy rather than imprecision. They can be reduced with careful experimental design, and by using different methods to perform the same measurement. Peer review, where scientists share data and methods for others to evaluate, also helps guard against systematic errors.

Calculating standard deviation

Variation is a quantitative measure of the distribution (spread or clustering) of values in a data set. Range and standard deviation are both used to summarize variability, but standard deviation is far more reliable.

Key terms Variation: a quantitative measure of the distribution (spread or clustering) of the values in a data set.
Range: the difference between the largest and smallest data values.
Standard deviation (SD): the spread of a set of normally distributed data from the mean of the sample; a measure of the variability of a population from a sample. A small SD indicates the data are more reliable.
Normal distribution: a data-set distribution that is symmetrical about the mean, forming a bell-shaped curve.

The range depends only on the two extreme values, so a single outlier can make the range suggest a high spread even though most of the data are tightly clustered. This makes range an unreliable descriptive statistic, though it remains useful for small data sets.

Standard deviation measures how spread out data are from the mean of a set of normally distributed values. A small standard deviation indicates data clustered closely around the mean (and therefore greater reliability); a large standard deviation indicates a wider spread.

Figure C4.1.4: The normal distribution and its standard deviation (SD)A symmetrical, bell-shaped curve centred on the arithmetical mean. Vertical lines mark one SD and two SD to either side of the mean. The area within one SD of the mean either side (±1 SD) contains 68% of the data; the area within two SD either side (±2 SD) contains more than 95% of the data.

The standard deviation can help decide whether the difference between two related means is significant: if the standard deviations are much larger than the difference between the means, the difference is unlikely to be significant; if the standard deviations are much smaller than the difference between the means, the difference is almost certainly significant.

How to calculate standard deviation
  1. Calculate the mean, x̄.
  2. Calculate the deviation of each value from the mean: x − x̄.
  3. Square each deviation: (x − x̄)2.
  4. Add the squared deviations: ∑(x − x̄)2.
  5. Divide by the number of values (n).
This gives the population standard deviation, used when the data set represents an entire population. When SD is calculated from a sample rather than the whole population (the sample standard deviation), the formula is modified to divide by n − 1 rather than n — this corrects the tendency of a sample to underestimate the true population variability, making the sample SD an unbiased estimate of the population SD.

Common mistake: standard deviation does not necessarily decrease as sample size increases — it can increase or decrease depending on the actual measurements in the sample. If there is a lot of variation in a population, the standard deviation will be large regardless of sample size.

Estimating population size for sessile organisms

Specific methods are used to estimate population size for non-mobile species (e.g. plants, sessile animals such as barnacles) as distinct from mobile species in the wild.

Random quadrat sampling

A quadrat is a square frame that outlines a known area for sampling. Quadrats are used to estimate the abundance of plants and sessile animals. Quadrat size varies with the size of the organisms being studied: a 10 cm² quadrat suits a single-celled alga such as epiphytic Pleurococcus on damp walls, while a 1 m² quadrat suits herbaceous grassland plants or earthworms and slugs extracted from the soil.

Figure C4.1.2: A quadrat being used in the fieldA photograph of a square quadrat frame placed on vegetation in a water meadow (Slapton Ley, Devon), used to estimate the species richness of plants within the enclosed area.
Method: random quadrat sampling
  1. A map of the habitat is marked out with gridlines along two edges of the area to be analysed (creating x- and y-axes).
  2. Coordinates for placing quadrats are obtained as sequences of random numbers, using computer software, a calculator, or published random number tables.
  3. Within each quadrat, the individual species present are identified, and the density, frequency, cover or abundance of each species is estimated.
  4. These estimates are quantified by measuring the total area of the habitat occupied by the population (in m²), then population size is calculated using the formula below.
Population size = (mean density per quadrat × total area) ÷ area of each quadrat
Figure C4.1.3: Random locating of quadratsA diagram of a gridded rectangular habitat map with numbered squares along the x-axis (0–100) and y-axis (0–100). Random number pairs are used to select grid coordinates, and a quadrat is placed at each selected coordinate. The accompanying four-step method (map → random coordinates → species identification within each quadrat → calculation of population size) matches the method described above.

Estimating population size for motile organisms

Capture–mark–release–recapture

It is not possible to collect every individual in a mobile population. Instead, a representative sample is captured, marked, released, and the population resampled later. The calculation relates two ratios: the number of marked animals recaptured compared with the size of the resampled population, and the size of the originally marked sample compared with the total population.

Marking methods vary by organism — pen marks on insect wing cases, enamel paint on snail shells, fur clipping in mammals. Marks must be difficult to see, since high visibility increases predation risk to the marked individual.

Method: capture–mark–release–recapture
  1. A random sample of the population is captured.
  2. Each captured individual is marked, then released back into the population, where marks mix evenly with the wider population.
  3. After a suitable interval (e.g. 24 hours), a second random sample is captured from the same location.
  4. The number of marked individuals within this second sample is noted, and the Lincoln index formula is used to estimate total population size.
Figure C4.1.5: Estimating animal populations by capture–mark–release–recaptureA diagram illustrating the method using a population of woodlice: a random sample is captured, marked, and released back into the mixed population; 24 hours later a second random sample is caught from the same location; the number of marked animals in this second sample is used with the formula Population size = (M × N) ÷ R to calculate the estimate. The worked example shown gives M = 5 (captured, marked and released), N = 8 (recaptured), R = 1 (marked animals recaptured), giving population size = (5 × 8) ÷ 1 = 40.

Calculating the Lincoln index

The Lincoln index is a statistical measure used to estimate population size for motile organisms.

Population size = (M × N) ÷ R
M = number of animals captured, marked and released
N = number of animals recaptured
R = number of marked animals recaptured

The method can be practised using a non-living model before being applied to real populations: cut out 100 identical paper squares, mark a known number (e.g. 20), mix them in a tray (the “habitat”), then pull out a random sample, count the sample size and the number of marked squares within it, and apply the Lincoln index formula. Repeating the experiment several times gives a mean estimate and a range of values.

Assumptions of the Lincoln index When applied to wild populations, the Lincoln index assumes that:
  • mixing is complete — marked individuals spread evenly through the population between capture and recapture
  • marks are not removed and do not disappear between capture and recapture
  • marks are not harmful to the animal and do not increase predation risk by increasing visibility
  • it is equally easy to catch every individual in the population
  • there is no immigration, emigration, birth or death in the population between sampling events (i.e. it is a closed population)

Carrying capacity and competition for limited resources

In most ecosystems, abiotic and biotic factors limit population size. Exponential growth cannot continue indefinitely: eventually resources run low, competition occurs, and other limiting factors take effect. Limiting factors restrict population growth or prevent it increasing further.

Top tip! Limiting factors in plants include light, soil nutrients, water, carbon dioxide and temperature. Limiting factors in animals include space, food, mates, nesting sites and water.

For example, rabbits introduced into a new meadow undergo initial exponential growth, but as numbers rise, vegetation is used up faster than it can regrow. Food becomes a limiting factor, further population increase stops, and the population reaches the carrying capacity of its environment.

Key termCarrying capacity: the maximum number of individuals of a species that can be supported by a given environment.

Negative feedback control of population size by density-dependent factors

Negative feedback refers to the return of a system to its original starting state — for a population, this is a return towards its carrying capacity. The predator–prey interaction is an example of population control by negative feedback.

Key terms Negative feedback: feedback that tends to counteract any deviation from equilibrium and promotes stability.
Density-dependent: factors that lower the birth rate or raise the death rate as a population grows.

Negative feedback control of populations is driven by density-dependent factors — biotic factors that limit population growth in proportion to population density:

  • Internal density-dependent factors: e.g. fertility, size of breeding territory.
  • External density-dependent factors: e.g. increased predation risk, transfer of pathogens or pests in dense populations.
Figure C4.1.6: Negative feedback in the snowshoe hare–lynx predator–prey relationshipA cyclical flow diagram with four linked stages: an increase in the snowshoe hare population leads to an increased lynx population (as more food is available); this in turn causes the snowshoe hare population to decrease (as more predation occurs); the reduced hare population then causes the lynx population to decrease (as less food is available); with fewer lynx, hare numbers begin to increase again, and the cycle repeats — illustrating negative feedback returning both populations towards a stable equilibrium.

Density-dependent versus density-independent factors

Density-dependent vs density-independent Density-independent factors (e.g. weather, climate, natural disasters) affect the populations of all species in an ecosystem regardless of population size or density. Because they are not related to population numbers, they do not affect carrying capacity, though they can cause fluctuations in population size.
Density-dependent factors have varying effects depending on population density, and because they limit population size they define the upper limit to population growth — the carrying capacity. Density-dependent factors tend to push a population back towards its carrying capacity.
Concept: InteractionDensity-dependent factors interact with organisms to cause population numbers to fluctuate, ultimately pushing populations back to carrying capacity.

Population growth curves

Exponential growth

Imagine a small number of rabbits entering a meadow from which other rabbits had been excluded. Initially, few individuals are reproducing, so population growth is small as the rabbits establish burrows. Once established, with rich food supplies and no limiting factors, reproduction accelerates rapidly — this rapid increase is called exponential growth.

Exponential growth occurs when:
  • limiting factors are not restricting population growth
  • resources (light, space, food) are plentiful and there is little competition with other species
  • abiotic conditions (temperature, rainfall) are favourable and predators or disease are scarce
Growth is initially slow but becomes increasingly rapid and does not slow down as the population increases.
Figure C4.1.7: An exponential growth curveA graph of population size (y-axis) against time (x-axis) showing a curve that rises slowly at first, then increasingly steeply, with no sign of levelling off — an ever-accelerating (J-shaped) rate of increase.

Sigmoid growth curve

The carrying capacity represents the population size at which environmental resistance limits further growth. Exponential growth eventually succumbs to environmental resistance, which decreases natality, increases mortality, or both. Plotting population size against time then produces an S-shaped, or sigmoid, growth curve: initial rapid (exponential) growth followed by a slowdown as carrying capacity is approached.

Figure C4.1.8: An S-shaped or sigmoid growth curveA graph of population size (y-axis) against time (x-axis) with a horizontal dashed line marking the carrying capacity (K). The curve has three numbered stages: (1) an initial exponential rise; (2) a curving transition where the rate of increase slows; (3) a plateau phase where the curve levels off and fluctuates around K.

The three stages of the sigmoid curve are:

  1. Exponential growth stage: limiting factors are not restricting growth. With low or reduced limiting factors, the population expands rapidly into the habitat — both numbers and rate of growth increase quickly.
  2. Transition phase: the increase in numbers, and the rate of growth, begin to slow as limiting factors start to affect the population. Increased competition for resources, more predators (attracted by the large population), and higher rates of disease and mortality (from crowding) all contribute to the slowdown.
  3. Plateau phase: limiting factors restrict the population to its carrying capacity. Changes in limiting factors, predation, disease and abiotic factors cause the population to fluctuate around K.
Figure C4.1.9: Changing conditions along an S-shaped population growth curveA sigmoid curve of population size against time, annotated to show that the initial steep, exponential portion of the curve corresponds to conditions of low or reduced limiting factors, while the curve begins to level off where competition for limiting factors increases.

The phases can also be described using rates of natality (N), mortality (M), immigration (I) and emigration (E):

  • Natality: the rate at which individuals are born (birth rate).
  • Mortality: the rate at which individuals die (death rate).
  • Immigration: movement of new individuals into a population from outside a given area.
  • Emigration: the departure of individuals from a population in a given area.

Logarithmic graphs

Biological data sometimes cover such a wide range of values that they are difficult to plot on a standard graph — for example, yeast population growth measured with a haemocytometer. In such cases it is preferable to plot the base-ten logarithm of the measurements rather than the raw data.

Some measurements, such as pH and absorbance, are already logarithmic and have no units, since logarithms are pure numbers. A logarithmic scale compresses the range of values, giving more space to smaller values and compressing the space for larger ones. Each cycle on the scale increases by a power of 10 (e.g. 1, 2, 3… then 10, 20, 30… then 100, 200, 300…).

To test population growth against the exponential growth model, population size can be plotted on a logarithmic y-axis against time on a linear x-axis (or calculated using =LOG10() in a spreadsheet). If growth is exponential, this produces a straight line, since the logarithm increases only slowly.

Figure C4.1.12: Log-linear graph paperA graph with a logarithmic y-axis divided into three cycles (0.1 to 1.0, 1.0 to 10.0, and 10.0 to 100.0) and a linear x-axis (0 to 15). Because the y-axis is already logarithmic, raw numbers can be plotted directly onto the paper without first calculating their log10 values.

Top tip! Quantities that represent ratios of two values, such as absorbance or pH, do not have units, because they are logarithmic functions and logarithms are always pure numbers.

Nature of science – models: the population growth curve is an idealized graphical model. Real population growth is unlikely to show such smooth curves — fluctuations occur due to both density-independent and density-dependent factors. Models are simplifications that reveal overall patterns rather than the fine detail of individual cases, and they allow predictions of the patterns expected under given parameters.

Modelling of the sigmoid population growth curve

Data on population growth can be collected practically using rapidly reproducing organisms such as yeast or duckweed.

Modelling the growth curve using duckweed

Duckweeds (genus Lemna) are free-floating aquatic plants that form a staple diet for ducks and other aquatic birds. Because they reproduce rapidly and follow the classic sigmoid growth curve, they are widely used as a model system in population ecology.

Figure C4.1.13: Duckweed (Lemna)A photograph of duckweed thalli covering a pond surface, alongside a labelled diagram of an individual duckweed thallus (a leaf unit over 1.5 mm in diameter) showing the thallus and its roots hanging below.
Method: modelling growth with duckweed
  1. A small number (e.g. 15) of duckweed thalli are placed in a cup or beaker of pond water (200–300 cm³).
  2. At least five replicates of the set-up are prepared.
  3. The number of thalli is counted twice a week for at least six weeks.
  4. The mean number of thalli is calculated and plotted against time, producing an S-shaped (sigmoid) growth curve.
Carrying capacity is reached when there is no room for further thalli and competition for space limits further growth. The effect of environmental variables such as salinity, pH, nitrate concentration or phosphate level on growth rate can also be investigated, changing one variable at a time.

Communities in ecosystems

A community consists of all the living things in an ecosystem — the total of all the populations of all species present. For example, the community of a well-stocked pond includes rooted, floating and submerged plant populations, bottom-living animals, open-water fish and invertebrates, and surface-living organisms. Communities include all the biotic parts of an ecosystem: plants, animals and fungi.

Key termCommunity: a group of different species living in an area.
Figure C4.1.14: A community of wild animals at a waterholeA photograph of wildebeest, zebras, and other species congregating together around a waterhole in Etosha National Park, Namibia — illustrating how many different populations of species interact and coexist within one community.

Common mistake: do not confuse “population” and “community” — in ecology a community consists of many species living together, whereas a population refers to just one species.

Concept: InteractionA community is made up of many interacting populations of different species within an ecosystem. These interactions maintain stability in the ecosystem.

Competition versus cooperation in intraspecific relationships

Interactions within a species may be competitive or cooperative. Competition can occur within the same species (intraspecific) or between different species (interspecific).

Intraspecific vs interspecific competition Intraspecific competition occurs between individuals of the same species. Because individuals of the same species share the same niche, intraspecific competition can be the most intense form of competition, since organisms compete for exactly the same resources (food, mates, territory, water, space).
Interspecific competition occurs between individuals of different species. Since competing species have different niches, there is generally less overlap in shared resources, so interspecific competition tends to be less intense than intraspecific competition.
Key terms Intraspecific competition: competition between individuals of the same species.
Interspecific competition: competition between individuals of different species.
Cooperation: the action or process of working together.
Figure C4.1.15: Intraspecific competition in blue wildebeestA photograph of two male blue wildebeest (Connochaetes taurinus) confronting each other, competing for territory in the Kalahari Desert — an example of intraspecific competition for a limited resource.

Many animals have evolved cooperative behaviours to minimize the impact of direct competition — for example, dominance hierarchies within a group reduce fighting and injury risk. Cooperation is widespread across biological levels: genes cooperate within genomes, cells cooperate within tissues, and individuals cooperate within societies.

Figure C4.1.16: Hyena clan cooperationA photograph of a clan of hyenas hunting together. Hyena clans are led by a dominant female (“queen”), but all clan members benefit from more effective, cooperative hunting and killing of prey compared with hunting alone.

Social insects (bees, wasps, ants, termites) live in colonies where a single reproductive female is supported by large numbers of non-breeding workers. Because all offspring share one mother, the whole colony is genetically similar and can be viewed as a single “superorganism”. Seemingly altruistic behaviours, such as risking oneself to protect the colony or raising others’ offspring instead of reproducing, can be explained by this shared genetic inheritance.

Key termAltruism: behaviour of an animal that benefits another at its own expense. A behaviour pattern is altruistic if it increases the number of offspring produced by the recipient and decreases that of the altruist.
Figure C4.1.17: A honeybee colonyA photograph of the queen bee (Apis mellifera, marked with a dot) surrounded by sterile worker bees, illustrating a reproductive division of labour within a eusocial insect colony.
Figure C4.1.18: Leafcutter antsA photograph of leafcutter ants (Atta sp.) carrying cut leaf fragments back to their nest.

Leafcutter ants of Central and South American rainforests build nests with thousands of compartments, covering up to 0.5 km², with mature colonies exceeding 8 million individuals. Working collectively, the ants cut and carry leaves (up to 20–50 times their own body weight) back to the colony, where the leaves are fed to a fungus, Leucoagaricus gongylophorus, which the ants cultivate like a crop. The ants cannot digest cellulose in plant cell walls, but the fungus can, and it serves as food for ant larvae. Through this cooperation, up to 17% of the vegetation in a forest can be harvested by leafcutter ants, supporting the colony.

Interspecific relationships within communities

When resources are limited, organisms must compete for them; interspecific competition may be intense enough to exclude one species entirely (e.g. species of Paramecium competing for plankton, or barnacle species competing for rocky-shore space).

Herbivory, predation, interspecific competition, mutualism, parasitism and pathogenicity are all categories of interspecific relationship within communities.

Herbivory

Herbivory is when an organism feeds on a plant. The carrying capacity of herbivores is affected by the quantity of plant resources available — an area rich in plant material supports a higher carrying capacity for herbivores than one with less plant material.

Predation

Predation is when one animal (or occasionally a plant) eats another animal. The number of prey is reduced by predation, lowering the prey’s carrying capacity; conversely, the predator’s carrying capacity is affected by prey availability, since predator numbers fall when prey become scarce.

Mutualism

Mutualism (a specific type of symbiosis) is an interaction in which both species derive benefit, and it can increase the carrying capacity of both species involved.

Figure C4.1.19: Mutualism between fly agaric fungus and a treeA photograph of a fly agaric mushroom (Amanita muscaria) growing near tree roots. The fungus takes sugars and amino acids from the tree’s roots via its underground hyphae, and in return supplies the tree with essential mineral ions.

Parasitism

Parasitism is a relationship in which the parasite benefits at the expense of the host organism from which it derives its food, lowering the host’s carrying capacity.

Pathogenicity

Diseases are caused by pathogens — bacteria, viruses, fungi and single-celled protistans. Pathogenicity is the capacity of a microbe to cause damage to a host, resulting in disease. A pathogen may reduce the carrying capacity of the population it infects, and changes in pathogen incidence can cause a population to rise and fall around its carrying capacity.

Figure C4.1.20: The evergreen robber frogA photograph of an evergreen robber frog (Craugastor gollmeri) in a conservation laboratory. This species has suffered significant population declines across Costa Rica and Panama due to chytridiomycosis, an amphibian disease caused by the fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, which has driven population declines in over 200 amphibian species (and some extinctions) over the past 30 years.
Key terms Mutualism: an interaction in which both species derive benefit; a specific type of symbiotic relationship.
Parasitism: a relationship where one organism (the parasite) benefits at the expense of another (the host) from which it derives its food.
Pathogenicity: the capacity of a microbe to cause damage in a host resulting in disease.

Mutualism as an interspecific relationship that benefits both species

Concept: InterdependenceIn a mutualistic relationship, there is an interdependence between two species from which both benefit.

Root nodules in the legume family (Fabaceae)

Rhizobium is a bacterium that lives symbiotically in the root nodules of legume plants (e.g. clover).

How the Rhizobium–legume mutualism works
  • Rhizobium fixes nitrogen gas into ammonium ions, which other bacteria convert to nitrates. The plant benefits from the nitrates; in exchange it supplies carbohydrates to the bacteria in the root nodule. This is nutritionally costly for the plant, but allows it to survive in nitrate-deficient soils that exclude non-symbiotic plants, giving legumes access to a niche other plants cannot exploit.
  • Rhizobium is aerobic: nitrogen fixation requires large amounts of ATP, which could not be produced in sufficient quantity anaerobically.
  • Rhizobium contains the enzyme nitrogenase, which catalyses nitrogen fixation. This reaction is blocked by oxygen through competitive inhibition, because nitrogen and oxygen molecules are similar in size and shape, so oxygen must be excluded from the site of fixation. This is achieved in two ways: Rhizobium has an exceptionally fast, efficient aerobic metabolism that prevents oxygen build-up; and the root nodule contains leghaemoglobin, an oxygen-scavenging molecule with a high affinity for oxygen (like haemoglobin), which removes oxygen from the nodule so nitrogenase can function.

Top tip! Farmers plant clover to raise soil nitrate content and plough the crop into the ground rather than harvesting it. Regular ploughing and good drainage keep soil conditions aerobic for the Rhizobium.

Figure C4.1.21: Root nodules on clover rootsA photograph of clover roots bearing small rounded nodules created by the nitrogen-fixing bacterium Rhizobium trifolii. The bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonium ions, which nitrifying bacteria further convert into nitrate ions — something clover cannot do itself — and the plant absorbs the nitrate and converts it into amino acids.
Key terms Root nodule: a small swelling on a plant root containing symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
Symbiotic: a close and long-term biological interaction between two different species.

Mycorrhizae in the orchid family (Orchidaceae)

Mycorrhiza is a fungus that grows in mutualism with plant roots, with hyphae penetrating both soil and plant tissue. Orchid seeds are very small with limited food reserves, so germinating orchids obtain nutrients and water from a fungal symbiont; in return the mycorrhiza gains photosynthetically-derived glucose from the orchid.

Figure C4.1.22: Mycorrhizal fungus and the lady-slipper orchidTwo images: (a) a scanning electron micrograph (SEM) of mycorrhizal fungal hyphae; (b) a photograph of the lady-slipper orchid (Cypripedium calceolus), a rare terrestrial orchid of temperate and northern regions that depends on a symbiotic relationship with specific mycorrhizae.

The lady-slipper orchid is very rare and threatened; conservation efforts using tissue culture can grow new plants in the lab, but reintroducing these seedlings to the wild is difficult because they lack the specific symbiotic mycorrhizae they need there.

Key termMycorrhiza (plural: mycorrhizae): a fungus that grows in association with the roots of a plant in a symbiotic relationship.

Corals and zooxanthellae

Coral reefs arise from a symbiotic relationship between a cnidarian animal (the coral, related to sea anemones and jellyfish) and a photosynthetic algae (zooxanthellae). Corals are colonial organisms made of many interconnected, interdependent polyps (each 1–3 mm across), each with stinging tentacles around a central mouth. Polyps are linked by a thin tissue layer allowing nutrient sharing, and secrete a calcium carbonate skeleton that forms the coral reef structure. Zooxanthellae live within the cells of the coral’s endodermis (innermost lining).

Figure C4.1.23: Anatomy of a coral polypA labelled cross-section of a coral polyp showing: tentacles bearing nematocysts (stinging cells) surrounding a central mouth; a gastrovascular cavity (digestive sac) inside the polyp; zooxanthellae (algal cells) embedded within the polyp’s tissue; a limestone calice (skeletal cup) beneath the polyp; and living tissue linking neighbouring polyps together across the colony.
Figure C4.1.24: The symbiotic relationship between coral and zooxanthellaeA diagram showing exchanges between the coral polyp and its zooxanthellae: sunlight drives photosynthesis in the zooxanthellae, using CO&sub2; released by the coral’s respiration and producing O&sub2; and nutrients (glucose) that are passed to the coral; the coral also captures plankton and secretes CaCO&sub3; (using Ca²⁺ and HCO₃⁻ from the water) to build its skeleton.

The coral provides the algae with a protected environment and the CO&sub2; needed for photosynthesis (a waste product of the coral’s own respiration). In return, the algae produce oxygen, use up the coral’s waste CO&sub2;, and supply the coral with glucose from photosynthesis — as much as 90% of the organic material the algae produce is transferred to the coral host tissue. The zooxanthellae also give coral its colour: millions of zooxanthellae live in just a few square centimetres of coral, with pigments visible through the polyp’s clear body, ranging from golden-yellow to brown.

Resource competition between endemic and invasive species

Alien species are introduced into an area by human activity. Invasive species are alien species that increase rapidly in number and have a negative effect on the environment and native species.

Figure C4.1.25: Red squirrel and grey squirrelA photograph comparing the native red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris) and the introduced grey squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis).

The grey squirrel, native to eastern North America, was introduced to the UK in the 1800s and has since driven the native red squirrel back to just two UK locations, from previously being widespread. The grey squirrel spread rapidly partly because it can digest polyphenolic substances in oak acorns that the red squirrel cannot, giving it a wider diet. Although the two species have similar niches, the grey squirrel’s wider diet let it outbreed and outcompete the red squirrel, even for resources both species use (hazelnuts, pine cones). Because the niches of the two species overlap, competitive exclusion occurs, with the less-effective competitor (the red squirrel) declining.

Introducing an alien species can force both the alien and the native species into smaller realized niches due to interspecific competition. If the alien species lacks natural predators, it may outcompete the native species and become invasive. Understanding the fundamental niche of invasive species helps predict their dispersal and invasion success, informing conservation and management policy.

Tests for interspecific competition

Interspecific competition is indicated, but not proven, if one species is more successful in the absence of another. Approaches to investigating it include laboratory experiments, field observations by random sampling, and field manipulation by removing one species.

Connell’s barnacle experiments

American ecologist Joseph Connell studied two barnacle species on rocky shores in Scotland to investigate the difference between fundamental and realized niche. He observed that Semibalanus (Balanus) balanoides was most abundant in the lower intertidal zone, while Chthamalus stellatus was most common in the upper intertidal zone, even though the free-swimming larvae of both species could settle anywhere on the shore. He asked why the two species did not grow together.

Connell’s two experiments Experiment 1 (upper shore): Connell removed Semibalanus from the upper shore. Over time, no Chthamalus replaced it. His explanation: Semibalanus could not survive the desiccation of the upper shore during low tide regardless of competition, so its realized niche there was the same as its fundamental niche (it was physiologically excluded, not competitively excluded).
Experiment 2 (middle shore): Connell removed Semibalanus from the middle shore. Over time, Chthamalus did replace it. His explanation: Semibalanus is normally the superior competitor in the middle intertidal zone and usually excludes Chthamalus from there through competition, not physiological tolerance.
Figure C4.1.26: Connell’s barnacle removal experiment resultsTwo line graphs of percentage survival of Chthamalus (log scale, 1–80%) against year (1954–1955), one for the upper intertidal zone and one for the middle intertidal zone, each comparing a line where Semibalanus was removed against a control line where Semibalanus remained present. In the upper zone, removing Semibalanus made little difference to Chthamalus survival (the two lines stay close together). In the middle zone, Chthamalus survival was much higher when Semibalanus was removed than when it was present (the “removed” line rises well above the “present” line).

Connell concluded that the fundamental and realized niches of Chthamalus were not the same — its realized niche was smaller than its fundamental niche because of interspecific competition with Semibalanus, resulting in competitive exclusion from the middle/lower shore.

Figure C4.1.27: Fundamental and realized niches of two barnacle species(a) A diagram of a rocky shore cross-section from high tide to low tide, showing that the fundamental niche of each barnacle species (the full zone it could physiologically occupy) is wider than its realized niche (the zone it is actually observed in), with Semibalanus confined by desiccation risk at the top and Chthamalus confined by competitive exclusion from Semibalanus below; paired photographs show Semibalanus (easily crowded out of exposed upper positions) and Chthamalus (able to withstand prolonged exposure and survive higher on the shore). (b) A diagram summarizing Connell’s second experiment: once Semibalanus was removed from the middle zone, Chthamalus expanded into that zone, showing that its fundamental niche covers both upper and middle zones, but competition normally restricts it to just the upper zone (its realized niche).
Key termCompetitive exclusion: when one species outcompetes and excludes another, where their niches overlap.

Connell’s barnacles show one outcome of competition — elimination of a species from part of its fundamental niche. Another possible outcome is restriction of both competing species without full elimination of either: for example, lions and leopards in Africa feed on some of the same prey and, where they coexist, each negatively affects the other’s food availability, restricting both species rather than eliminating either.

Top tip! Elimination of one competing species, or restriction of both to only part of their fundamental niche, are both possible outcomes of interspecific competition.

Hypotheses in ecological investigation

A hypothesis is a provisional, testable explanation of an observed phenomenon, proposing how an independent variable causally affects a dependent variable, generally with reference to a scientific model. Hypotheses can be tested by experiment (where one independent variable is changed and all others controlled where possible) or by observation (which is qualitative rather than quantitative). An experiment with controlled variables provides stronger support for a hypothesis than observation alone — illustrated by Connell’s work, where only removal experiments (not observation of natural distributions alone) could demonstrate the fundamental niches of the two barnacle species.

Key termHypothesis: a provisional explanation of an observed phenomenon or event that can be investigated using the scientific method; in a statistical test, the hypothesis states that there is a statistically significant difference between two variables.

Using the chi-squared test for association between two species

Quadrats can record the presence or absence of two species under investigation. The resulting data can be tested statistically using the chi-squared (χ2) test, which examines data falling into discrete categories, testing the significance of deviations between numbers observed (O) and numbers expected (E). The chi-squared value is converted to a probability using a chi-squared table, allowing a decision on whether observed differences are likely to be real or due to chance.

Key termNull hypothesis: there is no statistically significant difference (or, for association tests, no association) between two variables. The alternative hypothesis is the opposite — that there is a significant difference or association.

Predator–prey relationships

In a predator–prey relationship, one species (the predator) feeds on another (the prey) — e.g. a lynx preying on a snowshoe hare. Predator–prey relationships are an example of density-dependent control of animal populations, typically controlled by negative feedback mechanisms that regulate population densities over time.

Key termPredator–prey relationship: the inter-relationship of population sizes due to predation of one species (the predator) on another (the prey).
Figure C4.1.29: Snowshoe hare and lynx populations, 1845–1945A graph of population numbers (in thousands, y-axis) against year (x-axis, 1845–1945) with two cyclical lines: snowshoe hare (with much higher peak numbers) and lynx (with lower, delayed peaks). The two populations oscillate together over roughly 8–10 year cycles, with the lynx population peak consistently lagging slightly behind the hare population peak.

When lynx (predators) are relatively scarce, the hare (prey) population increases. As prey become more available, predator numbers rise, but only after a time-lag. As predator numbers increase, the prey population begins to decline (again after a time-lag). With fewer prey, predator numbers fall again, allowing prey numbers to begin recovering — and the cycle repeats.

Predation can actually benefit the prey population overall: predators tend to remove old and sick individuals first, as these are easiest to catch, leaving a healthier remaining population with a superior breeding pool.

Common mistake: “negative” feedback does not mean the feedback loop is harmful to the environment — it usually counteracts deviation away from a steady-state equilibrium. Likewise, “positive” feedback does not mean a beneficial effect — it increases change in a system, moving it further from steady-state equilibrium.

Top-down and bottom-up control of populations in communities

A trophic level is a feeding level within a food chain.

Top-down vs bottom-up control Top-down control (predator-controlled food web): a higher trophic level influences community structure at a lower trophic level through predation. Changes at the top of the food chain impact lower trophic levels.
Bottom-up control: a lower trophic level (producers) affects community structure at higher trophic levels through resource restriction (e.g. food source, habitat, space). Changes at the base of the food chain impact higher trophic levels.
Key terms Top-down control: changes to the food chain occur at the top trophic level and then impact the trophic levels lower in the food chain.
Bottom-up control: changes to the food chain occur at the lowest trophic level (producers) and then impact the trophic levels higher in the food chain.
Figure C4.1.30: Top-down and bottom-up control in a four-level marine food chainTwo paired sets of graphs (abundance against time) for a four-trophic-level marine food chain (predators → forage fish → zooplankton → phytoplankton). (a) Top-down control: hunting reduces the number of top predators, which leads to an increase in forage fish (the predators’ prey), which in turn causes a decrease in zooplankton (eaten more by the now-abundant forage fish), which in turn allows phytoplankton to increase (reduced grazing pressure from zooplankton) — effects cascade downward from the top of the food chain. (b) Bottom-up control: extreme weather reduces phytoplankton numbers, which in turn reduces zooplankton (less food), which reduces forage fish, which reduces predator numbers — effects cascade upward from the base of the food chain.

Both top-down and bottom-up control are possible in a community, but typically one or the other dominates.

Allelopathy and secretion of antibiotics

Allelopathy

Competition between plants can be severe, reducing carrying capacity for both species where competition is interspecific. Allelopathy is a mechanism giving one plant a competitive advantage over another. The term derives from Greek allelon (“each other”) and pathos (“to suffer”) — the same root as “pathogen”.

Key termAllelopathy: chemical inhibition of one plant (or other organism) by another, due to the release of chemicals (allelochemicals) that act as germination or growth inhibitors.

Allelopathic plants create adverse conditions for neighbouring competitor plants, reducing their seed germination, seedling growth or overall growth. Some allelochemicals reduce chlorophyll production, slowing or stopping photosynthesis and suppressing or killing the affected plant.

Mechanisms of allelopathy
  • Releasing allelochemicals from roots into the soil, which suppress or kill neighbouring plants when absorbed.
  • Releasing gaseous allelochemicals from leaf stomata, which suppress or kill plants that absorb them.
  • Dropping leaves containing toxic chemicals, which are released as the leaves decompose, inhibiting competitors’ growth.
Figure C4.1.31: Broccoli, an allelopathic plantA photograph of a broccoli plant. All brassica plants (cabbage, mustard, kale, radish, broccoli) show allelopathic properties — mustard extracts, for example, can suppress fungal pathogens and limit germination of some legumes (e.g. lentils) and oats. Broccoli is allelopathic to later-planted broccoli or other brassica crops, so farmers rotate crops and avoid replanting brassicas where broccoli was recently grown.
Figure C4.1.32: Common bracken fernA photograph of bracken (Pteridium aquilinum), which competitively excludes other plants through allelopathy, becoming dominant wherever it grows and reducing local biodiversity, since associated plants are severely inhibited or excluded from dense stands of the fern.

Secretion of antibiotics

The first mass-produced antibiotic for treating human bacterial disease was penicillin, derived from Penicillium fungi and accidentally discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1929 while studying Staphylococcus (the bacterium causing boils and sore throats). Fungi naturally produce antibiotics to kill or inhibit bacteria, reducing competition with the fungus. This is itself a form of allelopathy — the fungus releases a chemical into the environment to deter potential competitors — and these properties have been exploited by scientists to produce antibiotic medicines.

Top tip! Allelopathy and the secretion of antibiotics are similar processes: both involve releasing a chemical substance into the environment to deter potential competitors.
