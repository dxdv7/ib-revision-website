Production of Unspecialized Cells Following Fertilization

Key termsZygote: cell produced by the fusion of gametes. Cell cycle: repeating process of an orderly sequence of events where cells arise by the division of existing cells, grow, and then divide. Embryo: an organism in the earliest stages of growth, when its basic structures are being formed.

Multicellular organisms begin life as a single cell, the zygote, which grows and divides, at first producing unspecialized cells and then forming many specialized cells that eventually form the adult organism. Cells arise by the division of existing cells, and the time between one cell division and the next is the cell cycle.

Figure B2.3.1: The life history of a cell and the role of stem cellsA flow diagram showing a young cell undergoing cell division and cell growth; some cells continue to grow and divide repeatedly rather than specializing (stem cells, e.g. the stem cells of bone marrow, which divide to form the cells that develop into red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets, as well as more stem cells). Other cells undergo cell specialization to become a mature cell that is unlikely or unable to divide again – for example, a red blood cell, which loses its nucleus and fills its cytoplasm with the oxygen-carrying pigment haemoglobin and the enzyme carbonic anhydrase.

In healthy organisms, cells eventually die by programmed cell death (PCD), a process controlled by specific genes. In PCD, all parts of a cell are packaged in membrane and engulfed by surrounding cells. PCD normally removes superfluous, infected and damaged cells and is a key part of tissue and organ development.

Morphogens and differentiation

MorphogenA molecule present in a concentration gradient that specifies the fate of each cell along the gradient.

During early-stage embryo development, complex mechanisms of gene expression determine how cells differentiate and take on specific roles. A small number of genes determine body patterns during development. The signalling molecules involved are called morphogens. Morphogens are extracellular (they exist outside the cells of a tissue) and occur across a gradient of concentrations. This gradient drives the differentiation of unspecialized stem cells into different cell types: where morphogen concentration is high, cells change differently to cells where the concentration is lower. The initiation or inhibition of gene expression results from these different morphogen concentrations, controlling how cells differentiate and develop into specific tissues.

The concentration of morphogen in each cell determines a series of subsequent signals (cascades). Responses to these signals determine the direction and extent of cell growth and development, ultimately forming all the tissues and organs of the body. Expression of such gradients also controls the length of body structures such as toes and fingers, and the location of the nose and other body patterns.

Properties of Stem Cells

Key termsBlastocyst: embryo as a fluid-filled ball of cells, at the stage of implantation. Embryonic stem cell: undifferentiated cell in the early-stage embryo, capable of continual cell division and of developing into all the cell types of an adult organism. Stem cell: undifferentiated cell in the embryo or adult that can undergo unlimited division and can give rise to one of many different cell types.

The first step after formation of the zygote is continual cell division to produce a tiny ball of cells called the blastocyst. All these cells are capable of further division. The cells of the inner cell mass of the blastocyst are known as embryonic stem cells.

Stem cell propertiesA stem cell has the ability for endless repeated cell division. Stem cells maintain an undifferentiated state (called self-renewal), while also having the capacity to differentiate into mature cell types (potency) along different pathways. Stem cells are the “building blocks” of life, dividing to form cells that develop into the range of mature cell types of the organism, and are found in all multicellular organisms.

At the next stage of embryological development, most cells lose the ability to divide and differentiate as they develop into the tissues and organs of the organism, such as blood, nerves, liver and brain. However, a very few cells within these tissues keep many of the properties of embryonic stem cells: these are called adult stem cells.

Although all cells in a multicellular organism contain the same genetic code (because they formed from one cell that divided repeatedly by mitosis), cells can differentiate into different functions. Location and Function of Stem Cell Niches in Adult Humans

Stem cell nicheA specific microenvironment in the body that either maintains stem cells in an undifferentiated and self-renewable state or promotes their proliferation and differentiation. Stimuli in the niche include cell-to-cell and molecular signals that activate or repress genes and the subsequent transcription of proteins. As a result, stem cells are maintained in a dormant state, caused to self-renew, or committed to a more differentiated state. Stem cell niches have defined locations in the body; two examples are explored below.

Bone marrow

Bone marrow within the skeleton is the site of blood cell formation (haematopoiesis) for all vertebrates except fish and some amphibians. In human adults, osteoblasts (cells that regulate the creation of new bone) and haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs, which produce the cellular components of blood and blood plasma) are closely connected in the bone marrow. HSCs produce red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.

A subgroup of osteoblasts functions as a key component of the HSC niche (the osteoblastic niche), controlling HSC numbers – suggesting these cells rely on each other to determine their functions, with cells that give rise to mature skeletal cells being critical in regulating HSCs. The vascular niche (the site of blood vessels) in adult bone marrow is a place for stem cell mobilization, or for proliferation and differentiation.

Hair follicles

Hair follicles are structures of mammalian skin that can regenerate to continuously produce new hair. The stem cell niche of hair follicles is located between the opening of the sebaceous gland and the attachment site of the hair erector muscle: these are known as bulge hair follicle stem cells. These stem cells are multipotent and have the potential to increase rapidly. During the growth phase, hair follicle stem cells become activated to regenerate the hair follicle and hair, and hairs grow longer each day. During the resting phase, the stem cells are dormant and hairs can shed more easily.

Complex physiological changes take place in the hair follicle with age. Hair follicles have pigment cells that make melanin; certain stem cells act as pigment-producing cells, and when hair regenerates, some of the stem cells convert into pigment-producing cells that colour the hair. With age, fewer of these cells are active and the hair loses its ability to produce melanin, so hair greys or turns white. An imbalance in stem cell differentiation and altered stem cell activity can also lead to hair loss with age.

Totipotent, Pluripotent and Multipotent Stem Cells

Key termsTotipotent: capable of giving rise to any cell type in an organism, including making more totipotent stem cells and making the cells that become the placenta. Pluripotent: able to develop into many different types of cells or tissues in the body, except for becoming placental cells or totipotent stem cells. Multipotent: can create, maintain and repair the cells of one particular organ or tissue.

The first-formed stem cells of a zygote are totipotent and are the most versatile type of stem cell: they can become any type of body cell, including making more totipotent stem cells and the cells that become the placenta. Only the cells within the first couple of cell divisions after fertilization are totipotent.

Cell division continues, and the cells organize into the fluid-filled blastocyst. Stem cells in these subsequent divisions of the embryo are pluripotent – a little more limited in potential, as they can give rise to all of the cell types that make up the body except placental cells or totipotent stem cells.

By the time differentiation has occurred, some stem cells are able only to develop and form the nervous system: this restricted potency is called multipotent. Multipotent stem cells can develop into more than one cell type but are more limited than pluripotent cells, having a limited capacity for self-renewal; they can create, maintain and repair the cells of one particular organ or tissue. Stem cells in adult tissue, such as bone marrow, are multipotent.

The blastocyst’s inner cell mass gives rise to embryonic stem cells (pluripotent). Common mistakeMake sure you know the difference between totipotent, pluripotent and multipotent. Simply referring to “stem cells” is ambiguous, so the specific type of stem cell should be named, especially when discussing a particular stage of development. As stem cells mature, their function becomes more limited: early stem cells can differentiate into any cell type in the body, including placental cells, while later stem cells can only become a limited range of cells in a particular tissue or organ.

Cell Size as an Aspect of Specialization

Cells exist at a wide variety of different scales, with each type of cell adapted to its function in part by its size.

Figure B2.3.4: Different sizes of cells (not to scale)A comparative diagram of cell sizes: a neuron in the brain (300–400 µm long), a plant cell (100–150 µm), a muscle fibre (300 mm in length), a Paramecium (250–300 µm), a human cheek cell (30–50 µm), a white blood cell (12–17 µm), a red blood cell (8 µm), a human egg (100 µm) and a human sperm cell (55 µm).

Male and female gametes

Human male and female gametes are very different in size. The egg cell is around 100 µm, whereas the main body of the sperm is 5 µm, with a mid-piece (containing mitochondria) and flagellum of 50 µm. The larger cell body of the egg allows it to store nutrients for early development of the fertilized egg, whereas the cell body of the sperm only needs to hold the nucleus for delivery to the egg, so it can be much smaller. The long flagellum of the sperm provides propulsion to reach the egg.

Figure B2.3.5: Scanning electron micrograph of a sperm cell fertilizing an egg cell (×3600)An SEM image showing the marked difference in size between a single sperm cell attaching to the much larger surface of an egg cell during fertilization.

Blood cells

Red blood cells (erythrocytes) are around 8 µm, much smaller than white blood cells (around 12–17 µm). Red blood cells need to be small so they can fit through the narrow lumen of capillaries; being small also gives the red blood cell a larger surface area relative to its size, allowing oxygen to diffuse in and out at a faster rate. White blood cells attack and destroy foreign matter such as bacteria, viruses or other organisms entering the body. They are bigger than red blood cells but fewer in number (though they increase on infection); their larger size allows them either to engulf and digest pathogens or to produce antibodies that bind to and destroy the body’s foreign invaders.

Figure B2.3.6: A light micrograph of a blood smear showing erythrocytes and white blood cellsA stained blood smear in which red blood cells are clearly discernible by their colour and lack of nucleus, while white blood cells show a purple-stained nucleus that is either large and rounded (lymphocytes) or “lobed”, with several sections joined together (phagocytes).

Neurons

Neurons (nerve cells) transmit electrochemical impulses through the body, allowing coordination and response to stimuli. They differ in length – the longest neuron in the human body reaches from the base of the spine to the foot, up to one metre. This long length enables the electrical impulse to be sent without interruption over a long distance; fast transmission, achieved by the long, myelinated neuron, is especially important for rapid responses such as those to pain.

Striated muscle fibres

Striated muscle fibres are multinucleated cells that attach to muscles to allow movement. Instead of being made from many individual cells, muscle fibres are extended cellular structures that can be several centimetres long. This extended length allows them to coordinate contraction and has a significant effect on muscle force generation.

Surface Area-to-Volume Ratios and Constraints on Cell Size

The materials required for growth and maintenance of a cell enter through the plasma membrane, and metabolic waste products must leave through it too. The rate at which materials can enter and leave a cell depends on the cell’s surface area, but the rate at which materials are used and waste is produced depends on the amount of cytoplasm present (the volume). Heat transfer between the cytoplasm and the cell’s environment is likewise determined by surface area.

As cells grow and increase in size, an important difference develops between the surface area available for exchange and the volume of cytoplasm in which the chemical reactions of life occur. Volume increases faster than surface area, so the surface area-to-volume ratio (SA:V) falls as cell size increases. With increasing cell size, therefore, less and less of the cytoplasm has access to the cell surface for exchange of gases, supply of nutrients and loss of metabolic waste products.

The exchange of materials across a cell surface depends on its surface area, whereas the need for exchange depends on cell volume. The smaller a cell is, the more quickly and easily materials can be exchanged between its cytoplasm and environment. One consequence is that cells cannot grow larger indefinitely: when a maximum size is reached, cell growth stops and the cell may then divide.

The square–cube lawThe relative differences in the changes of surface area and volume as objects get bigger or smaller is known as the square–cube law: the volume of an object grows faster than its surface area, because surface area changes as a factor of the squared value of length, whereas volume changes as a factor of the cubed value. For example, if the edge length of a cube is doubled, its surface area becomes four times its original value, while its volume becomes eight times its original value. This limits cell size, because the smaller relative surface area of larger cells means oxygen and food cannot be transported in, and wastes cannot be removed, at a sufficient rate to maintain metabolic activities.

Higher Level Adaptations to Increase Surface Area-to-Volume Ratios of Cells

Red blood cells (erythrocytes)

Red blood cells transport respiratory gases (primarily oxygen) in the bloodstream. They are approximately 8 µm in diameter and have the shape of a flat, round disk with an indentation in the centre – described as biconcave. The flattening of the cell increases its surface area relative to its size, increasing the diffusion of oxygen across its surface, and ensures a short diffusion distance so oxygen can quickly move throughout the cell. The biconcave shape, achievable because the erythrocyte lacks a nucleus, further increases surface area and also increases the cell’s flexibility, helping it move through the narrow lumen of capillaries.

Figure B2.3.9: An erythrocyteA three-dimensional depiction of a red blood cell showing its flattened, biconcave (round disk with a central indentation on each face) shape.

Proximal convoluted tubule cells in the nephron

Key termsMicrovillus (plural, microvilli): one of many tiny infoldings of the plasma membrane, making up a brush border. Brush border: tiny, finger-like projections (microvilli) on the surface of epithelial cells, for example in the small intestine. Invagination: the intucking of a surface or membrane.

The kidney maintains homeostatic balance of water in the body and filters out metabolic wastes (e.g. urea); its functional units are the nephrons. The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the longest section of the nephron, and here a large part of the filtrate is reabsorbed into the capillary network. Epithelial cells in the PCT reabsorb components of the filtrate with nutritional significance (such as glucose, amino acids and ions). The walls of the tubule are one cell thick, and these cells are packed with mitochondria.

The surface facing the lumen (the apical surface) is folded into microvilli, forming a brush border, which greatly increases the surface area for absorption. On the opposite side – the membrane facing the blood at the base of the cell (the basolateral region) – invaginations (infoldings) of the plasma membrane similarly increase surface area. Invaginations of the basolateral membrane are commonly found in cells engaged in active transport of molecules and ions, where the infoldings increase the surface area available for transport.

Higher Level Adaptations of Type I and Type II Pneumocytes in Alveoli

Key termsAlveolus (plural, alveoli): air sac in the lung. Pneumocyte: specialized cell occurring in the alveoli of the lungs. Epithelium: sheet of cells, bound strongly together, covering internal or external surfaces of multicellular organisms. Surfactant: a detergent secreted by cells in the wall of alveoli which breaks surface tension and stops alveolar walls from sticking together, keeping the alveoli open.

There are some 700 million alveoli in our lungs, providing a total surface area of about 70 m² – an area 30–40 times greater than that of the body’s external skin. The alveoli are the major sites of gas exchange in the lungs, and their enormous surface area helps maximize gas exchange. Two types of epithelial cell, called pneumocytes, line the alveoli, with different adaptations for their different roles – the alveolar epithelium is an example of a tissue in which more than one cell type is present, because different adaptations are required for the tissue’s overall function.

Figure B2.3.12: The position of the alveoli in the human lungsA diagram of the human thorax showing the nasal cavity leading via the trachea to the two lungs, each supplied by a bronchus that branches into bronchioles ending in clusters of alveoli; also shown are the ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm and pleural membranes.

Type I pneumocytes

The wall of an alveolus is one cell thick, and a capillary lies very close to the alveolar wall, so the combined thickness of the alveolar and capillary walls separating air and blood is typically only 2–4 µm. The capillary is extremely narrow, just wide enough for red blood cells to squeeze through, so red blood cells are close to, or in contact with, the capillary walls. Type I pneumocytes carry out gas exchange between the alveoli and capillaries: they have a flattened shape, extremely thin to minimize diffusion distance, and are tightly connected to prevent leakage of tissue fluid into the alveolar air space.

Type II pneumocytes

Type II pneumocytes produce a detergent-like mixture of lipoproteins and phospholipid-rich secretion called surfactant, which lines the inner surface of the alveoli. Type II cells are cuboidal in shape and bigger than type I cells so they can store surfactant components, and they contain many secretory vesicles (lamellar bodies) in their cytoplasm that discharge surfactant into the alveolar lumen. Surface tension is a force acting to minimize the surface area of a liquid; because of the tiny diameter of the alveoli (about 0.25 mm), they would tend to collapse under surface tension during expiration, with the walls sticking together. The surfactant secreted by type II pneumocytes lowers surface tension, permitting the alveoli to flex easily as pressure within the thorax falls and rises.

Figure B2.3.13: Cells of the alveolar epitheliumA diagram of an alveolus opening off an airway, showing a flattened type I pneumocyte forming most of the alveolar wall in close contact with a capillary carrying a red blood cell, a cuboidal type II pneumocyte (surfactant cell) containing secretory vesicles, and an alveolar macrophage patrolling the inner surface.

Macrophages

The delicate structure of the alveoli is protected by macrophages (dust cells), abundant in the surface film of moisture – the main detritus-collecting cells of the body. They originate from bone marrow stem cells and are dispersed around the body in the blood circulation. These phagocytic white cells migrate into the alveoli from the capillaries, where they ingest debris, fine dust particles, bacteria and fungal spores, and also line the surfaces of the airways leading to the alveoli.

Higher Level Adaptations of Cardiac Muscle Cells and Striated Muscle Fibres

Key termsMyogenic: originating in heart muscle cells themselves, as in the generation of the heartbeat. Myofibril: contractile protein filament from which muscle is composed. Striated: muscle tissue marked by alternating dark and light bands. Sarcolemma: membranous sheath around a muscle fibre. Intercalated discs: link cardiac cells together and define their borders, and facilitate cell-to-cell communication needed for coordinated muscle contraction.

Cardiac muscle cells

Cardiac muscle is unique to the heart. Heart muscle fibres contract rhythmically from formation until they die. Muscles typically contract when stimulated by an external nerve supply; in the heart, however, the impulse to contract is generated within the heart muscle itself – it is said to have a myogenic origin, meaning the central nervous system is not needed to stimulate cardiac muscle to contract. This myogenic control plays a role in autoregulation: a constant flow of blood must be balanced with blood pressure, so the myogenic origin of the impulse allows the heart, and therefore blood circulation, to adapt quickly to changes in the body, such as those following exercise.

Individual cardiac muscle cells have a tubular structure composed of chains of fibres. Each fibre is composed of a mass of myofibrils, made up of repeating sections called sarcomeres, the fundamental contractile units of muscle cells. Cardiac muscle consists of cylindrical, branching columns of fibres forming a unique, three-dimensional network, allowing contraction in three dimensions and faster spread of the contraction signal. Each fibre has a single nucleus, and the repeating sarcomeres give it a striped or striated appearance under the microscope. Fibres are surrounded by a special plasma membrane, the sarcolemma, and are very well supplied by mitochondria and capillaries.

Figure B2.3.14: Cardiac muscleA photomicrograph-style diagram of cardiac muscle fibres, labelled with a branch point where one fibre splits into two, the striations (alternating dark and light bands), an intercalated disc at a junction between two cells, the sarcolemma (plasma membrane of the muscle fibre), and a single nucleus within a fibre.

Propagation of stimuli throughout the heart wall

The structure of cardiac muscle cells allows propagation of stimuli through the heart wall. Intercalated discs, present at the junctions between cardiac muscle cells, hold cells together so they cannot separate and consist of a double membrane with gap junctions, through which cytoplasmic connections form between adjacent cells. Gap junctions form channels allowing continuous flow of cytoplasm between cells; this direct electrochemical coupling allows waves of depolarization to pass through the entire network, synchronizing contraction of the muscle as if it were a single cell. The branching structure of cardiac muscle allows connection to multiple cells, providing a larger surface area of contact between cells and allowing groups of cells to work together and synchronize their activity. Although cardiac muscle fibres form an interconnected network, the network of the atrial walls is entirely separate from that of the ventricles, ensuring a transmission delay of the electrical signal between atria and ventricles.

Striated muscle fibres

TendonFibrous connective tissue connecting a muscle to bone.

Skeletal muscles are attached to the moveable parts of skeletons by tendons and work in antagonistic pairs; their contraction brings about movement. Skeletal muscle consists of bundles of muscle fibres. A remarkable feature of a muscle fibre is its ability to shorten to half, or even a third, of its relaxed or resting length. Fibres appear striped under the light microscope, so skeletal muscle is also known as striated muscle (like cardiac muscle). Each muscle fibre is composed of a mass of myofibrils.

Figure B2.3.15: The structure of skeletal muscleA diagram showing skeletal muscle cut to reveal a bundle of thousands of individual muscle fibres, connected to a tendon and surrounded by connective tissue; each individual fibre is shown containing several nuclei (a syncytium) and consisting of a mass of myofibrils, with a photomicrograph inset (×1500) showing the alternating light and dark bands (striations) of the fibres.

The ultrastructure of skeletal muscle

Using electron microscopy, each muscle fibre is seen to consist of many parallel myofibrils within a sarcolemma, together with cytoplasm (sarcoplasm) containing mitochondria packed between the myofibrils. Skeletal muscle fibres are multinucleate (containing multiple nuclei per cell) and contain specialized endoplasmic reticulum: the sarcolemma is folded to form a system of transverse tubular endoplasmic reticulum known as the sarcoplasmic reticulum, arranged as a network around individual myofibrils.

Figure B2.3.16: The ultrastructure of a muscle fibreA stereogram of part of a single muscle fibre alongside an electron micrograph of a transverse section (×36,000), labelled with the sarcoplasmic membrane (sarcolemma), myofibrils, sarcoplasm (cytoplasm of the muscle cell), mitochondria packed between myofibrils, and the sarcoplasmic reticulum with its transverse tubules forming a network around each myofibril.

Is a striated muscle fibre a cell?

The striated muscle fibres of mammalian skeletal muscle have an atypical cell structure: the fusion of cells to create one elongated, multinucleated fibre is very different from typical cell structure. Myofibrils give the impression of a structure beyond that of a simple cell, and muscle fibres are long and can measure 300 mm or more – much larger than regular cells. However, the fibres contain the usual organelles of a cell (such as mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum) and are surrounded by one cell membrane, indicating they are indeed cells – though striated muscle fibres challenge the view that cells act as autonomous (self-sufficient) units.

Striated muscle shares many similarities with cardiac muscle: both are surrounded and enclosed by a sarcolemma, from which transverse tubules (T tubules) tunnel in and around the sarcomeres; both possess a sarcoplasmic reticulum (a modified form of endoplasmic reticulum); both are striated in appearance with a similar arrangement of actin and myosin filaments; and both consist of fibres that can shorten by a half to a third of their length and contain many mitochondria to supply ATP for contraction.

Higher Level Adaptations of Sperm and Egg Cells

Key termsEgg/ovum (plural, ova): a female gamete. Sperm: motile male gamete. Oocyte: a female sex cell in the process of a meiotic division to become an ovum.

Sexual reproduction is the production of offspring from two parents using gametes – egg and sperm cells in animals – and involves two stages: meiosis, the special cell division that makes gametes with half the number of chromosomes (haploid); and fertilization, the fusion of two gametes to form a zygote (restoring two sets of chromosomes). Human gametes have specific adaptations that allow them to carry out their function.

Sperm cell

Figure B2.3.17: A human sperm cellA labelled diagram of a sperm cell showing the acrosome (containing hydrolytic enzymes) capping the haploid nucleus at the head, a centriole, the middle piece packed with mitochondria, and a flagellum containing microtubules in a 9 + 2 arrangement, all enclosed within a plasma membrane.

Structures and functions of a mature sperm cell: the flagellum provides motility; the middle piece contains many mitochondria to provide the energy required for movement of the flagellum; the head is surrounded by a plasma membrane and contains a haploid nucleus, smooth and oval in shape to make the cell streamlined for fast movement; and the acrosome (at the top of the head) contains enzymes for digesting the zona pellucida (jelly coat, containing glycoproteins) surrounding the oocyte (egg).

The role of a sperm is to deliver a nucleus from a male into the egg of a female, so fertilization can take place and a new zygote can form. Only the nucleus enters the egg cell – the rest of the cell (the neck and flagellum) remains outside the egg – which means all mitochondria in the resulting zygote come from the mother, via the egg cell.

Egg cell

Egg cells, or ova, go through several stages of development. Primary oocytes (immature egg cells) form after the first stage of meiosis. During the menstrual cycle, once a month, one primary oocyte begins the second stage of meiosis to produce a secondary oocyte and a polar body (containing a second haploid nucleus, but with unequal division of cytoplasm, so it fails to develop further). Meiosis is only completed at fertilization. By delaying the two divisions of meiosis (meiosis I and meiosis II), the oocyte is provided with more genetic material for longer, for mRNA production and subsequent protein synthesis – important as the cell grows and matures. Eggs are much larger than sperm as they contain all the nutrients needed for growth and development of the zygote, and only become the final egg cell on fertilization.

Structures and functions of a mature secondary oocyte: follicle cells nourish and protect the oocyte; the zona pellucida (jelly coat) allows binding of sperm cells, prevents polyspermy (several sperm haploid nuclei entering) and premature implantation of the embryo (ectopic pregnancy), and is also where the polar body is found; the plasma membrane surrounds the haploid nucleus and contains microvilli to absorb nutrients from follicle cells; cortical granules prevent polyspermy during fertilization; the cytoplasm is the site of metabolic reactions and where all organelles are located; and the nucleus is a haploid nucleus (half the chromosome number).

Figure B2.3.18: The structure of a mature secondary oocyteA labelled diagram of a secondary oocyte showing the haploid nucleus and surrounding cytoplasm, centrioles, cortical granules positioned just inside the plasma membrane, the plasma membrane of the secondary oocyte, a layer of follicle cells on the outside, the jelly coat (zona pellucida) between the follicle cells and the plasma membrane, and the polar body located in the space between the oocyte membrane and the zona pellucida.

Gas Exchange as a Vital Function in All Organisms

Gas exchangeThe exchange of respiratory gases (oxygen, carbon dioxide) between cells/organisms and the environment.

Cellular respiration is the controlled release of energy, in the form of ATP, from organic compounds in cells. Respiration occurs continuously in living cells, largely in the mitochondria of eukaryotic organisms and the cytoplasm of prokaryotic cells. To support aerobic cellular respiration, cells take in oxygen from their environment and release carbon dioxide by gas exchange. In animals, gas exchange means the uptake of oxygen and release of carbon dioxide. Plants uptake oxygen and release carbon dioxide at night, and take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen during the day.

Figure B3.1.1: Gas exchange in an animal cellA diagram of a generalized cell showing the mitochondrion, the organelle where some steps of energy transfer (respiration) occur. The exchange of gases between an individual cell and its environment takes place by diffusion. For example, in cells respiring aerobically there is a higher concentration of oxygen outside the cell than inside, so there is a continuous net inward diffusion of oxygen.

Factors determining rate of diffusion at a respiratory surfaceThe size of the surface area available for gas exchange (the respiratory surface) – the greater the surface area, the greater the rate of diffusion (in a single cell, the respiratory surface is the whole plasma membrane); the difference in concentration (concentration gradient) – a rapidly respiring organism has a much lower oxygen concentration in its cells and a higher-than-normal carbon dioxide concentration, and the greater this gradient across the respiratory surface, the greater the rate of diffusion; and the length of the diffusion path – the shorter the path, the greater the rate of diffusion, so the respiratory surface must be as thin as possible.

Energy for diffusion comes from the kinetic energy of molecules: the higher the temperature, the faster the rate of diffusion, due to the increased kinetic energy of molecules. In warm-blooded animals, temperature is kept constant through homeostatic mechanisms, so the effects of temperature on diffusion do not vary.

Challenges of gas exchange

The challenges of gas exchange become greater as organisms increase in size, because their surface area-to-volume ratio decreases with increasing size, and the distance from the centre of an organism to its exterior increases. The surface area of a single-celled organism is large in relation to the amount of cytoplasm it contains, so the surface of the cell is sufficient for efficient gas exchange. In large multicellular organisms, however, most cells are too far from the body surface to receive enough oxygen by diffusion alone. Animals also often develop a tough or hardened outer skin, which protects the body but is not suitable for gas exchange, so an alternative respiratory surface is required. Active organisms have an increased metabolic rate and a higher demand for oxygen than sluggish, inactive organisms, so large, active organisms have specialized organs for gas exchange.

Properties of Gas-Exchange Surfaces

Properties of an efficient gas-exchange surfacePermeability – to allow gases across; a thin tissue layer – to make the diffusion distance as short as possible; moisture – gases dissolve in the moisture, helping them pass across the surface; and a large surface area – so that large quantities of respiratory gases can cross at the same time.

Maintenance of Concentration Gradients at Exchange Surfaces in Animals

Concentration gradientThe difference in concentration of a substance between one area and another. Diffusion requires a concentration gradient to be maintained – the steeper the gradient, the faster the diffusion.
Figure B3.1.2: The concentration gradient in alveoli allows carbon dioxide to diffuse from the bloodA diagram showing the concentration of carbon dioxide plotted against location in the body, from the blood (high CO2 concentration) to the lungs (low CO2 concentration), illustrating the gradient down which CO2 diffuses from blood into the alveolar air.

A concentration gradient needs to be maintained at the gas-exchange surface, allowing oxygen to diffuse into the body and carbon dioxide to diffuse out. There are three ways a concentration gradient is maintained at exchange surfaces: a dense network of blood vessels – capillaries provide a large surface area for diffusion of respiratory gases, with blood carrying gases either in red blood cells (mainly oxygen) or in plasma (carbon dioxide); continuous blood flow – maintains the difference in concentration between air and blood by carrying oxygen away from the gas-exchange surface and bringing carbon dioxide to it; and ventilation – with air for lungs and water for gills, ventilation brings oxygen to the gas-exchange surface and removes carbon dioxide.

Adaptations of Mammalian Lungs for Gas Exchange

Key termsThorax: in mammals, the upper part of the body separated from the abdomen. Intercostal muscles: muscles between the ribs involved in ventilation. Diaphragm: sheet of tissue, largely muscle, separating the thorax from the abdomen in mammals. Pleural membrane: lines the lungs and thorax cavity; secretes the pleural fluid. Trachea: windpipe. Bronchus (plural, bronchi): a tube connecting the trachea with the lungs. Bronchiole: small terminal branch of a bronchus.

In mammals, the lungs are the organs where gas exchange occurs, providing a large, thin, moist surface area suitable for gas exchange. However, the lungs are in a protected position inside the thorax, so air must be brought to the respiratory surface – the lungs must be ventilated. A ventilation system is a pumping mechanism that moves air into and out of the lungs efficiently, maintaining the concentration gradients of oxygen and carbon dioxide for diffusion. In mammals, diffusion at the respiratory surface is further improved by a blood circulation system, which rapidly moves oxygen to body cells as soon as it has crossed the respiratory surface, maintaining the concentration gradient in the lungs; and a respiratory pigment, haemoglobin, in the numerous red blood cells, which increases the oxygen-carrying ability of the blood.

The structure of the mammalian lung

The lungs sit within the thorax, an airtight chamber formed by the ribcage and its intercostal muscles, with a domed floor, the diaphragm, a sheet of muscle attached to the body wall at the base of the ribcage, separating thorax from abdomen. The internal surfaces of the thorax are lined by the pleural membrane, which secretes and maintains pleural fluid – a lubricating liquid derived from blood plasma that protects the lungs from friction during breathing movements.

Figure B3.1.3: The structure of the human thoraxA diagram of the human thorax in section, labelled with the nasal cavity, larynx, trachea, oesophagus, bronchus, bronchioles, ribs (in section), intercostal muscles (internal and external), lung, diaphragm, abdominal cavity, position of the heart, pleural membranes, and the pleural cavity containing pleural fluid.

The lungs connect with the rear of the mouth via the trachea, which divides into two bronchi, one to each lung. Within the lungs, the bronchi divide into smaller bronchioles, the finest of which end in the alveoli (air sacs). The walls of bronchi and larger bronchioles contain smooth muscle and are supported by rings or plates of cartilage, preventing collapse that might be triggered by a sudden pressure reduction during powerful inspirations. Lungs are extremely efficient but cannot prevent some water loss during breathing – an issue for most terrestrial organisms.

Alveolar structure and gaseous exchange

Lung tissue consists of alveoli arranged in clusters, each served by a tiny bronchiole; bronchioles form a branched network spreading throughout the lung to provide an even distribution of alveoli. Alveoli have elastic connective tissue as an integral part of their walls. There are very many small alveoli in each lung – their small size and large number provide a large surface area for gas exchange.

Common mistakeA common misunderstanding is that the spherical shape of alveoli gives the lungs a large surface area for gas exchange. In fact, a sphere has less surface area for a given volume than any other shape – it is the small size and large number of alveoli that gives the large surface area.

A capillary system wraps around the clusters of alveoli, providing a large surface area for diffusion of oxygen from the alveoli into the blood, and carbon dioxide out of the blood into the alveoli. Each capillary is connected to a branch of the pulmonary artery and drained by a branch of the pulmonary vein; the pulmonary circulation is supplied with deoxygenated blood from the right side of the heart and returns oxygenated blood to the left side of the heart, to be pumped around the rest of the body. Surfactant lines the inner surface of the alveoli, secreted by cells in the alveolar wall, reducing surface tension so that the tiny alveoli (about 0.25 mm in diameter) do not collapse and stick together during expiration.

Blood arriving in the lungs is low in oxygen but high in carbon dioxide. As blood flows past the alveoli, gaseous exchange occurs by diffusion: oxygen dissolves in the alveolar surface film of water, diffuses across into the blood plasma and into the red blood cells, where it chemically combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin, while carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli.

How effective are mammalian lungs?

Air flow in mammalian lungs is tidal: air enters and leaves by the same route, so there is a residual volume of air that cannot be expelled. Incoming air mixes with and dilutes this residual air, rather than replacing it, so alveolar air contains significantly less oxygen than the atmosphere outside (see Table B3.1.1). Nevertheless, the lungs are efficient organs, thanks to the numerous features of the alveoli listed in Table B3.1.2.

Ventilation of the Lungs

Air is drawn into the alveoli when air pressure in the lungs is lower than atmospheric pressure, and forced out when pressure is higher in the lungs than atmospheric pressure. Since the thorax is an airtight chamber, pressure changes in the lungs occur when the volume of the thorax changes.

On expiration, the external intercostal muscles relax while the internal intercostal muscles contract, and the diaphragm muscles relax; this moves the ribs downwards and inwards and the diaphragm up, decreasing thoracic volume, increasing pressure above atmospheric, so air flows out. Different muscles are required for inspiration and expiration because muscles can only actively pull (by contracting), never push – this is referred to as antagonistic muscle action. Be careful with cause and effect when describing ventilation: it is not the movement of air into the lungs that causes the diaphragm to move down, but rather the diaphragm contracting and moving down that causes reduced pressure in the thoracic cavity, drawing air into the lungs. Descriptions of ventilation should refer to changes in thoracic volume, not changes in lung volume, and it should be remembered that both inhaled and exhaled air contain a mixture of oxygen and carbon dioxide – exhaled air simply has a higher concentration of carbon dioxide and a lower concentration of oxygen than inhaled air.

Measurement of Lung Volumes

Key termsTidal volume: volume of air normally exchanged in breathing. Inspiratory reserve: difference between the maximum inspiratory level and the tidal volume. Expiratory reserve: difference between the maximum expiratory level and the tidal volume. Vital capacity (VC): the total amount of air exhaled after maximal inhalation. Ventilation rate: number of inhalations or exhalations per minute.

Ventilation (the movement of air into and out of the lungs) can be monitored by data logging using an apparatus called a spirometer. This consists of a plastic lid, hinged over a tank of water, that moves up and down in the spirometer chamber; as the person taking part breathes into a mouthpiece connected to the chamber by flexible tubing, the lid rises and falls as the volume of air in the chamber changes. With the chamber filled with air, lung capacity at different breathing rates can be investigated; if the chamber is instead filled with oxygen, and a carbon dioxide-absorbing chemical such as soda lime is added to a compartment on the air return circuit, the apparatus can also measure oxygen consumption by the body.

The movements of the lid may be recorded by a position transducer connecting the box to a computer; from traces of human breathing under different conditions (at rest, and after mild and vigorous activity), ventilation rate and tidal volume can be measured.

Tidal volume and inspiratory and expiratory reserves

The volume of air breathed in and out during normal, relaxed, rhythmical breathing – the tidal volume – is typically 400–500 cm³ (0.4–0.5 dm³). We also have the potential for an extra-large intake (maximum inspiratory capacity) and an extra-large expiration (expiratory reserve volume) when required. The difference between the maximum inspiratory level and the tidal volume is the inspiratory reserve; the difference between the maximum expiratory level and the tidal volume is the expiratory reserve. Together, these reserves make up about 4.5 dm³.

Vital capacity

Vital capacity (VC) is the total amount of air exhaled after maximal inhalation. Its value varies with age, body size and level of regular physical activity, and is calculated by adding the tidal volume (TV), inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) and expiratory reserve volume (ERV).

VC = TV + IRV + ERV

Ventilation rate

The spirometer can also be used to investigate steady breathing over a short period, with the y-axis of the trace set to “time”, allowing the ventilation rate (the number of inhalations or exhalations per minute) to be investigated. Alternative experimental approaches are possible, including the use of a chest belt and pressure monitor linked to a data logger.

Adaptations for Gas Exchange in Leaves

Key termsEpidermis: outer layer(s) of cells. Mesophyll tissue: parenchyma cells containing chloroplasts. Vascular tissue: xylem and phloem of plants. Vascular bundle: strands of xylem and phloem (often with fibres), separated by cambium; the site of water and food movement up and down the stem. Palisade mesophyll: columnar (oblong-shaped) cells containing many chloroplasts, found beneath the upper epidermis in leaves. Spongy mesophyll: rounded cells in the leaf that are loosely packed, creating air spaces where air circulates, providing a large surface area for gas exchange; contains chloroplasts but fewer than palisade mesophyll.

The leaf is the plant organ where gas exchange occurs. The top and bottom of the leaf are covered by a single layer of cells, the epidermis, and the inner part of the leaf contains mesophyll tissue and vascular tissue in a system of vascular bundles, referred to in leaves as veins. The bulk of the leaf is taken up by mesophyll tissue, supported by veins arranged in a branching network. There are two types of mesophyll cell: palisade mesophyll, forming a single layer of cells towards the top of the leaf, full of chloroplasts to maximize photosynthesis; and spongy mesophyll, containing air spaces where gas exchange occurs.

Figure B3.1.8: The distribution of tissues in the leafA photomicrograph of part of a leaf in cross-section (×120) alongside a plan diagram of the same section, labelled with the upper epidermis, the waxy cuticle covering it, the palisade mesophyll, part of a vascular bundle (running parallel to the plane of section, showing xylem and phloem), the spongy mesophyll, and the lower epidermis (which contains stomata).
Stoma (plural, stomata)A pore in the epidermis of a leaf, surrounded by two guard cells. Turgid: condition where the vacuole of a plant cell is full of water, pushing the cell membrane against the cell wall. Flaccid: plant cell that has become soft and less rigid than normal because the cytoplasm within its cells has shrunk and contracted away from the cell walls through loss of water.

The tiny pores of the leaf epidermis, through which gas exchange occurs, are known as stomata; most occur in the lower epidermis of leaves, but some occur in stems too. Each stoma is surrounded by two elongated guard cells, which change shape to open and close the stomata.

Common mistakeDo not confuse “guard cell” with “stoma”. Guard cells create the stoma when they open to form a pore (or hole) between them – the stoma itself – through which gas exchange occurs. “Stoma” refers to a single pore, “stomata” to several.

Stomata open and close due to changes in the turgor pressure of the guard cells. They open when water is absorbed by the guard cells from surrounding epidermal cells: the guard cells become fully turgid and each pushes into the epidermal cell beside it (because of the way cellulose is laid down in their walls), creating a pore between the guard cells. When water is lost and the guard cells become flaccid, the pore closes again.

Figure B3.1.9: The distribution and structure of stomataA composite figure. A photomicrograph of the lower surface of a leaf (×100) shows the distribution of stomata among ordinary epidermal cells; a photomicrograph of an individual stoma (×500) shows a pore partially open between two guard cells. A cutaway diagram of the leaf shows, in section, the upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, an air space, a leaf vascular bundle, and the lower epidermis where most stomata are located; each guard cell is shown with thin lateral walls but greatly thickened dorsal and ventral walls (reinforced with cellulose), and a thick outer wall of the epidermis covered by waxy cuticle.

The adaptations of the leaf for gas exchange include: a waxy cuticle, forming a largely impermeable barrier, so gases and water vapour must leave via the stomata, allowing gas exchange and water loss to be controlled; the epidermis, whose lower surface contains many stomata (kept away from the brightest sunlight, so humidity and temperature there are lower than above the leaf, reducing water loss); air spaces, allowing gases to circulate around the loosely packed spongy mesophyll cells, maintaining a concentration gradient between air and cells; the spongy mesophyll, providing a large surface area for gas exchange (carbon dioxide entering the cells, oxygen leaving); stomatal guard cells, opening and closing to control gas exchange and water loss; and veins (vascular bundles), carrying water to the leaves in xylem vessels, which is then lost through stomata by transpiration – a small proportion of the water entering the leaf is used in photosynthesis, and ongoing photosynthesis (driven by water supply via veins and carbon dioxide through stomata) maintains concentration gradients of both molecules.

Further adaptations to increase gas exchange include: leaves provide a large surface area so many stomata can be incorporated, allowing movement of gases in and out of the internal air spaces to maintain a steep carbon dioxide concentration gradient; leaves are flat, giving a large surface area-to-volume ratio; and leaves are thin, facilitating fast diffusion of carbon dioxide to all cells.

Transpiration as a Consequence of Gas Exchange in a Leaf

TranspirationThe evaporation of water from the spongy mesophyll tissue and its subsequent diffusion through the stomata.

When stomata open for gas exchange, water vapour is lost from the leaf, diffusing from the interior of the leaf to the air outside down a concentration gradient. Transpiration is dramatically affected by environmental conditions around the plant.

Common mistakesIt is incorrect to say that “water” diffuses from the leaf during transpiration – it is water vapour that diffuses through the stomata. It is also incorrect to describe this movement as osmosis, because water is not travelling through a cell membrane (which is what defines osmosis) – it is moving as a gas from a liquid state inside the leaf to the air outside.

Transpiration occurs because water molecules evaporate continuously from the cellulose walls of cells in the leaf, increasing the water potential of the air spaces between mesophyll cells. If the air outside the plant has a lower water potential (i.e. it is less humid, as it very often is) and the stomata are open, water vapour diffuses out into the drier air outside, from higher to lower water potential.

Figure B3.1.11: The site of transpirationA diagram of a leaf and stem in sunlight, showing: the waxy cuticle covering the epidermis, which prevents evaporation of water from the outer surfaces of the plant; a high concentration of water vapour in the air spaces of the leaf; diffusion of water vapour through open stomata (each surrounded by two guard cells) to the drier air outside; air movements carrying water vapour away and maintaining the concentration gradient; xylem vessels in the leaf stalk and stem (phloem tissue omitted) from which water is drawn to replace that lost from the leaf; and a continuous column of water drawn up the xylem in the stem by the evaporation occurring in the leaves.

Factors affecting the rate of transpiration: temperature increases evaporation of water molecules from leaf cell surfaces, raising the concentration of water vapour in the air spaces and so the concentration difference driving diffusion, while also making water vapour molecules move faster – so a rise in leaf temperature raises the transpiration rate. High humidity slows transpiration, because humid air around a leaf decreases the concentration difference of water vapour between the leaf’s interior and exterior, slowing diffusion. Wind sweeps away water vapour molecules accumulating outside the stomata, enhancing the concentration difference and so increasing transpiration. Light intensity affects transpiration because stomata tend to be open in the light (so carbon dioxide can diffuse in for photosynthesis), resulting in water vapour loss at the same time; sunlight also contains infrared rays that warm the leaf, raising its temperature.

Investigating transpiration with a potometerA potometer measures the rate of water uptake in plants as an indirect measure of transpiration rate. An air bubble is introduced into the capillary, and as water transpires from the leaf, water is pulled up the tube and along the capillary – the rate of movement of the air bubble (distance moved per minute) indirectly measures the transpiration rate. A tap below the reservoir allows the bubble to be reset for a new measurement. The potometer can be set up under different environmental conditions (e.g. light versus dark, low versus high temperature, moving versus still air) to investigate the effect of an abiotic variable on transpiration rate.

Stomata tend to open in daylight and close in the dark, but this diurnal pattern is overridden if the plant becomes short of water and starts to wilt: in very dry conditions, with an inadequate water supply, stomata close relatively early in the day, limiting water vapour loss by transpiration and halting further wilting. Adequate water reserves taken up from the soil subsequently may allow stomata to reopen the following day – so stomata regulate transpiration by preventing excessive water loss.

Stomatal Density

The more stomata per unit area (stomatal density), the more carbon dioxide can be taken into the spongy mesophyll and the more water can be released by transpiration – so higher stomatal density can greatly increase both water loss and carbon dioxide uptake. Stomatal density can be measured using micrographs of leaves, or by using leaf casts.

Performing a leaf cast to determine stomatal density
  1. Spread nail varnish thinly on the underside of a leaf, and leave it to dry (this can be accelerated by creating air currents over the leaf).
  2. Place a piece of transparent sticky tape over the dried varnish, then slowly and carefully pull the tape up from the leaf surface, taking a layer of varnish with it.
  3. Put the tape sticky-side down onto a slide and view it under a microscope, starting at low power and then increasing to see the details of stomata.
  4. Count the number of stomata in the field of view, and work out the size of the field of view (using a ruler under the objective lens to estimate its diameter, then calculating area as π × r², where r is the radius of the field of view).
  5. Measure the surface area of the leaf by tracing its outline on graph paper, then extrapolate the number of stomata counted in the field of view to the full surface area of the leaf.

Higher Level Adaptations of Foetal and Adult Haemoglobin for the Transport of Oxygen

Partial pressureThe pressure of a specific gas in a mixture of gases. The pressure of a mixture of gases is the sum of the partial pressures of its component gases; the symbol for partial pressure is p, so the partial pressure of oxygen is written pO2.

As terrestrial animals living at or near sea level, we live at the bottom of a “column” of air, which exerts a significant pressure; in a gas mixture, each component gas exerts a partial pressure.

Worked example: calculating a partial pressureAt sea level, atmospheric pressure is typically about 101.3 kPa, and air is 20.9% oxygen. The partial pressure of oxygen is therefore (101.3 × 20.9) ÷ 100 = 21.2 kPa. This figure is reflected in the properties of the respiratory pigment haemoglobin, which transports oxygen in the blood.

Oxygen dissociation curves as a means of representing the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen

Key termsOxygen dissociation curve: a graph of the percentage saturation (with oxygen) of haemoglobin against the concentration of available oxygen. Cooperative binding: the process where the addition of a substance to one subunit of a macromolecule increases the affinity of a neighbouring subunit for the same substance.

Haemoglobin occurs in red blood cells; each red blood cell contains about 280 million molecules of haemoglobin. The haemoglobin molecule is built of four interlocking polypeptide subunits, each a large globular protein with a non-protein haem group attached, containing iron. One molecule of oxygen combines with each haem group at the oxygen concentration found in the lungs, so each haemoglobin molecule can transport four molecules of oxygen.

Hb + 4O2 → HbO8 (haemoglobin + oxygen → oxyhaemoglobin)

The affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen is measured experimentally by finding the percentage saturation with oxygen of blood exposed to air mixtures containing different partial pressures of oxygen – the result is called an oxygen dissociation curve.

Figure B3.1.16: The structure of haemoglobin and its affinity for oxygenA two-part figure. The first part shows the structure of haemoglobin: four subunits interlocked to form a compact molecule; each subunit is a combination of protein (globin) and a non-protein haem group (making haemoglobin a conjugated protein), with O2 retained at the haem group. The second part is the oxygen dissociation curve of human haemoglobin at body temperature: an S-shaped (sigmoid) graph of percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen (y-axis, 0–100%) against partial pressure of oxygen, pO2/kPa (x-axis), with a secondary y-axis showing oxygen concentration/cm³ per 100 cm³ of blood. The curve is annotated to show low affinity for oxygen at low pO2 (where oxygen is released) and high affinity at high pO2 (where oxygen binds).

The shape of haemoglobin is altered as each oxygen molecule binds, making each successive binding of oxygen easier – this is cooperative binding. As a result, haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen in oxygen-rich areas (such as the air spaces in lung alveoli), promoting oxygen loading, and a lower affinity in oxygen-depleted areas (such as the muscles), promoting oxygen unloading. The S-shaped (sigmoid) curve shows that the first oxygen molecule attaches to haemoglobin with difficulty, but once it has, the second combines more easily, and so on until all four are attached and the molecule is saturated – so the amount of oxygen held by haemoglobin depends on the partial pressure of oxygen.

In the lungs, air is saturated with water vapour, so the partial pressure of the component gases differs from that in dry air outside (Table B3.1.4). From the oxygen dissociation curve, the haemoglobin in red blood cells in the capillaries around the alveoli will be about 95% saturated. In respiring tissues, however, oxygen partial pressure is much lower due to aerobic respiration there – it may be as low as 0.0–4.0 kPa in actively respiring tissues. At these low partial pressures, oxyhaemoglobin breaks down, releasing oxygen in solution that rapidly diffuses into the surrounding tissues, making haemoglobin an efficient way of transporting oxygen given the difference in partial pressure between lungs and respiring tissue.

Foetal versus adult haemoglobin

Allosteric proteinA protein that can exist in multiple conformations (shapes) depending on the binding of a molecule at a site other than the catalytic site.

The foetus obtains oxygen from its mother’s blood through the placenta, where the maternal and foetal circulations come very close together but do not mix. Adult and foetal haemoglobin differ slightly in molecular composition, and foetal haemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen – the haemoglobin in the foetal circulation combines with oxygen more readily than maternal haemoglobin at the same partial pressure, so its dissociation curve is shifted to the left. This is advantageous because the foetus’s only access to an oxygen supply is via the placenta: foetal haemoglobin loads oxygen at the same time as adult haemoglobin is unloading it, in the placenta. If foetal haemoglobin had a lower affinity than maternal haemoglobin, oxygen would pass from the foetus back to the mother instead. Following birth, foetal haemoglobin is almost completely replaced by adult haemoglobin.

The foetal haemoglobin curve lies to the left of the adult haemoglobin curve, showing that at any given pO2, foetal haemoglobin reaches a higher percentage saturation, i.e. it has a higher affinity for oxygen.

Haemoglobin is an allosteric protein: the binding of oxygen to one subunit causes conformational changes relayed to the other subunits, raising their affinity for oxygen (cooperative binding). Carbon dioxide is an allosteric effector of haemoglobin – it attaches to haemoglobin, making it more difficult for oxygen to bind and lowering haemoglobin’s affinity for oxygen. In foetal red blood cells, carbon dioxide has a lower allosteric effect, which leads to the higher affinity of oxygen for foetal haemoglobin.

Higher Level Bohr Shift

Bohr effectThe decrease in the oxygen affinity of haemoglobin in response to decreased blood pH, resulting from increased carbon dioxide concentration in the blood.

Blood circulation transports carbon dioxide from respiring tissues, where it is at relatively high partial pressure, to the lungs. In respiring cells, carbon dioxide concentration is approximately 9.3 kPa, compared with 5.3 kPa in the lungs. This difference in carbon dioxide partial pressure has a noticeable effect on the oxygen dissociation curve of haemoglobin: an increase in carbon dioxide concentration shifts the curve to the right. Where carbon dioxide concentration is high, as in actively respiring cells, oxygen is released from oxyhaemoglobin even more readily. Carbon dioxide lowers the pH (caused by protons released from the dissociation of carbonic acid), which causes haemoglobin to release its oxygen – this very useful outcome for living tissues is known as the Bohr effect.

Figure B3.1.18: How carbon dioxide favours release of oxygen in respiring tissuesA graph of percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen against partial pressure of oxygen, pO2/kPa, showing two oxygen dissociation curves. The curve for a CO2 concentration of about 5.3 kPa (the concentration of blood in transit around the body, as in the lungs) lies to the left. The curve for a CO2 concentration of about 9.3 kPa (the concentration around respiring cells) is shifted to the right. The pO2 in the lungs and the (lower) pO2 in respiring cells are marked on the x-axis, with an arrow showing that, as CO2 concentration increases, more O2 is released from haemoglobin at a given pO2 (the Bohr effect).

An increase in carbon dioxide causes increased dissociation of oxygen, which benefits cells with increased metabolism, such as actively respiring tissues, in two ways: greater amounts of carbon dioxide (a product of cell respiration) are released from the tissues into the blood, and haemoglobin releases its oxygen (required for aerobic cell respiration) at regions of greatest respiratory need.
