Amount of Substance: Moles and Avogadro's Constant

Chemists such as John Dalton discovered that elements combine in fixed, precise proportions of mass to form compounds. By finding the ratio of masses needed for atoms of different elements to react completely with one another (with nothing left over), scientists could work out the relative masses of different atoms. Amedeo Avogadro extended this by hypothesising that equal volumes of any gas, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules – which made it possible to relate the volumes of reacting gases to the ratios in which their atoms combine.

It can be shown that 12 g of carbon-12 contains 6.02 × 1023 atoms. This quantity of material is called a mole, and this particular number is called the Avogadro constant, NA (named after Avogadro, although not calculated by him). The mass of a mole of any substance, in grams, is numerically equal to its relative molecular (or atomic) mass, giving a convenient way of measuring amount of substance by counting molecules rather than by weighing.

Amount of substance, nThe amount of substance (measured in moles) is the number of elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, etc.) present, expressed as a multiple of the Avogadro constant.
n = N / NA

n is the amount of substance in moles, N is the actual number of molecules (or atoms) present, and NA is the Avogadro constant, 6.02 × 1023 mol−1. This formula lets you convert between a count of individual particles and the more practical unit of moles.

Worked example

A mole of carbon has a mass of 12 g. How many atoms of carbon are in a 2 g sample? One mole (12 g) contains NA = 6.02 × 1023 atoms. A mass of 2 g is 2/12 of a mole, so it contains (2/12) × 6.02 × 1023 = 1.00 × 1023 atoms. This shows how the mass of a small sample can be converted directly into a number of atoms once the molar mass is known.

Molar volume at STPThe volume of one mole of any ideal gas at normal atmospheric pressure (101.3 kPa) and a temperature of 0°C is 22.4 litres (L), equivalent to 0.0224 m3. This value is the same for every ideal gas, regardless of the identity of its molecules, which is the practical content of Avogadro's hypothesis.

The Ideal Gas Model

Of the three states of matter, gases have the simplest model because the forces between gas molecules are very small, allowing the molecules to move freely. The simple kinetic model treats a gas as a large number of perfectly elastic, tiny spheres moving in random motion.

Assumptions of the ideal gas model
  • Collisions between molecules (and with the container walls) are perfectly elastic.
  • The molecules behave as spheres.
  • The molecules are identical to one another.
  • There are no forces between molecules except during collisions, so between collisions each molecule moves with constant velocity.
  • The molecules are very small, so their total volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container.

Real gases only satisfy these assumptions approximately, and the approximation breaks down when a gas is compressed to high density, since the molecules are then close enough to exert forces on one another and the gas starts to behave more like a liquid. A gas that behaves according to this model is called an ideal gas; ideal gas behaviour is a good approximation for a real gas whenever the pressure and density are low and the temperature is high.

Defining the State of a Gas

To fully describe the state of an amount of gas, enough information must be given that someone else could reproduce a sample with identical properties. For a gas this means specifying its pressure, volume, temperature and amount (number of moles), since (unlike a solid) the volume of a gas is simply the volume of its container and is not fixed by the substance itself.

Pressure

PressurePressure at a surface is the force acting per unit area, where the force acts perpendicular to the surface. Pressure is measured in pascals (Pa), where 1 Pa is equivalent to 1 N m−2.
P = F / A

P is pressure, F is the magnitude of the force acting perpendicular to a surface, and A is the area of that surface. When gas molecules collide with the walls of their container, each collision changes the momentum of a molecule; by Newton's third law the wall experiences an equal and opposite force, and the combined effect of all these tiny collisions over an area is what we measure macroscopically as gas pressure.

Worked example

A trapped gas exerts a force of 50 N on a piston of cross-sectional area 2.0 × 10−3 m2. The pressure of the gas is P = F/A = 50 / (2.0 × 10−3) = 2.5 × 104 Pa = 25 kPa. This is the outward force per square metre that the gas exerts on the piston, and by Newton's third law, the force the piston must exert inward to hold the gas in place.

Temperature and molecular kinetic energy

Because gas molecules exert no forces on one another except during collisions, no work is done as they move around, so the molecules have no potential energy associated with their position – all of their energy is kinetic. The absolute temperature of a gas (in kelvin) is directly proportional to the average (mean) kinetic energy of a single molecule.

Ek, mean = (3/2)kT

Ek, mean is the average kinetic energy of one molecule, T is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and k is the Boltzmann constant, 1.38 × 10−23 J K−1. Because higher kinetic energy implies higher speed, molecules of a gas at a higher temperature have a higher average speed than the same gas at a lower temperature; and at the same temperature, a gas made of lighter molecules must have a higher average speed than a gas made of heavier molecules, since both have the same mean kinetic energy.

Worked example

Calculate the mean kinetic energy of a gas molecule at a temperature of 300 K. Ek, mean = (3/2)kT = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10−23 × 300 = 6.21 × 10−21 J. This tiny value shows why individual molecular energies are always described using the extremely small joule quantities typical of atomic-scale physics.

Volume

The volume of a gas is simply the volume of the container holding it. To vary the volume of a trapped gas sample, it can be placed in a cylinder fitted with a movable end, called a piston.

Kinetic Theory: How Molecular Motion Produces Pressure

To see how pressure arises from molecular motion, first consider a single molecule of mass m bouncing back and forth, perfectly elastically, between a piston and the far wall of a cylinder of length L, moving with speed v perpendicular to both walls. Each time it strikes the piston its momentum reverses, so the magnitude of the change in momentum per collision is 2mv. The molecule travels a distance 2L (to the far wall and back) between successive collisions with the piston, so it strikes the piston at a rate of v / (2L) collisions per second. Applying Newton's second law (force equals rate of change of momentum), the average force this one molecule exerts on the piston is 2mv × v / (2L) = mv2/L. Since pressure is force per unit area, and the area A multiplied by the length L equals the container's volume V, this single-molecule result scales up: for a gas of many molecules with density ρ, moving randomly in three dimensions rather than bouncing along just one axis, the overall pressure on the container walls is related to the mean of the squared molecular speeds.

P = (1/3)ρv2

P is the pressure exerted by the gas, ρ is the density of the gas, and v2 here denotes the mean value of the square of the molecular speed (found by averaging v2 over all the molecules, not the square of the average speed). The factor of one-third arises because, averaged over many randomly moving molecules, only one-third of the total mean square speed acts along any one of the three perpendicular directions in the container. This equation shows that pressure increases if the gas is compressed to a higher density and/or if its molecules move faster (i.e. the gas is hotter).

Worked example

A sample of gas has a density of 1.2 kg m−3, and its molecules have a mean square speed of 2.5 × 105 m2 s−2 (corresponding to typical molecular speeds of around 500 m s−1). The pressure this gas exerts is P = (1/3)ρv2 = (1/3) × 1.2 × 2.5 × 105 = 1.0 × 105 Pa. This value is close to normal atmospheric pressure, consistent with the density and typical molecular speeds of air at room temperature.

The Three Empirical Gas Laws

Because pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas are all interrelated, it is easiest to study the relationship between just two of these quantities at a time, while holding the other two constant. Doing this in three different ways produces three empirical relationships, known as the gas laws.

Boyle-Mariotte law

Boyle-Mariotte lawThe pressure of a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature is inversely proportional to its volume: P ∝ 1/V.
P1V1 = P2V2

P1 and V1 are the pressure and volume in the initial state, and P2 and V2 are the pressure and volume in the final state, for the same fixed mass of gas at the same constant temperature. As a gas is compressed into a smaller volume, its molecules become more closely packed, colliding with the walls more often; since the area of the walls also decreases, both effects combine to raise the pressure.

Worked example

A gas syringe holds 60 cm3 of gas at a pressure of 100 kPa. The syringe is compressed, at constant temperature, until the volume is 20 cm3. Using P1V1 = P2V2: 100 × 60 = P2 × 20, so P2 = 6000 / 20 = 300 kPa. Tripling the pressure by reducing the volume to a third of its original size confirms the inverse proportionality between pressure and volume.

Gay-Lussac's law

Gay-Lussac's lawThe pressure of a fixed mass of gas at constant volume is directly proportional to its absolute temperature: P ∝ T.
P1T2 = P2T1

P1 and T1 are the pressure and absolute temperature in the initial state, and P2 and T2 are the pressure and absolute temperature in the final state, for the same fixed mass of gas held at constant volume. As temperature rises, the average kinetic energy (and hence speed) of the molecules increases, so each collision with the walls involves a greater change in momentum and collisions happen more frequently; by Newton's second law this increases the force on the walls, and hence the pressure.

Worked example

A rigid, sealed can of gas is at a pressure of 100 kPa and a temperature of 300 K. It is heated, at constant volume, to 400 K. Using P1T2 = P2T1: 100 × 400 = P2 × 300, so P2 = 40000/300 = 133 kPa. Heating a sealed, rigid can raises its internal pressure, which is why such cans carry warnings against being placed near heat sources.

Charles' law

Charles' lawThe volume of a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure is directly proportional to its absolute temperature: V ∝ T.
V1T2 = V2T1

V1 and T1 are the volume and absolute temperature in the initial state, and V2 and T2 are the volume and absolute temperature in the final state, for the same fixed mass of gas held at constant pressure. As the temperature of a gas increases, its molecules move faster and tend to increase the pressure; but if the container is free to expand (keeping pressure constant), the volume increases in proportion to the temperature, which is exactly enough to keep the pressure unchanged.

Worked example

A balloon holds 2.0 L of gas at 280 K, at constant atmospheric pressure. It is warmed to 350 K. Using V1T2 = V2T1: 2.0 × 350 = V2 × 280, so V2 = 700/280 = 2.5 L. The balloon expands as it warms, since its internal pressure must stay equal to the constant atmospheric pressure outside it.

The Ideal Gas Equation

The three empirical gas laws can be combined into a single equation relating pressure, volume, absolute temperature and amount of gas all at once.

PV = nRT

P is pressure (Pa), V is volume (m3), n is the amount of gas in moles, T is the absolute temperature (K), and R is the molar gas constant, 8.31 J mol−1 K−1. The same law can equivalently be written PV = NkBT, where N is the number of molecules (rather than moles) and kB is the Boltzmann constant; since n = N/NA, the two forms are related by R = NAkB. Either equation can be rearranged to find any one of the four variables given the other three at a single instant in time.

Worked example

A rigid container of volume 0.10 m3 holds 4.0 mol of an ideal gas at a temperature of 300 K. Calculate the pressure of the gas. Rearranging PV = nRT: P = nRT/V = (4.0 × 8.31 × 300) / 0.10 = 9972/0.10 = 9.97 × 104 Pa ≈ 100 kPa. This shows how the ideal gas equation gives the pressure directly once the amount, volume and temperature of a gas sample are all known.

Combining changes: before-and-after problems

When a fixed amount of gas changes from one state to another (a "before and after" problem), PV/T must remain constant provided the number of moles does not change, since PV = nRT with n and R both constant. More generally, allowing the amount of gas to change too:

P1V1T2n2 = P2V2T1n1

Subscript 1 labels the initial state and subscript 2 labels the final state of the same sample of gas. Any quantity that stays constant between the two states (for example, if the amount of gas n does not change) can simply be cancelled from both sides, recovering one of the three simpler empirical gas laws as a special case.

Worked example

The pressure of a gas inside a cylinder is 300 kPa. The gas is compressed to half its original volume while its temperature rises from 27°C (300 K) to 327°C (600 K). What is the new pressure? Since PV/T is constant (n is unchanged): (P1V1)/T1 = (P2V2)/T2, i.e. (300 000 × V) / 300 = (P2 × 0.5V) / 600. Solving for P2: P2 = 300 000 × 600 × 0.5 / 300 = 1 200 000 Pa = 1200 kPa. Both compressing the gas and heating it act to raise the pressure, so the combined effect is a fourfold increase in pressure. Note that temperatures must always be converted to kelvin before using this equation, since it involves absolute values rather than temperature differences.

Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

Since the molecules of an ideal gas exert no forces on each other except during collisions, they have no potential energy; the internal energy of an ideal gas is therefore just the total kinetic energy of all of its molecules.

U = (3/2)nRT

U is the internal energy of the gas, n is the amount of gas in moles, R is the molar gas constant (8.31 J mol−1 K−1), and T is the absolute temperature. This equivalently follows from multiplying the mean kinetic energy of one molecule, (3/2)kT, by the total number of molecules N, and using NAk = R. This relationship, U = (3/2)nRT, is only true for a monatomic ideal gas (such as helium, neon or argon), since polyatomic molecules can also store energy in rotation and vibration in addition to translational kinetic energy. If no work is done on or by the gas, the heat Q transferred to a fixed volume of gas equals the increase in internal energy: Q = ΔU.

Worked example

500 J of thermal energy is transferred to 2 g of helium gas held at constant volume. Calculate the temperature rise. The molar mass of helium is 4 g mol−1, so n = 2/4 = 0.5 mol. Since the volume is constant, all the heat goes into increasing internal energy: ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT = Q = 500 J. Rearranging: ΔT = (2 × 500) / (3 × 0.5 × 8.31) = 1000/12.47 = 80 K. Supplying 500 J to this small sample of helium raises its temperature by a substantial 80 K, because there are relatively few moles present to share the added energy.

Real Gases and the Limits of the Ideal Gas Model

The assumptions underlying the ideal gas model only fully apply to real gases under certain conditions: when pressure is low and temperature is high. At high pressures and densities, gas molecules are pushed close enough together that their volume is no longer negligible compared with the container, and the assumption of no intermolecular forces breaks down. At sufficiently low temperatures, a real gas can condense into a liquid, which does not behave like a gas at all. Despite this, no real gas behaves exactly like an ideal gas, but air at ordinary room temperature and pressure comes very close, which is why the ideal gas equations are such a useful and widely applicable approximation in practice.
