Current, Charge and Potential Difference

Electric current in a conductor is due to the flow of charged particles – in a metal wire these are free electrons, but in other conductors (such as dissolved salts or ionised gases) the charge carriers can be ions. A complete circuit needs at least one source of electrical energy (such as a cell) and a component, such as a resistor, for charge to flow through.

Electric current, ICurrent is the rate of flow of charge past a point. It is measured in amperes (A), where 1 A is a flow of 1 coulomb of charge per second.
I = q / t

I is the current in amperes, q is the charge in coulombs that flows past a point, and t is the time taken in seconds. Rearranged as q = It, this equation also lets you calculate the total charge that has flowed in a given time if the current is known.

Worked example

A charge of 6 C flows past a point in a wire in 3 s. What is the current? I = q/t = 6 / 3 = 2 A. A steady current of 2 A is needed to transfer this much charge in this time.

Potential difference, VThe potential difference between two points is the work done (energy transferred) per unit charge as a positive charge moves between those two points. It is measured in volts (V), where 1 V is equivalent to 1 joule per coulomb.
V = W / q

V is the potential difference in volts, W is the work done (energy transferred) in joules, and q is the charge in coulombs that moves between the two points. A large potential difference means a large amount of energy is transferred to (or from) each unit of charge that passes through a component.

Worked example

12 J of work is done moving 4 C of charge between two points in a circuit. What is the potential difference between the points? V = W/q = 12 / 4 = 3 V. Every coulomb of charge that moves between these two points transfers 3 J of energy.

Conventional current vs electron flowElectrons are negatively charged, so they actually drift from the low-potential (negative) terminal of a cell, through the external circuit, to the high-potential (positive) terminal. By convention, however, physicists always describe circuits in terms of conventional current, defined as the flow of positive charge from high potential to low potential – the opposite direction to the actual electron flow. This makes no mathematical difference to any circuit calculation, since current has the same magnitude at every point around a single loop regardless of which direction is called "positive". Current that flows in one constant direction is called direct current (DC); current that periodically reverses direction is called alternating current (AC).

Resistance and Resistivity

For a given potential difference, a good conductor allows a large current to flow, while a poor conductor only allows a small current to flow. This property of a component is called its resistance.

Resistance, RResistance is the ratio of the potential difference across a component to the current flowing through it. It is measured in ohms (Ω), where 1 Ω = 1 V A−1.
R = V / I

R is the resistance in ohms, V is the potential difference across the component in volts, and I is the current through it in amperes. Note that this is simply the ratio of V to I at a given moment; for a component whose resistance changes (a non-ohmic conductor), this is not the same as the gradient of a current–potential difference graph.

Worked example

A potential difference of 9 V causes a current of 3 mA to flow through a wire. What is its resistance? R = V/I = 9 / (3 × 10−3) = 3000 Ω = 3 kΩ. This is a fairly high resistance, consistent with only a small (milliamp-scale) current flowing for a moderate voltage.

Resistivity

The resistance of a particular sample of material depends not only on what the material is, but also on its dimensions: resistance is directly proportional to the length L of the conductor (a longer path means more collisions) and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area A (a wider conductor gives charge carriers more room to flow, like a wider pipe).

Resistivity, ρResistivity is a property of a material (independent of its shape or size) that relates its resistance to its dimensions. It is measured in ohm metres (Ω m).
R = ρL / A

R is resistance in ohms, ρ is the resistivity of the material in Ω m, L is the length of the sample in metres, and A is its cross-sectional area in square metres. Rearranged as ρ = RA/L, this shows that resistivity is numerically equal to the resistance of a 1 m length of the material with a cross-sectional area of 1 m2; because real wires have a far smaller cross-section than 1 m2, resistivity values for good conductors like copper are extremely small.

Worked example

The resistivity of copper is 1.72 × 10−8 Ω m. What is the resistance of a 1 m length of 2 mm diameter copper wire? The radius is 1 mm = 0.001 m, so the cross-sectional area A = πr2 = π(0.001)2 = 3.14 × 10−6 m2. Then R = ρL/A = (1.72 × 10−8 × 1) / (3.14 × 10−6) = 5.5 × 10−3 Ω = 0.0055 Ω. This very small resistance shows why the resistance of connecting wires can usually be ignored compared with the resistance of other circuit components.

Ohm's Law and I–V Characteristics

Ohm's lawThe current through an ohmic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided the temperature remains constant.
V = IR

V is the potential difference across a component in volts, I is the current through it in amperes, and R is its (constant) resistance in ohms. This rearranged form of the resistance definition is used to find whichever of V, I or R is unknown, provided the other two quantities are known and the component is ohmic.

Worked example

The potential difference across a 3 Ω resistor is 9 V. What current flows? Rearranging V = IR gives I = V/R = 9 / 3 = 3 A. A relatively large current flows because this is a small resistance.

Ohmic and non-ohmic conductors

Ohmic vs non-ohmic conductorsAn ohmic conductor (such as a metal wire at constant temperature) has a constant resistance, so its current–potential difference (I–V) graph is a straight line through the origin. A non-ohmic conductor, such as a light bulb filament, does not have constant resistance: as current flows, electrical energy is transferred to thermal energy, raising the filament's temperature; the increased lattice vibrations cause more frequent electron–lattice collisions, so the resistance increases as the current (and hence temperature) increases. Its I–V graph therefore curves, becoming less steep at higher current. A semiconductor behaves oppositely: raising its temperature releases more charge carriers, so its resistance decreases as temperature rises.

Electrical Power and Energy

Electrical power is the rate at which energy is transferred from one store to another – for example, from electrical potential energy to thermal energy in a resistor.

Electrical power, PPower dissipated in (or delivered by) a circuit component is the electrical energy transferred per unit time.
P = IV

P is power in watts (1 W = 1 J s−1), I is the current through the component in amperes, and V is the potential difference across it in volts. This equation applies both to power dissipated by a resistor (heating effect) and to power delivered by a source such as a cell, where V is replaced by the emf.

Worked example

A current of 2 A flows through a resistor with a potential difference of 4 V across it. How much power is dissipated? P = IV = 2 × 4 = 8 W. The resistor is converting 8 joules of electrical energy to heat every second.

Alternative forms of the power equationSubstituting V = IR (Ohm's law) into P = IV gives two further equivalent forms of the power equation, useful when only one of V or I is known.
P = I2R = V2 / R

These are obtained by substituting V = IR into P = IV (giving P = I2R) or by substituting I = V/R into P = IV (giving P = V2/R). They allow the power dissipated in a resistor to be calculated directly from just the current and resistance, or just the potential difference and resistance, without first finding the other quantity.

Worked example

What power is dissipated when a current of 4 A flows through a resistance of 55 Ω? Using P = I2R: P = 42 × 55 = 16 × 55 = 880 W. (Check: V = IR = 4 × 55 = 220 V, and P = VI = 220 × 4 = 880 W, confirming the same answer.) This is a substantial power, typical of a mains heating appliance.

The kilowatt-hourHousehold electrical energy use is often measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh) rather than joules. One kilowatt-hour is the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance running for 1 hour: since 1 W = 1 J s−1, 1 kWh = 1000 × 60 × 60 J = 3.6 × 106 J. This unit is sometimes wrongly called "kilowatts per hour", but a kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, not a rate.

In an incandescent light bulb, only about 10% of the electrical energy dissipated is transferred to light, with the rest lost as heat; fluorescent tubes and LEDs are far more efficient at converting electrical energy to light. Electric kettles and motors are other everyday applications of P = IV: a kettle transfers electrical energy directly to heat in the water, while a motor transfers electrical energy to mechanical (kinetic or gravitational potential) energy, and in each case the current drawn can be found by rearranging P = IV once the power rating and supply voltage (or the mechanical power required) are known.

EMF and Internal Resistance

An electric cell uses stored chemical energy (or, for a solar cell, light energy, and for a generator, mechanical energy) to separate positive and negative charge, creating a potential difference that can drive a current around a circuit.

Electromotive force, ε (emf)The emf of a source is the work done (energy transferred to electrical energy from another store) per unit charge that passes through it. It is measured in volts. Despite its name, emf is not a force; the term is a historical label, which is why it is usually referred to by the abbreviation "emf" rather than written out in full.

Every real cell has some internal resistance, arising from the resistive components of the cell itself and from its changing chemical composition as it is used. This is represented on a circuit diagram by a small resistor, r, drawn next to the cell symbol. When current flows, some of the cell's energy is dissipated inside the cell itself, across this internal resistance.

Emf vs terminal potential differenceIf no current is drawn from a cell, there is no potential drop across its internal resistance, so the potential difference measured across its terminals (the terminal potential difference) equals its emf. As soon as current flows, some potential is "lost" driving charge through the internal resistance, so the terminal potential difference becomes less than the emf. As a cell discharges over its lifetime, its internal resistance tends to increase, which is why the terminal potential difference of a well-used cell drops below its rated value, especially when supplying a large current.
ε = I(R + r)

ε is the emf of the cell in volts, I is the current flowing in amperes, R is the resistance of the external circuit in ohms, and r is the internal resistance of the cell in ohms. This follows from conservation of energy: the chemical energy converted per unit charge (ε) must equal the total electrical energy transferred to heat per unit charge in both the external resistor and the internal resistance combined (IR + Ir).

Worked example

A cell of emf 9 V with internal resistance 1 Ω is connected to a 2 Ω resistor. What current flows, and what is the potential difference across the 2 Ω resistor? I = ε/(R + r) = 9/(2 + 1) = 3 A. The potential difference across the resistor is V = IR = 3 × 2 = 6 V. Only 6 V of the cell's 9 V emf reaches the external resistor; the remaining 3 V is "lost" driving current through the cell's own internal resistance.

V = ε − Ir

V is the terminal potential difference in volts, ε is the emf in volts, I is the current in amperes, and r is the internal resistance in ohms. This rearranged form of the same energy-conservation relationship directly gives the terminal potential difference without needing to know the external resistance.

Worked example

A cell of emf 6 V has an internal resistance of 1 Ω. When connected to an external circuit, a current of 0.5 A flows. What is the terminal potential difference? V = ε − Ir = 6 − (0.5 × 1) = 5.5 V. This confirms that the terminal pd is slightly below the emf because of the small voltage drop across the internal resistance.

Sources of emf

A chemical cell (such as a simple zinc-copper cell in acid) separates charge using a chemical reaction: the more reactive metal (zinc) loses positive ions to the acid faster than the copper does, leaving the zinc plate more negative (at lower potential) than the copper plate, creating a potential difference of about 1 V. A battery is strictly a row of cells connected together, though the word is often used loosely for any device that converts chemical energy to electrical energy. A cell that cannot be recharged is a primary cell; a rechargeable cell is a secondary cell.

Figure B.5.12: A simple cellA zinc plate and a copper plate dipped into acid. Positive charges accumulate around both plates, but more strongly around the zinc, since zinc reacts with the acid faster than copper, leaving the zinc plate at a lower (more negative) potential than the copper plate and producing a potential difference between them.

A generator converts mechanical energy to electrical energy: a coil is rotated (for example, by a turbine driven by steam, falling water or wind) inside a magnetic field, which induces an emf in the coil. The changing magnetic field due to the induced current itself opposes the coil's motion, so a driving force must keep doing work on the coil to maintain the current, converting mechanical work into electrical energy.

Figure B.5.17: A simple AC generatorA coil connected to an external resistor via slip rings, positioned between the poles of a magnet, with a handle used to rotate the coil. Turning the handle rotates the coil within the magnetic field, inducing a current that flows through the external resistor.

A photovoltaic (solar) cell is a semiconductor device based on a PN junction: one side of a silicon slice is doped so that it has an excess of free electrons (N-type), and the other so that it has an excess of "holes" (P-type, effectively a lack of electrons). At the junction, electrons and holes migrate across and combine, leaving behind a region with an electric field (the depletion layer). Light striking the N-type side releases additional free electrons (the photovoltaic effect); the electric field at the junction separates and traps this charge, creating a potential difference that can drive a current if the cell is connected to a circuit. A thin anti-reflective coating on top of the cell reduces the amount of light energy lost by reflection.

Series and Parallel Circuits

Circuit components can be connected in series (one after another, forming a single loop for current to flow around) or in parallel (side by side, providing separate branches for current to flow through). These two arrangements obey different rules, which follow directly from conservation of charge and conservation of energy.

QuantitySeries circuitParallel circuit
CurrentSame through every component: I = I1 = I2 = ...Splits between branches: I = I1 + I2 + ...
Potential differenceShared between components: V = V1 + V2 + ...Same across every branch: V = V1 = V2 = ...
Combined resistanceR = R1 + R2 + ...1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...

In a series circuit, the same current must flow through each component in turn (there is nowhere else for the charge to go), while the total potential difference across the combination is shared between the components, since each unit of charge does work passing through each resistor in turn. In a parallel circuit, each branch has the same potential difference across it (since each branch connects the same two points), while the total current splits between the branches according to conservation of charge (the current flowing into a junction equals the current flowing out).

R = R1 + R2 + ...

R is the combined (total) resistance of resistors connected in series, and R1, R2, etc. are the individual resistances. Adding resistors in series always increases the total resistance, since the charge carriers must pass through every resistor in turn.

Worked example

What is the total resistance of a 4 Ω and an 8 Ω resistor connected in series? R = R1 + R2 = 4 + 8 = 12 Ω. Connecting resistors in series always gives a total resistance greater than any individual resistor.

1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...

R is the combined resistance of resistors connected in parallel, and R1, R2, etc. are the individual resistances; note that this formula gives 1/R, which must be inverted to find R itself. Adding resistors in parallel always decreases the total resistance below the smallest individual resistance, since extra branches give the charge carriers more alternative paths to flow through.

Worked example

What is the total resistance of two 8 Ω resistors connected in parallel? 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 = 1/8 + 1/8 = 2/8 = 1/4, so R = 4 Ω. This is exactly half the resistance of one of the individual resistors, which is always true for two equal resistors in parallel.

Solving combined circuitsMany practical circuits combine series and parallel sections. These can be solved by repeatedly replacing each series or parallel group of resistors with a single equivalent resistance, working from the smallest sub-groups outward, until the whole network is reduced to a single total resistance. Not every possible arrangement of resistors, however, can be reduced this way using only the simple series and parallel formulas.

Combining Cells

Cells (and batteries) are often connected together, most commonly in series, to obtain a larger emf than a single cell can provide.

εtotal = ε1 + ε2 + ...

εtotal is the combined emf of cells connected in series (all oriented the same way round), and ε1, ε2, etc. are the individual emfs. Their internal resistances also add in series, just like ordinary resistors, so the total internal resistance increases as more cells are added.

Worked example

Two 12 V cells, each with internal resistance 1 Ω, are connected in series to a 10 Ω resistor. What current flows? Total emf = 12 + 12 = 24 V. Total resistance = 1 + 1 + 10 = 12 Ω (internal resistances of both cells plus the external resistor). Using I = ε/R: I = 24/12 = 2 A. Connecting the two cells in series doubles the emf compared with using just one cell, driving a larger current through the same external resistor.

Identical cells vs different cells in parallelWhen two identical cells are connected in parallel, the combined emf equals the emf of a single cell (the arrangement can supply more current, or the same current for longer, but not a higher voltage). If two cells with different emfs are connected in parallel, their internal resistances become essential to the analysis: the potential drops across the internal resistance of the higher-emf cell and rises across the internal resistance of the lower-emf cell, forcing a current to flow into (recharging) the weaker cell. Without any internal resistance, connecting two different emfs directly in parallel would be physically inconsistent, since the same two points could not have two different potential differences between them.

Circuit Components and Electrical Measurement

Variable resistors and potential dividers

Because the resistance of a length of wire is proportional to its length, a resistor with a sliding contact can be used to vary the resistance in a circuit continuously, simply by changing how much of the wire's length is included between the contact and one end.

Potential divider (potentiometer)A potential divider is a circuit, usually made from a fixed voltage source connected across two resistors (or a single resistor with a sliding contact) in series, that provides a continuously variable output potential difference taken from across part of the resistance.
Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Vout is the potential difference taken across resistor R2, Vin is the total potential difference supplied across both resistors in series, and R1 and R2 are the two resistances (with R2 being the one the output is measured across). This follows because the same current flows through both series resistors, so the potential difference divides between them in proportion to their resistances. A potential divider is used whenever a variable, adjustable potential difference is needed, such as in a dimmer switch or when measuring the I–V characteristics of a component like a light bulb.

Worked example

A 9 V supply is connected across a 3 kΩ resistor (R1) in series with a 6 kΩ resistor (R2). What is the output potential difference taken across the 6 kΩ resistor? Vout = Vin × R2/(R1 + R2) = 9 × 6/(3 + 6) = 9 × 6/9 = 6 V. Because R2 is twice R1, it receives twice the share of the potential difference, exactly as the series-circuit resistor ratio predicts.

Light-dependent resistors and thermistors

LDR vs thermistor vs metallic conductorA light-dependent resistor (LDR) is a semiconductor device whose resistance decreases as the light intensity falling on it increases, since more light releases more charge carriers. A thermistor (in most common, NTC, types) is a semiconductor device whose resistance decreases as its temperature increases, again because more charge carriers are released; this is the opposite temperature behaviour to a metallic conductor, whose resistance increases with temperature because increased lattice vibrations cause more frequent collisions with the charge carriers.

Measuring current and potential difference

Ideal ammeters and voltmetersAn ammeter measures current and must be connected in series in the branch of the circuit whose current is being measured, so that the same current flows through the ammeter as through that branch; an ideal ammeter has zero resistance, so that it does not reduce the current it is measuring. A voltmeter measures potential difference and must be connected in parallel across the two points whose potential difference is being measured; an ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance, so that it draws negligible current and does not affect the rest of the circuit.
Figures B.5.29 and B.5.30: Connecting a voltmeter and an ammeterA voltmeter is connected with one lead touching point A and the other touching point B, in parallel with (alongside) the component whose potential difference is being measured, without breaking the circuit. An ammeter is connected in series: one of the wires to a resistor is disconnected and the ammeter is inserted into the gap, so that all of the current that flows through the resistor must also flow through the ammeter.

In practice, real ammeters have a small but non-zero resistance and real voltmeters have a large but finite resistance, so connecting a meter into a circuit always changes the current or potential difference very slightly from its true, unmeasured value; this must be taken into account when analysing experimental data. A multimeter is a single instrument that can be switched between measuring potential difference, current and resistance.
