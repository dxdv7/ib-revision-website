Force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field

Magnetic force on a wire

A straight, current-carrying wire placed in a magnetic field (but not parallel to it) experiences a force. Unlike the force on a small bar magnet, this force does not cause rotation: it acts perpendicular to both the current direction and the field direction.

Fleming's left-hand rule

Hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of the left hand mutually at right angles. The First finger points in the direction of the magnetic Field, the seCond finger points in the direction of the Current (conventional current, or the direction opposite to electron flow), and the thumb then gives the direction of the resulting Force (thrust). A field is often drawn as crosses (into the page) or dots (out of the page), representing the tail or tip of the field-line arrows.

F = BIL

F is the force on the wire (N), B is the magnetic flux density (T), I is the current (A), and L is the length of wire within the field (m), with the wire perpendicular to the field. This equation defines the tesla: a flux density of 1 T causes a 1 m length of wire carrying 1 A, perpendicular to the field, to experience a force of 1 N.

Worked example

A straight wire of length 0.60 m, perpendicular to a magnetic field of flux density 0.50 T, carries a current of 3.0 A. The force on the wire is F = BIL = 0.50 × 3.0 × 0.60 = 0.90 N, directed perpendicular to both the wire and the field (found using Fleming's left-hand rule).

Non-perpendicular conductors

F = BILsinθ

When the wire is not perpendicular to the field, only the component of the field perpendicular to the wire, Bsinθ, contributes to the force, where θ is the angle between the wire and the field. This reduces to F = BIL when θ = 90° (wire perpendicular to field) and to zero when the wire is parallel to the field (θ = 0).

Worked example

A wire of length 0.40 m carrying a current of 5.0 A lies at 30° to a magnetic field of flux density 0.20 T. The force on it is F = BILsinθ = 0.20 × 5.0 × 0.40 × sin30° = 0.40 × 0.5 = 0.20 N. This is smaller than if the wire were perpendicular to the field (which would give 0.40 N), since only part of the field is effective at producing a force.

Force between parallel current-carrying conductors

Mutual force between parallel wires

Each current-carrying wire produces its own circular magnetic field around itself. When two current-carrying wires are placed near each other, each wire lies within the other's field, so each experiences a force. Using Fleming's left-hand rule, two wires carrying currents in the same direction attract each other, while two wires carrying currents in opposite directions repel each other.

F/L = μ0I1I2 / (2πr)

F/L is the force per unit length between the two wires (N m-1), μ0 is the permeability of free space (4π × 10-7 T m A-1), I1 and I2 are the currents in the two wires, and r is the separation between them. Since μ0/(2π) works out to exactly 2 × 10-7 T m A-1, the formula is often written F/L = (2 × 10-7)I1I2/r. Historically (before 2019) this equation was used to define the ampere: one ampere is the current that produces a force of exactly 2 × 10-7 N per metre between two long parallel wires 1 m apart in a vacuum.

Worked example

Two long straight parallel wires, 0.050 m apart, carry currents of 3.0 A and 5.0 A. The force per unit length is F/L = (2 × 10-7 × 3.0 × 5.0)/0.050 = (2 × 10-7 × 15)/0.050 = 6.0 × 10-5 N m-1. If the currents flow in the same direction, this is an attractive force pulling the wires together; if they flow in opposite directions, it pushes the wires apart.

Force on a moving charge in a magnetic field

From wire to single charge

The current in a wire is carried by moving electrons. The total force on the wire, F = BIL, can be rewritten as F = BNev (where N is the number of charge carriers in the length of wire and v is their drift speed), which shows the force on each individual charge is Bqv. So any free charge moving through a magnetic field experiences a magnetic (Lorentz) force, even outside a wire.

Magnetic (Lorentz) force on a charge

A charged particle moving through a magnetic field experiences a force that is always perpendicular to its direction of motion. Because the force is always perpendicular to velocity, it changes the particle's direction but never its speed, resulting in circular motion.

F = qvB (v perpendicular to B);    F = qvBsinθ (general case, θ between v and B)

F is the magnetic force on the charge (N), q is the size of its charge (C), v is its speed (m s-1), and B is the magnetic flux density (T). The force is maximum when the velocity is perpendicular to the field (θ = 90°) and zero when the velocity is parallel to the field. Its direction is found with Fleming's left-hand rule, using the direction of conventional current flow (i.e. the direction a positive charge moves, or the opposite direction to electron flow).

Worked example

A proton (q = 1.60 × 10-19 C) moves at 2.0 × 106 m s-1 perpendicular to a magnetic field of flux density 0.35 T. The force on it is F = qvB = 1.60 × 10-19 × 2.0 × 106 × 0.35 = 1.12 × 10-13 N. This force acts perpendicular to the proton's velocity at every instant, so it continuously changes direction without changing speed – the definition of centripetal force.

Circular motion of a charged particle in a magnetic field

Circular path in a uniform field

If a charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field with velocity perpendicular to the field lines, the magnetic force is always directed toward one fixed point: the center of a circle. As the particle moves, the force stays perpendicular to the (changing) velocity, so it continues to act toward the same center, producing a full circular path of constant radius and constant speed.

r = mv / (qB)

This comes from setting the magnetic force equal to the centripetal force required for circular motion: qvB = mv2/r, which rearranges to give the radius r. Here m is the mass of the particle, v is its speed, q is its charge, and B is the magnetic flux density. A larger mass or speed gives a larger radius (harder to turn), while a larger charge or field strength gives a smaller radius (stronger turning force).

Worked example

A proton (mass 1.67 × 10-27 kg, charge 1.60 × 10-19 C) moves at 2.0 × 106 m s-1 perpendicular to a field of 0.35 T. Its radius of curvature is r = mv/(qB) = (1.67 × 10-27 × 2.0 × 106)/(1.60 × 10-19 × 0.35) = (3.34 × 10-21)/(5.60 × 10-20) = 0.060 m, i.e. about 6.0 cm. Measuring this radius experimentally, along with the field strength, allows the charge-to-mass ratio of a particle to be found – this is how the nature of a newly discovered charged particle can be investigated.

Non-perpendicular fields and helical motion

If a charged particle's velocity has a component parallel to the field as well as a component perpendicular to it, the perpendicular component (vsinθ, giving force F = Bqvsinθ) causes circular motion, while the parallel component is unaffected by the field (since a charge moving parallel to a field experiences no magnetic force) and continues at constant velocity. Combining constant circular motion in one plane with constant velocity along the field produces a helical (corkscrew) path that spirals along the direction of the field.

Acceleration of charges in a uniform electric field

The electron gun

An electron gun consists of a heated filament (which releases electrons by thermionic emission) and a metal anode plate held at a higher potential, with a hole in it. Electrons are accelerated from the filament toward the anode by the electric field between them, then pass through the hole and travel in a straight line at constant velocity once outside the field (in the field-free region beyond the anode).

eV = 0.5mv2

As an electron (charge e) is accelerated through a potential difference V, its electric potential energy eV is entirely transferred into kinetic energy 0.5mv2 (assuming it starts from rest). m is the electron's mass and v is its final speed on leaving the accelerating field. This equation lets the final speed of an accelerated charged particle be found directly from the accelerating voltage, without needing to know the field strength, the distance, or the time taken.

Worked example

An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 500 V. Its kinetic energy gained is eV = 1.60 × 10-19 × 500 = 8.0 × 10-17 J. Using eV = 0.5mv2: v = √(2eV/m) = √((2 × 8.0 × 10-17)/(9.11 × 10-31)) = √(1.76 × 1014) = 1.3 × 107 m s-1. This is the speed at which the electron emerges from the electron gun, ready to enter a region of electric or magnetic field for further study.

Motion of a charged particle in a uniform electric field

Electric field deflection vs projectile motion

A charged particle Q projected perpendicular to a uniform electric field behaves exactly like a projectile thrown horizontally in a uniform gravitational field (topic A.1): it has constant velocity in the direction perpendicular to the field, and constant acceleration in the direction parallel to the field lines. The only difference is that the acceleration is QE/m instead of g, so the resulting path is still a parabola. A positive charge accelerates in the direction of the field lines; a negative charge (such as an electron) accelerates in the opposite direction to the field lines.

a = qE/m;    t = L/v;    y = 0.5at2

A charge q of mass m in a field of strength E experiences acceleration a = qE/m (from F = qE and F = ma) perpendicular to its original direction of travel. If it enters a field region of length L (along its original direction) with speed v, it spends time t = L/v inside the field, and is deflected sideways by y = 0.5at2 by the time it leaves – exactly analogous to the equations for a horizontally launched projectile.

Worked example

An electron travels horizontally at 2.0 × 107 m s-1 into a 0.020 m long region between parallel plates, where a uniform field of 2000 V m-1 acts vertically. The acceleration is a = eE/m = (1.60 × 10-19 × 2000)/(9.11 × 10-31) = 3.5 × 1014 m s-2. Time in the field: t = L/v = 0.020/(2.0 × 107) = 1.0 × 10-9 s. Vertical deflection: y = 0.5at2 = 0.5 × 3.5 × 1014 × (1.0 × 10-9)2 = 1.8 × 10-4 m, about 0.18 mm. This small but measurable deflection is the basis of the parabolic trace seen when a beam of charged particles passes through a uniform electric field.

Crossed electric and magnetic fields (velocity selector)

Balanced fields

If a charged particle moves through a region containing both an electric field and a magnetic field, arranged perpendicular to each other and to the particle's velocity, the electric and magnetic forces on the particle can point in opposite directions. For a particular speed, these two forces exactly balance, so the particle passes straight through undeflected (Newton's first law: zero resultant force means no change in velocity). Particles with any other speed are deflected and do not pass straight through, so this arrangement acts as a velocity selector, or filter.

v = E/B

This follows from setting the magnetic force equal to the electric force: FB = FE, so Bqv = Eq, giving Bv = E and hence v = E/B. E is the electric field strength and B is the magnetic flux density; both must be set up perpendicular to each other and to the particle's velocity. Only particles moving at exactly this speed travel through the crossed fields in a straight line, regardless of their charge or mass.

Worked example

A beam of charged particles enters a velocity selector with an electric field of 2.0 × 104 V m-1 and a magnetic field of 0.40 T, arranged perpendicular to each other and to the beam. Only particles with speed v = E/B = (2.0 × 104)/0.40 = 5.0 × 104 m s-1 travel straight through; faster or slower particles are deflected and blocked. This is the principle used in J. J. Thomson's experiment to measure the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron: once the speed is known from the balanced fields, switching off the electric field and measuring the radius of the resulting circular path in the magnetic field alone gives the specific charge, e/m.

Summary: electric vs magnetic field motion

Electric fields vs magnetic fields

A charged particle entering a uniform electric field experiences a force along the field lines, producing constant acceleration and a parabolic path (like a projectile). A charged particle entering a uniform magnetic field, moving perpendicular to it, experiences a force perpendicular to its velocity at all times, producing constant-speed circular motion, with no change in kinetic energy since the magnetic force does no work on the particle (it is always perpendicular to displacement). Combinations of both fields therefore allow a particle's charge, mass, and speed to be investigated independently – the basis of many classic experiments in atomic and particle physics, including the determination of the electron's specific charge.
