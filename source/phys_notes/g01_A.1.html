Displacement and Distance

Kinematics is the description of motion without worrying about what causes it. To describe motion precisely, physicists reduce a moving object to a single particle and track a small set of measurable quantities: position, velocity and acceleration. The first distinction to make is between distance and displacement — two quantities that sound similar in everyday speech but mean very different things in physics.

DistanceHow far a body has actually traveled, measured along the path taken. Distance is a scalar quantity (magnitude only, no direction) and is measured in meters (m).
DisplacementThe shortest straight-line distance from a body’s starting point to its final position, together with the direction of that straight line. Displacement is a vector quantity and is also measured in meters (m). Because it is a vector, a displacement value is meaningless without a stated direction.
Distance vs displacementIf you run once all the way around a 400 m circular track, the distance you have traveled is 400 m, but because you end up back where you started, your displacement is 0 m. Similarly, a journey from Paris to Lyon by road covers a distance of about 450 km, while the displacement (straight line from Paris to Lyon) is only about 336 km, in a southeast direction. In everyday life the distance traveled is often the more useful quantity; in physics, once motion is reduced to a straight line, displacement is usually more useful because it also carries directional information.
Figure A.1.1: Route across a cityA street map showing a winding road route from point A to point B. The displacement is drawn as a single straight arrow from A to B (for example, 5 km due west); the distance is the length of the actual winding road followed to get from A to B (for example, about 10 km) — noticeably longer than the displacement because the road is not straight.

Speed and Velocity

Speed and velocity both describe how fast a body is moving, but, like distance and displacement, one is a scalar and the other a vector.

SpeedThe distance traveled per unit time. Speed is a scalar quantity, measured in m s−1.
VelocityThe rate of change of position, i.e. displacement per unit time. Velocity is a vector quantity, also measured in m s−1, and a direction must always be stated alongside its value.
average speed = distance ÷ time
average velocity = displacement ÷ time

Both formulas divide a length by a time interval, but one uses the scalar distance and the other the vector displacement. Because displacement already has a direction built into it, the velocity calculated from it automatically has the same direction. When converting speeds between units (for example km h−1 to m s−1), remember that 1 km h−1 = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s, so you multiply a value in km h−1 by (1000 ÷ 3600), or divide by 3.6, to convert it to m s−1.

Worked example

A car travels from the north of a city to the south, a distance of 16 km, in a straight line taking 4 hours. Find its average velocity.
Using average velocity = displacement ÷ time: average velocity = 16 km ÷ 4 h = 4 km h−1, directed south. Converting to SI units: 4 km h−1 ÷ 3.6 ≈ 1.1 m s−1 south. This single number tells us nothing about how the speed varied during the 4 hours — it is only the overall (average) rate, found from the start and end points of the journey.

Average velocity and instantaneous velocity

Average velocity, calculated as above, only compares the start and the end of a journey; it gives no information about what happened in between (unless the body moved at a genuinely constant velocity in a straight line the whole time). If a journey is broken down into very small time intervals, then during each tiny interval the body can be treated as moving in a straight line at an almost constant speed. The velocity found this way — how fast a body is moving, and in what direction, at one particular instant — is called the instantaneous velocity.

Instantaneous velocityThe velocity of a body at one specific moment in time, found by considering the displacement over an extremely short time interval around that moment.
Figure A.1.2: A journey with a constantly changing velocityA winding route across a city, with the direction and length of the path arrows changing constantly along the way — illustrating that it is not possible to travel this route at a genuinely constant velocity, since both speed and direction change repeatedly.
Constant velocity, a special caseIf (and only if) the velocity of a body is constant, then its instantaneous velocity is the same at every moment and is therefore equal to its average velocity over any interval. Since velocity is a vector, “constant velocity” also requires the direction of motion to stay the same, not just the speed.

Measuring Velocity Experimentally

Since displacement = velocity × time for a constant velocity, a graph of displacement against time should be a straight line through the origin, with gradient equal to the velocity. This relationship can be tested experimentally. In one method, several students with stopwatches are positioned at known, equally spaced distances along a straight path; all the stopwatches are started together as a moving body (e.g. a bicycle) passes a start marker, and each student stops their own clock as the body passes them. Indoors, distances are too short for hand-operated stopwatches to give small enough percentage uncertainties, so laboratories instead use photogates connected to a data logger, which record the precise instant a moving object passes through an infrared beam. Since it would be expensive to use many photogates at once, a common compromise is to use just two: one fixed at the start of the motion, and a second one moved to different positions along the track, repeating a reproducible motion (such as a ball rolling down a ramp onto a horizontal track) each time. A small card of known length attached to the moving object, combined with a single photogate, can also be used to measure an instantaneous velocity directly: the photogate times how long the card takes to pass through the beam, and instantaneous velocity is found from (length of card) ÷ (time taken for the card to pass).

Figure A.1.4: Card and photogate methodA card of known, fixed length is attached to a moving trolley or ball. As the object moves, the card passes through a single photogate, briefly interrupting an infrared beam. The data logger records the short time interval for which the beam is broken; dividing the card’s length by this short time gives a good estimate of the instantaneous velocity of the object as it passed through the gate.

Relative Velocity

Because velocity is a vector, velocities must be combined using vector addition (and subtraction), not simply added as numbers unless they lie along exactly the same line. The velocity of one object as seen by an observer who is themselves moving is called its relative velocity.

Relative velocityThe velocity of an object as measured or observed from a particular reference point (observer), found by vector subtraction of the observer’s velocity from the object’s velocity (both measured relative to a common fixed point, such as the ground).
velocity of A relative to B = velocity of A (relative to ground) − velocity of B (relative to ground)

To find how fast and in what direction object A appears to move to an observer on object B, subtract B’s velocity vector from A’s velocity vector. In one dimension this just means subtracting signed numbers (remembering that subtracting a negative velocity is the same as adding its magnitude); in two dimensions, the velocities must be subtracted as vectors, for example by scale drawing or by resolving into perpendicular components.

Worked example

A blue boat travels east at 4 m s−1 and a green boat travels west at 3 m s−1, heading toward each other. Taking east as the positive direction, find the velocity of the green boat relative to an observer on the blue boat.
The blue boat’s velocity is +4 m s−1 and the green boat’s velocity is −3 m s−1. Velocity of green relative to blue = (−3) − (+4) = −7 m s−1. This means an observer standing on the blue boat sees the green boat approaching at 7 m s−1 — faster than either boat’s speed relative to the water, because their velocities are in opposite directions and so add up when combined.

Figure A.1.5: Relative velocity of two boats moving perpendicular to each otherOne boat travels north and a second, observing boat travels east. From the perspective of the observer on the east-traveling boat, the water itself appears to flow west at the observer boat’s own speed, and this apparent westward flow must be added (as a vector) to the true northward velocity of the other boat. The resulting vector diagram is a right-angled triangle, and the relative velocity (its hypotenuse) points in a direction between north and west, with a magnitude found using Pythagoras’ theorem.

Acceleration

In everyday language, “accelerate” usually just means “speed up”. In physics, acceleration has a broader and more precise meaning.

AccelerationThe rate of change of velocity. Acceleration is a vector quantity, measured in m s−2.
acceleration = (final velocity − initial velocity) ÷ time
a = (v − u) ÷ t

Here u is the initial (starting) velocity, v is the final velocity, t is the time taken for the velocity to change from u to v, and a is the resulting acceleration. Because velocity is a vector, a body accelerates whenever its speed changes, its direction changes, or both — not only when it “speeds up”. A body moving in a straight line and slowing down is still accelerating (in the everyday sense it is “decelerating”), just with an acceleration vector that points opposite to its velocity.

Worked example

A car is traveling to the left. Taking rightward as the positive direction, its velocity decreases in magnitude from 10 m s−1 to 5 m s−1 (both leftward) over 5 s. Find its acceleration.
Since the car moves left and left is negative, u = −10 m s−1 and v = −5 m s−1. a = (v − u) ÷ t = (−5 − (−10)) ÷ 5 = 5 ÷ 5 = +1 m s−2. The positive sign shows the acceleration points to the right, even though the car itself is still moving to the left — this is exactly what “slowing down while moving left” looks like: the acceleration opposes the velocity.

Equations of Uniformly Accelerated Motion (suvat)

For most of this topic we consider the simplest case of accelerated motion: constant (uniform) acceleration. In one dimension, displacement, velocity and acceleration all lie along the same line, so they can be combined directly using signed numbers rather than full vector diagrams. Five quantities describe this motion and give the equations their name: s (displacement), u (initial velocity), v (final velocity), a (acceleration) and t (time).

suvat equationsSince velocity changes at a constant rate under constant acceleration, average velocity = (u + v) ÷ 2 as well as = s ÷ t. Combining this with the definition of acceleration, a = (v − u) ÷ t, and eliminating variables between the two produces four standard equations that connect s, u, v, a and t.
v = u + at
s = ½(u + v)t
s = ut + ½at2
v2 = u2 + 2as

Each equation involves only four of the five quantities s, u, v, a, t, so if you know any three of them, you can choose the equation containing the fourth unknown and solve for it directly, without needing to find the fifth quantity first. These equations only apply when the acceleration is genuinely constant throughout the motion being considered.

Worked example

A car traveling at 10 m s−1 accelerates at 2 m s−2 for 5 s. What is its displacement?
List the known quantities: s = ?, u = 10 m s−1, v = ?, a = 2 m s−2, t = 5 s. The only equation containing s, u, a and t (and not the unknown v) is s = ut + ½at2. Substituting: s = (10 × 5) + ½(2)(52) = 50 + 25 = 75 m. The car travels 75 m forward while it accelerates for those 5 seconds.

Signs of s, u, v and aTaking one direction as positive (commonly “to the right”), a positive displacement, velocity or acceleration points in that direction, and a negative one points the opposite way. A positive acceleration does not always mean “speeding up” — it means the acceleration vector points in the positive direction, regardless of which way the body happens to be moving at that moment.
Worked example

A body has a constant acceleration of −5 m s−2 while initially traveling to the right (positive direction) at 20 m s−1. What is its displacement after 20 s?
s = ut + ½at2 = (20 × 20) + ½(−5)(202) = 400 − 1000 = −600 m. The negative answer means the body’s final position is 600 m to the left of where it started, even though it began by moving to the right: it moved forward, decelerated to rest, and then accelerated backward past its starting point.

Free Fall Motion

A body falling under gravity alone, with no other forces (such as air resistance) acting on it, is said to be in free fall. Near the Earth’s surface, freely falling bodies accelerate downward at a constant rate, conventionally given the symbol g, with an average value of about 9.81 m s−2. This value varies slightly with location on Earth and decreases slightly with altitude, but these variations are ignored for laboratory-scale experiments. Because the acceleration is constant, free-fall motion can be analyzed with the suvat equations, using a = −g if upward is taken as positive (or a = +g if downward is taken as positive). For simplicity, many worked examples and exam questions use g = 10 m s−2 — but only when a question explicitly says to use that approximate value.

Free fall / acceleration of free fall (g)Free fall: the motion of a body falling under the influence of gravity alone. Acceleration of free fall, g: the constant acceleration (about 9.81 m s−2, directed downward) experienced by any freely falling body near the Earth’s surface, independent of the body’s mass.

Measuring the acceleration due to gravity

If a body is released from rest (u = 0) and falls freely through a distance s in time t, then s = ut + ½at2 simplifies to s = ½gt2. Since s is directly proportional to t2, plotting measured fall distance (s) against t2 should give a straight line through the origin with gradient ½g.

s = ½gt2 (starting from rest, u = 0)
gradient of an s vs t2 graph = ½g

This relationship turns a free-fall experiment into a straight-line graphing problem: time the fall from several different heights (starting the clock at release and stopping it on impact), plot height against the square of the time taken, draw a best-fit line, and find its gradient.

Worked example

A “ball-drop” experiment gives a best-fit line, on a graph of fall height against t2, with gradient 4.81 m s−2. Find the experimental value of g.
Since gradient = ½g, g = 2 × gradient = 2 × 4.81 = 9.6 m s−2. Using the steepest and shallowest lines that still fit within the data’s error bars gives an uncertainty of about ±0.5 m s−2, so the final result, 9.6 ± 0.5 m s−2, agrees with the accepted value of 9.81 m s−2 within experimental uncertainty.

Figure A.1.6: Height vs time-squared for a falling objectA graph with fall height (m) on the y-axis and t2 (s2) on the x-axis. The plotted points lie very close to a straight best-fit line passing near the origin, confirming that height is proportional to t2 for constant acceleration starting from rest. Two additional lines, one slightly steeper and one slightly shallower than the best-fit line, are drawn through the extremes of the error bars to estimate the uncertainty in the gradient (and hence in g).

Graphical Representation of Motion

Displacement-time (s-t), velocity-time (v-t) and acceleration-time (a-t) graphs are the standard way to represent how a body’s motion changes over time. A good strategy for sketching them is to work out the s-t graph shape first by thinking about where the body is at each stage of its motion, and then to derive the v-t and a-t graphs from the shape of the s-t graph, rather than trying to picture all three at once.

Figure A.1.7: Four types of motion (bodies A, B, C, D)Three stacked graphs (s-t, v-t and a-t, all sharing a time axis) each showing four different lines, labeled A, B, C and D. Body A is stationary: a horizontal line at a constant (non-zero) height on the s-t graph, a horizontal line at zero on the v-t graph, and a horizontal line at zero on the a-t graph. Body B moves with constant positive velocity: a straight line with positive gradient on the s-t graph, a horizontal line at a constant positive value on the v-t graph, and a horizontal line at zero on the a-t graph. Body C moves with constant negative velocity: a straight line with negative gradient on the s-t graph, a horizontal line at a constant negative value on the v-t graph, and a horizontal line at zero on the a-t graph. Body D has constant positive acceleration: a curve (an offset parabola, since s is proportional to t2 when accelerating from a non-zero initial velocity) that gets steeper with time on the s-t graph, a straight line with positive gradient on the v-t graph, and a horizontal line at a constant positive value on the a-t graph.

Gradient of a displacement-time graph

gradient of s-t graph = Δs ÷ Δt = velocity

The gradient of any graph is (change in y) ÷ (change in x). For an s-t graph, the y-axis is displacement and the x-axis is time, so the gradient equals Δs ÷ Δt — exactly the definition of velocity. A steeper s-t graph therefore means a greater speed; where the line curves, the instantaneous velocity at any point is found from the gradient of the tangent to the curve at that point. A negative gradient means the body is moving in the negative direction.

Worked example

Using the bicycle data in Table A.1.1, estimate the bike’s velocity from the displacement-time graph.
Taking two points from the best-fit line, for example (3.40 s, 10.0 m) and (17.21 s, 60.0 m): gradient = Δs ÷ Δt = (60.0 − 10.0) ÷ (17.21 − 3.40) = 50.0 ÷ 13.81 ≈ 3.6 m s−1, consistent with the best-fit gradient of about 3.5 m s−1 found from the full data set. This is the bicycle’s (roughly constant) velocity during the timed section of road.

Gradient of a velocity-time graph

By the same reasoning, the gradient of a velocity-time graph is Δv ÷ Δt, which is exactly the definition of acceleration (already given as a formula above). A horizontal v-t line means zero acceleration (constant velocity); a straight line with constant gradient means constant acceleration; a curve means the acceleration itself is changing.

Area under a velocity-time graph

area under a v-t graph (between two times) = Δs (displacement during that interval)
for constant velocity: Δs = v × Δt

For a body moving at constant velocity v, the area under its v-t graph between two times is a rectangle of height v and width Δt, so its area is v × Δt — which, from the definition of velocity, is exactly the displacement Δs during that time. This result (area under a v-t graph gives displacement) holds generally, even when velocity is changing, not only for the constant-velocity case used to derive it.

Worked example

An object moves at a constant velocity of 6 m s−1 for 8 s. Find its displacement using the area under its v-t graph.
The v-t graph is a horizontal line at 6 m s−1, so the area underneath it, from t = 0 to t = 8 s, is a rectangle: area = 6 × 8 = 48 m. The object’s displacement over those 8 seconds is 48 m.

Area under an acceleration-time graph

area under an a-t graph (between two times) = Δv (change in velocity during that interval)
for constant acceleration: Δv = a × Δt

By the same logic applied to the definition of acceleration, a = (v − u) ÷ t rearranges to v − u = a × t, so the area under an acceleration-time graph between two times gives the change in velocity over that interval.

Worked example

An object accelerates at a constant 3 m s−2 for 4 s. Find the change in its velocity using the area under its a-t graph.
The a-t graph is a horizontal line at 3 m s−2, so the area beneath it from t = 0 to t = 4 s is a rectangle: area = 3 × 4 = 12 m s−1. The object’s velocity therefore increases by 12 m s−1 over those 4 seconds.

Calculus and motion (for students who have covered differentiation and integration)

v = ds/dt a = dv/dt = d2s/dt2
s = ∫v dt v = ∫a dt

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement with respect to time, so it is the derivative of s with respect to t; acceleration is the derivative of v with respect to t (equivalently the second derivative of s with respect to t). Working the other way, integrating a velocity function with respect to time gives displacement, and integrating an acceleration function with respect to time gives velocity — matching the “gradient” and “area under the graph” relationships described above, since differentiation finds a gradient and integration finds an area.

Worked example

The displacement of an object is given by s = 5t2 (in meters, with t in seconds). Find its velocity and acceleration at t = 3 s.
v = ds/dt = 10t, so at t = 3 s, v = 10 × 3 = 30 m s−1. a = dv/dt = 10 m s−2 (constant, since it does not depend on t). This object has a constant acceleration of 10 m s−2, and its velocity at t = 3 s is 30 m s−1.

After each bounce the ball rises to a smaller maximum height than before (each hump is shorter than the last), since energy is lost at each bounce, and the gradient becomes zero at the top of each bounce before the ball starts to fall again.

The curve starts flat (zero gradient, released from rest) and becomes progressively steeper as the ball speeds up, exactly like free fall at first. However, as air resistance grows with speed, the curve’s increase in steepness slows down, and eventually the graph becomes a straight line with constant (non-zero) gradient: the ball has stopped accelerating and is now falling at a constant terminal velocity, since air resistance has grown large enough to balance the pull of gravity.

Projectile Motion

A projectile is a body launched into the air and then left to move freely under gravity, following a curved (parabolic, if air resistance is ignored) path. Although this motion happens in two dimensions, it can be analyzed as two completely independent one-dimensional problems, one horizontal and one vertical, because the horizontal and vertical components of displacement, velocity and acceleration do not affect each other. (This independence can be demonstrated by dropping one object straight down while simultaneously flicking an identical object forward from the same height: both hit the ground at the same time, showing that the forward motion does not change the rate of falling.)

ProjectileA body that, once launched, moves through the air under the influence of gravity alone (no other forces, such as air resistance, are considered in the basic model). Its horizontal and vertical motions can be treated as independent one-dimensional problems, each analyzed using the suvat equations.

The horizontal component of the initial velocity is v cosθ and the vertical component is v sinθ.

Because the horizontal acceleration is always zero, the horizontal velocity never changes; because the vertical acceleration is a constant −g throughout, the vertical motion is a standard suvat problem.

Common mistakeAt the top of a projectile’s flight, the vertical velocity is momentarily zero, but the vertical acceleration is not zero — it is still −g, exactly as it is throughout the whole flight. Gravity does not switch off at the highest point; it is what causes the object to stop rising and then fall back down.

Because the horizontal motion has zero acceleration, only one equation is needed for it (displacement = velocity × time). The vertical motion, having constant acceleration −g, can be analyzed with the standard suvat equations. Combining the two components gives three particularly useful results for a projectile launched and landing at the same height:

R = v cosθ × t (horizontal range)

R is the total horizontal distance traveled, v is the launch speed, θ is the launch angle above the horizontal, and t is the total time of flight. Because horizontal velocity is constant, range is simply horizontal velocity multiplied by however long the projectile stays in the air.

Worked example

A ball is thrown at 20 m s−1, at 30° to the horizontal, and lands at the same height it was launched from, after a total flight time of 2 s (found below). Calculate its range.
R = v cosθ × t = 20 × cos30° × 2 = 20 × 0.866 × 2 ≈ 34.6 m. The ball lands 34.6 m from where it was thrown.

h = v2sin2θ ÷ (2g) (maximum height)

h is the maximum height reached above the launch point. This comes from applying v2 = u2 + 2as to the vertical motion between launch and the highest point, where the vertical velocity has dropped to zero.

Worked example

Using the same ball (20 m s−1 at 30°, g = 10 m s−2), find the maximum height reached.
h = v2sin2θ ÷ (2g) = (202 × sin230°) ÷ (2 × 10) = (400 × 0.25) ÷ 20 = 100 ÷ 20 = 5 m. The ball reaches a maximum height of 5 m above the point it was launched from.

t = 2v sinθ ÷ g (time of flight, launch and landing at the same height)

t is the total time the projectile spends in the air. This comes from applying s = ut + ½at2 to the whole vertical motion, where the total vertical displacement is zero because the projectile lands at the same height it was launched from.

Worked example

Using the same ball (20 m s−1 at 30°, g = 10 m s−2), find its total time of flight.
t = 2v sinθ ÷ g = (2 × 20 × sin30°) ÷ 10 = (2 × 20 × 0.5) ÷ 10 = 20 ÷ 10 = 2 s. This confirms the flight time of 2 s used in the range calculation above.

Worked example: launching horizontally from a height

A ball is thrown horizontally at 10 m s−1 from the top of a 20 m cliff. Find its range.
Here the initial vertical velocity is zero (the launch is horizontal), so applying s = ut + ½at2 to the vertical drop of 20 m (taking downward as negative and u = 0): −20 = 0 − ½(10)t2, so t2 = (2 × 20) ÷ 10 = 4, giving t = 2 s. The horizontal motion is unaffected by the fall, so the range is R = (horizontal velocity) × t = 10 × 2 = 20 m. Note that this problem could not use the t = 2v sinθ ÷ g formula directly, since launch and landing heights are different — the general suvat equations always work, while the three boxed range/height/time formulas above are shortcuts that only apply when launch and landing heights are equal.

R = v2sin(2θ) ÷ g (range, launch and landing at the same height)

Combining the range and time-of-flight formulas above, and using the trigonometric identity 2sinθcosθ = sin(2θ), gives range directly in terms of the launch angle. Since sin(2θ) has its largest possible value (1) when 2θ = 90°, this shows that, for a fixed launch speed with no air resistance, range is maximized when the launch angle θ = 45°.

Worked example

A ball is thrown at 20 m s−1 at the optimal angle for maximum range (45°), with g = 10 m s−2. Find the range.
R = v2sin(2θ) ÷ g = 202 × sin(90°) ÷ 10 = 400 × 1 ÷ 10 = 40 m. This is the greatest possible range achievable at this launch speed; any other launch angle would give a shorter range.

Projectile Motion with Air Resistance

All of the equations above assume there is no air resistance. In reality, air resistance opposes motion and increases with speed, changing the shape and range of a real trajectory.

Figure A.1.12: Trajectory with and without air resistanceTwo paths are shown launched from the same point at the same angle and speed. The path “without air resistance” is a full, symmetric parabola. The path “with air resistance” reaches a lower maximum height, lands at a shorter range, and is noticeably asymmetric: the descending part of the path is steeper (closer to vertical) than the ascending part, because the horizontal velocity has been continuously reduced by drag throughout the flight.
Motion with air resistance vs withoutWithout air resistance, both maximum height and range are maximized for a given launch speed, the path is a symmetric parabola, and the suvat equations apply exactly (constant accelerations, zero horizontally and −g vertically). With air resistance, both maximum height and range are reduced, the path becomes asymmetric (steeper on the way down), the horizontal velocity continuously decreases rather than staying constant, and the vertical acceleration is no longer constant (it is larger in magnitude on the way up, where drag and gravity both act downward, and smaller in magnitude on the way down, where drag acts upward against gravity) — so the suvat equations can no longer be used.

Air resistance does not always reduce range, however. A spinning, dimpled golf ball travels further through the air than an identical smooth ball launched the same way in a vacuum, because the combination of its dimples and spin generates an upward aerodynamic force (lift), in much the same way that air flowing over a wing supports an aircraft — holding the ball up for longer rather than simply slowing it down.

Figure A.1.13: Smooth ball vs dimpled, spinning golf ballTwo trajectories compared: a smooth ball following a shortened, asymmetric path (as in Figure A.1.12, reduced by air resistance), and a dimpled, spinning golf ball following a noticeably longer and flatter path, because lift generated by its spin and dimples partially counteracts gravity and keeps it airborne for a greater distance.
