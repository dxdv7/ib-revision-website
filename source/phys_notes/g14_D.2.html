Electric Charge

Rubbing a plastic ruler on a woollen sweater and holding it near small bits of paper makes the paper jump up and stick to the ruler – evidence of an unbalanced force acting on the paper, called the electric force. Rubbing two balloons on a sweater and bringing them close together makes them push apart (repel); a rubbed balloon and the sweater it was rubbed on pull together (attract). Because the electric force, unlike gravity, can be either attractive or repulsive, the property responsible for it – charge – must exist in two types, called positive and negative.

Electric charge, QCharge is the property of matter that causes the electric force. Bodies are positively charged, negatively charged, or neutral (zero net charge). Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge is a scalar quantity, measured in coulombs (C), and it is conserved: it can be transferred from one body to another but never created or destroyed.
Fundamental charge, eAll matter is made of atoms, each with a positive nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by negative electrons. The proton and electron carry exactly the same magnitude of charge but opposite sign: e = 1.60 × 10−19 C. This is the smallest amount of charge found in ordinary matter, so all charge is quantized – it exists only in whole-number multiples of e.
Q = ne

Q is the total charge on a body (C), n is a whole number (the number of excess or missing elementary charges, positive or negative), and e is the fundamental charge, 1.60 × 10−19 C. This equation expresses the fact that charge cannot take on just any value – it must be an integer multiple of e.

Worked example

An object carries a charge of −3.20 × 10−18 C. How many excess electrons does it carry? n = Q/e = (3.20 × 10−18) / (1.60 × 10−19) = 20. The object has 20 more electrons than protons.

Millikan's oil-drop experimentMillikan and Fletcher sprayed a fine mist of oil drops into a chamber, ionising them with X-rays. The charged drops fell under gravity between two horizontal plates; with no potential difference applied, a drop reached terminal velocity when drag balanced its weight. When an electric field was applied between the plates, the resulting electric force on each drop could be adjusted until the drop became stationary or moved with a new terminal velocity, allowing the charge on that individual drop to be calculated from the balance of forces.

Although no single oil drop carried exactly the fundamental charge, every charge measured this way turned out to be a whole-number multiple of 1.60 × 10−19 C. This common factor is direct experimental evidence that electric charge is quantized, and it remains the smallest quantity of charge ever measured by any experimental method.

Millikan's oil-drop apparatusA fine oil spray enters a chamber through a nozzle, where X-rays ionise the droplets. The droplets fall between two horizontal parallel plates connected to a high-voltage supply (several thousand volts), and a microscope on the side of the chamber is used to observe and time the motion of individual charged drops as the applied voltage is varied.

Charging Mechanisms and Earthing

Charge can be transferred between bodies in three ways: by friction (rubbing two materials together, as with the balloon and sweater, transfers electrons from one surface to the other), by contact (touching a charged conductor to an uncharged one shares the charge between them), and by electrostatic induction (charge is redistributed within a nearby body without any charge actually being transferred to it).

Charging by induction vs charging by contactCharging by contact transfers charge directly: electrons physically move from one body to the other, so both bodies end up with the same sign of net charge. Charging by induction involves no net transfer of charge to the induced body while it remains isolated – a nearby charge simply repels or attracts the free electrons within it, creating a temporary separation of charge (for example, why a charged balloon can “stick” to a neutral wall: electrons in the wall's surface are repelled away, leaving a net positive surface charge that attracts the negative balloon). If the induced body is then earthed while the inducing charge stays nearby, and the earth connection is removed before the inducing charge is taken away, the body is left with an overall net charge opposite in sign to the inducing charge, even though the two never touched.
Earthing (grounding)The Earth is so large, and holds such an abundant reserve of charge, that connecting a charged body to the Earth (grounding it) brings that body to a potential of 0 V: charge flows until the body's potential matches the Earth's. Earthing a charged object removes its net charge (or, during induction, fixes the sign of the charge left behind) and prevents the sparking that could otherwise occur between a charged body and its surroundings.
Charging an isolated conductor by inductionAn isolated, initially uncharged conductor B is brought near a charged body A. A's field pushes B's free electrons to the far side of B (if A is positive) or the near side (if A is negative), giving B a non-uniform surface charge with zero net charge overall. If B is then connected to the Earth, charge flows between B and the Earth until B reaches 0 V; when the earth connection is broken (while A is still nearby) and A is then removed, B is left with a net charge of the opposite sign to A, despite no direct contact ever occurring between A and B.

Electric Field and Field Strength

A region of space where an electric force is experienced by a charge is called an electric field. The space around a sphere of positive charge is an electric field, since a small positive test charge placed anywhere in that region experiences an outward force.

Electric field strength, EElectric field strength at a point is defined as the electric force experienced per unit charge by a small positive test charge placed at that point. It is a vector quantity with the same direction as the force on a positive test charge, measured in N C−1 (equivalent to V m−1).
E = F / q

E is the field strength (N C−1), F is the electric force on a small positive test charge q placed at that point. A charge +q at a point of field strength E experiences a force Eq in the direction of the field; a charge −q experiences a force of the same size but in the opposite direction.

Worked example

A charge of +5 × 10−6 C is placed at a point where the field strength is 40 N C−1. F = Eq = 40 × 5 × 10−6 = 2.0 × 10−4 N. This is the force the charge experiences, acting in the direction of the field (since the charge is positive).

Electric field linesField lines point radially outward from an isolated positive point charge, and radially inward toward an isolated negative point charge, showing the direction of the force on a small positive test charge at each point. As with gravitational field lines, the density of the lines shows the strength of the field: they are closest together near the charge (strongest field) and spread out with distance (weaker field). The field inside a uniformly charged hollow sphere is exactly zero everywhere, because the forces from every part of the charged shell cancel at any interior point; outside the sphere, the field is identical to that of a point charge of the same total value placed at the centre.
Uniform field between parallel platesBetween two oppositely charged parallel plates, the field lines run straight from the positive plate to the negative plate: parallel and evenly spaced (except very close to the edges of the plates, where the field is no longer uniform). This means a test charge placed anywhere in the central region between the plates experiences a force of the same size and direction, regardless of its exact position.

Coulomb's Law

Just as the gravitational force between masses is given by Newton's law, the electric force between charges is given by Coulomb's law – the direct electrical analogue of Newton's law of gravitation.

Coulomb's lawThe force experienced by two point charges is directly proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
F = kQ1Q2 / r2    where k = 1 / (4πε0)

F is the force between two point charges Q1 and Q2 separated by distance r in a vacuum; a positive F (from like-signed charges) is repulsive and a negative F (from opposite-signed charges) is attractive. The Coulomb constant k = 8.99 × 109 N m2 C−2 can also be written in terms of the permittivity of free space, ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 C2 N−1 m−2. Coulomb's law also applies to spheres of charge, using the separation between their centres, in exactly the same way that Newton's law applies to spheres of mass. Permittivity differs for different materials; the relative permittivity εr of a medium is the ratio of its permittivity to that of a vacuum, and reduces the force and field compared to a vacuum by that factor.

Worked example

A 5 µC point charge is placed 20 cm from a 10 µC point charge. F = kQ1Q2/r2 = (8.99 × 109 × 5 × 10−6 × 10 × 10−6) / (0.20)2 ≈ 11.2 N. By Newton's third law, the 10 µC charge experiences an equal and opposite force of the same size, 11.2 N.

E = kQ / r2

This gives the electric field strength E at a distance r from a single point (or spherical) charge Q, found by dividing Coulomb's law by the test charge q (E = F/q = kQq/r2 ÷ q). It is the electric equivalent of g = GM/r2 for gravitational fields.

Worked example

Continuing the example above, find the field strength 20 cm from the 10 µC charge. E = kQ/r2 = (8.99 × 109 × 10 × 10−6) / (0.20)2 ≈ 2.25 × 106 N C−1. Checking this against the force found above: F = Eq = 2.25 × 106 × 5 × 10−6 ≈ 11.2 N, which matches, confirming the two calculations are consistent.

Coulomb's law vs Newton's law of gravitationBoth laws have exactly the same mathematical form: force proportional to the product of two source quantities (charge or mass) and inversely proportional to the square of the separation. But gravity, produced by mass, is always attractive, whereas the electric force, produced by charge, can be attractive or repulsive depending on the signs of the two charges. The electric force is also vastly stronger than gravity between everyday objects (k is about 1020 times larger than G), which is why the tiny electric force between a charged balloon and a wall can easily overcome the wall's gravitational attraction. Field lines follow the same distinction: gravitational field lines always point inward, toward a mass (since there is no such thing as negative, repelling mass), while electric field lines point outward from positive charge and inward toward negative charge.

Electric Potential Energy and Electric Potential

As with gravitational fields, it is useful to define an energy associated purely with a charge's position in an electric field, and a potential that does not depend on the size of the charge placed in the field. Both are defined relative to infinity, where the influence of the source charge has fallen to zero.

Electric potential energy, EpThe electric potential energy of a point charge q at a distance r from another point charge Q is the work done to bring q from infinity (where Ep = 0) to that point.
Ep = kQq / r

Unlike gravitational potential energy, which is always negative, electric potential energy can be positive or negative depending on the signs of Q and q. If Q and q have the same sign (repulsive interaction), positive work must be done to push q in from infinity against the repulsion, so Ep is positive; if they have opposite signs (attractive interaction), Ep is negative, exactly as in the gravitational case.

Worked example

Two positive point charges, Q = 2 µC and q = 3 µC, are separated by r = 0.50 m. Ep = kQq/r = (8.99 × 109 × 2 × 10−6 × 3 × 10−6) / 0.50 ≈ 0.108 J. This positive value means 0.108 J of work was needed to bring the two (mutually repelling) charges to this separation from far apart, and that same amount of energy would be released if they were allowed to fly apart again.

Electric potential, VElectric potential at a point is the electric potential energy per unit charge at that point – the work done per unit positive charge to bring a small test charge from infinity to that point.
V = kQ / r

V is the electric potential (in volts, V, equivalent to J C−1) at distance r from a point charge Q. It is a scalar, so potentials from multiple charges are simply added algebraically (taking sign into account), unlike field strength. The potential energy of a charge q at a point of potential V is Ep = qV, and the potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge moving a charge between them – the unit used in atomic and particle physics, the electronvolt (eV), is defined as the energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V (1 eV = 1.60 × 10−19 J).

Worked example

Find the potential 0.50 m from the 2 µC charge in the example above. V = kQ/r = (8.99 × 109 × 2 × 10−6) / 0.50 ≈ 3.60 × 104 V. Checking against Ep = qV for the 3 µC charge placed there: Ep = 3 × 10−6 × 3.60 × 104 ≈ 0.108 J, matching the potential energy calculated directly above.

E = V / d

For a uniform field between two parallel plates separated by distance d with potential difference V between them, the field strength E equals the potential gradient V/d. This follows because the work done moving a charge q across the uniform field is W = (force) × (distance) = Eqd, and potential difference is work done per unit charge, so V = Eqd/q = Ed.

Worked example

The parallel plates of an ionisation chamber are separated by d = 12 mm = 0.012 m, with a potential difference of V = 5.2 kV = 5200 V applied between them. E = V/d = 5200 / 0.012 ≈ 4.3 × 105 N C−1. This uniform field is strong enough to sweep ions, produced when charged particles pass through the chamber, quickly toward the plates, generating a measurable current.

Electric potential vs gravitational potentialBoth are defined as potential energy per unit of the relevant property (charge or mass), both use infinity as the zero reference, and both follow a 1/r relationship with distance. But because mass is always positive and gravity is always attractive, gravitational potential V = −GM/r is always negative and always increases (becomes less negative) with distance. Electric potential V = kQ/r takes the sign of the source charge Q: it is positive everywhere around a positive charge and negative everywhere around a negative charge, and decreases in magnitude with distance in either case.

Capacitance

A capacitor is a device (commonly two parallel conducting plates separated by an insulator) used to store electric charge and energy. As charge builds up on the plates, a potential difference develops between them; how much charge a particular capacitor can store per volt of potential difference is called its capacitance.

Capacitance, CCapacitance is the ratio of the charge stored on a capacitor to the potential difference across it. It is measured in farads (F), where 1 F = 1 C V−1; because the farad is a very large unit, capacitances are usually given in microfarads (µF), nanofarads (nF) or picofarads (pF).
C = Q / V

C is the capacitance (F), Q is the magnitude of charge stored on either plate (C), and V is the potential difference across the capacitor (V). For a given capacitor, C is a fixed property of its geometry and does not change as it charges or discharges – Q and V simply increase together, in proportion, keeping their ratio constant.

Worked example

A capacitor stores 2.0 × 10−4 C of charge when the potential difference across it is 100 V. C = Q/V = (2.0 × 10−4) / 100 = 2.0 × 10−6 F = 2.0 µF. This capacitor would store twice as much charge, 4.0 × 10−4 C, if the potential difference were doubled to 200 V.

C = ε0A / d

For a parallel-plate capacitor with vacuum (or air) between the plates, capacitance depends only on its geometry: A is the area of overlap of the two plates (m2), d is the separation between them (m), and ε0 is the permittivity of free space. Capacitance increases with larger plate area (more room to store charge) and decreases with greater plate separation (a weaker field, and more work needed to add further charge).

Worked example

Find the capacitance of two parallel plates of area 0.020 m2, separated by 1.0 mm of air. C = ε0A/d = (8.85 × 10−12 × 0.020) / (1.0 × 10−3) ≈ 1.8 × 10−10 F = 177 pF. This is a typical order of magnitude for a small laboratory parallel-plate capacitor, showing why capacitors of a few microfarads or more usually need a much larger plate area or a thin insulating material with high relative permittivity between the plates.

W = ½QV = ½CV2 = Q2 / (2C)

These three equivalent forms give the electrical potential energy stored in a charged capacitor. The factor of ½ arises because, while charging, the potential difference across the capacitor rises steadily from 0 to its final value V, so the average potential difference during charging is only ½V (unlike moving a charge through a constant field, where the full V applies throughout).

Worked example

The 2.0 µF capacitor above is charged to a potential difference of 100 V. W = ½CV2 = 0.5 × 2.0 × 10−6 × 1002 = 1.0 × 10−2 J = 10 mJ. This is the total electrical energy stored in the capacitor, available to be released when it discharges.

Magnetic Fields and Field Lines

Some stones, and all magnets, exert a force on other magnets without touching them. Placing a small magnetic stone next to a large one makes it experience a force that turns it – this defines a magnetic field.

Magnetic fieldA magnetic field is a region of space where a small magnet experiences a turning force (torque). Every magnet is a dipole, with a north-seeking pole and a south-seeking pole; a freely suspended small magnet rotates until its north-seeking pole points north, which is why that end is named as it is. Unlike poles (N and S) attract; like poles (N and N, or S and S) repel.
Magnetic field lines around a bar magnetA small compass, used as a test magnet, is placed at various points around a bar magnet; joining the directions in which its north pole points traces out the magnetic field lines. These lines run from the north pole of the magnet, curve around through the space outside it, and enter the south pole, forming closed loops (the field continues inside the magnet as well, from S back to N). As with electric and gravitational field lines, the density of the lines shows the strength of the field – the lines are closest together, and the field strongest, near the poles.
The Earth's magnetic fieldBecause a compass needle's north-seeking pole always points toward the Earth's geographic north, the region near the Earth's geographic North Pole must actually contain a magnetic south pole (since unlike poles attract). The Earth behaves approximately as if it contained a giant bar magnet tilted slightly from its rotation axis, producing a field pattern like that of a bar magnet on a global scale.
Uniform magnetic field between flat magnetsBetween two flat magnetic poles facing each other (one N, one S), the field lines run straight across from the N pole to the S pole, parallel and evenly spaced – just as the electric field is uniform between charged parallel plates, and the gravitational field is uniform close to the Earth's surface. The field departs from uniform only near the edges of the poles.

Magnetic fields caused by electric currents

A compass placed near a straight current-carrying wire deflects, showing that a magnetic field surrounds any wire carrying a current – magnetic fields are produced by moving charge. This connection between electricity and magnetism was discovered by Hans Christian Oersted in 1819.

Field around a straight current-carrying wireThe field lines form concentric circles centred on the wire, in planes perpendicular to it, and are closest together (strongest field) nearest the wire. The direction of the field is found with the right-hand grip rule: point the right thumb in the direction of conventional current flow, and the curled fingers show the direction the field circles the wire.
Field inside a coil and a solenoidBending a current-carrying wire into a circular loop causes the field contributions from every part of the loop to add together in the centre, producing a stronger field there than around a straight wire; adding more loops (forming a coil) increases the field further. A solenoid is a coil wound as a long helix; applying the right-hand grip rule to a single turn shows that the fields of all the turns add together along the axis, producing a field pattern down the centre and around the outside that closely resembles that of a bar magnet, with clearly defined north and south ends.

Magnetic Flux Density and Magnetic Forces

The strength of a magnetic field is described by its magnetic flux density, given the symbol B (so a magnetic field is often called a B field), measured in tesla (T). It plays the same role for magnetic fields that field strength g and E play for gravitational and electric fields, but it is defined operationally through the force it produces on a current or a moving charge, rather than as a simple force-per-unit-quantity like g or E.

Magnetic flux density, BMagnetic flux density is a measure of how strong a magnetic field is, shown visually by how closely spaced the magnetic field lines are. One tesla is the flux density of a field that produces a force of 1 N on each metre of a wire carrying a current of 1 A, when the wire is perpendicular to the field.
F = BIL sinθ

F is the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field (N), B is the magnetic flux density (T), I is the current in the wire (A), L is the length of wire within the field (m), and θ is the angle between the wire (current direction) and the field. The force is maximum (F = BIL) when the wire is perpendicular to the field, and zero when the wire runs parallel to the field. The direction of the force is perpendicular to both the current and the field, and can be found using Fleming's left-hand rule (or the right-hand slap rule), with thumb, first finger and second finger representing force, field, and current respectively.

Worked example

A 0.50 m length of wire carries a current of 3.0 A perpendicular to a magnetic field of flux density 0.20 T. F = BIL = 0.20 × 3.0 × 0.50 = 0.30 N. This is the force pushing the wire sideways, perpendicular to both the current and the field – the basic mechanism behind electric motors and loudspeakers.

F = qvB sinθ

F is the magnetic force on a single moving charge (N), q is the magnitude of the charge (C), v is its speed (m s−1), B is the magnetic flux density (T), and θ is the angle between the charge's velocity and the field. As with the force on a wire, this force is greatest (F = qvB) when the charge moves perpendicular to the field, and zero when it moves parallel to the field; the force is always perpendicular to both v and B, so it changes the direction of the charge's motion but never its speed, which is why a charged particle moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field follows a circular path.

Worked example

An electron (q = 1.60 × 10−19 C) moves at 2.0 × 106 m s−1 perpendicular to a magnetic field of flux density 0.010 T. F = qvB = 1.60 × 10−19 × 2.0 × 106 × 0.010 = 3.2 × 10−15 N. Although tiny in absolute terms, this force is enormous compared to the electron's mass (9.1 × 10−31 kg), giving an acceleration of over 1015 m s−2 and causing the electron to curve into a circular path – the principle used to steer particle beams in instruments such as mass spectrometers and particle accelerators.

Magnetic field lines vs electric and gravitational field linesElectric field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges; gravitational field lines only ever point in toward a mass, since there is no negative, repelling mass. Magnetic field lines are different again: because no isolated magnetic pole (monopole) has ever been found, magnetic field lines never start or end anywhere – they always form closed loops, running from the north pole of a magnet, out through the surrounding space, into the south pole, and then back through the interior of the magnet to the north pole again.
