The strong nuclear force

Why a nuclear force is needed

Removing an electron from an atom typically requires only about 1 eV of energy, showing that the electromagnetic force holding electrons in place is relatively weak. Removing a nucleon from a nucleus requires roughly a million times more energy, showing that whatever force holds the nucleus together must be far stronger than the electromagnetic force. This is called the strong nuclear force.

Range and behavior of the strong force

Unlike gravity and the electric force, the strong nuclear force is not inversely proportional to distance. It is very short-range, acting only over distances of about 10-15 m (1 to 3 femtometers): attractive between nucleons that are close together but not touching, and strongly repulsive if nucleons are pushed even closer. Beyond about 3 fm, the strong force falls away and the electric (Coulomb) repulsion between protons dominates. Because the force does not reach far, each nucleon effectively only interacts with its nearest neighbors, not with every other nucleon in the nucleus.

Charge-independence of the strong force

Measurements show that all nuclei have approximately the same density, regardless of their particular combination of protons and neutrons. This tells us the strong nuclear force is the same in strength for proton-proton, proton-neutron and neutron-neutron interactions. If the neutron-neutron force were stronger, neutron-rich nuclides would be measurably denser, which is not observed.

Binding energy and mass defect

Binding energy

The binding energy of a nucleus can be defined in two equivalent ways: (1) the energy required to completely separate a nucleus into its individual protons and neutrons, or (2) the energy released when separate protons and neutrons come together to form the nucleus. Binding energy is not something the nucleons "have" while bound together; it is the amount of work needed to pull them apart, or equivalently, the energy given out when they combine.

Δm = [Z × m(1H) + N × mn] − m(atom)

Z is the proton number, N is the neutron number, m(1H) is the mass of a hydrogen-1 atom, mn is the mass of a neutron, and m(atom) is the measured mass of the neutral atom in question. Tables of nuclear data usually give atomic masses (which include the atomic electrons), so using the mass of a hydrogen atom instead of a bare proton mass makes the electron masses cancel out exactly, leaving the true nuclear mass defect Δm.

Worked example

Iron-54 (5426Fe) has Z = 26 protons and N = 54 − 26 = 28 neutrons. Using m(1H) = 1.00782 u, mn = 1.00866 u and m(54Fe atom) = 53.9396 u: Δm = (26 × 1.00782) + (28 × 1.00866) − 53.9396 = 26.2033 + 28.2425 − 53.9396 = 0.5062 u. This is the mass "missing" from the iron-54 atom compared to its 26 separate hydrogen atoms and 28 separate neutrons.

E = Δm c2    with   1 u = 1.661 × 10-27 kg = 931.5 MeV c-2

This is Einstein's mass-energy equivalence applied to the mass defect: the "missing" mass Δm has been converted into the binding energy released when the nucleus formed. Because nuclear masses are usually quoted in unified atomic mass units (u), it is far quicker to multiply a mass defect in u directly by 931.5 to get the binding energy in MeV, rather than converting to kg and using c = 3.00 × 108 m s-1 throughout.

Worked example

For iron-54, Δm = 0.5062 u (from the previous example). The binding energy is E = 0.5062 × 931.5 = 471.5 MeV. This is the total energy that would need to be supplied to break one iron-54 nucleus completely apart into 26 separate protons and 28 separate neutrons.

Binding energy per nucleon

Why divide by nucleon number?

Larger nuclei generally have a greater total binding energy simply because they have more nucleons to separate. To fairly compare the stability of different nuclides, physicists instead compare the binding energy per nucleon: the total binding energy divided by the total number of nucleons, A. A higher binding energy per nucleon means a more tightly bound, more stable nucleus.

Binding energy per nucleon = Ebinding / A

Ebinding is the total binding energy of the nucleus (in MeV) and A is the nucleon number. This quantity, rather than total binding energy, is what should be compared when judging which of two different nuclides is more stable.

Worked example

Iron-54 has a total binding energy of 471.5 MeV (from the previous example) and A = 54 nucleons. Its binding energy per nucleon is 471.5 / 54 = 8.7 MeV per nucleon. This is close to the maximum value found anywhere on the binding energy curve, which is why iron and nickel are among the most stable nuclides that exist.

The binding energy per nucleon curve

Plotting binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number for all known nuclides produces a curve that rises steeply for the lightest elements, peaks at around 8.7 to 8.8 MeV per nucleon in the region of iron (A ≈ 56) and nickel, then decreases slowly for heavier nuclides up to uranium and beyond. Nuclei near the peak (iron, nickel) are the most stable nuclei that exist. Light nuclei can release energy by fusing together to move up the left-hand slope toward the peak (fusion); very heavy nuclei can release energy by splitting into two medium-sized nuclei to move up the right-hand slope toward the peak (fission). This single curve explains both how stars generate energy and why heavy elements like uranium can release energy through fission.

Why nuclei decay

The drive toward greater stability

Just as a ball rolls downhill toward lower potential energy, a nucleus will spontaneously rearrange its protons and neutrons if doing so increases its binding energy per nucleon, moving it to a more stable configuration. Since binding energy is released (not absorbed) when stability increases, this process releases energy in the form of emitted particles and radiation – this is radioactive decay. There are three main ways an unstable nucleus can change its composition, plus a fourth way it can simply lose excess energy without changing composition.

Alpha decay

Alpha particles

An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons and 2 neutrons) ejected from an unstable nucleus. Alpha emission reduces the nucleon number by 4 and the proton number by 2. Because it removes 4 nucleons at once, alpha decay is only favorable (in terms of the binding energy curve) for large, heavy nuclei, typically with A greater than about 200.

AZX → A−4Z−2Y + 42He

X is the parent nuclide and Y is the daughter nuclide. The nucleon number decreases by 4 and the proton number decreases by 2, since 2 protons and 2 neutrons leave with the alpha particle. Both nucleon number and total charge are conserved across the equation.

Worked example

Radium-226 decays by alpha emission into radon: 22688Ra → 22286Rn + 42He. Using atomic masses m(Ra) = 226.0254 u, m(Rn) = 222.0176 u and m(He) = 4.002602 u: Δm = 226.0254 − (222.0176 + 4.002602) = 0.005198 u. Energy released = 0.005198 × 931.5 = 4.84 MeV, carried away mostly as the kinetic energy of the fast-moving, much lighter alpha particle.

Beta-minus decay

Beta-minus particles

A beta-minus particle is an ordinary electron, created and ejected when a neutron inside the nucleus transforms into a proton. An antineutrino is also emitted at the same time. Beta-minus decay occurs in nuclei that have too many neutrons relative to protons for stability – it moves the nuclide toward the band of stability by converting a neutron into a proton.

AZX → AZ+1Y + 0−1e + 00ν (antineutrino)

The nucleon number A stays the same (a neutron is simply replaced by a proton), but the proton number increases by 1, so the daughter Y is a different element one place higher in the periodic table. The antineutrino carries away some of the reaction's energy and momentum, which is why beta particles are emitted with a continuous range of energies up to a maximum, rather than a single fixed energy as with alpha particles.

Worked example

Carbon-14 decays by beta-minus emission into nitrogen-14: 146C → 147N + 0−1e + antineutrino. Using atomic masses m(14C) = 14.003241 u and m(14N) = 14.003074 u (the atomic electrons cancel out when atomic masses are used this way): Δm = 14.003241 − 14.003074 = 0.000167 u. Energy released = 0.000167 × 931.5 = 0.16 MeV, shared between the beta particle, the antineutrino and the recoiling nitrogen nucleus.

Beta-plus decay

Beta-plus particles

A beta-plus particle is a positron: a particle identical to an electron in mass but with positive charge (the antiparticle of the electron). It is created and ejected when a proton inside the nucleus transforms into a neutron, together with the emission of a neutrino. Beta-plus decay occurs in nuclei that have too few neutrons relative to protons (a proton excess) and is rarer than beta-minus decay.

AZX → AZ−1Y + 0+1e + 00ν (neutrino)

The nucleon number A is unchanged, but the proton number decreases by 1, so the daughter Y is a different element one place lower in the periodic table. Once emitted, a positron loses energy by ionizing surrounding atoms and eventually annihilates with an ordinary electron, producing gamma radiation.

Worked example

Sodium-22 decays by beta-plus emission into neon-22: 2211Na → 2210Ne + 0+1e + neutrino. Checking conservation: nucleon number 22 = 22 on both sides, and charge +11 = (+10) + (+1) on both sides. This confirms the equation is balanced; energy is released in this decay, carried away mainly as kinetic energy of the positron and neutrino.

Gamma decay

Gamma radiation

Gamma radiation is high-frequency electromagnetic radiation (a photon) emitted from a nucleus that is left in an excited (higher-energy) state, often immediately after an alpha or beta decay. Unlike alpha and beta decay, gamma emission does not change the number of protons or neutrons in the nucleus – the nucleus simply loses energy by dropping to a lower energy state, exactly as an atomic electron does when it emits a photon.

AZX* → AZX + γ

The asterisk marks the parent nucleus as being in an excited (metastable) state. Both A and Z are unchanged, since only a photon (no charge, no mass number) is emitted. Because the energy released in nuclear processes is very large (order of MeV), gamma photons have very high frequency and are highly ionizing and penetrating.

Worked example

Metastable technetium-99m decays by gamma emission into ordinary technetium-99: 9943Tc* → 9943Tc + γ. Both the nucleon number (99) and proton number (43) are unchanged; only a gamma photon carrying away excess nuclear energy is emitted. This is the basis of technetium-99m's use as a medical tracer, discussed later in this chapter.

Properties of alpha, beta and gamma radiation

Comparing the three types of radiation

Alpha particles are the most massive and most highly charged (+2e), so they interact strongly with matter: they are the most strongly ionizing but the least penetrating, losing all their energy within a few centimeters of air and being stopped completely by a sheet of paper or the outer layer of human skin. Beta particles are far less massive and carry a single charge, so they ionize less per collision but travel further: roughly ten times the range of alpha particles in air, passing through paper but stopped by a few millimeters of aluminum. Gamma rays carry no charge and are electromagnetic radiation, so they interact only weakly with matter: they are the most weakly ionizing but by far the most penetrating, travelling large distances in air and requiring several centimeters of lead (or thicker concrete or water) to significantly reduce their intensity, which decreases roughly following an inverse square law with distance rather than being sharply absorbed.

Detecting radiation

Geiger-Muller (GM) tubes

A Geiger-Muller tube is a type of ionization chamber containing a low-pressure gas. When an ionizing particle passes through, it ionizes the gas, allowing a brief pulse of current to flow between two electrodes; this pulse is counted electronically, giving a count rate. A GM tube on its own cannot distinguish between alpha, beta and gamma radiation directly, but the different penetrating powers of each type (shown above) can be used to identify them, for example by placing sheets of paper, aluminum or lead between the source and the tube.

Cloud and bubble chambers

A cloud chamber contains a supersaturated vapor that condenses into visible droplets along the path of an ionizing particle, forming a track much like the vapor trail behind an airplane. A bubble chamber works similarly but forms a track of small bubbles in a superheated liquid. Alpha particles leave short, thick, straight tracks because they are heavily ionizing and easily deflected very little; beta particles leave thinner, longer, more easily scattered tracks. A single gamma photon, being weakly ionizing, usually leaves no track at all unless it happens to eject an energetic electron from an atom, which then leaves its own track.

The random and spontaneous nature of decay

Random and spontaneous

Radioactive decay is random: it is impossible to predict exactly when any individual nucleus will decay, or which nucleus in a sample will be the next to decay – only the probability of decay in a given time interval is known. Radioactive decay is also spontaneous: the decay rate of a given isotope is not affected by external conditions such as temperature, pressure, or chemical state. Although any single nucleus's decay is unpredictable, a large sample containing huge numbers of identical nuclei behaves predictably overall, because the number of decays per second is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present.

Half-life, activity and exponential decay

Half-life

The half-life, T1/2, of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for half of the nuclei in a sample to decay (equivalently, the time for the activity or count rate to fall to half its original value). The half-life of a given isotope is a fixed, constant quantity, regardless of how much of the isotope is present or how old the sample is.

Worked example

Cobalt-60 decays by beta emission with a half-life of approximately 5 years. If a sample initially emits 40 beta particles per second, then after 5 years (one half-life) the activity has fallen to 20 particles per second; after a further 5 years (10 years total, two half-lives) it has fallen to 10 particles per second; after a further 5 years (15 years total, three half-lives) it has fallen to 5 particles per second. This "halving" method works directly whenever the elapsed time is a whole number of half-lives.

Activity

The activity of a radioactive sample is the number of nuclear decays occurring per second, measured in becquerels (Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. Since each decay releases one particle (or photon), the activity equals the rate at which radiation is emitted. Because the decay rate is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present, a graph of activity against time has exactly the same exponential shape as a graph of the number of undecayed nuclei against time.

λ = ln(2) / T1/2

λ is the decay constant: the probability that any individual nucleus will decay in one second (units s-1), and T1/2 is the half-life. A large decay constant means rapid decay and a short half-life; a small decay constant means slow decay and a long half-life. This equation lets the half-life of an isotope be converted into its decay constant, and vice versa.

Worked example

Cobalt-60 has a half-life of T1/2 = 5 years = 5 × 3.156 × 107 = 1.578 × 108 s. Its decay constant is λ = ln(2) / (1.578 × 108) = 0.693 / (1.578 × 108) = 4.39 × 10-9 s-1. This very small value shows that, at any instant, an individual cobalt-60 nucleus has only a tiny probability of decaying in the next second, consistent with its multi-year half-life.

N = N0 e−λt

N is the number of undecayed nuclei remaining at time t, N0 is the initial number of undecayed nuclei (at t = 0), λ is the decay constant, and e is Euler's number. This equation applies for any elapsed time t, not just whole numbers of half-lives, and is the general mathematical form of exponential radioactive decay. The same form applies to activity (A = A0e−λt) and to corrected count rate, since both are directly proportional to N.

Worked example

Nitrogen-17 decays into oxygen-17 with a half-life of 4 s. Starting with N0 = 200 g, find the amount remaining after t = 16 s. The decay constant is λ = ln(2)/4 = 0.1733 s-1. Then N = 200 × e−0.1733 × 16 = 200 × e−2.773 = 200 × 0.0625 = 12.5 g. This matches the simple halving method, since 16 s is exactly 4 half-lives: 200 → 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 g.

A = λN

A is the activity of a sample (Bq), λ is the decay constant (s-1), and N is the number of undecayed nuclei present at that moment. This equation connects the microscopic decay probability of a single nucleus (λ) to the macroscopic, measurable activity of an entire sample.

Worked example

A cobalt-60 sample has an initial activity of A = 40 Bq and a decay constant of λ = 4.39 × 10-9 s-1 (from the earlier example). Rearranging A = λN gives N = A/λ = 40 / (4.39 × 10-9) = 9.11 × 109 nuclei. This shows that even a small, safe activity of 40 Bq corresponds to billions of undecayed cobalt-60 nuclei still present in the sample.

Background radiation

Sources of background radiation

Background radiation is the ionizing radiation constantly present in the environment from natural (and some artificial) sources, typically giving a count rate in the region of 20 counts per minute on an unshielded GM tube. Its main sources are: radioactive isotopes such as uranium and thorium naturally present in rocks and soil; radon gas, an alpha-emitting gas released when radium in certain rocks decays, which can accumulate in poorly ventilated buildings built on radium-rich ground; and cosmic rays, high-energy particles arriving from the Sun and other astronomical sources.

Corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate

Because background radiation is always present, any experimental measurement of a source's count rate is too high unless the background contribution is subtracted first. The background count rate should be measured separately (with the source removed or shielded) under the same conditions, then subtracted from every subsequent reading.

Worked example

A GM tube placed near a radioactive source records 45 counts per minute. With the source removed, the same tube records a background rate of 20 counts per minute. The corrected count rate due to the source alone is 45 − 20 = 25 counts per minute. Failing to subtract the background would overestimate the source's true activity by nearly a factor of two in this case.

Biological effects and safety

Biological effects of ionizing radiation

Ionizing radiation can damage living cells by ionizing atoms within them, breaking chemical bonds and disrupting molecules such as DNA. This can kill cells outright, or cause mutations that may lead to cancer if the cell survives and divides. Alpha sources are the most dangerous if inhaled or ingested (for example in contaminated food, dust or radon gas), since their intense but short-range ionization is then deposited directly inside body tissue; externally, alpha is the least dangerous of the three, since it cannot even penetrate skin. Gamma radiation is the most dangerous as an external hazard because of its high penetrating power, which allows it to reach and damage internal organs from outside the body.

Safety precautions

Exposure to ionizing radiation is reduced by minimizing time near a source, maximizing distance from it (since intensity falls off with distance, especially for gamma sources), and using shielding appropriate to the radiation type (paper or clothing for alpha, aluminum for beta, thick lead or concrete for gamma). Radioactive sources are handled with tongs rather than bare hands, stored in shielded containers when not in use, and workers who may be exposed regularly wear film badges or dosimeters to monitor their cumulative radiation dose.

Uses of radioisotopes

Choosing an isotope for a task

Selecting a radioisotope for a practical application depends mainly on two factors: the penetrating power of the radiation it emits (which must be appropriate for where the isotope will be used and detected), and its half-life (which must be long enough for the application to be completed, but short enough to avoid unnecessary long-term hazard or waste).

Radioactive dating

Living organisms constantly exchange carbon with the atmosphere, so their ratio of carbon-14 to carbon-12 matches the atmosphere's ratio while alive. After death, carbon is no longer replaced, so the carbon-14 present steadily decays away with its characteristic half-life of about 5700 years while the carbon-12 amount stays fixed. Measuring how far the carbon-14 to carbon-12 ratio has fallen below the atmospheric value allows the time since death to be calculated using the decay law.
