What a Wave Is

A wave is a propagating disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. If a stone is thrown into a pool, the disturbance spreads out across the surface as a wave, but the water itself does not travel outward – each bit of water simply moves up and down about a fixed position. The energy given to the water by the stone spreads out in the form of the wave. A big storm far out in the Atlantic Ocean can produce waves that break on beaches thousands of kilometres away, because the storm gives the water energy that is then carried across the ocean by the wave.

WaveA wave is the transfer of energy through a medium (or field) by means of a propagating disturbance, without any net transfer of the medium itself.
A wave is not the same as bulk movement of matterIn a "stadium wave", spectators stand and sit in sequence so that a pulse appears to move around the stadium, but no person actually travels around the ground – only the disturbance moves. This is a useful analogy, but it is not a true wave: nothing is transferred from one spectator to another (no energy is passed on), and the pattern does not reflect off a wall or add together with another such wave when the two meet. Real waves transmit energy and show reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction.

Anything that reflects, refracts, interferes with other waves, and diffracts can be classed as a wave. These four behaviours (studied in detail in the wave phenomena topic) are the defining tests for wave-like behaviour, whether the "wave" is on water, in a string, in air (sound), or is an electromagnetic wave such as light.

Wave Pulses and How Energy Is Transferred

To see how a continuous wave is built up, it helps to first look at a single pulse travelling along a stretched string. Flicking one end of a long string up and then back down sends a single hump, or pulse, travelling along the string at a constant speed called the wave speed. Imagine the string as a line of balls each joined to the next by a small piece of string: when the first ball is lifted, it pulls the next one up, which pulls the next, and so on, passing the disturbance (and the energy given to the string) along the line. As the pulse moves along, each part of the string repeats the same up-then-down motion as the part before it, but at a slightly later time – the different points on the string are out of phase with each other, in the same way spectators in a stadium wave stand up at different times.

Figure C.2.1-2: A pulse travelling along a stringA single upward hump (pulse) moves along a horizontal string from left to right. At any instant, the particles at the leading edge of the pulse are moving upward and the particles at the trailing edge are moving downward, even though the pulse shape as a whole is travelling steadily to the right.

Reflection of a pulse

When a pulse meets a fixed end (such as a wall the string is tied to), the string pushes up on the wall, and by Newton's third law the wall pushes back down on the string. This sends a reflected pulse back along the string that is inverted (a peak becomes a trough, or vice versa). If instead the end of the string is free to move (a loose end), the pulse is reflected without any inversion – the free end simply moves up and back down just like the hand that made the original pulse, and an upright reflected pulse travels back.

A reflected pulse is produced whenever a wave meets any change in the medium it is travelling through, not only at a fixed or free end. If two strings of different thickness are tied together, part of a pulse is reflected at the join and part is transmitted into the second string. If the second string is heavier (denser), the reflected pulse behaves as though it met a fixed end (it is inverted); if the second string is lighter, the reflected pulse behaves as though it met a free end (it is not inverted).

Superposition of pulses

If two pulses are sent along a string from opposite ends, they pass through each other, and where they overlap their displacements add together vectorially – this is called superposition. Two peaks meeting add to give a bigger peak; a peak and a trough meeting tend to cancel out. After passing through the point of overlap, each pulse continues on unaffected, as if the other pulse had never been there. When two opposite pulses momentarily cancel out completely, the energy has not disappeared: the particles of the string are still moving (with kinetic energy) even though the net displacement is briefly zero.

Describing a Continuous Wave: Key Quantities

If the end of a string is moved up and down continuously with simple harmonic motion (SHM), a continuous wave travels along the string: every part of the string eventually oscillates with the same amplitude and frequency as the end, but each part is out of phase with its neighbours because the disturbance takes a finite time to reach it.

QuantitySymbolDefinitionSI unit
AmplitudeAThe maximum displacement of a point on the wave from its equilibrium (undisturbed) position.m
WavelengthλThe distance between two consecutive crests (or troughs), or between any two consecutive points that are in phase.m
FrequencyfThe number of complete wave cycles passing a fixed point per unit time.Hz (s−1)
PeriodTThe time taken for one complete wave cycle to pass a fixed point.s
Wave speedvThe distance travelled by the wave profile (the disturbance) per unit time.m s−1
T = 1 / f

The period T and the frequency f are reciprocals of each other: a wave that completes more cycles per second (higher f) takes less time to complete each cycle (shorter T). This relationship applies to any periodic wave, mechanical or electromagnetic.

Worked example

An ocean wave has a period T = 10 s. Its frequency is f = 1/T = 1/10 = 0.1 Hz. This means only one-tenth of a complete wave cycle passes a fixed point each second – a very slow, low-frequency wave compared to sound or light.

v = fλ

The wave speed v equals the frequency f multiplied by the wavelength λ. This follows because in one period T the wave profile advances exactly one wavelength λ, so v = distance/time = λ/T = fλ (since f = 1/T). Always convert wavelength to metres before substituting.

Worked example

The A string of a guitar vibrates at f = 110 Hz, producing a wave of wavelength λ = 153 cm = 1.53 m in the string. The wave speed is v = fλ = 110 × 1.53 = 168.3 m s−1. This is the speed at which the disturbance travels along the string, which is much faster than the string itself ever moves sideways.

PhaseTwo points on a wave are "in phase" if they are always moving in the same direction with the same displacement at the same time (a whole number of wavelengths apart); they are "out of phase" if their motions do not match up in this way. Neighbouring points on a continuous wave are all slightly out of phase with each other because the disturbance takes time to travel between them.

Transverse Waves

Transverse waveA wave in which the direction of the disturbance (the oscillation of the medium) is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer.

Waves on a string or on water are transverse: the string moves up and down while the wave itself travels horizontally along the string. Electromagnetic waves (see below) are also transverse.

A transverse wave can be represented graphically in two different ways.

Figure C.2.9-12: Reading transverse wave graphsA displacement-time graph for a single particle looks identical in shape to a displacement-position snapshot of the whole wave, but the horizontal axis has a completely different meaning: on the time graph, the spacing of peaks is the period T; on the position graph, the spacing of peaks is the wavelength λ. On a position (snapshot) graph, time moves the whole wave pattern to the right (for a wave travelling right), so the part of the wave that will arrive at a point next is the part currently to the left of that point on the graph, not the part to the right.

The equation of a transverse wave

Every point on a transverse wave oscillates with SHM. If the end of a string moves as y = A sin(ωt), then a point further along the string undergoes the same motion but delayed by a phase angle θ, so its displacement is y = A sin(ωt − θ). Since points one whole wavelength apart are back in phase, and θ is proportional to distance x along the string, writing θ = kx and requiring kλ = 2π gives k = 2π/λ.

y = A sin(ωt − kx), where k = 2π / λ

Here y is the displacement of the point at position x and time t, A is the amplitude, ω = 2πf is the angular frequency, and k (the wavenumber) sets how quickly the phase changes with distance along the wave. This single equation describes the motion of every point on the wave once A, f and λ are known.

Worked example

A transverse wave has amplitude A = 0.20 m, frequency f = 5.0 Hz, and wavelength λ = 0.40 m. Then ω = 2πf = 2π(5.0) = 31.4 rad s−1, and k = 2π/λ = 2π/0.40 = 15.7 rad m−1. The wave equation for this wave is y = 0.20 sin(31.4t − 15.7x). Substituting any chosen values of position x and time t into this equation gives the displacement of that point on the string at that moment.

Longitudinal Waves

Longitudinal waveA wave in which the direction of the disturbance (the oscillation of the medium) is parallel to the direction of energy transfer.

Moving the end of a long spring (a "slinky") in and out, rather than up and down, sends compressions (squashed, closely spaced regions) and rarefactions (stretched, spread-out regions) travelling along the spring instead of peaks and troughs. This is a longitudinal wave. Although it looks different from a transverse wave, it has exactly the same defining properties: amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period and wave speed are all defined and related in the same way (v = fλ still applies).

Figure C.2.15, 17-20: Longitudinal wave in a springA row of balls connected by springs is disturbed at one end so that regions of bunched-together balls (compressions) and spread-out balls (rarefactions) travel along the row. The distance between two successive compressions (or between any two balls in phase) is the wavelength. On a displacement-position graph for a longitudinal wave, a positive displacement means a particle has moved in the direction of wave travel and a negative displacement means it has moved against it; particles at the centre of a compression or rarefaction have zero displacement, while particles midway between have maximum displacement.
Transverse vs longitudinal wavesIn a transverse wave the particles oscillate perpendicular to the direction the wave (and its energy) travels, producing peaks and troughs (example: waves on a string, water waves, electromagnetic waves). In a longitudinal wave the particles oscillate parallel to the direction of energy transfer, producing compressions and rarefactions (example: sound waves, a pulse in a slinky spring). Because their particles oscillate in only one direction (along the line of travel), longitudinal waves cannot be polarized, whereas transverse waves can.

Sound Waves

Sound is a longitudinal wave. When an object (such as a loudspeaker cone) moves, it compresses the layer of air immediately in front of it; that compressed layer then expands and compresses the next layer of air, and so on, passing a pressure disturbance from one layer of air to the next. If the source oscillates continuously, a continuous sound wave is propagated through the air as a travelling variation in pressure. The molecules of the gas itself still move with random thermal motion; it is the regular pattern of compressions and rarefactions superimposed on this random motion that constitutes the sound wave.

Perceived qualityRelated wave property
Pitch (how high or low a note sounds)Frequency: a high pitch corresponds to a high frequency.
LoudnessAmplitude: a loud sound has a large amplitude. Loudness is measured with a sound meter, in decibels (dB).

The speed of sound in air at 20°C and normal atmospheric pressure is about 340 m s−1; at higher temperatures the speed is greater because the air molecules move faster on average. Sound reflects off hard surfaces (a cliff produces an echo; the walls of a room reflect sound, which is why a room and the outdoors sound different), and sound refracts when it passes between air layers of different temperature – this is why, on a still night, sound can travel further than usual: it reaches a distant listener both directly and by a refracted path through cooler air near the ground, reinforcing the sound.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Electromagnetic waveA propagating disturbance in electric and magnetic fields. Electromagnetic waves are transverse (the oscillating electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to the direction the wave travels) and, unlike mechanical waves such as sound or water waves, do not require a medium – they can travel through a vacuum.

Electromagnetic waves are classified by wavelength (or equivalently by frequency, since v = fλ and all electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum, c ≈ 3 × 108 m s−1). Visible light occupies only a small part of the full spectrum, roughly 400 nm (violet) to about 700-800 nm (red); different wavelengths within this range are perceived as different colours, though colour perception is more complex than wavelength alone (for example, red and green light mixed together are perceived as yellow).

Mechanical waves vs electromagnetic wavesMechanical waves (sound, water waves, waves on a string or spring) are disturbances of a physical medium and cannot travel through a vacuum; their speed depends on the properties of that medium (for example, sound travels faster in denser media). Electromagnetic waves are disturbances of electric and magnetic fields, need no medium at all, and all travel at the same speed c in a vacuum regardless of frequency. Both types of wave are described by the same basic quantities (amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period, speed related by v = fλ) and can both reflect, refract, diffract and interfere.

Wave Intensity

IntensityThe intensity of a wave is the power it delivers per unit area, measured in W m−2. For light, intensity corresponds to brightness; it is measured with a light meter.
I ∝ A2

The intensity I of a wave is proportional to the square of its amplitude A. This means that doubling the amplitude of a wave (for example, by increasing the power delivered to its source) quadruples its intensity, because power depends on the square of how far the particles of the medium are displaced from equilibrium.

Worked example

A wave of amplitude A1 has intensity I1 = 10 W m−2. If the amplitude is doubled to A2 = 2A1, the new intensity is I2 = I1 × (A2/A1)2 = 10 × 22 = 40 W m−2. Doubling the amplitude of a wave makes it four times as intense.

I = P / (4πr2)

For a point source that radiates power P equally in all directions, the energy spreads out over the surface of an imaginary sphere of radius r centred on the source, which has surface area 4πr2. As you move further from a point source, the same total power is spread over an ever-larger area, so the intensity falls off as the square of the distance – this is the inverse square law.

Worked example

A light bulb radiates P = 100 W of light uniformly in all directions. At a distance r = 2.0 m, the intensity is I = P / (4πr2) = 100 / (4π × 2.02) = 100 / 50.3 = 2.0 W m−2. This is how bright the light appears to a detector held 2.0 m from the bulb; moving to 4.0 m away (double the distance) would reduce the intensity to a quarter of this value.
