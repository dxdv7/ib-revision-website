Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation

Newton recognised that the force which pulls an apple to the ground is the same force that holds the Earth in orbit around the Sun. Every single particle of mass in the Universe attracts every other particle of mass; there is even a (vanishingly small) force between the end of your nose and a lump of rock on the Moon.

Newton's universal law of gravitationEvery point mass attracts every other point mass with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
F = Gm1m2 / r2

F is the magnitude of the attractive force (in newtons) experienced equally by both masses (Newton's third law), m1 and m2 are the two point masses (in kg), r is the separation between their centres (in m), and G is the universal gravitational constant, G = 6.67 × 10−11 m3 kg−1 s−2. The law also applies to uniform spheres of mass, treating each as a point mass at its centre, since spheres are symmetrically spherical.

Worked example

Find the force on a 250 g apple at the Earth's equator (mass of Earth m1 = 5.97 × 1024 kg, radius r = 6378 km). F = Gm1m2/r2 = (6.67 × 10−11 × 5.97 × 1024 × 0.25) / (6.378 × 106)2 = 2.45 N. From Newton's second law, a = F/m = 2.45 / 0.25 = 9.79 m s−2, which is very close to the standard value of the acceleration of free fall, g = 9.81 m s−2 (the two are not exactly equal because 9.81 m s−2 is an average over the whole Earth, whereas the equatorial radius used here is the Earth's maximum radius).

Extended bodies, centre of mass and centre of gravityAn extended body is any object whose mass is not confined to a single point. Every extended body has a centre of mass, the point about which its mass is uniformly distributed; for a regular, uniform body this is its geometric centre. The centre of gravity is the point at which the entire weight of the body can be taken to act. In a uniform field the centre of mass and centre of gravity coincide, so a uniform sphere (such as a planet) can be treated as a point mass at its centre. In a non-uniform field the centre of gravity shifts slightly toward the region of stronger field – for example, the Moon's centre of gravity lies slightly toward the Earth rather than at its geometric centre.

Gravitational Field and Field Strength

The apple and the Earth pull on each other without being in contact, which is different from every other type of force encountered so far. To model this we introduce the idea of a field: a region of space where forces are experienced by bodies with the appropriate property. A gravitational field is a region of space where a mass experiences a force because of its mass.

Gravitational field strength, gThe gravitational field strength at a point is the gravitational force experienced per unit mass by a small point (or small) test mass placed at that point. It is a vector quantity, measured in N kg−1, pointing in the direction of the force.
g = F / m

g is the field strength (N kg−1), F is the gravitational force experienced by a test mass m placed at that point. The test mass is assumed to be small enough that its own gravitational field does not disturb the field being measured. When more than one mass contributes to a field, the field strengths add as vectors.

Worked example

A 2.0 kg mass at the Earth's surface experiences a gravitational force of 19.6 N. g = F/m = 19.6 / 2.0 = 9.8 N kg−1, directed toward the centre of the Earth – the familiar value of the acceleration of free fall, now expressed as a field strength.

g = GM / r2

Combining g = F/m with Newton's law of gravitation (F = GMm/r2) gives the field strength at a distance r from a point or spherical mass M. This equation only applies outside (or on the surface of) a uniform sphere; the field strength inside a uniform hollow shell of mass is zero, for the same reason the electric field inside a hollow charged sphere is zero (see D.2).

Worked example

Calculate the resultant gravitational field strength at point A, which lies 2.5 m from a 1000 kg mass and 2.5 m from a 100 kg mass, with the two masses on opposite sides of A along the same line. Field due to the 1000 kg mass: g1 = GM/r2 = (6.67 × 10−11 × 1000) / 2.52 = 1.07 × 10−8 N kg−1. Field due to the 100 kg mass: g2 = (6.67 × 10−11 × 100) / 2.52 = 1.07 × 10−9 N kg−1. Because the two masses pull A in opposite directions along the line joining them, the resultant is the difference of the two vectors: g = 1.07 × 10−8 − 1.07 × 10−9 = 9.63 × 10−9 N kg−1, directed toward the larger (1000 kg) mass. Since field strength is a vector, whenever the contributing fields are not collinear they must instead be combined using vector addition (for example, with Pythagoras' theorem if they are at right angles).

Field lines of a spherical massField lines point radially inward toward the centre of a spherical mass, with arrows showing the direction a test mass would accelerate. The lines are closest together (densest) near the surface of the mass, showing that the field is strongest there, and spread further apart at greater distances, showing the field weakening with distance.
Uniform field close to the Earth's surfaceOver the small scale of a room or laboratory, the field lines close to the Earth's surface are essentially parallel and equally spaced, pointing straight down. This means the gravitational field strength is effectively constant (uniform) throughout the room, so every mass in it experiences the same downward force per unit mass.

Kepler's Laws and Orbital Motion

The solar system consists of the Sun at the centre with eight planets in orbit. Although the true orbits are slightly elliptical, they are treated as circular for calculations at this level. Kepler derived three empirical laws of planetary motion by studying observational data; all three can also be derived from Newton's law of gravitation combined with the mechanics of circular motion.

LawStatement
First lawPlanetary orbits are elliptical, with the star at one focus of the ellipse. (Circular orbits, used throughout this course, are a special case of an ellipse.)
Second lawThe radius vector from the star to the orbiting body sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Qualitatively, this means the orbiting body moves faster when it is closer to the star, because its kinetic energy increases as its gravitational potential energy decreases (total energy is conserved).
Third lawThe square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbital radius (or, for an ellipse, the semi-major axis): T2 ∝ r3.

For a body of mass m in a circular orbit of radius r around a much larger mass M, gravity supplies the centripetal force. Equating the two gives the equation of orbital motion.

GMm / r2 = mv2 / r  ⇒  v = √(GM / r)

v is the constant orbital speed of the orbiting body, M is the mass being orbited, and r is the orbital radius. The orbiting body's own mass m cancels out entirely, so the orbital speed (and period) of a satellite does not depend on the satellite's own mass, only on M and r.

Worked example

A satellite orbits 400 km above the Earth's surface (Earth's radius 6400 km, so r = 6.80 × 106 m; Earth's mass M = 5.97 × 1024 kg). v = √(GM/r) = √[(6.67 × 10−11 × 5.97 × 1024) / (6.80 × 106)] = √(5.86 × 107) = 7.65 × 103 m s−1. This is close to the real orbital speed of the International Space Station, about 7.7 km s−1.

Writing the orbital speed in terms of angular velocity (v = ωr, with ω = 2π/T) and substituting into the equation above gives Kepler's third law in full.

T2 = (4π2 / GM) r3

T is the orbital period (in seconds), r is the orbital radius, and M is the mass of the body being orbited. This shows that T2/r3 is a constant for every body orbiting the same central mass M – for example, this ratio is the same for every planet orbiting the Sun, and the same for every satellite orbiting the Earth (with a different constant, since M differs).

Worked example

A geostationary TV satellite has an orbital period equal to one day, T = 86 400 s, so that it stays above the same point on the Earth's surface. Rearranging: r3 = GMT2/4π2 = (6.67 × 10−11 × 5.97 × 1024 × 86 4002) / (4π2) = 7.53 × 1022 m3, so r = 4.22 × 107 m ≈ 42 200 km from the Earth's centre, or about 35 800 km above the surface. This matches the known altitude of geostationary satellites (about 35 786 km), confirming that low-orbit satellites (like the spy satellite above, with a period of under two hours) must orbit much closer to the Earth than geostationary satellites.

Circular orbits vs Kepler's lawsThis course only requires circular orbits, for which Kepler's first law reduces to a circle (a special ellipse with the star or planet at the centre rather than at an off-centre focus), and Kepler's second law is automatically satisfied because a body in a circular orbit already moves at constant speed, sweeping out equal angles – and therefore equal areas – in equal times.

Gravitational Potential Energy and Gravitational Potential

Just as an object near the Earth's surface has gravitational potential energy mgh relative to the ground, every mass has gravitational potential energy due to its position in a gravitational field. Because gravitational fields extend to infinity and get weaker (rather than stronger) close to the source, it is most useful to define potential energy relative to a point infinitely far from the mass, where the field strength is zero.

Gravitational potential energy, EpThe gravitational potential energy of a mass at a point is the work done to bring it from infinity (where Ep is defined as zero) to that point. Because gravity is always attractive, this work is negative – the field itself does positive work pulling the mass in – so gravitational potential energy is always negative for two masses that attract each other.
Ep = −GMm / r

Ep is the gravitational potential energy (in joules) of a mass m at a distance r from a mass M. The negative sign shows that Ep becomes less negative (increases) as r increases, consistent with work having to be done against the attractive force to move the masses further apart, and Ep → 0 as r → ∞.

Worked example

Find the gravitational potential energy of a 500 kg satellite at r = 7.0 × 106 m from the centre of the Earth. Ep = −GMm/r = −(6.67 × 10−11 × 5.97 × 1024 × 500) / (7.0 × 106) = −2.84 × 1010 J. The negative value means 2.84 × 1010 J of work would have to be done on the satellite to remove it completely from the Earth's gravitational field (to move it to infinity, where Ep = 0).

Gravitational potential, VGravitational potential at a point is the gravitational potential energy per unit mass at that point – equivalently, the work done per unit mass to bring a small test mass from infinity to that point. Like field strength, it is defined so that it does not depend on the size of the mass being brought in.
V = −GM / r

V is the gravitational potential (in J kg−1) at a distance r from a mass M. Unlike field strength g, potential V is a scalar, so potentials from several masses are simply added (with their signs) rather than combined as vectors. The potential energy of a mass m placed at a point of potential V is Ep = mV.

Worked example

Find the gravitational potential at the Earth's surface, r = 6.371 × 106 m. V = −GM/r = −(6.67 × 10−11 × 5.97 × 1024) / (6.371 × 106) = −6.25 × 107 J kg−1. This means every kilogram of mass at the Earth's surface would need 6.25 × 107 J of energy supplied to it to be removed completely from the Earth's gravitational pull.

Escape Velocity

To completely escape a planet's gravitational field, a projectile launched from the surface (ignoring air resistance) must be given at least enough kinetic energy to reach r = ∞ with zero speed remaining, at which point its total energy is exactly zero (Ep = 0 there, and KE = 0 too). The minimum launch speed for which this is possible is the escape velocity.

Escape velocityThe escape velocity from a planet's surface is the minimum speed an object must be launched with (with no further propulsion, and neglecting air resistance) so that it can escape to an infinite distance from the planet without falling back.
vesc = √(2GM / r)

This follows from setting the initial kinetic energy equal in size to the (negative) gravitational potential energy at the surface: ½mv2 = GMm/r, so that total energy (KE + Ep) is exactly zero. M is the mass of the planet, r is the distance from its centre (its radius, if launched from the surface), and vesc does not depend on the mass of the escaping object.

Worked example

Find the escape velocity from the Earth's surface. vesc = √(2GM/r) = √[(2 × 6.67 × 10−11 × 5.97 × 1024) / (6.371 × 106)] = √(1.25 × 108) = 1.12 × 104 m s−1 ≈ 11.2 km s−1. Any object launched from the Earth's surface at 11.2 km s−1 or faster, with no further thrust and ignoring air resistance, will escape the Earth's gravitational field completely.

Orbital speed vs escape velocityAt a given distance r from a planet's centre, the orbital speed v = √(GM/r) is the speed needed to move in a stable circular orbit at that distance, while the escape velocity vesc = √(2GM/r) is the (larger) speed needed to leave the field entirely. Comparing the two formulas shows that vesc = √2 × v (orbital speed at the same r) – escape velocity is always exactly √2 times the corresponding circular orbital speed.
