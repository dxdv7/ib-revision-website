Formation of Standing Waves

A standing (or stationary) wave is formed when two identical waves – same frequency, wavelength and amplitude – travel through the same region in opposite directions and superpose. This happens, for example, whenever a wave reflects back on itself: the reflected wave travels in the opposite direction to the original wave and the two combine. Unlike a progressive (travelling) wave, the resulting wave pattern does not move through the medium.

Standing waveA standing wave is the pattern produced by the superposition of two waves of equal frequency, wavelength and amplitude travelling in opposite directions through the same medium. The resulting wave profile stays in a fixed position; only the amplitude of oscillation at each point changes with time.
Figure C.4.1: Two waves passing through each otherTwo identical waves travel toward each other along a string. As they cross, their displacements add together (the principle of superposition). At the instant the two waves are exactly out of phase, they cancel completely and the string is flat. A quarter of a period later they are back in phase, and the resultant (shown as a green line) has twice the amplitude of either wave alone. Stepping through the motion in quarter-period intervals shows that the midpoint of the overlap region never moves at all, while points on either side swing between a maximum positive and a maximum negative displacement.
Nodes and antinodesA node is a point on a standing wave that always has zero displacement, because the two component waves are always exactly out of phase there and permanently cancel. An antinode is a point that oscillates with maximum amplitude, because the two component waves are always exactly in phase there and always reinforce.
distance between adjacent nodes = λ/2

λ is the wavelength of the two component waves that form the standing wave. Adjacent nodes (and, equally, adjacent antinodes) are always separated by half a wavelength, and a node and its neighbouring antinode are always a quarter of a wavelength apart. This spacing is fixed by the wavelength alone and does not depend on the amplitude of the wave.

Worked example

Two adjacent nodes on a standing wave in a stretched string are observed to be 0.15 m apart. What is the wavelength of the waves forming this standing wave? Since adjacent nodes are separated by λ/2: λ = 2 × 0.15 = 0.30 m. Measuring the node spacing on a standing wave is therefore a simple, direct way to find the wavelength of the waves that produced it.

Standing Waves versus Progressive Waves

Although a standing wave can look similar to a progressive (travelling) wave when sketched at one instant, the two are fundamentally different. A useful mental picture is the contrast between a skipping rope, shaken up and down so that it settles into a fixed sinusoidal shape (a standing wave), and a stadium wave, where each spectator stands up slightly after their neighbour so that a hump of motion visibly travels around the stadium (a progressive wave).

PropertyProgressive waveStanding wave
Energy transferEnergy is carried along with the wave as it travelsNo net energy is transferred; the wave profile does not progress
PhasePoints within one wavelength of each other are all out of phase by varying amountsAll points between two adjacent nodes oscillate in phase with each other (and in antiphase with points in the neighbouring loop)
AmplitudeEvery point on the wave has the same amplitudeAmplitude varies from zero at the nodes to a maximum at the antinodes
Progressive wave vs standing waveA progressive wave transports energy and has a wave profile that physically moves through the medium at the wave speed, with every particle sharing the same amplitude but each vibrating slightly out of phase with its neighbours. A standing wave transports no net energy, its profile is fixed in space, and while all particles within one loop (between two nodes) vibrate in phase with each other, the amplitude of vibration itself varies from point to point, from zero at the nodes to a maximum at the antinodes.

Standing Waves on Strings

Many musical instruments (guitar, violin, piano) use a stretched string fixed at both ends. When the string is plucked, a wave travels to one fixed end, reflects, and superposes with the original wave travelling the other way, forming a standing wave. Because the ends of the string are fixed, they cannot move, so they must always be nodes. This restricts the string to only those standing wave patterns that have a node at each end.

Figure C.4.2: Standing waves on a string of length LThe first harmonic (fundamental) has a node at each end and a single antinode in the middle: only half a wavelength fits along the string, so λ = 2L, and its frequency is called f1. The second harmonic has an extra node in the middle, fitting one full wavelength along the string (λ = L), giving frequency 2f1. The third harmonic fits one and a half wavelengths along the string (λ = 2L/3), giving frequency 3f1. Each successive harmonic adds one more node and antinode, and the pattern continues indefinitely.

When a string is plucked it vibrates simultaneously with a mixture of many of these harmonic frequencies, but only frequencies with nodes at both ends can survive as standing waves. Analysing the sound of a plucked guitar string with a computer produces a frequency spectrum with sharp peaks at 100 Hz, 200 Hz, 300 Hz and so on (an example harmonic series); the tallest peak is normally the first harmonic, and this is the frequency of the musical note that is heard, even though the higher harmonics are also present and contribute to the timbre of the sound.

Since wave speed, frequency and wavelength are related by v = fλ, the speed of the wave on the string sets its harmonic frequencies once the length is fixed. The speed of a transverse wave on a stretched string itself depends on the string's tension and its mass per unit length.

v = √(FT/μ)

v is the speed of the wave on the string, FT is the tension in the string, and μ is the mass per unit length of the string (its mass divided by its length). A higher tension increases the wave speed, while a greater mass per unit length decreases it – this is why a thick guitar string sounds a lower note than a thin one at the same tension, and why tightening a string (increasing FT) raises its pitch.

Worked example

A guitar string has a mass per unit length of 1.2 × 10−3 kg m−1 and is under a tension of 40 N. Calculate the speed of the wave on the string. v = √(FT/μ) = √(40 / 1.2 × 10−3) = √(33 333) = 183 m s−1. This wave speed, together with the vibrating length of the string, is what determines the frequencies of the harmonics the string can produce.

fn = nf1 = nv / (2L)

fn is the frequency of the nth harmonic, f1 is the frequency of the first harmonic, n is a positive integer (1, 2, 3, …), v is the wave speed on the string, and L is the vibrating length of the string. Because every harmonic on a string fixed at both ends is simply a whole-number multiple of the first harmonic frequency, all harmonics (1st, 2nd, 3rd, …) are possible.

Worked example

Using the same string as above (wave speed 183 m s−1), with a vibrating length of 63.5 cm, calculate the frequency of the first harmonic. f1 = v / (2L) = 183 / (2 × 0.635) = 183/1.27 = 144 Hz. This is the pitch of the note heard when the open string is plucked; pressing a fret shortens L, which raises f1 and therefore raises the pitch.

A string can also be made to sound one of its higher harmonics alone (a "harmonic" in guitar-playing terms) by lightly touching a finger at a node of that harmonic (for example, the midpoint, for the second harmonic) at the moment of plucking, then releasing the finger immediately afterwards so the string is left free to vibrate in that pattern.

Standing Waves in Pipes

A sound wave travelling along a pipe reflects off the end of the pipe, and the reflected wave superposes with the original wave to form a standing wave, in exactly the same way as on a string. A sound wave is a longitudinal pressure wave: the diagrams used to show it as a displacement-against-position graph make it look transverse, but the actual displacement of the air is along the length of the pipe, not perpendicular to it.

Boundary conditions in a pipeAt a closed end of a pipe, the air cannot move back and forth, so a node must always form there. At an open end of a pipe, the air is free to move with maximum amplitude, so an antinode always forms there.

Closed pipes

In a pipe closed at one end (and open at the other), there must be a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end. This boundary condition only allows a quarter wavelength, three-quarters of a wavelength, five-quarters of a wavelength, and so on, to fit along the length of the pipe, which restricts the possible standing waves to the odd harmonics only.

Figure C.4.4: Standing waves in a closed pipe of length LThe first harmonic has a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end, with only a quarter of a wavelength fitting inside the pipe, so λ = 4L and frequency f1. The next possible pattern (the third harmonic) fits three-quarters of a wavelength into the pipe (λ = 4L/3), giving frequency 3f1; the next after that is the fifth harmonic, with frequency 5f1. Even harmonics (2nd, 4th, …) cannot occur, since they would require an antinode rather than a node at the closed end.
fn = nv / (4L), for n = 1, 3, 5, …

fn is the frequency of the nth harmonic (n restricted to odd whole numbers), v is the speed of sound, and L is the length of the pipe. A frequency spectrum of a closed pipe therefore shows peaks only at f1, 3f1, 5f1, and so on, with no energy at the even multiples of f1.

Worked example

Calculate the first harmonic produced when a standing wave forms in a closed pipe of length 50 cm, given the speed of sound in air is 340 m s−1. f1 = v / (4L) = 340 / (4 × 0.50) = 340/2.0 = 170 Hz. The next possible resonant frequency of this pipe would be the third harmonic, 3 × 170 = 510 Hz, with no standing wave possible at 340 Hz (which would be the "missing" second harmonic).

Open pipes

In a pipe open at both ends, an antinode forms at each end. This boundary condition allows a half wavelength, a full wavelength, one and a half wavelengths, and so on, to fit along the pipe, so – unlike a closed pipe – all harmonics (1st, 2nd, 3rd, …) are possible, exactly as for a string fixed at both ends.

Figure C.4.7: Standing waves in an open pipe of length LThe first harmonic has an antinode at each open end and a single node in the middle, with half a wavelength fitting inside the pipe, so λ = 2L and frequency f1. The second harmonic has antinodes at both ends and two nodes inside, fitting a full wavelength into the pipe (λ = L), giving frequency 2f1. The third harmonic fits one and a half wavelengths into the pipe (λ = 2L/3), giving frequency 3f1.
fn = nv / (2L), for n = 1, 2, 3, …

fn is the frequency of the nth harmonic, v is the speed of sound, and L is the length of the pipe. This has exactly the same mathematical form as the equation for a string fixed at both ends, since both situations have the same type of boundary condition (a fixed feature – a node for the string, an antinode for the open pipe end – at each end).

Worked example

A pipe open at both ends produces a first-harmonic note of 512 Hz. Given the speed of sound in air is 340 m s−1, calculate the length of the pipe. Rearranging f1 = v/(2L): L = v / (2f1) = 340 / (2 × 512) = 340/1024 = 0.332 m. This shows why instruments designed to play high notes (small L) tend to be physically short, while instruments designed to play low notes (large L) tend to be long.

All wind instruments (flute, clarinet, trumpet, organ) rely on standing waves set up in a pipe or tube. Instruments differ mainly in how the air column is made to vibrate: a reed vibrates in a clarinet, the player's lips vibrate in a trumpet, and air is made to vibrate across a sharp edge in a flute. Opening or closing holes changes the effective length of the vibrating air column and so changes the note produced, while blowing harder tends to favour the higher harmonics, raising the pitch (and, if overdone, producing an unwanted high-pitched squeal, as on a poorly played recorder).

Resonance and Natural Frequency

Any system that is displaced from equilibrium and then released, such as a mass on a spring, a pendulum, or a stretched string, will oscillate at its own characteristic natural frequency, which depends only on the physical properties of the system (such as its mass and stiffness), not on how it was set into motion.

Natural frequencyThe natural frequency of a system is the frequency at which it oscillates when displaced and left to swing freely, with no external driving force other than the restoring force that produces the oscillation.
f0 = (1 / 2π) × √(k/m)

f0 is the natural frequency of a mass-spring system, k is the spring constant (a measure of the spring's stiffness), and m is the oscillating mass. A stiffer spring (larger k) or a smaller mass gives a higher natural frequency; this same general idea – that natural frequency depends on the physical properties of the oscillating system – applies just as much to a pendulum's length or a string's tension and mass per unit length.

Worked example

A mass of 0.20 kg is attached to a spring of spring constant 50 N m−1. Calculate its natural frequency of oscillation. f0 = (1/2π) × √(k/m) = (1/2π) × √(50/0.20) = (1/6.283) × √250 = 0.1592 × 15.81 = 2.5 Hz. A mass-spring system with these properties will always oscillate at this same 2.5 Hz whenever it is displaced and released, regardless of how large the initial displacement was.

A system can also be forced to oscillate at a frequency other than its natural frequency by applying a sinusoidally varying external (driving) force, for example by shaking one end of a string or vibrating a platform that a spring is mounted on. Energy is transferred continuously from the driver to the oscillating system, which then vibrates at the frequency of the driver, not at its own natural frequency.

Figure C.4.16: Amplitude of a driven oscillator vs driving frequencyA graph of the amplitude of a forced mass-spring oscillator (vertical axis) against the frequency of the driving force (horizontal axis) starts small at low driving frequency, rises to a sharp peak at a particular frequency, then falls away again at higher driving frequencies. This peak occurs exactly where the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the mass-spring system, illustrating resonance.
ResonanceResonance occurs when a system is driven at a frequency equal to its own natural frequency, causing the amplitude of oscillation to build up to a maximum value. Driving the same system at any other frequency produces a much smaller amplitude of oscillation.

Resonance is a widespread phenomenon, not limited to mechanical systems: examples include a truck driving past making plates rattle on a shelf, an opera singer shattering a wine glass by singing at its resonant frequency, infrared radiation resonating with the vibration of a carbon dioxide molecule, the air in a wind instrument resonating to produce its note, a quartz crystal resonating to keep time in a clock, an engine part failing because it resonates with the engine's running frequency, and a building collapsing because its natural frequency matches the frequency of an earthquake. An analogous resonance effect also occurs in electrical circuits containing a capacitor, resistor and coil, where the current becomes a maximum when the frequency of an applied alternating signal matches the circuit's own natural frequency – the basis of tuning a radio receiver to a particular station.

Damping

In practice, a real oscillating system loses energy to its surroundings (for example, to the fluid – air, water or oil – that surrounds it), so the amplitude of a freely oscillating system does not stay constant but gradually dies away. This loss of energy, and the resulting decrease in amplitude, is called damping.

DampingDamping is the loss of energy from an oscillating system, usually due to a resistive force (such as friction or fluid drag) that opposes the motion, causing the amplitude of oscillation to decrease over time.
Figure C.4.12 and C.4.13: Free and lightly damped oscillationA displacement-against-time graph for an undamped, freely oscillating mass on a spring is a constant-amplitude sine wave that continues indefinitely. Once damping (for example, from a surrounding fluid) is introduced, the same system instead produces a sine wave whose amplitude decreases smoothly over time, tracing out a decaying exponential envelope (shown as a dashed curve) around the oscillation; this is called light (or under-) damping.
x = x0 × e(−bt / 2m)

x0 is the initial amplitude, b is a constant describing the strength of the damping (relating the drag force to speed via Fd = bv), m is the oscillating mass, and t is the time elapsed. This expression describes the exponential envelope that the oscillation amplitude follows as it dies away; a larger value of b (stronger damping, for example from a more viscous fluid) makes the amplitude decrease more rapidly.

Worked example

A lightly damped oscillator has an initial amplitude of 5.0 cm, a mass of 0.20 kg, and a damping constant b of 0.40 kg s−1. Calculate its amplitude after 1.0 s. x = x0e(−bt/2m) = 5.0 × e(−0.40 × 1.0 / (2 × 0.20)) = 5.0 × e−1.0 = 5.0 × 0.368 = 1.8 cm. Just one second of light damping has already reduced the amplitude to roughly a third of its original value, showing how quickly the exponential decay envelope can act.

Light, critical and heavy dampingWith light damping, the system oscillates with an amplitude that decreases exponentially over many cycles before eventually coming to rest. With critical damping, the system returns to its equilibrium position in the shortest possible time without overshooting or oscillating past it at all. With heavy (over-) damping, the resistive force is so large that the system returns to equilibrium even more slowly than in the critical case, again without oscillating; if the damping is heavy enough, the system may not complete even a quarter of an oscillation.
Figure C.4.14: Critically damped and over-damped motionA displacement-against-time graph compares two non-oscillating returns to equilibrium after a system is released from a displaced position. The critically damped curve falls back to zero displacement the fastest, without crossing it. The over-damped curve also returns to zero displacement without crossing it, but takes noticeably longer to get there. A car's suspension system is designed to be critically damped, so that the car settles back down quickly after passing over a bump, without continuing to bounce.

Effect of damping on resonance

Damping also changes how a driven system responds near resonance. When a damped oscillator is driven, the energy supplied by the driving force must both maintain the oscillation and do work against the damping force, so the maximum amplitude that can be reached at resonance is reduced.

Figure C.4.17: Resonance curves with light and heavy dampingA graph of oscillation amplitude (vertical axis) against driving frequency (horizontal axis) shows two curves for the same system. The lightly damped curve has a tall, narrow peak, reaching a large maximum amplitude very close to the system's undamped natural frequency. The heavily damped curve has a shorter, broader peak, reaching a smaller maximum amplitude, and its peak occurs at a slightly lower frequency than the lightly damped case.

Increasing the amount of damping in a driven system therefore has two effects: it reduces the maximum amplitude that can be reached at resonance, and it slightly reduces the resonant frequency (the driving frequency at which the amplitude peaks) compared with the system's natural (undamped) frequency. The resonance peak also becomes broader and less sharply defined as damping increases, which is why, for example, reducing the resistance in a radio tuning circuit makes the circuit more selective (able to distinguish between stations of similar frequency), since a lightly damped circuit has a narrower resonance peak.
