Wavefronts and Rays

When a wave spreads out in two or three dimensions (rather than along a single line, as in a string), it is useful to describe it using wavefronts and rays rather than a single displacement graph.

WavefrontA wavefront is any line (or surface) joining points on a wave that are in phase – for example, the line running along a single wave crest. Wavefronts are always perpendicular to the direction in which the wave's energy is being transferred.
RayA ray is an arrow showing the direction in which a wave (and its energy) is travelling. A ray is always perpendicular to the wavefronts it crosses.

A point source, such as a stone dropped in a pond, produces circular (or spherical, in three dimensions) wavefronts spreading outward, with rays pointing radially outward from the source. If the source is very far away, or the wave has travelled a long distance, the wavefronts appear locally flat and parallel – these are called plane wavefronts, and the associated rays are parallel straight lines.

Figure C.3.1-2: Circular and plane wavefrontsRipples spreading out from a point where the water surface was disturbed form concentric circles (wavefronts), with rays drawn as arrows pointing outward from the centre, perpendicular to each circle. Far from the source, or for waves approaching a straight shoreline, the wavefronts become straight parallel lines and the rays become parallel arrows perpendicular to those lines.

Huygens' construction

Huygens' construction models a wavefront as being made up of an infinite number of new point sources of disturbance. Each of these point sources produces its own circular "wavelet" that spreads out in the direction of travel; the new wavefront a short time later is found by adding together (taking the envelope of) all of these wavelets. This simple geometric method correctly predicts both the law of reflection and Snell's law of refraction for waves.

Reflection of Waves

When a wavefront meets a barrier, each point of the barrier that the wave reaches acts as a new source of wavelets travelling back into the original medium (by Huygens' construction). Adding these wavelets together produces the reflected wavefront. For a circular wavefront reflecting off a straight barrier, the reflected wavefront is circular and appears to spread out from a point behind the barrier, as if a second source were located there. For a plane wavefront, the reflected wavefront is also planar and leaves the barrier at the same angle to the barrier as the incident wavefront arrived.

Law of reflectionThe angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, where both angles are measured between the ray and the normal (the line perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence).
Figure C.3.4-5, 14: Reflection of wavefronts and raysAn incident ray strikes a flat barrier and a reflected ray leaves the same point; both rays make equal angles with the normal drawn perpendicular to the barrier at that point. For water waves, a plane wavefront hitting a straight barrier at an angle reflects as a plane wavefront leaving at the same angle to the barrier, exactly analogous to a light ray reflecting off a flat mirror.

If a reflecting surface is rough or uneven, incoming parallel rays are reflected in many different directions (diffuse reflection); if the surface is smooth and flat, incoming parallel rays are all reflected in the same, predictable direction, which is why a flat mirror or a calm water surface produces a clear reflected image.

Refraction and Snell's Law

RefractionRefraction is the change of direction of a wave as it passes from one medium into another (or into a region where its speed changes), caused by a change in the wave's speed. The frequency of the wave does not change during refraction, but because v = fλ and f stays constant, a change in speed v must be accompanied by a change in wavelength λ.

For water waves, refraction happens because wave speed depends on water depth: waves slow down as they move into shallower water, causing the wavelength to shorten and the wavefronts to bend. For light, refraction happens when light crosses a boundary between transparent media in which it travels at different speeds (for example, air and glass).

Refractive index, nThe refractive index of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in that medium.
n = c / v

Here c is the speed of light in a vacuum (3.00 × 108 m s−1) and v is the speed of light in the medium. Refractive index has no units, since it is a ratio of two speeds. Since light always travels slower in a medium than in a vacuum, n is always greater than 1 for a transparent material; a medium with a higher refractive index is described as "optically denser" and slows light down more.

Worked example

Glass has a refractive index of n = 1.5. Rearranging n = c/v gives v = c/n = (3.00 × 108) / 1.5 = 2.00 × 108 m s−1. Light travels noticeably slower inside glass than it does in a vacuum or in air.

sinθ1 / sinθ2 = v1 / v2 = n2 / n1

This is Snell's law: θ1 is the angle of incidence and θ2 the angle of refraction, both measured between the ray and the normal to the boundary; v1 and v2 are the wave speeds in the first and second media; n1 and n2 are the corresponding refractive indices. The bigger the difference in refractive index between the two media, the more the ray is bent at the boundary.

Worked example

A water wave travelling at v1 = 20 m s−1 in deep water enters a shallow region where its speed is v2 = 15 m s−1, meeting the boundary at an angle of incidence of 50 degrees. Using Snell's law: sinθ2 = sinθ1 × (v2/v1) = sin(50°) × (15/20) = 0.766 × 0.75 = 0.575, so θ2 = 35°. The wave bends toward the normal as it slows down going into shallower water.

A second, equivalent worked example using light: a ray of light in air (n1 = 1) is incident on a glass block (n2 = 1.5) at 56 degrees. Since sinθ1/sinθ2 = n2/n1, sinθ2 = sin(56°)/1.5 = 0.829/1.5 = 0.55, giving θ2 = 34°: the ray bends toward the normal on entering the optically denser glass.

Figure C.3.6-8, 15: Refraction at a boundaryAn incident ray in air strikes the flat surface of a glass block at an angle to the normal; inside the glass, the refracted ray bends toward the normal (because glass is optically denser and slows the light down), so the angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence. When light instead passes from glass into air, it bends away from the normal.

Dispersion

The refractive index of a transparent medium depends slightly on the wavelength of light passing through it, so different colours of light are refracted by slightly different amounts. When white light enters a glass prism, blue light (shorter wavelength) is refracted more than red light (longer wavelength), spreading the white light out into a spectrum of colours. The same effect, occurring in raindrops, produces rainbows.

Critical Angle and Total Internal Reflection

When light travels from an optically denser medium into a less dense one (for example, from glass into air), it refracts away from the normal, so the angle of refraction is larger than the angle of incidence. As the angle of incidence is increased, the angle of refraction increases faster, until eventually the refracted ray lies exactly along the boundary itself (an angle of refraction of 90 degrees). The angle of incidence at which this occurs is called the critical angle, C.

sin C = n2 / n1

This follows from Snell's law by substituting θ1 = C and θ2 = 90° (so sinθ2 = 1). Here n1 is the refractive index of the denser medium the light starts in, and n2 is the refractive index of the less dense medium it is trying to enter (for light travelling from glass into air, n2 ≈ 1).

Worked example

For light travelling from glass (n1 = 1.5) into air (n2 = 1): sin C = n2/n1 = 1/1.5 = 0.667, so C = 42°. Any ray inside the glass striking the glass-air boundary at more than 42 degrees to the normal will not be refracted out into the air at all.

Total internal reflectionIf light inside a denser medium strikes a boundary with a less dense medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, all of the light is reflected back into the denser medium and none is transmitted. This differs from reflection off an ordinary mirror, where some light is always absorbed; in total internal reflection, 100% of the light energy is reflected.

Optical fibers exploit total internal reflection: a fiber is a very thin strand of glass or clear plastic, and light entering one end at a small angle strikes the side of the fiber at an angle greater than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected back and forth along the fiber until it emerges at the far end. Because the fiber's sides are parallel, the ray keeps meeting the side at the same angle each time, so it stays trapped and travels the length of the fiber with very little energy loss. This principle underlies fiber-optic communication.

Figure C.3.19-20: Total internal reflection in an optical fiberA ray of light entering the flat end of a thin glass fiber at a shallow angle strikes the curved side of the fiber at an angle greater than the critical angle, so instead of refracting out it reflects back into the fiber; because the sides of the fiber are effectively parallel, the ray continues to reflect back and forth at the same angle along the whole length of the fiber until it reaches the far end.

Diffraction

DiffractionDiffraction is the spreading out of a wave as it passes through a gap (aperture) or around an obstacle. It occurs for all types of wave and can be explained using Huygens' construction: within a narrow gap, the wavefront effectively reduces to a small number of new point sources, whose wavelets spread out in (nearly) all directions beyond the gap.

The amount of spreading depends on the size of the gap or obstacle relative to the wavelength of the wave. If the gap is much larger than the wavelength, the wave passes through with only a small amount of spreading at the edges of the beam. If the gap is comparable to or smaller than the wavelength, the wave spreads out strongly, behaving almost like a new point source radiating in a circle (or sphere) beyond the gap. Diffraction effects are therefore most noticeable when the size of the gap or obstacle is approximately equal to the wavelength of the wave.

Figure C.3.9-10: Diffraction through gaps and around obstaclesPlane water wavefronts approaching a gap in a barrier: through a wide gap, the wavefronts pass through mostly unchanged, curving only slightly at the two edges; through a narrow gap (comparable to the wavelength), the wavefronts emerging on the far side are almost perfectly circular, as though the gap itself were a new point source. Waves passing a small isolated obstacle bend around it and reunite on the far side, so the obstacle casts very little "shadow".

Diffraction Through a Single Slit

When light passes through a single narrow slit, it diffracts and produces a characteristic pattern on a screen: a wide, bright central band (the central maximum) flanked by a series of much dimmer and narrower secondary bright fringes, separated by dark fringes (minima) where the light waves from different parts of the slit interfere destructively.

sinθ = λ / b

This equation gives the angle θ (measured from the centre of the pattern) to the first minimum (the first dark fringe) either side of the central maximum, where λ is the wavelength of the light and b is the width of the slit. The narrower the slit (smaller b), the larger the angle to the first minimum, meaning the central bright band spreads out wider – a narrower slit diffracts light more strongly, consistent with the general rule that diffraction is greatest when the aperture size is close to the wavelength.

Worked example

Light of wavelength λ = 500 nm = 5.0 × 10−7 m passes through a slit of width b = 2.0 × 10−5 m (0.020 mm). The first minimum occurs at sinθ = λ/b = (5.0 × 10−7) / (2.0 × 10−5) = 0.025, so θ = 1.4°. Halving the slit width would double this angle, spreading the central maximum out twice as wide.

Single-slit diffraction intensity patternA graph of light intensity against angle (or position on the screen) shows a tall, wide central peak centred on zero, dropping to zero intensity at the first minima on either side, then rising again to much smaller secondary peaks (each roughly a fifth or less the height of the central peak) separated by further minima. The central maximum is about twice as wide (angularly) as each of the secondary maxima.

Superposition and Interference

SuperpositionWhen two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement at that point is the vector sum of the individual displacements of each wave. This is the principle of superposition, and it applies to any waves, not just light.
CoherenceFor two sources of waves to produce a stable, observable interference pattern, they must be coherent: they must have the same frequency, a similar amplitude, and a constant (unchanging) phase difference between them. In practice, coherent light sources are usually made by splitting light from a single source into two (using two slits, or a thin film), rather than using two separate lamps, since independent light sources are not coherent.

When two coherent waves of equal frequency meet, the resultant amplitude at any point depends on the phase difference between them there. If the waves arrive in phase, their displacements add to give a larger resultant amplitude (constructive interference). If they arrive exactly out of phase, their displacements cancel (destructive interference).

Figure C.3.11-13: Interference pattern from two point sourcesTwo identical point sources produce overlapping circular wavefronts on the surface of a ripple tank. Along certain lines radiating out from between the sources, the waves from each source always arrive in phase, producing a region of continuous large-amplitude oscillation (constructive interference); along other lines in between, the waves always arrive exactly out of phase, producing a region that stays almost undisturbed (destructive interference). Whether a point undergoes constructive or destructive interference depends on the difference between the distances travelled by the wave from each source to reach that point – the path difference.
Constructive interference: path difference = nλ
Destructive interference: path difference = (n + 0.5)λ

Here the path difference is the difference between the distances travelled by the two waves from their sources to the point in question, λ is the wavelength, and n is any whole number (n = 0, 1, 2, ...). A path difference of a whole number of wavelengths means the two waves arrive in step (constructive interference); a path difference of a whole number plus one-half wavelengths means they arrive exactly out of step (destructive interference).

Worked example

Two boys in a swimming pool, 10 m apart, make identical waves of wavelength 2 m travelling toward each other. Their sister swims between them; when she is 4 m from the first boy she is 10 − 4 = 6 m from the second boy, a path difference of 6 − 4 = 2 m. Since 2 m is exactly one whole wavelength (n = 1), the two waves arrive in phase at her position, so she experiences constructive interference (a big wave).

phase difference = (2π / λ) × path difference

This equation converts a path difference (a distance) into a phase difference (an angle, in radians): a path difference of one whole wavelength corresponds to a phase difference of 2π radians (360°), and a path difference of half a wavelength corresponds to a phase difference of π radians (180°), consistent with the constructive/destructive interference conditions above.

Worked example

Two waves of wavelength λ = 4.0 cm travel to a point with a path difference of 1.0 cm. The phase difference is (2π/4.0) × 1.0 = 0.5π radians = 90°. Since this is neither a whole number nor a whole-number-plus-a-half of wavelengths, the point experiences partial (neither fully constructive nor fully destructive) interference.

Young's Double-Slit Interference

Light from a single monochromatic source is directed at two parallel, very narrow slits close together. Because the slits are narrow, the light diffracts strongly as it passes through each one, so the light spreading out from the two slits overlaps in the region beyond them and interferes. Where this happens, a pattern of equally spaced bright and dark parallel lines, called fringes, appears on a screen: bright fringes where the light interferes constructively, dark fringes where it interferes destructively.

s = λD / d

Here s is the fringe spacing (the distance between adjacent bright fringes, or adjacent dark fringes, on the screen), λ is the wavelength of the light, D is the distance from the double slit to the screen, and d is the separation between the two slits. This formula assumes D is much larger than d, so that the angles involved are small. Making the slits closer together (smaller d) or moving the screen further away (larger D) increases the fringe spacing, making the pattern easier to see; increasing the wavelength also increases the fringe spacing.

Worked example

Two slits separated by d = 0.20 mm = 2.0 × 10−4 m are illuminated by light of wavelength λ = 500 nm = 5.0 × 10−7 m, and the screen is D = 2.0 m away. The fringe spacing is s = λD/d = (5.0 × 10−7 × 2.0) / (2.0 × 10−4) = 5.0 × 10−3 m = 5.0 mm. The bright fringes on the screen would be spaced 5.0 mm apart, easily visible to the naked eye.

Effect of changing each variable in s = λD/dIncreasing the wavelength λ (for example, switching from blue to red light) increases the fringe spacing, spreading the fringes further apart. Increasing the slit-to-screen distance D also increases the fringe spacing. Increasing the slit separation d has the opposite effect: it decreases the fringe spacing, squeezing the fringes closer together.

The Diffraction Grating

Diffraction gratingA diffraction grating is a plate with a very large number of equally spaced, extremely narrow parallel slits (often thousands per millimetre). Light passed through it diffracts at each slit and interferes, producing sharp, narrow, widely separated bright fringes (maxima) rather than the broader fringes seen in two-slit interference, because light from very many slits must interfere constructively for a bright line to appear at all.
d sinθ = nλ

Here d is the spacing between adjacent slits of the grating (equal to 1 divided by the number of slits per unit length, N), θ is the angle from the straight-through (undeviated) direction to the n-th order bright maximum, λ is the wavelength of the light, and n is a whole number (n = 0, 1, 2, ...) called the order of the maximum. The n = 0 maximum always appears straight ahead (undeviated); higher orders appear at increasing angles either side of it.

Worked example

A diffraction grating has 500 lines per millimetre, so the slit spacing is d = 1 / (500 × 1000 lines per metre) = 1/(5.0 × 105) = 2.0 × 10−6 m. For light of wavelength λ = 600 nm = 6.0 × 10−7 m, the first-order (n = 1) maximum occurs at sinθ = nλ/d = (6.0 × 10−7) / (2.0 × 10−6) = 0.30, so θ = 17.5°. A grating with more lines per millimetre (smaller d) spreads its diffraction orders out to larger angles.

Polarization

PolarizationA transverse wave oscillates in a plane perpendicular to its direction of travel; if it oscillates in only one fixed direction within that plane, it is described as plane-polarized. Only transverse waves can be polarized, since a longitudinal wave's oscillation direction is already fixed along its single direction of travel and has no other plane to restrict.

Light from most everyday sources (such as the Sun or a light bulb) is unpolarized: it is made up of waves oscillating in every possible direction perpendicular to the direction of travel. A polarizing filter (Polaroid) contains long-chain molecules aligned in one direction that absorb the component of the electric field oscillating parallel to those chains, transmitting only the component oscillating perpendicular to them. The result is that light leaving a single polarizing filter is plane-polarized, oscillating in only one direction, and its intensity is reduced (roughly by half, for initially unpolarized light).

I = I0 cos2θ

This is Malus's law, describing what happens when already plane-polarized light of intensity I0 passes through a second polarizing filter (an "analyser"). Here θ is the angle between the transmission axis of the analyser and the plane of polarization of the incoming light, and I is the intensity transmitted through the analyser. When the two axes are parallel (θ = 0), all of the polarized light is transmitted (I = I0); when they are perpendicular (θ = 90°), no light is transmitted (I = 0).

Worked example

Plane-polarized light of intensity I0 = 8.0 W m−2 is incident on an analyser whose transmission axis is at 30 degrees to the light's plane of polarization. The transmitted intensity is I = I0cos2θ = 8.0 × cos2(30°) = 8.0 × 0.75 = 6.0 W m−2. Rotating the analyser toward 90 degrees would smoothly reduce the transmitted intensity to zero.

Two polarizing filters in seriesUnpolarized light passes through a first polarizing filter and emerges plane-polarized, oscillating in a single fixed direction, with about half of the original intensity. This polarized light then meets a second filter (the analyser): if the analyser's axis is parallel to the light's polarization direction, nearly all of this light passes through; as the analyser is rotated, the transmitted intensity smoothly decreases following Malus's law, reaching zero when the axes are exactly perpendicular ("crossed polarizers").
