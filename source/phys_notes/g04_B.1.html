The particle model of matter

All matter is made of particles that are too small to see directly (atoms are around 10-10 m in diameter) but whose existence explains the macroscopic behavior of solids, liquids and gases. Atoms can be modeled as small, perfectly elastic spheres: when they collide, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.

Element and compound

There are 118 different types of atom. A material made of only one type of atom is called an element. When atoms of different elements join together they form molecules, and a material made of molecules containing more than one type of atom is called a compound.

Intermolecular forces and the three states of matter

Intermolecular force

The force between molecules that pulls them back together if they are pulled apart, and pushes them apart if they are pushed too close together. It arises from the electric charge of the particles (covered fully in B.5) and is responsible for tension in a stretched string and the normal reaction force between pushed-together surfaces.

Solid vs liquid vs gas

Going from solid to liquid to gas, the intermolecular forces get progressively less able to hold molecules in fixed positions. Water is an atypical example: ice is less dense than liquid water because the solid structure is more open, so ice floats.

Pressure and buoyancy in fluids

In a liquid or gas within a gravitational field, the weight of fluid above any depth compresses the fluid below it, so pressure (force per unit area) increases with depth. A submerged cube experiences a greater force pushing up on its lower face than pushing down on its upper face (because the lower face is deeper), giving a net upward buoyant force equal to the weight of fluid displaced. The same reasoning explains why atmospheric density and pressure are greatest near the ground: gas near the Earth's surface is compressed by the weight of the gas above it.

Brownian motion

Brownian motion

Evidence for the particle nature of matter observed by Robert Brown in 1827: pollen grains suspended in water, viewed under a microscope, move in a random, erratic zigzag path. This is explained by invisible water molecules constantly colliding with the pollen grains from random directions. Large objects do not show this jittering because they are bombarded evenly from all sides and the effects cancel out; only small enough particles show a visible net effect from moment to moment.

Density

Density links a macroscopic, measurable quantity (mass and volume) to a microscopic idea (how closely packed the particles are). Because it does not depend on the size or shape of a sample, density is a property of the material itself.

ρ = m / V

ρ is density in kg m-3, m is mass in kg, and V is volume in m3. A given material always has the same density regardless of how much of it there is or what shape it is formed into — this is why density can be used to identify or distinguish materials.

Worked example

A block of aluminum has a mass of 540 g and a volume of 200 cm3. Find its density.
Convert units: m = 0.540 kg, V = 200 × 10-6 m3 = 2.00 × 10-4 m3.
ρ = m / V = 0.540 / (2.00 × 10-4) = 2700 kg m-3.
This matches the known density of aluminum, confirming the block is a solid, closely packed metal rather than a porous or hollow object.

Internal energy

Internal energy

The total intermolecular potential energy arising from the forces between molecules, plus the total random kinetic energy of the molecules arising from their random motion. It is the sum of the energies of all the molecules in a body, not the kinetic or potential energy of the body as a whole.

Solids/liquids vs gases

In solids and liquids, intermolecular forces exist, so moving molecules apart requires work (like stretching a spring) — internal energy is the total kinetic plus potential energy of the molecules. In an (ideal) gas there is no force between molecules except during collisions, so changing their separation needs no work — internal energy is purely the total kinetic energy of the molecules.

This idea explains where "lost" mechanical energy goes when a body does not speed up or rise. A car moving at constant velocity does work against air resistance: the car collides with air molecules and gives them kinetic energy, increasing the internal energy of the air. Similarly, a block sliding down a slope at constant speed loses gravitational potential energy but gains no kinetic energy; friction between the rough surfaces transfers this energy into the internal energy (kinetic and potential) of the molecules in the block and slope, which is why rubbing your hands together or a car's brakes get hot. In both cases, mechanical energy that appears to "disappear" macroscopically has actually gone into the internal energy of the particles.

Temperature

Temperature

A measure of how hot or cold a body is. On a microscopic level, temperature (measured in kelvin) is directly related to the average random kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.

Defining the Celsius scale

To build a temperature scale, a directly measurable quantity that varies with temperature is needed, such as the length of a column of liquid in a thin tube. Two fixed points are chosen (the freezing and boiling points of pure water at normal atmospheric pressure), the tube is marked at the liquid length at each fixed point, and the interval between them is divided into 100 equal units. This defines the Celsius scale.

T = [(LT − L0) / (L100 − L0)] × 100

T is the unknown Celsius temperature, LT is the length of the thermometric liquid at that unknown temperature, L0 is its length at the ice point (0 °C) and L100 is its length at the steam point (100 °C). The fraction gives the proportion of the way the liquid column has expanded between the two fixed points, which is then converted into a percentage (out of 100 Celsius degrees).

Worked example

A mercury thread is 2.0 cm long at 0 °C and 22.0 cm long at 100 °C. What temperature corresponds to a thread length of 8.0 cm?
T = [(8.0 − 2.0) / (22.0 − 2.0)] × 100 = (6.0 / 20.0) × 100 = 30 °C.
So a length of 8.0 cm on this thermometer indicates a temperature of 30 °C.

The Kelvin scale and absolute zero

The average kinetic energy of particles is not zero at 0 °C, so Celsius temperature is not directly proportional to average kinetic energy. As temperature falls, particle motion slows until, at the lowest possible temperature (absolute zero, −273 °C), particles have (theoretically) zero kinetic energy. Using absolute zero as the origin of a new scale, together with the triple point of water (0.01 °C, defined as 273.16 on this new scale) as a single fixed point, defines the Kelvin scale. Because the size of one kelvin equals the size of one Celsius degree, a temperature change is numerically the same on both scales.

T (in K) = T (in °C) + 273

To convert a Celsius temperature to kelvin, add 273 (273.15 for more precision, though 273 is normally used). Because the unit size is identical on both scales, a change in temperature (ΔT) has the same numerical value in kelvin and in Celsius degrees.

Worked example

Convert 10 °C and 50 °C to kelvin, and find the temperature change both ways.
10 °C = 10 + 273 = 283 K. 50 °C = 50 + 273 = 323 K.
Change in Celsius: 50 − 10 = 40 °C. Change in kelvin: 323 − 283 = 40 K.
The temperature change is the same size (40) on both scales, even though the absolute readings differ by 273.

Pressure vs temperature graph

A graph of the pressure of a fixed volume of gas against temperature is a straight line. Plotted against Celsius temperature, the line crosses the temperature axis at −273 °C (extrapolated, since a real gas liquefies before this point). Plotted against kelvin temperature, the same line passes through the origin (0 K, zero pressure), showing that pressure (and hence average molecular kinetic energy) is directly proportional to absolute temperature. Both graphs have the same gradient, confirming that the kelvin and Celsius units are the same size.

Temperature and molecular kinetic energy

For an ideal gas, the average random kinetic energy of the molecules is directly proportional to the absolute (kelvin) temperature.

Ek = (3/2) kB T

Ek is the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule in joules, kB is the Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10-23 J K-1), and T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. This equation only works with T in kelvin, since it relies on average kinetic energy being zero at absolute zero.

Worked example

Find the average kinetic energy of a gas molecule at room temperature, 20 °C.
Convert to kelvin: T = 20 + 273 = 293 K.
Ek = (3/2)(1.38 × 10-23)(293) = 6.07 × 10-21 J.
This is an extremely small amount of energy for a single molecule, consistent with the huge number of molecules (around 1023) needed to make up any everyday quantity of internal energy.

Molecular speed distribution

Not all molecules in a gas move at the same speed. A graph of number of molecules against molecular speed rises from zero, reaches a peak, and then falls away with a long tail toward high speeds; it is not symmetrical, so the mean speed lies to the right of the most common (peak) speed. At a higher temperature, the whole curve shifts to the right (higher speeds) and flattens/spreads out, showing a higher average kinetic energy while the total number of molecules (area under the curve) stays the same.

Thermal energy and thermal equilibrium

Thermal energy (heat)

Energy transferred between bodies as a result of a temperature difference between them. The internal energy of a body can be increased either by doing work on it (e.g. friction) or by putting it in contact with a hotter body.

Thermal equilibrium

The state reached when two bodies in thermal contact are at the same temperature, so there is no net transfer of thermal energy between them. Thermal energy always flows from the higher-temperature body to the lower-temperature body, never the reverse, until thermal equilibrium is reached.

Heat flow to equilibrium

Two bodies at different temperatures placed in contact (e.g. one at 100 °C and one at 20 °C) show a net thermal energy flow from the hot body to the cold body. As energy transfers, the hot body cools and the cold body warms until both reach the same intermediate temperature, at which point thermal energy transfer stops (thermal equilibrium) even though the molecules are still colliding and exchanging energy back and forth at the microscopic level.

Conduction

Conduction

Thermal energy transfer that occurs when bodies (or parts of the same body) are in physical contact. Vibrating molecules in the hotter region collide with slower-moving molecules in the cooler region, transferring kinetic energy from fast to slow. Metals conduct particularly well because free electrons can move through the material and carry energy quickly; gases conduct poorly because their molecules are far apart and collide infrequently.

ΔQ / Δt = k A ΔT / Δx

ΔQ/Δt is the rate of thermal energy transfer (power, in watts), k is the thermal conductivity of the material (W K-1 m-1), A is the cross-sectional area through which the energy flows (m2), and ΔT/Δx is the temperature gradient: the temperature difference ΔT across a thickness Δx (K m-1). A larger area, a bigger temperature difference, a thinner material or a higher conductivity all increase the rate of conduction.

Worked example

A copper rod (k = 400 W K-1 m-1) is 2.0 m long with a cross-sectional area of 1.0 × 10-3 m2. One end is held at a temperature 50 K higher than the other. Find the rate of thermal energy conduction along the rod.
ΔQ/Δt = kAΔT/Δx = (400)(1.0 × 10-3)(50) / 2.0 = 10 W.
So 10 joules of thermal energy pass along the rod every second while this temperature difference is maintained.

Convection

Convection

The transfer of thermal energy through a fluid by the bulk movement of the fluid itself, driven by density differences. When a region of fluid is heated, its molecules move faster, the fluid expands, and it becomes less dense than its surroundings. The resulting buoyant force pushes the hot, less dense fluid upward, and cooler, denser fluid moves in to replace it, setting up a circulating convection current. This is a qualitative (not calculation-based) mechanism at SL.

Radiation

Thermal radiation

The transfer of thermal energy by electromagnetic waves (infrared radiation) emitted from the surface of a body. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation needs no medium and can travel through a vacuum. Dark, dull (matte) surfaces both emit and absorb radiation more effectively than light, shiny surfaces, which tend to reflect radiation instead.

Because trapped air is a poor conductor and cannot easily convect, fibrous insulating materials (which trap pockets of air) reduce conductive and convective heat loss; a reflective (e.g. silver) surface reduces radiative heat loss.

Black-body radiation

Black body

An idealized perfect emitter (and absorber) of thermal radiation. A black body's power output depends only on its surface area and its absolute temperature, not on the material it is made of.

Measuring radiated power vs temperature

In an experiment using a tungsten lightbulb filament, the filament's temperature is found from its electrical resistance (measured via voltage and current), while the power it radiates is measured using a thermopile connected to a voltmeter (the thermopile's potential difference is proportional to the power it absorbs per unit area). Plotting thermopile potential difference against filament temperature gives a curve, not a straight line; the curve fits the shape y = constant × x4, showing that radiated power per unit area is proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature.

P = σ A T4

P is the total power radiated (luminosity, in watts), σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant (5.67 × 10-8 W m-2 K-4), A is the surface area of the body (m2), and T is the absolute (kelvin) surface temperature. This is the Stefan-Boltzmann law for a perfect black-body radiator; because of the T4 dependence, a small increase in temperature causes a large increase in radiated power.

Worked example

A black spherical ball of radius 3.0 cm is at a temperature of 400 K. Calculate the total power it radiates.
Surface area: A = 4πr2 = 4π(0.030)2 = 1.13 × 10-2 m2.
T4 = 4004 = 2.56 × 1010 K4.
P = σAT4 = (5.67 × 10-8)(1.13 × 10-2)(2.56 × 1010) = 16.4 W.
The small warm sphere radiates about 16 W of thermal power in total, spread across the infrared and other wavelengths.

The black-body spectrum and Wien's displacement law

Black-body spectra at different temperatures

A graph of radiated intensity against wavelength for a black body is a smooth, single-peaked curve that rises from near zero at short wavelengths, reaches a maximum, then falls away gradually toward longer wavelengths. For a tungsten filament lamp, the peak actually lies in the infrared (invisible) region, which is why an incandescent bulb radiates more heat than light. As temperature increases: the peak wavelength shifts to shorter wavelengths (moves left), and the peak height increases while the whole curve rises, so the total area under the curve (total power emitted) increases sharply.

λmax T = 2.9 × 10-3 m K

λmax is the peak (most intensely emitted) wavelength in meters, T is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and 2.9 × 10-3 m K is Wien's constant (note the unit is meter-kelvin, not millikelvin). This is Wien's displacement law: peak wavelength and absolute temperature are inversely proportional, so hotter bodies radiate most strongly at shorter wavelengths.

Worked example

The surface of the Sun is at about 5800 K. Estimate the peak wavelength of its emitted spectrum.
λmax = (2.9 × 10-3) / T = (2.9 × 10-3) / 5800 = 5.0 × 10-7 m = 500 nm.
This wavelength (500 nm) is in the visible (green) part of the spectrum, which is consistent with the Sun appearing roughly white/yellow to our eyes.

The inverse square law and apparent brightness

Radiation emitted from a small (point-like) source spreads out equally in all directions, covering the surface of an ever-larger imaginary sphere as it travels outward. Since the same total power is spread over a larger area, the power received per unit area falls off with distance.

I = P / (4πr2)    (or, for a star: b = L / (4πd2))

I is the intensity (power per unit area) at distance r from a source of total power P; 4πr2 is the surface area of the sphere of radius r that the radiation has spread across. For astronomical bodies, the same relationship is written with apparent brightness b in place of intensity, luminosity L in place of total power, and distance d in place of r. Doubling the distance from a source reduces the received intensity to one quarter.

Worked example

The Sun has a luminosity of about 3.8 × 1026 W and the Earth is 1.5 × 1011 m away. Estimate the apparent brightness (intensity) of sunlight at the Earth.
4πd2 = 4π(1.5 × 1011)2 = 2.83 × 1023 m2.
b = L / (4πd2) = (3.8 × 1026) / (2.83 × 1023) = 1340 W m-2.
This is close to the accepted solar constant (about 1360 W m-2), showing the inverse square law correctly predicts how much solar power reaches each square meter of the Earth's surface (before atmospheric absorption).

Thermal capacity and specific heat capacity

Thermal capacity (C)

The amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of a particular body (of whatever size or material) by one unit (one kelvin or one Celsius degree). Units: J K-1 or J °C-1. Thermal capacity depends on the size of the object as well as what it is made of.

C = Q / ΔT

C is the thermal capacity of a specific body, Q is the heat added (J), and ΔT is the resulting temperature change. Rearranged, Q = CΔT gives the heat needed for a given temperature change of that particular object.

Worked example

A quantity of water has a thermal capacity of 5000 J K-1. How much heat is needed to raise its temperature from 20 °C to 100 °C?
Q = CΔT = 5000 × (100 − 20) = 5000 × 80 = 400 000 J = 400 kJ.
So 400 kJ of thermal energy must be supplied to heat this water through the full 80 °C rise.

Specific heat capacity (c)

The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a unit mass (1 kg) of a material by one unit (one kelvin or Celsius degree). Unlike thermal capacity, specific heat capacity depends only on the material, not on how much of it there is. Units: J kg-1 K-1 or J kg-1 °C-1.

Q = m c ΔT

Q is the heat added or removed (J), m is the mass of the substance (kg), c is its specific heat capacity, and ΔT is the temperature change. This is the key quantitative equation for calculating thermal energy transfers that change an object's temperature (without a change of state).

Worked example

The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J kg-1 K-1. How much heat is needed to heat 300 g of water from 20 °C to 60 °C?
Convert mass: m = 0.300 kg. ΔT = 60 − 20 = 40 K.
Q = mcΔT = 4200 × 0.300 × 40 = 50 400 J = 50.4 kJ.
About 50 kJ of energy is needed for this everyday-sized heating task.

Thermal capacity vs specific heat capacity

Thermal capacity (C, in J K-1) applies to one specific object and depends on both its mass and material. Specific heat capacity (c, in J kg-1 K-1) is a property of the material alone; for a particular object, C = mc. Water's unusually high specific heat capacity (4200 J kg-1 K-1) means it takes a lot of energy to heat, which is why water is useful for storing/transferring thermal energy but expensive to heat for a shower.

Phase change and specific latent heat

When a substance changes state (phase), its temperature stays constant throughout the change, provided no particles are added or removed, even though thermal energy is continuously being supplied or removed.

Molecular model of a phase change

In a solid, molecules vibrate about fixed positions with only kinetic energy. As energy is added and the solid melts, the molecules become free to move around (gaining potential energy, since work must be done against intermolecular forces to separate them) while their average kinetic energy — and hence temperature — stays the same. This is why energy added during melting or boiling seems to "disappear": it goes into intermolecular potential energy, not into raising temperature.

Boiling vs evaporation

Boiling occurs throughout the whole liquid, only at one specific temperature for a given pressure (e.g. 100 °C for water at atmospheric pressure). Evaporation occurs only at the free surface of a liquid and can happen at any temperature. During evaporation, the fastest-moving (most energetic) molecules escape from the surface, which lowers the average kinetic energy — and therefore the temperature — of the liquid left behind, producing a cooling effect (this is why sweating cools the body).

The rate of evaporation increases with: a larger free surface area (more molecules near the surface can escape); air movement across the surface (blowing away the "vapor cloud" of escaped molecules lets more escape); and higher temperature (molecules have more kinetic energy, so more can escape).

Specific latent heat (L)

The amount of heat required to change the state of a unit mass (1 kg) of a material, without any change in temperature. "Latent" means hidden, because this energy does not cause a temperature rise. Units: J kg-1. The specific latent heat of fusion applies to melting/freezing; the specific latent heat of vaporization applies to boiling/condensing.

L = Q / m

L is the specific latent heat (fusion or vaporization, as appropriate), Q is the heat needed to completely change the state of the sample, and m is the mass of the sample. Rearranged, Q = mL gives the total energy needed (or released) for a given mass to fully change state; the same equation is used whether energy is being absorbed (melting, boiling) or released (freezing, condensing).

Worked example

The specific latent heat of fusion of water is 3.35 × 105 J kg-1. How much energy is needed to change 500 g of ice at 0 °C into water at 0 °C?
Convert mass: m = 0.500 kg.
Q = mL = 0.500 × (3.35 × 105) = 1.675 × 105 J = 167.5 kJ.
This much energy must be added, at constant temperature, just to melt the ice — no further temperature rise occurs until all the ice has melted.

Graphical analysis of heating, and the method of mixtures

Temperature-time graph while heating

When a substance is heated at a constant rate, a graph of temperature against time is a straight line while the substance stays in one phase, with the line becoming flat (zero gradient, a plateau) during any phase change (e.g. while water boils at 100 °C), before rising again once the phase change is complete. If some heat is lost to the surroundings, the line curves rather than staying straight, with the gradient decreasing as the object gets hotter (since the hotter the object, the faster it loses energy to its cooler surroundings, so less of the supplied energy goes into raising its temperature).

P = m c × gradient

The gradient of a temperature-time graph (ΔT / Δt) is the rate of temperature rise. Combining this with Q = mcΔT and power = energy / time gives the power being delivered to the substance: P = mc × (gradient of the temperature-time graph). This lets you find the heating power from a graph, or predict how a graph's slope should look given a known heater power.

Worked example

1 kg of water in a kettle is heated from 20 °C to 60 °C in 240 s, giving a straight-line graph. Find the power delivered to the water (specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J kg-1 K-1).
Gradient = (60 − 20) / 240 = 40 / 240 = 0.167 °C s-1.
P = mc × gradient = 1 × 4200 × 0.167 = 700 W.
The heater is delivering thermal energy to the water at a rate of about 700 W.

The same reasoning links a graph's boiling-time plateau to specific latent heat: if the same 700 W heater keeps the water boiling for 480 s, the energy supplied during boiling is 700 × 480 = 3.36 × 105 J. Using the specific latent heat of vaporization of water (2.27 × 106 J kg-1), the mass turned to steam is (3.36 × 105) / (2.27 × 106) = 0.15 kg. This shows how a temperature-time graph can be used to find both specific heat capacity (from the sloped sections) and specific latent heat (from the flat sections), given a known heating power.

Method of mixtures

The method of mixtures is an experimental technique for measuring specific heat capacity or specific latent heat, based on conservation of energy: if no heat is lost to the surroundings, the thermal energy lost by a hotter substance equals the thermal energy gained by a cooler substance it is mixed or brought into contact with.

For specific heat capacity: a hot metal sample (heated to a known temperature, e.g. 100 °C in boiling water) is transferred quickly into an insulated container of cold water of known mass and temperature. The final (equilibrium) temperature is measured, and mcΔT for the metal is set equal to mcΔT for the water (whose specific heat capacity is already known) to find the unknown specific heat capacity of the metal.

For specific latent heat of vaporization: steam is passed into cold water until some condenses, raising the water's temperature. The energy released by the steam has two parts — the latent heat released as it condenses (mL) plus the heat released as the resulting hot water cools from 100 °C to the final temperature (mcΔT) — and this total is set equal to the heat gained by the original cold water (mcΔT), allowing L to be calculated. In both experiments, starting the cold substance an equal amount below room temperature and finishing an equal amount above it helps cancel out any heat exchanged with the surroundings.
