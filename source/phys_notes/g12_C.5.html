The Doppler Effect

Anyone who has stood near a busy road, or better still a race track, will have noticed that a fast-moving vehicle sounds different as it approaches compared with as it moves away: the pitch drops noticeably as the vehicle passes. This change in the observed frequency of a wave, caused by relative motion between the source of the wave and the observer, is called the Doppler effect. It occurs whenever there is relative motion between the source and the observer, whether the source is moving, the observer is moving, or both are moving.

Doppler effectThe Doppler effect is the change in the observed frequency (or wavelength) of a wave that occurs because of relative motion between the source of the wave and the observer.

Sound and light both show a Doppler effect, but the underlying reason is different for each, because sound requires a medium to travel through while light does not.

Doppler effect: sound vs lightFor sound, there is a definite reference frame to measure velocities against: the medium (usually air) that the sound travels through. Whether it is the source or the observer that is moving relative to this medium matters, and the two cases (moving source, moving observer) arise for physically different reasons, even though they produce similar effects. For light, no medium is needed for the wave to travel, so there is nothing to measure velocity relative to except the source and observer themselves; the size of the frequency shift depends only on the relative velocity between source and observer, and it makes no difference which of the two is described as "moving".

Doppler Effect for Sound: Moving Source

When a source of sound moves, each successive wave crest is emitted from a slightly different position than the one before it. Ahead of the moving source, this squashes the wave crests closer together, shortening the wavelength; behind the moving source, the wave crests are spread further apart, lengthening the wavelength.

Figure C.5.1: Wavefronts from a moving sound sourceA series of circular wavefronts is shown, each one centred on the position the source occupied at the instant it emitted that particular wavefront. Because the source itself is moving (say, to the right), the circles centred on its more recent positions are shifted in the direction of motion. This causes the wavefronts ahead of the source (to the right) to be crowded closely together, while the wavefronts behind the source (to the left) are spread further apart than they would be from a stationary source.

The speed of sound in the air is not affected by the motion of the source, since it depends only on the properties of the air itself. Because v = fλ and v stays the same, a shorter wavelength ahead of the source must correspond to a higher observed frequency, while the longer wavelength behind the source corresponds to a lower observed frequency. This is why an approaching source (such as an ambulance siren or a passing race car) sounds higher in pitch, and a receding source sounds lower in pitch, than the same source would sound if it were stationary.

Sonic boomIf a source moves through air at exactly the speed of sound, the wavefronts ahead of it cannot spread out fast enough to get away from the source, so they pile up on top of each other, forming a single, very high-amplitude shock wave. This is heard as a sonic boom, such as when an aircraft breaks the sound barrier.

Doppler Effect for Sound: Moving Observer

If instead the source stays still and the observer moves, the wavelength of the sound emitted by the source does not change at all – the wave crests are still spaced by the same, constant wavelength throughout the air. What changes is the speed of the sound relative to the moving observer, since the speed of sound is defined relative to the air (the medium), not relative to the observer.

Figure C.5.2: Moving observer, stationary sourceA stationary source produces wavefronts that are evenly spaced throughout the surrounding air, since the source itself is not moving; the wavelength is therefore constant in every direction. An observer moving toward the source travels into the oncoming wavefronts, encountering them more frequently than a stationary observer would (equivalent to a higher speed of sound relative to the observer); an observer moving away from the source encounters the wavefronts less frequently (equivalent to a lower speed of sound relative to the observer).

An observer moving toward a stationary source is, in effect, moving through the air toward the source, which increases the sound's velocity relative to the observer; this means wave crests arrive at the observer more frequently, so the observer measures a higher frequency than someone at rest. Conversely, an observer moving away from a stationary source experiences a lower speed of sound relative to themselves, so wave crests arrive less often, and a lower frequency is measured. In both cases the wavelength of the sound in the air is unchanged – only the rate at which wavefronts are encountered by the moving observer changes.

Doppler Effect for Electromagnetic Waves

The Doppler effect also applies to electromagnetic radiation, such as radio waves, microwaves and visible light. The physical explanation is different from the sound case, since light needs no medium and its speed in a vacuum is the same for every observer regardless of their motion; the frequency shift for light arises from relativistic effects rather than from a changing wave speed relative to a medium. Despite this difference in cause, when the relative speed v between source and observer is much smaller than the speed of light c, the size of the frequency (or wavelength) shift can be approximated by a simple formula.

Δf / f0 ≈ Δλ / λ ≈ v / c

Δf is the change in the observed frequency compared with the frequency f0 emitted by the source, Δλ is the corresponding change in wavelength, v is the relative speed between the source and the observer, and c is the speed of light in a vacuum (3.00 × 108 m s−1). This approximation is valid whenever v is much smaller than c, which is true for essentially all everyday sources of light and radio waves, and even for the majority of astronomical objects. Because it does not matter, for light, whether the source or the observer is described as moving, only their relative speed v appears in the formula.

Worked example

A police speed radar emits a microwave beam of wavelength 1.0 cm, which reflects off a car travelling at 150 km h−1 (42 m s−1). First find the frequency of the beam: f0 = c/λ = (3.00 × 108) / (1.0 × 10−2) = 3.0 × 1010 Hz. The frequency shift due to the car's motion is Δf = (v/c) × f0 = (42 / 3.00 × 108) × 3.0 × 1010 = 4.2 × 103 Hz. This shift then doubles, since the car (now acting as a moving source reflecting the beam) is also travelling toward the detector, giving a total received frequency shift of 8.4 × 103 Hz. The double shift is what allows the small motion of the car to produce a large enough frequency change for the radar device to measure accurately.

Redshift and Blueshift

When a source of light moves away from an observer, the light detected has a longer wavelength than the wavelength that was actually emitted. Since red light sits at the long-wavelength end of the visible spectrum, this stretching of the wavelength is called redshift. Conversely, a source moving toward an observer produces light detected with a shorter wavelength, shifted toward the blue end of the spectrum, called blueshift.

Redshift and blueshiftRedshift is an increase in the observed wavelength of light (a shift toward the red end of the spectrum), caused by a source moving away from the observer. Blueshift is a decrease in the observed wavelength of light (a shift toward the blue end of the spectrum), caused by a source moving toward the observer.
Figure C.5.3: Hydrogen spectral lines and redshiftThe full visible spectrum runs from short-wavelength blue light on one side to long-wavelength red light on the other. Hydrogen gas produces a characteristic pattern of narrow absorption or emission lines at specific, known wavelengths within this spectrum. When the same hydrogen spectral lines are measured coming from a star that is receding from the Earth, the entire pattern of lines is shifted, as a group, toward the red end of the spectrum compared with a stationary hydrogen source, while the spacing pattern between the lines themselves stays the same.

This effect is extremely useful in astronomy, because the light from stars and galaxies contains characteristic absorption lines produced by elements such as hydrogen, whose unshifted wavelengths are known precisely from laboratory measurements. By comparing the wavelength of a line as observed with its known laboratory wavelength, astronomers can measure how much the light has been shifted, and hence calculate the speed at which the star or galaxy is moving toward or away from the Earth.

v / c = Δλ / λem = z

v is the recessional velocity of the source (its speed moving away from the observer), c is the speed of light, Δλ is the observed change in wavelength, λem is the wavelength originally emitted by the source (its known, unshifted wavelength), and z is the redshift parameter, a dimensionless fractional increase in wavelength. Rearranging gives the recession speed directly: v = (Δλ / λem) × c.

Worked example

A spectral line from a distant galaxy that is normally emitted at 434.0 nm is observed to be redshifted to 479.8 nm. Calculate the recession speed of the galaxy. The change in wavelength is Δλ = 479.8 − 434.0 = 45.8 nm. Using v = (Δλ/λem) × c: v = (45.8 / 434.0) × 3.00 × 108 = 0.1055 × 3.00 × 108 = 3.17 × 107 m s−1. This galaxy is therefore receding from the Earth at just over 10% of the speed of light, which is why the v/c approximation used here remains valid.

Hubble's Law and the Expanding Universe

In the early 1900s, Vesto Slipher measured the line spectra of light from many distant galaxies and found that the spectral lines from almost all of them were shifted toward the red end of the spectrum. Interpreted as a Doppler shift, this meant that nearly all distant galaxies are moving away from the Earth. Edwin Hubble combined this information with independent measurements of how far away each galaxy was, and in 1929 published the discovery that a galaxy's recessional velocity (found from its redshift) is directly proportional to its distance from the Earth: the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away from us. This relationship, now known as Hubble's law, is some of the strongest evidence that the Universe is expanding.

Figure C.5.4: Recession speed against distance for galaxiesA scatter graph plots the measured recession speed of many galaxies (found from their redshift) on the vertical axis against their distance from the Earth on the horizontal axis. The points lie approximately along a single straight line passing through the origin, showing that recession speed increases in direct proportion to distance; the gradient of this best-fit line gives the Hubble constant.
v = H0d

v is the recessional velocity of a galaxy, d is its distance from the Earth, and H0 is the Hubble constant (the gradient of the recession speed against distance graph), with a currently accepted value of approximately 72 km s−1 Mpc−1 (where 1 Mpc, one megaparsec, is a large astronomical unit of distance). The Hubble constant gives a measure of the current rate of expansion of the Universe; it is called a "constant" because it is the same for every galaxy at the present time, although its value may not stay the same over the very long timescales of cosmic history, since the gravitational attraction between galaxies can act to slow the rate of expansion.

Worked example

Use Hubble's law to estimate the distance from the Earth to a galaxy with a measured recessional velocity of 150 km s−1, taking H0 = 72 km s−1 Mpc−1. Rearranging v = H0d: d = v/H0 = 150 / 72 = 2.1 Mpc. Measuring a galaxy's redshift (and hence its recession velocity) therefore gives astronomers an indirect way of estimating its distance, without needing to measure that distance directly.

Applications of the Doppler Effect

The Doppler effect for electromagnetic waves has a number of important practical uses, most of which rely on a double Doppler shift, since the wave undergoes two shifts in frequency: once when the moving object acts as a moving observer of the original signal, and again when the moving object re-emits or reflects the wave, now acting as a moving source.

Δf = 2fv / c

Δf is the total change in frequency measured back at the (stationary) transmitter/receiver, f is the frequency originally transmitted, v is the speed of the moving reflecting object (for example, a car), and c is the speed of the wave (the speed of light, for radar). The factor of 2 arises because the reflected wave experiences the Doppler shift twice over: once as the signal reaches the moving object, and again as the reflected signal returns from the moving object back to the receiver.

Worked example

A police radar transmits microwaves at a frequency of 40 GHz and measures a frequency shift of 9.5 kHz from a reflected signal. The speed limit is 28 m s−1. Is the car speeding? Rearranging Δf = 2fv/c: v = Δf × c / (2f) = (9.5 × 103 × 3.00 × 108) / (2 × 40 × 109) = (2.85 × 1012) / (8.0 × 1010) = 35.6 m s−1. Since 35.6 m s−1 is greater than the 28 m s−1 speed limit, the car is exceeding the speed limit.

Applications of the Doppler effectSpeed cameras and police radar use the double Doppler shift of reflected radio or microwave signals to calculate the speed of a moving vehicle. In medicine, the Doppler shift of ultrasound waves reflected from moving blood cells is used to measure the rate and direction of blood flow, again relying on a double shift (the blood cell first acts as a moving observer, then as a moving source as it reflects the wave). In cosmology, the redshift of light from stars and galaxies reveals their motion relative to the Earth, and underpins the evidence for the expansion of the Universe.
