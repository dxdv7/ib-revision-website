Nuclear fusion and energy release

Nuclear fusion

Nuclear fusion is the joining of two small (light) nuclei to form one larger nucleus. Because light nuclei sit on the steeply rising part of the binding energy per nucleon curve, the product nucleus has a greater binding energy per nucleon than the original nuclei, so it is more stable. The "missing" binding energy is released, usually as kinetic energy of the products and gamma radiation.

Binding energy per nucleon vs nucleon number curve

This graph has nucleon number, A, on the x-axis and average binding energy per nucleon (in MeV) on the y-axis. The curve rises very steeply for the lightest nuclei, reaches a broad maximum around iron (A of about 56), and then falls off slowly toward the heaviest nuclei such as uranium. Because the curve rises steeply on the left, fusing two very light nuclei (for example two hydrogen isotopes into helium) produces a large increase in binding energy per nucleon, releasing a large amount of energy relative to the mass involved. Fusing nuclei heavier than iron would require an input of energy rather than releasing any, since the curve is falling in that region. This is why iron marks the practical end point of energy-releasing fusion inside a star.

E = Δm × c2    (equivalently, in atomic mass units: E (MeV) = Δm (u) × 931.5)

E is the energy released by a nuclear reaction, Δm is the mass defect — the total mass of the products subtracted from the total mass of the reactants — and c is the speed of light in a vacuum. Because 1 u of mass is equivalent to 931.5 MeV of energy, it is often quicker to find Δm in atomic mass units and multiply directly by 931.5 rather than converting to kilograms first.

Worked example

Find the energy released in the fusion reaction 2H + 3H → 4He + 1n.

Adding the masses of the reactants and subtracting the total mass of the products gives a mass defect of Δm = 0.018883 u. Since 1 u is equivalent to 931.5 MeV, the energy released is E = 0.018883 × 931.5 = 17.6 MeV. This confirms that fusing deuterium and tritium releases a large amount of energy (about 17.6 MeV) from a tiny amount of mass, which is why fusion reactions are such a concentrated source of energy.

Conditions needed for fusion

Every nucleus carries a positive charge, so two nuclei repel each other electrostatically (the Coulomb barrier) as they approach. For fusion to occur, nuclei must be pushed close enough together that the much shorter-range strong nuclear force can take over and bind them. This requires very high temperature, so that nuclei have enough kinetic energy to overcome the repulsion, and very high density, so that collisions between nuclei happen often enough for fusion to occur at a significant rate. In the core of the Sun the temperature is around 1.3 × 107 K, and the density is also extremely high, which together sustain a steady rate of fusion.

The proton-proton chain (supplementary)

The raw source text gives an example fusion reaction but does not name the specific chain of reactions that powers hydrogen-burning stars like the Sun; this is added here since it directly underlies "fusion as a source of energy in stars." In the proton-proton chain, two protons (1H nuclei) fuse to form a deuterium nucleus, releasing a positron and a neutrino. The deuterium nucleus then fuses with another proton to form helium-3, releasing a gamma ray. Finally, two helium-3 nuclei fuse together to form one helium-4 nucleus, releasing two protons back into the plasma to continue the chain. The net effect is that four hydrogen-1 nuclei are converted into one helium-4 nucleus, with the mass defect released as energy (about 26.7 MeV in total, consistent with the same E = Δm c2 relationship used above).

Stellar equilibrium

Hydrostatic equilibrium

A star is a hot sphere of gas held together by its own gravity. As gas particles fall together under gravity, they lose gravitational potential energy and gain kinetic energy, heating the core until it becomes dense and hot enough for nuclear fusion to begin. The energy released by fusion radiates outward and creates an outward radiation (and gas) pressure that exactly balances the inward pull of gravity. A star in this balanced state is stable and is said to be in hydrostatic equilibrium; this is the state of every main sequence star, including the Sun.

Astronomical distances

Light year (ly) and astronomical unit (AU)

The metre is far too small a unit for astronomical distances, so two other units are commonly used. A light year is the distance travelled by light through a vacuum in one year: 1 ly = 9.46 × 1015 m. An astronomical unit is the average distance between the Earth and the Sun: 1 AU = 1.5 × 1011 m, and is most useful for distances within the Solar System.

Stellar parallax

Parallax is the apparent change in position of a nearby object relative to much more distant background objects as the observer's viewpoint moves. If you move your head sideways while looking at nearby and distant trees, the near tree appears to shift more against the distant background than a slightly farther tree does, while very distant trees barely move at all. Astronomers apply the same idea to stars: a telescope is pointed at a nearby star and lined up against very distant "fixed" background stars. Six months later, when the Earth has moved to the opposite side of its orbit (a baseline of 2 AU), the same measurement is repeated. The nearby star appears to have shifted slightly against the fixed background stars, and half of this total angular shift is called the parallax angle, p. Measuring six months apart, on opposite sides of the Sun, gives the largest possible baseline and therefore the largest, most measurable parallax angle.

d (parsec) = 1 ÷ p (arc second)

d is the distance to the star in parsecs (pc), and p is the parallax angle measured in arc seconds (there are 3600 arc seconds in one degree). A smaller parallax angle corresponds to a larger distance. One parsec is equivalent to 3.26 light years, and by definition is the distance at which a baseline of 1 AU subtends an angle of exactly 1 arc second.

Worked example

The star Mintaka has a measured parallax angle of 3.64 × 10−3 arc seconds. Find its distance from Earth in parsecs.

d = 1 ÷ p = 1 ÷ (3.64 × 10−3) = 275 pc (to 3 s.f.). This is about 895 light years, illustrating how even a very small parallax angle corresponds to an enormous real distance.

Limits of the parallax method

Stellar parallax can only be used for relatively nearby stars because the parallax angle becomes too small to measure accurately at large distances. A telescope on the Earth's surface can typically resolve angles down to about 0.01 arc seconds, while a space telescope such as Hubble can resolve down to about 0.001 arc seconds. This limits parallax measurements to distances of roughly 1 kiloparsec, which does not even reach beyond our own galaxy; other methods must be used for more distant stars.

Radiation from stars

Apparent brightness, b

The apparent brightness of a star is the power received per unit area, measured perpendicular to the direction the radiation is travelling, as observed from the Earth. It depends both on the star's actual power output and on its distance from the Earth. Its unit is W m−2.

Luminosity, L

The luminosity of a star is the total amount of energy it radiates per unit time in all directions — in other words, its total power output. Its unit is the watt (W). The Sun's luminosity is LSun = 3.84 × 1026 W, and the luminosity of other stars is often quoted as a multiple of this value.

b = L ÷ (4πd2)

b is the apparent brightness at distance d from a star of luminosity L. Since the star radiates energy equally in all directions, at distance d that power is spread evenly over the surface of an imaginary sphere of area 4πd2, so brightness falls off according to an inverse square law: doubling the distance to a star reduces its apparent brightness by a factor of four.

Worked example

The Sun has a luminosity of 3.839 × 1026 W and is 1.5 × 1011 m from the Earth. Calculate its apparent brightness at the Earth.

b = L ÷ (4πd2) = 3.839 × 1026 ÷ (4π × (1.5 × 1011)2) = 3.839 × 1026 ÷ (2.83 × 1023) = 1.4 × 103 W m−2. This matches the well-known "solar constant" — the intensity of sunlight actually measured at the top of the Earth's atmosphere.

Stars are almost perfect black-body radiators, so their intensity-vs-wavelength spectrum matches the standard black-body curve for their surface temperature. Hotter stars have their peak intensity at shorter wavelengths, which shifts their overall color toward blue; cooler stars peak at longer wavelengths and appear red.

λmax = (2.9 × 10−3 m K) ÷ T

This is the Wien displacement law. λmax is the wavelength (in metres) at which the star's black-body spectrum has its peak intensity, and T is the star's surface temperature in kelvin. Because the constant is fixed, measuring the peak wavelength of a star's spectrum is enough to calculate its surface temperature directly, without needing to measure the whole spectrum.

Worked example

The Sun's black-body spectrum peaks at a wavelength of 480 nm. Calculate the Sun's surface temperature.

Rearranging, T = (2.9 × 10−3) ÷ λmax = (2.9 × 10−3) ÷ (480 × 10−9) = 6000 K. This shows the Sun's surface is around 6000 K, consistent with its classification as a yellow-white star.

power per unit area = σT4

This is the Stefan-Boltzmann law applied to a star's surface. σ = 5.6 × 10−8 W m−2 K−4 is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, and T is the surface temperature in kelvin. Because power per unit area depends on the fourth power of temperature, even a modest increase in surface temperature causes a large increase in the power radiated from each square metre of a star's surface.

Worked example

Using the Sun's surface temperature of 6000 K (found above from Wien's law), calculate the power radiated per square metre of the Sun's surface.

power per unit area = σT4 = 5.6 × 10−8 × (6000)4 = 7.3 × 107 W m−2. Every square metre of the Sun's visible surface radiates about 73 million watts.

L = AσT4 = 4πR2σT4

L is the total luminosity of a star treated as a sphere of radius R and surface area A = 4πR2, radiating at surface temperature T. This combines the Stefan-Boltzmann law with the surface area of a sphere, and is the key equation linking a star's size, temperature and total power output — it is what allows radius to be found once luminosity and temperature are known.

Worked example

Using the Sun's radius of 7.0 × 108 m and its power output of 7.3 × 107 W m−2 (found above), calculate the Sun's luminosity.

Surface area A = 4πR2 = 4π × (7.0 × 108)2 = 6.2 × 1018 m2. Total luminosity L = A × (power per unit area) = 6.2 × 1018 × 7.3 × 107 = 4.5 × 1026 W. This is very close to the accepted solar luminosity of 3.84 × 1026 W, the small difference coming from rounding the intermediate values.

Absorption spectra and stellar composition

As black-body radiation from a star's hot interior passes outward through its cooler outer layers, atoms of different elements absorb light at very specific wavelengths, producing dark absorption lines superimposed on the otherwise continuous black-body spectrum. Because each element has a unique pattern of absorption lines, these lines reveal the chemical composition of the star's outer layers, which (since a star's layers mix continuously) is representative of the whole star. Studies of many stars' spectra show a broadly similar composition: about 72% hydrogen, 25% helium and 3% heavier elements by mass.

The Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram

Reading an HR diagram

A Hertzsprung-Russell diagram plots the luminosity of stars (y-axis, on a logarithmic scale, typically from about 10−4 LSun at the bottom to 106 LSun or more at the top) against surface temperature (x-axis, non-linear, running backwards from hottest on the left, around 40 000 K, to coolest on the right, around 2500 K). Every point represents one star. Because luminosity depends on both temperature and surface area (L = 4πR2σT4), diagonal lines running from lower-left to upper-right on the diagram represent constant stellar radius: a star in the upper right is cool but very luminous, which can only happen if it has an enormous surface area, so it must be a huge star; a star in the lower left is hot but faint, so it must be tiny. This means a star's approximate radius can be read directly from its position on the diagram.

When large numbers of real stars are plotted, they cluster into distinct regions rather than being spread evenly across the diagram. About 90% of stars, including the Sun, lie along a diagonal band called the main sequence, running from hot, luminous, blue stars in the upper left down to cool, faint, red stars in the lower right. Above and to the right of the main sequence lie the red giants (cool but very luminous, so very large) and, further up still, the rare supergiants (extremely luminous cool stars with radii up to 1000 times that of the Sun). Below and to the left of the main sequence lie the white dwarfs (hot but faint, so very small — only about Earth-sized). A narrow, roughly vertical band crossing the upper-middle of the diagram, where pulsating variable stars such as Cepheids are found, is called the instability strip.

R ÷ RSun = [(L ÷ LSun) ÷ (T ÷ TSun)4]0.5

This is a rearrangement of L = 4πR2σT4, applied to a star and to the Sun and then divided, so that the constants π and σ cancel. It lets you find a star's radius (as a multiple of the Sun's radius) directly from its luminosity and temperature relative to the Sun's, without needing to know σ or convert units — exactly the calculation used to work out where the diagonal constant-radius lines on an HR diagram come from.

Worked example

Vega has a luminosity of 1.54 × 1028 W and a surface temperature of 9600 K. The Sun has luminosity 3.85 × 1026 W and surface temperature 5800 K. Find Vega's radius in terms of solar radii.

L ÷ LSun = 1.54 × 1028 ÷ 3.85 × 1026 = 40.0. T ÷ TSun = 9600 ÷ 5800 = 1.655, so (T ÷ TSun)4 = 7.51. Then R ÷ RSun = (40.0 ÷ 7.51)0.5 = (5.33)0.5 = 2.3. Vega's radius is therefore about 2.3 times the Sun's radius, which places it well above the Sun on the main sequence portion of the HR diagram.

Main sequence vs. red giants vs. supergiants vs. white dwarfs

Main sequence stars are of ordinary size, still fusing hydrogen to helium in a stable core, and can range from hot blue giants to cool red dwarfs while remaining on the main sequence band. Red giants are cool (low surface temperature) but highly luminous, meaning they have expanded to a large radius (roughly 10 times the Sun's for a luminosity about 100 times the Sun's, at similar or lower temperature); their core has stopped fusing hydrogen and is contracting while an outer shell of hydrogen still burns. Supergiants are even more extreme versions of the same idea, with luminosities up to about 106 times the Sun's and radii up to about 1000 times the Sun's. White dwarfs are hot but faint, meaning they must be extremely small (roughly Earth-sized); they are the exposed, no-longer-fusing cores left behind after a Sun-like star sheds its outer layers.

Variable stars

A variable star has a luminosity that changes over time because its size is changing, so its position on the HR diagram is not fixed. Some variable stars, such as Cepheid variables, pulsate in a regular, cyclic way as they expand and contract, and are found within the instability strip of the HR diagram.

Stellar evolution

Birth of a star

Stars form inside nebulae — giant molecular clouds of mostly hydrogen gas and dust, at a temperature of only about 10 K, left over from earlier star and galaxy formation. Normally the cloud's gravity is balanced by the thermal pressure of gas particles moving randomly. If the cloud is disturbed, for example by a shock wave from a nearby exploding star or a collision between clouds, gravity can overcome this pressure in places and the cloud begins to collapse. Collapsing regions heat up and emit infrared radiation (detectable with IR telescopes, since the forming stars are still hidden inside the dust) and become protostars. Gravity continues to compress a protostar until its core becomes hot and dense enough for hydrogen fusion to begin; a star smaller than about 0.25 solar masses will never reach this point and instead cools into a failed star called a brown dwarf. Once fusion starts, the outward radiation pressure balances gravity and the star settles onto the main sequence, at a position determined by its mass.

Main sequence lifetime

A star remains stable on the main sequence for as long as it can sustain hydrogen fusion in its core. It is tempting to think that more massive stars, having more hydrogen fuel, should last longer, but the opposite is true: more massive stars are far more luminous, so they burn through their fuel supply much faster than the extra fuel can compensate for, giving them much shorter main sequence lifetimes than smaller stars.

Leaving the main sequence

A star leaves the main sequence once roughly 10% of its hydrogen (the amount in the core) has been converted to helium. As the fusion rate in the core drops, the core, no longer fully supported by radiation pressure, contracts and heats up further. This extra heat is transferred to the star's outer layers, causing them to expand and cool, turning the star into a red giant. Because only a small fraction of the star's total mass has changed, this shift happens without much change in the star's total mass.

Fate of Sun-sized stars

As a Sun-sized star's contracting core is compressed further, the core's electrons eventually resist being pushed any closer together; the core stops shrinking but keeps heating up. Once the core temperature exceeds about 108 K, helium itself begins to fuse: two helium-4 nuclei fuse to form beryllium-8, which can then fuse with another helium-4 to form carbon-12, and carbon-12 can further fuse with helium-4 to form oxygen-16. During this stage the outer layers are only loosely held by gravity and are gradually blown away into space, forming what is called a planetary nebula. Left behind is the exposed core, which can no longer generate energy and contracts until electron pressure again halts it; this hot, dense, Earth-sized remnant is a white dwarf.

Fate of large stars

In stars with mass greater than about 4 solar masses, the core becomes hot enough for carbon and oxygen to fuse further into heavier elements such as neon and magnesium. In the most massive stars, above about 8 solar masses, successive fusion stages continue, layer by layer, until the core is composed largely of iron. Because fusing iron into heavier elements absorbs energy rather than releasing it, the core can no longer generate the pressure needed to support itself once its fuel is exhausted, so it collapses catastrophically in a fraction of a second. This collapse squeezes electrons and protons together to form neutrons, and the resulting sudden halt sends a shock wave through the infalling outer layers, blasting them outward in a huge explosion called a type II supernova. What remains of the collapsed core becomes either a neutron star or, for the most massive cores, a black hole.

Low-mass vs. high-mass stellar evolution

Stars of roughly solar mass evolve gently: main sequence to red giant to planetary nebula (ejected outer layers) to white dwarf remnant, with no explosion. Massive stars (above about 8 solar masses) evolve much more violently: main sequence to (super)giant to a layered iron core, followed by sudden core collapse, a type II supernova explosion, and a neutron star or black hole remnant. In both cases, the star's mass is the single factor that determines which path is followed and how long each stage lasts.
