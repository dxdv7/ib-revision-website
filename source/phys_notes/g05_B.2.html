The Sun as a Black-Body Radiator

The Sun behaves like an ideal black-body radiator: a hot object that emits electromagnetic radiation across a continuous spectrum of wavelengths, with the shape of that spectrum depending only on the object's surface temperature. Two laws let us use the Sun's emission spectrum to work out its surface temperature and the total power it radiates.

Wien's displacement lawA hotter black body radiates most strongly at a shorter peak wavelength. The peak wavelength of the spectrum of light arriving at the edge of the Earth's atmosphere is about 500 nm, in the green part of the visible spectrum.
λmaxT = 2.898 × 10−3 m K

Here λmax is the peak wavelength of the black-body spectrum (in metres) and T is the absolute surface temperature of the radiator (in kelvin). Rearranging to T = (2.898 × 10−3) / λmax lets you find the surface temperature of any star from its measured spectrum.

Worked example

The Sun's spectrum peaks at λmax = 500 nm = 5.0 × 10−7 m. Using Wien's law: T = (2.898 × 10−3) / (5.0 × 10−7) = 5800 K. This tells us the surface of the Sun is at about 5800 K.

Stefan-Boltzmann lawThe total power radiated per unit surface area by a black body depends only on its absolute temperature, rising very steeply (as the fourth power of T).
P = σAT4

P is the total power radiated (in watts), A is the surface area of the radiator (in m2), T is the absolute temperature (in kelvin), and σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, 5.67 × 10−8 W m−2 K−4. Power per unit area is simply σT4.

Worked example

Using T = 5800 K for the Sun: power radiated per unit area = σT4 = 5.67 × 10−8 × 58004 = 6.42 × 107 W m−2. The Sun's radius is 6.9 × 108 m, so its surface area is 4πr2 = 6.0 × 1018 m2. Total power radiated = 6.42 × 107 × 6.0 × 1018 = 3.9 × 1026 W. This is the Sun's luminosity, L, the total power it emits in every direction.

The solar constant, SAs the Sun's radiation spreads out into space it obeys an inverse square relationship, so the intensity (power per unit area, also called apparent brightness) falls off with the square of the distance from the Sun. The intensity of solar radiation arriving at the Earth's distance from the Sun is called the solar constant.
S = P / (4πd2)

S is the solar constant (intensity at distance d, in W m−2), P is the total power radiated by the Sun, and d is the distance from the Sun. The factor 4πd2 is the surface area of an imaginary sphere of radius d centred on the Sun, over which the Sun's total power is spread evenly.

Worked example

The Earth is 1.5 × 1011 m from the Sun. S = P / (4πd2) = (3.9 × 1026) / (4π(1.5 × 1011)2) = 1400 W m−2. This value, the solar constant, is the intensity of sunlight arriving at the top of the Earth's atmosphere on a surface held perpendicular to the Sun's rays.

Interaction Between Solar Radiation and the Atmosphere

Electromagnetic radiation spreads out as a wave in three dimensions, with peaks and troughs in electromagnetic fields travelling through a vacuum at speed v. The distance between successive peaks is the wavelength λ, and the number of oscillations per second is the frequency f, related by v = fλ. Solar radiation reaching the Earth spans three main regions, in order of decreasing frequency (increasing wavelength): ultraviolet, visible and infrared. Each interacts with the gases of the atmosphere in a different way.

Figure B.2.2: The electromagnetic spectrumA horizontal scale of wavelength from 0.01 nm up to 10 cm, divided into named regions in order of increasing wavelength: gamma rays, X-rays, ultraviolet, visible light (roughly 400-700 nm, itself a small band within the spectrum), near infrared, thermal infrared, microwaves and radio waves. Each region is associated with a different mode of production and different practical uses.

Ultraviolet radiation and ozone

Ultraviolet radiation carries the highest photon energy of the three regions, high enough to split molecules apart. Oxygen molecules (O2) in the upper atmosphere absorb ultraviolet radiation and split into two separate oxygen atoms (2O). These atoms can then combine with further O2 molecules to form ozone (O3), forming a thin layer high in the atmosphere. This ozone layer itself absorbs ultraviolet radiation as it splits back into O2 and O, so most of the higher-energy ultraviolet radiation from the Sun never reaches the ground.

Figure B.2.3: Absorption of electromagnetic radiation by ozoneA graph of percentage absorption (0-100%) against wavelength, spanning the UV, visible and IR regions. Absorption is close to 100% throughout most of the ultraviolet region, then drops sharply to almost 0% across the whole visible region and remains low through much of the infrared, showing that ozone is a highly effective, almost total absorber of ultraviolet radiation but is essentially transparent to visible light.

Visible light

Visible light excites atoms, meaning the atoms absorb the light's energy; an excited atom is unstable and re-emits the light a short time later, but not necessarily in the same direction as the original ray. You might expect the atmosphere to absorb all visible light, making it opaque, but atoms can only be excited by certain specific (discrete) energies, so only a few particular wavelengths are absorbed while the rest pass straight through – this is why the atmosphere is transparent to visible light. The wavelengths that are absorbed and re-emitted in a different direction show up as narrow dark lines in the spectrum of sunlight received at the Earth's surface.

Infrared radiation and greenhouse gases

Infrared radiation is produced by the vibration of molecules, so it is associated with hot bodies: it is the radiation felt from a fire and detected by night-vision cameras. When infrared radiation is absorbed by a molecule, it makes that molecule vibrate more, which is why standing in front of a fire makes you feel hot. Certain molecules present in the atmosphere – carbon dioxide (CO2), water vapour (H2O), nitrous oxide (N2O) and methane (CH4) – can be made to vibrate by infrared radiation, but each only absorbs the specific frequency of radiation that matches its own natural (resonant) frequency of vibration.

Resonance model of greenhouse gas absorptionA greenhouse gas molecule absorbs infrared radiation strongly only at its own natural frequency of vibration, a phenomenon called resonance – similar to how pushing a child on a swing at exactly the swing's natural frequency transfers the maximum possible energy from the pusher to the swing. Once a molecule starts to vibrate from absorbing infrared radiation, it re-emits that radiation in a random direction (including back out towards space or back down towards Earth) a short time later.

Absorption is near zero through the UV and visible regions, then shows three distinct, narrow peaks of high absorption within the infrared region, each peak corresponding to a different mode of vibration of the CO2 molecule.
Figure B.2.5: Absorption of electromagnetic radiation by waterA graph of percentage absorption against wavelength across the UV, visible and IR regions, similar in form to the CO2 graph but with broader and more numerous absorption bands spread across the infrared region. Although CO2 is the most talked-about greenhouse gas, water vapour has the greatest overall warming effect, both because of these broader absorption characteristics and because it is far more abundant in the atmosphere.
Figure B.2.6: Spectrum of light reaching the Earth's surfaceA graph of intensity against wavelength (0-3000 nm) showing two curves: the smooth spectrum arriving at the top of the atmosphere, and a second, heavily notched curve for the spectrum arriving at the Earth's surface, which dips sharply at wavelengths where ozone, water vapour and other atmospheric gases have absorbed the radiation. The actual amount of energy absorbed varies with latitude, since sunlight must pass through a greater thickness of atmosphere to reach the surface near the poles than near the equator.

Interaction Between Light and Solids: Albedo

When radiation reaches the surface of the Earth, some is reflected and some is absorbed. Because the molecules in a solid are packed much closer together than in a gas, solids have electron energy bands rather than the sharp, discrete energy levels of an isolated atom, so solids can absorb and emit a much wider range of wavelengths than a low-pressure gas. When electromagnetic radiation hits a solid surface there are three possibilities: it can be reflected or scattered straight back out; it can be absorbed, transferring energy to electrons and then to the kinetic energy of molecules, raising the temperature; or it can pass through the material without being absorbed at all. For the surface of the Earth the third option can be ignored, since no radiation passes through the solid ground. Whether a given wavelength is absorbed or reflected depends on the material of the surface, which is why different materials appear as different colours: a red object reflects red photons but absorbs the rest, a white object reflects all colours, and a black object reflects almost none.

AlbedoAlbedo is a measure of the average fraction of incident power that a surface (or the whole planet) scatters back rather than absorbing.
albedo = total scattered power / total incident power

Albedo is a ratio with no units, and always lies between 0 (a perfectly absorbing black surface) and 1 (a perfectly reflecting surface). Multiplying albedo by the incident power gives the reflected power; multiplying (1 − albedo) by the incident power gives the power actually absorbed.

Worked example

Asphalt has an albedo of 0.04. If 1000 W of sunlight is incident on a 5 m2 patch of asphalt road, the reflected power = albedo × incident power = 0.04 × 1000 = 40 W, so 960 W is absorbed and available to heat the road surface.

Typical albedo values
Snow0.9
Average albedo of the whole Earth (including clouds)0.3
Asphalt0.04

Different surfaces have very different albedos – snow reflects almost all incident light (albedo 0.9), while dark asphalt reflects very little (albedo 0.04). When the whole planet is considered, the light scattered by clouds must also be included, giving the Earth an average albedo of about 0.3. Earth's albedo is not fixed: it varies daily and depends on cloud cover and on latitude (since ice and snow cover, which have high albedo, are concentrated at high latitudes).

Emissivity and the Greenhouse Effect

Emissivity, eNot every body is a perfect black-body radiator, so most emit less radiation than an ideal black body at the same temperature would. Emissivity is the ratio of the power actually radiated per unit area by a surface to the power per unit area that would be radiated by an ideal black body at that same temperature.
e = (power radiated per unit area) / σT4

Rearranged, the power radiated by a real surface of area A and emissivity e at temperature T is P = eσAT4. A perfect black body has emissivity e = 1; every other material has emissivity less than 1. Emissivity has no units, since it is a ratio.

Worked example

Dull black paper has an emissivity of 0.94. At a temperature of T = 400 K, the power radiated per unit area = eσT4 = 0.94 × 5.67 × 10−8 × 4004 = 1.37 × 103 W m−2, noticeably less than the 1.45 × 103 W m−2 a perfect black body at 400 K would radiate.

How the greenhouse effect arises

Because the atmosphere does not absorb much visible light, most visible light reflected from the Earth's surface passes straight back out through the atmosphere into space. The light that is absorbed at the surface raises the temperature of the ground, which then re-emits radiation at a peak wavelength given by Wien's displacement law. Because the Earth's surface is not very hot, this peak lies in the infrared region – exactly the wavelength range that carbon dioxide, water vapour, nitrous oxide and methane molecules in the atmosphere absorb strongly. These molecules then re-emit that infrared radiation in random directions, so some of it returns back down to the Earth's surface rather than escaping to space. The net effect is that less energy leaves the Earth-atmosphere system than would leave a bare black body at the same surface temperature, which is equivalent to saying the system has a reduced overall emissivity. This warming effect is called the greenhouse effect, named for its similarity to the way glass traps thermal energy inside a greenhouse, and the gases responsible are called greenhouse gases.

Figure B.2.7: The Earth viewed from the SunTo an imaginary observer positioned on the Sun, the Earth appears as a flat circle (disk) rather than a sphere, because the Sun's rays all arrive from essentially one direction. This is why the power absorbed by the Earth is calculated using the cross-sectional (disk) area πr2, even though the Earth radiates energy from its full spherical surface area 4πr2 in all directions.

Energy Balance of the Earth

Imagine building a simplified solar system by placing a Sun at 5800 K and a planet at 0 K in orbit at the Earth's actual distance. The planet would absorb energy from the Sun and its temperature would rise; as its temperature rose above 0 K it would begin radiating energy itself, and the rate of radiated energy would increase until it exactly balanced the rate of absorbed energy. At that point the temperature stops changing: this is thermal equilibrium.

Earth without an atmosphere

As a simplified first model, treat the Earth as a perfectly black sphere with no atmosphere. Because the Sun's rays all arrive from one direction, the Earth absorbs energy as if it were a flat disk of radius r facing the Sun, but it radiates energy from its entire spherical surface in all directions.

Sπr2 = σT4 × 4πr2

The left-hand side is the total power absorbed (solar constant S multiplied by the Earth's cross-sectional disk area πr2); the right-hand side is the total power radiated by a black body of radius r at equilibrium temperature T, using the full sphere's surface area 4πr2. Setting these equal and solving for T gives the equilibrium surface temperature of an airless, black Earth.

Worked example

With S = 1400 W m−2 and Earth's radius r = 6.4 × 106 m: incident power = 1400 × π × (6.4 × 106)2 = 1.8 × 1017 W. Radiated power = σT4 × 4πr2 = 2.9 × 107 × T4 W. Setting incident power = radiated power: 1.8 × 1017 = 2.9 × 107 × T4, so T4 = 6.21 × 109, giving T = 280 K. A completely black, airless Earth at Earth's distance from the Sun would settle at an equilibrium temperature of about 280 K.

Earth with an atmosphere

This simple model is unrealistic because not all incoming radiation is absorbed by the ground: the Earth's average albedo of 0.3 means clouds and the surface together scatter 30% of incoming radiation straight back to space, so only 70% is actually absorbed. At the same time, the greenhouse gases re-radiate some of the outgoing infrared radiation back down to the surface, which is equivalent to the Earth-atmosphere system having a reduced overall emissivity of around 0.6 rather than 1.

(1 − albedo) × Sπr2 = eσT4 × 4πr2

The left-hand side is now reduced by the factor (1 − albedo), since only this fraction of incident power is actually absorbed rather than reflected; the right-hand side is reduced by the effective emissivity e, since the greenhouse effect prevents the system from radiating as efficiently as a perfect black body. This equation shows that albedo and emissivity act as two competing effects on climate: clouds raising the albedo reduce the power reaching the Earth (a cooling effect), while greenhouse gases lowering the emissivity trap more outgoing radiation (a warming effect).

Worked example

With albedo = 0.3, the power absorbed = 0.7 × 1.8 × 1017 = 1.26 × 1017 W. With emissivity e = 0.6, the power radiated = 0.6 × 2.9 × 107 × T4 = 1.74 × 107 × T4 W. Setting these equal: 1.26 × 1017 = 1.74 × 107 × T4, giving T4 = 7.24 × 109 and T = 292 K. Including the atmosphere's albedo and greenhouse effect together raises the Earth's equilibrium surface temperature from 280 K to about 292 K (19°C), warm enough to sustain liquid water and life.

Albedo versus emissivity: opposite effects on temperatureA higher albedo means more incoming sunlight is reflected away before it can be absorbed, which lowers the equilibrium temperature. A lower emissivity means the Earth-atmosphere system radiates less efficiently for a given temperature, so it must reach a higher temperature before outgoing and incoming power balance – this raises the equilibrium temperature. Clouds tend to increase albedo (cooling), while greenhouse gases tend to decrease effective emissivity (warming); real climate outcomes depend on the balance between these two competing effects.
Figure B.2.8 and B.2.9: The Earth's energy budgetSimplified diagrams of the flow of energy (in W m−2) between the Sun, the atmosphere, and the Earth's surface. Incoming solar radiation arrives at the top of the atmosphere; some is reflected straight back to space, and the rest is either absorbed directly by the atmosphere or passes through to be absorbed at the surface. The more detailed version shows that the atmosphere gains energy in two separate ways: about 358 W m−2 is absorbed directly by greenhouse gases from outgoing infrared radiation, and a further 105 W m−2 arrives at the atmosphere through convection currents and the latent heat released when surface water evaporates into water vapour. The atmosphere then re-radiates part of this absorbed energy back down towards the surface, closing the energy balance.

Modelling the Earth's Temperature

Because it is not possible to run a real experiment on the whole Earth's atmosphere to see what happens if albedo or greenhouse gas levels change, scientists instead build computer models (such as a spreadsheet simulation) to predict how the Earth's average temperature would evolve over time from a set of starting conditions and known physical relationships. Such a model starts with values for the solar constant, albedo, emissivity and an initial temperature, then repeatedly calculates, for each small time step: the energy arriving (solar constant × (1 − albedo) × time interval), the power radiated at the current temperature (emissivity × σ × T4), the resulting energy lost, the net energy added (energy in minus energy out), the resulting temperature change (using the surface heat capacity, which relates energy added per unit area to temperature change in a way similar to specific heat capacity), and finally the new temperature for the next step.

Figure B.2.10: Graph of temperature against time from the spreadsheet modelA graph with time (in years) on the horizontal axis and temperature (in kelvin) on the vertical axis. Starting from a low initial temperature, the curve rises steeply at first and then levels off, approaching the equilibrium temperature predicted by the energy balance equation as time increases – this is the characteristic shape of a system approaching thermal equilibrium.

Such a simulation is a much simplified version of reality and should not be taken as a literal prediction, but it captures the underlying physics correctly: if the energy absorbed is not balanced by the energy radiated, the Earth's average temperature will keep changing until a new equilibrium is reached. Far more complex computer climate models, incorporating many more variables, are used by climate scientists to produce more realistic predictions.

Global Warming and the Enhanced Greenhouse Effect

Global warming is a rise in the Earth's average surface temperature. From the energy balance equation, this can be caused by a change in any of four factors: an increase in the solar constant, a reduction in albedo, a reduction in emissivity, or an increase in the quantity of greenhouse gases (which itself further reduces emissivity).

Changes in the solar constant

The energy reaching the Earth depends on both the power output of the Sun and the Earth-Sun distance, and neither is perfectly constant. The Earth's orbit is elliptical, so the orbital radius varies; the tilt of the Earth's axis relative to its orbital plane also changes slowly over long timescales, in patterns called Milankovitch cycles. On the Sun's side, the number of visible sunspots follows an 11-year cycle and gives an indication of the Sun's energy output: more sunspots corresponds to more energy being emitted overall, because it is the swirling gas around each sunspot (not the (relatively cooler) sunspot itself) that radiates the extra energy, sometimes as dramatic solar flares.

Reduced albedo

If the Earth's albedo falls, more incoming radiation is absorbed rather than reflected, raising the equilibrium temperature. Snow and ice have a high albedo, so a reduction in the extent of snow cover at the poles and on glaciers (itself often a consequence of earlier warming) reduces the Earth's overall albedo, creating a self-reinforcing (positive feedback) effect.

Reduced emissivity and the enhanced greenhouse effect

Reduced emissivity is directly linked to the greenhouse effect: increasing the concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere reduces the effective emissivity further, so the Earth must reach a higher equilibrium temperature before incoming and outgoing power balance again. There is extensive evidence that burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide faster than it can be removed by living trees, increasing the percentage of CO2 in the atmosphere. This human-caused strengthening of the natural greenhouse effect is called the enhanced greenhouse effect: more infrared radiation is absorbed and re-radiated back to the surface, so more must ultimately be emitted, which requires a higher average surface temperature. Deforestation worsens this effect, since fewer trees remain available to absorb and store atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Enhanced greenhouse effectThe augmentation (strengthening) of the natural greenhouse effect caused by human activities, principally the burning of fossil fuels and deforestation, which increase the concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere beyond natural levels.

Reducing the Enhanced Greenhouse Effect

Because global warming is an international problem, tackling it requires an internationally coordinated solution; the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) was established in 1988 by the United Nations Environmental Programme and the World Meteorological Organization to give the world's decision-makers a clear, agreed scientific summary of the state of knowledge on climate change and its environmental and socio-economic impacts. Several practical strategies can reduce the rate at which greenhouse gas levels rise:

StrategyHow it helps
Greater efficiency of power productionMore efficient power plants (though never 100% efficient, per the second law of thermodynamics) need less fuel to generate the same power, cutting CO2 emissions per unit of electricity produced.
Replacing coal and oil with natural gasGas-fired power stations are more efficient than coal or oil stations and release less CO2 per unit of energy generated.
Combined heat and power (CHP) systemsUsing the excess thermal energy from power stations (that would otherwise be wasted) to heat nearby homes makes overall fuel use more efficient.
Renewable energy and nuclear powerReplacing fossil-fuel power stations with wave, solar and wind power, or with nuclear power, reduces CO2 emissions from electricity generation.
Electric vehiclesSince much oil use is for transport, switching cars (and eventually aircraft) to electricity or alternative fuels reduces reliance on burning oil.
Carbon capture and storageCO2 can be removed from the waste gases of power stations and stored underground rather than released to the atmosphere.
