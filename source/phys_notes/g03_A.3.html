Work Done by a Force

WorkWork is done when the point of application of a force moves in the direction of the force. Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force. The unit of work is the newton metre (N m), which is the same as the joule (J). Work is a scalar quantity.

When one body pushes another, it does work on it, and this is what allows the second body's velocity (or position, or state) to change. Two blocks pulled by the same force gain different velocities if the force acts over different distances – the block pulled over the longer distance has had more work done on it.

work done = F × s

Here F is the constant force (in newtons) and s is the distance moved in the direction of that force (in metres). This simple form applies only when the force and the displacement point in exactly the same direction.

Worked example

A tractor pulls a felled tree along the ground for a distance of 200 m, exerting a constant force of 5000 N in the direction of motion. Work done = force × distance = 5000 × 200 = 1,000,000 J = 1 MJ. This is the energy transferred from the tractor's engine to the tree (and the ground) as it is dragged along.

Work done at an angle

W = Fs cosθ

Here θ is the angle between the force F and the displacement s. Only the component of the force along the direction of displacement, F cosθ, contributes to the work done; the perpendicular component does no work at all. If θ < 90°, cosθ is positive and work is positive; if θ = 90°, cosθ = 0 and no work is done; if θ > 90°, cosθ is negative and the work done is negative.

Worked example

A force of 10 N is applied to a block at 30° above the horizontal, pulling it 50 m along the ground. The horizontal component of the force is 10 cos30°. Work done = 10 × cos30° × 50 = 433 J. Only this horizontal component moves the block, so it is the only part of the force that does work.

Sign of work: three casesA car braking exerts a friction force of 500 N opposing the motion over a stopping distance of 25 m: work done by friction = −500 × 25 = −12,500 J. The negative sign shows the car's kinetic energy is decreasing; the “missing” energy has become internal (thermal) energy in the brake disks, on which positive work has been done. By contrast, a woman walking at constant velocity while holding a suitcase does zero work on the suitcase, because the supporting force is vertical (perpendicular to the horizontal motion) – even though it feels like effort, no energy is transferred to the case itself (energy is instead used inside her contracting and relaxing muscle fibres).

Graphical method for work done

Figure A.3.1: Force vs distance for a constant forceA graph with force on the vertical axis and distance on the horizontal axis. For a constant force F acting over a distance Δx, the plotted line is horizontal, forming a rectangle of height F and width Δx. The shaded area under the line, F × Δx, is numerically equal to the work done.
work done = area under a force vs distance graph

Since work done = FΔx for a constant force, and this is exactly the area of the rectangle under a force-distance graph, the area under any force-distance graph (whether the force is constant or varying) gives the work done. This method works even when the force is not constant, as long as the shape of the enclosed area can be calculated.

Worked example

A constant force of 15 N acts on a trolley over a distance of 4 m. On a force-distance graph this is a rectangle of height 15 N and width 4 m, so the work done = area = 15 × 4 = 60 J.

Hooke's Law and Elastic Potential Energy

Hooke's lawWhen a spring is stretched (or compressed) within certain limits, the force needed is directly proportional to the extension (or compression): FH = kΔx, where k is the spring constant. This relationship was first recognized by Robert Hooke in 1676.
FH = kΔx

FH is the force needed to stretch (or the force exerted by) the spring, in newtons; k is the spring constant, in N m−1, which is the gradient of a force vs extension graph and measures the stiffness of the spring; Δx is the extension (or compression) from the spring's natural length, in metres. A stiffer spring has a larger k.

Worked example

A spring has a spring constant of 250 N m−1. What force is needed to stretch it by 4 cm (0.04 m)? FH = kΔx = 250 × 0.04 = 10 N. A force of 10 N must be applied to hold the spring at this extension.

Figure A.3.2: Force vs extension for a springA graph of force against extension, with force on the vertical axis and extension on the horizontal axis. The plotted line is a straight line through the origin (obeying Hooke's law), whose gradient F/x is equal to the spring constant, k. The area under this line up to a given extension is a right-angled triangle.

Elastic potential energy

As a spring is stretched, the force needed grows steadily, so the work done cannot be found simply by F × s – the area under the graph must be used instead. For a linear force-extension graph, this area is a triangle: area = (1/2) × base × height = (1/2) × Δx × FH. Substituting FH = kΔx gives the elastic potential energy stored.

EH = (1/2)k(Δx)2

EH is the elastic potential energy stored in the spring (in joules); k is the spring constant (N m−1); Δx is the extension or compression (m). This is the work done in stretching or compressing the spring, and it applies equally to compression (for example, a spring being compressed by a loaded holder) as to stretching.

Worked example

Using the same spring as above (k = 250 N m−1) stretched by 0.04 m: EH = (1/2) × 250 × (0.04)2 = (1/2) × 250 × 0.0016 = 0.2 J. This 0.2 J is stored as elastic potential energy and would be released as kinetic energy if the spring were allowed to snap back.

Energy, Energy Stores and the Law of Conservation of Energy

EnergyEnergy is the quantity that enables a body to do work. When body A does work on body B, energy is transferred from body A to body B. The unit of energy is the joule (J); energy is a scalar quantity.

There are two simple ways a body can do work: because it is moving (kinetic energy) or because of its position (potential energy, sometimes better thought of as “positional” energy, since a moving body also has the potential to do work).

Kinetic energy

Kinetic energy, EkThe energy a body has because of its motion. To give a body kinetic energy, work must be done on it; the work done equals the gain in kinetic energy. Using F = ma and v2 = u2 + 2as (with u = 0), the work done accelerating a body from rest, Fs = mas, can be rewritten using as = v2/2.
Ek = (1/2)mv2

m is the mass of the body (kg) and v is its speed (m s−1). Kinetic energy is always positive (or zero) since it depends on v2, regardless of the direction of motion.

Worked example

A ball of mass 0.2 kg is thrown vertically upward with a speed of 2 m s−1. Its kinetic energy at launch is Ek = (1/2) × 0.2 × 22 = 0.4 J. This is the energy available to do work against gravity as the ball rises.

Gravitational potential energy

Gravitational potential energy, EpThe energy a body has because of its position above the Earth. For a body to have gained potential energy, it must at some time have been lifted to that position; the work done in lifting it (at constant velocity, so the lifting force balances the weight) equals the gain in Ep.
ΔEp = mgΔh

m is the mass (kg), g is the gravitational field strength (9.8 m s−2 near the Earth's surface), and Δh is the change in height (m). This formula only applies close to the Earth's surface, where g can be treated as constant.

Worked example

A hydroelectric reservoir has an outlet 100 m above the turbine, with the lake bed 75 m above the turbine, so the average height of the water above the turbine is (100 + 75)/2 = 87.5 m. The lake has a volume of 5 × 107 m3, so its mass is 5 × 107 × 1000 = 5 × 1010 kg (using the density of water, 1000 kg m−3). Ep = mgΔh = 5 × 1010 × 9.8 × 87.5 = 4.29 × 1013 J. This huge store of gravitational PE is why hydroelectric power stations can generate very large amounts of electrical energy as the water falls to the turbines.

Law of conservation of energyEnergy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one store to another.

This law underlies both the kinetic and potential energy formulas above – they were derived by assuming that work done equals the gain in energy. It is one of the most fundamental laws in physics: if it were false, a car could suddenly speed up with no one touching the accelerator, or you could arrive at the top of a staircase without having done any work to climb it. Because the law is so strongly supported by experiment, a device claimed to produce energy “from nowhere” can be dismissed as fraudulent without needing to examine how it works.

Mechanical energyMechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy and elastic potential energy: Emech = Ek + Ep + EH. In the absence of frictional or resistive forces, the total mechanical energy of a system is conserved. If mechanical energy is conserved, work is simply the amount of energy transformed between these different forms within the system.

Conservation of Mechanical Energy in Practice

Using conservation of energy is often a much simpler way to solve motion problems than working with forces and the suvat equations, because it does not require uniform acceleration and does not depend on the route taken between two states – only on the total energy transformed.

loss in Ep = gain in Ek → v = √(2gΔh)

When a body falls (or slides down a frictionless slope) through a height Δh with no resistive forces acting, all the gravitational PE lost is converted into kinetic energy: mgΔh = (1/2)mv2. The mass cancels, so the final speed depends only on the height fallen, not on the mass or the shape of the path taken.

Worked example

A block slides down a frictionless ramp, starting from rest at a height of 5 m. Using loss of Ep = gain of Ek: mgh = (1/2)mv2, so v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 5) = 10 m s−1. This is the same answer that using F = ma with the suvat equations would give, but obtained without needing to resolve forces along the slope.

The same principle works in reverse: for the m = 0.2 kg ball launched upward at 2 m s−1 considered earlier (Ek = 0.4 J at launch), all of this kinetic energy converts to gravitational PE at the top of its flight, so mgh = 0.4 J gives a maximum height of h = 0.4/(0.2 × 10) = 0.2 m.

Circular Motion and Energy

Figure A.3.3: Looping the loopA rollercoaster track showing a car starting from rest at the top of a slope of height h, descending, and then looping around a vertical circular loop of radius r. The car's height above the ground at the top of the loop is 2r.
Why the centripetal force does no workWhen a body moves in a circle at constant speed, its kinetic energy is constant, so no net work is done on it. Since velocity is changing (direction, not magnitude), a resultant force must still act. If this force had any component along the direction of motion, it would do work and change the kinetic energy – so the force must be entirely perpendicular to the velocity, i.e. directed toward the centre of the circle. This gives an energy-based justification for why centripetal force is always perpendicular to velocity.

At the top of a vertical loop, the minimum speed to maintain contact with the track occurs when the entire centripetal force is provided by gravity (normal reaction force = 0):

mg = mv2min/r → v2min = gr

Combining this with conservation of energy between the top of the starting slope (height h, at rest) and the top of the loop (height 2r, speed vmin) gives the minimum starting height. At the top of the loop, Ek = (1/2)mv2min = (1/2)mgr and Ep = mg(2r), so total mechanical energy there = 2mgr + (1/2)mgr = 2.5mgr. Since this must equal mgh at the start, h = 2.5r.

Worked example

A loop has radius r = 5 m. Minimum speed at the top: vmin = √(gr) = √(10 × 5) = 7.1 m s−1. Minimum starting height: h = 2.5r = 2.5 × 5 = 12.5 m. A rollercoaster designer must release the car from at least 12.5 m above ground level, otherwise the car loses contact with the track before reaching the top of the loop. In any real ride, energy lost to friction and air resistance means the slope must start even higher than this ideal value.

Energy Sources, Sankey Diagrams and Efficiency

Machines such as petrol engines, electric motors and turbines must be supplied with energy from a store before they can do work. Common energy stores include petrol, gas, coal, nuclear fuel, solar radiation and electricity; all except solar energy are ultimately related to the kinetic or potential energy of particles.

Fuel and energy densityA fuel is a chemical that can be burned to release heat, which can be used to do work. Different fuels release different amounts of energy per unit volume – this is called their energy density.

For example, 1 litre of petrol contains about 36 MJ of energy. A car travelling at a constant speed needs a driving force to balance the drag (air resistance) force acting against it; the work done against drag comes from the chemical energy in the fuel. If a car experiences a constant drag force of 500 N, then all 36 MJ in 1 litre of fuel converted into work would move the car a distance of work/force = 36 × 106/500 = 72 km. Reducing the drag force on a car increases the distance it can travel per litre of fuel.

Figure A.3.4: Sankey diagram for a coal-fired power stationA flow diagram with a single wide arrow entering on the left, labelled with the total energy input, which splits and narrows into several arrows on the right. Some arrows branch off and are labelled as energy losses (e.g. energy lost within the power station, energy lost in transmission to customers), while the remaining, narrower arrow on the right represents useful energy delivered to customers. The width of each arrow is proportional to the amount of energy it represents, and because the combined width of all the output arrows equals the width of the input arrow, the diagram visually displays conservation of energy while showing where energy is “lost” (i.e. transferred to a non-useful store, usually as thermal energy).
EfficiencyEfficiency, η, gives the proportion of the total input energy that is transferred to a useful store. It has no units and cannot exceed 1 (or 100%).
η = useful work (or energy, or power) out / total work (or energy, or power) in

Efficiency can be expressed using work, energy, or power, since power is just work or energy divided by time – if the useful output and total input occur over the same time interval, dividing both work quantities by that time gives the same ratio in terms of power. Efficiency is often given as a percentage.

Worked example

A car experiencing a constant drag force of 500 N travels 20 km (20,000 m) on 1 litre of fuel (36 MJ). Useful work done by the engine = force × distance = 500 × 20,000 = 10 MJ. Efficiency = useful work out / total energy in = 10/36 = 0.28, or 28%. The rest of the chemical energy in the fuel (72%) is transferred to the internal energy of the engine parts, exhaust gases and surrounding air, rather than to useful driving work.

Power

PowerPower is the rate at which work is done, or the rate at which energy is transferred: power = work done per unit time. The unit of power is the watt (W), equal to 1 J s−1. Power is a scalar quantity.
P = W/t

W is the work done (or energy transferred), in joules, and t is the time taken, in seconds. A task that transfers a fixed amount of energy requires more power the faster it is completed – a powerful car is one that can gain kinetic energy quickly, and a powerful weightlifter is one who can lift a large weight in a short time (not simply one who can lift a large weight at all, however slowly).

Worked example

A car of mass 1000 kg accelerates from rest to 100 km h−1 (28 m s−1) in 5 s. Gain in kinetic energy = (1/2)mv2 = (1/2) × 1000 × 282 = 392,000 J = 392 kJ. Average power = work done/time = 392,000/5 = 78,400 W = 78.4 kW. (Using 746 W = 1 horsepower, this is about 105 hp.)

P = Fv

Since P = W/t = FΔs/t, and Δs/t is velocity, power can also be calculated as force multiplied by velocity. This is a useful shortcut for a body moving at constant velocity, where the driving force is known and there is no need to calculate work and time separately.

Worked example

A car moves at a constant velocity of 20 m s−1 against a resistive (drag) force of 1000 N. Since the car is not accelerating, the driving force from the engine must also be 1000 N. Power developed by the engine = Fv = 1000 × 20 = 20,000 W = 20 kW. This is the power needed just to overcome drag and maintain constant speed, not to accelerate the car.

Energy Transfers in Collisions and Explosions

Elastic vs. inelastic collisionsAn elastic collision is one in which both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. In an inelastic collision, momentum is still conserved but kinetic energy is not – some kinetic energy is transferred to other stores (heat, sound, permanent deformation) as the colliding bodies do work squashing or deforming each other. A totally inelastic collision is one in which the two bodies stick together (coalesce) after impact.

For two identical balls colliding elastically head-on, momentum and kinetic energy conservation together show that the balls simply exchange velocities. For an elastic collision in two dimensions between identical balls, where one ball is initially stationary, combining the vector conservation of momentum (forming a triangle of velocity vectors) with conservation of kinetic energy (Pythagoras' theorem for that triangle) shows that the two balls always move off at right angles to each other after the collision (except in a perfectly head-on collision) – a useful check when analysing two-dimensional collision data.

Figure A.3.5: Elastic collision between two identical ballsBefore the collision, one ball approaches at speed u while the second, identical ball is stationary. After an off-centre (non head-on) elastic collision, the two balls move away from the point of impact along paths that are exactly perpendicular to each other, forming a right-angled triangle with the original velocity vector when the two final velocity vectors are added together.
ΔEk = Ek, after − Ek, before

Applying conservation of energy to a collision or explosion, any change in total kinetic energy must equal the energy transferred to or from another store. In an inelastic collision, the kinetic energy lost equals the work done deforming the bodies (this energy becomes internal/thermal energy and sound). In an explosion, the kinetic energy gained equals the chemical (or other) energy released by the explosive, since the colliding or exploding bodies' momentum is unaffected by this internal exchange.

Worked example

Two identical pieces of modelling clay collide and stick together (a totally inelastic collision). If their total kinetic energy is 1.8 J just before impact and 0.3 J just after, the kinetic energy lost, 1.8 − 0.3 = 1.5 J, is the work done squashing the clay – this energy has been transferred to internal (thermal) energy and sound, not destroyed.

Explosions are never elasticExplosions cannot be elastic collisions, because without work being done, the separating fragments would have no kinetic energy and so would not be moving. The chemical energy stored in an explosive supplies the extra kinetic energy of the fragments. When a body explodes into two unequal fragments, the smaller fragment always ends up with most of the kinetic energy – a result used later when analysing nuclear decay.
Worked example

A ball at rest (total mass 0.12 kg) explodes into two fragments: a 20 g (0.02 kg) piece moving at 25 m s−1 and a 100 g (0.1 kg) piece moving at 5 m s−1 in the opposite direction. Kinetic energy gained = Ek, after − Ek, before = [(1/2)(0.02)(252) + (1/2)(0.1)(52)] − 0 = 6.25 + 1.25 = 7.5 J. This 7.5 J was supplied to the ball by the explosive – notice the much lighter fragment (20 g) ended up with five times as much kinetic energy as the heavier one (100 g), illustrating that the smaller fragment of an explosion carries away most of the energy.
