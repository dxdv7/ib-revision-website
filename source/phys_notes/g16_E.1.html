Early models of the atom

The electron

The electron is a fundamental particle with charge −1.60 × 10-19 C and mass 9.11 × 10-31 kg. It was discovered by J. J. Thomson in 1897, who identified it as the small negative particle responsible for carrying charge when current flows in a conductor.

The plum pudding model

By measuring the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron, Thomson realized electrons were very small compared to the whole atom. Since atoms are electrically neutral overall, he proposed that an atom consists of a sphere of positive charge (like a pudding) spread evenly throughout the atom's volume, with tiny negative electrons (the "plums") embedded within it. This model was accepted until it was tested experimentally by Geiger and Marsden, working under Ernest Rutherford.

The Geiger-Marsden-Rutherford scattering experiment

Experimental setup and results

Alpha particles (fast-moving, positively charged) from a radioactive source were fired at a very thin sheet of gold foil, with a detector screen used to record the angle at which particles emerged. According to the plum pudding model, the positive charge of each gold atom is spread out, so alpha particles should pass through with little or no deflection. The actual results showed that most alpha particles passed straight through undeflected, a significant number were deflected through large angles, and a very small number bounced almost straight back toward the source.

Conclusions: the nuclear model

Since a particle can only bounce back off another particle that is much more massive than itself, something small and heavy must exist within the atom. Since most alpha particles pass straight through, the atom must be mostly empty space. Since the small heavy object repels the positive alpha particles, it must itself be positively charged (too heavy and dense to be made of light electrons). This dense, positively charged center of the atom is called the nucleus; the atom itself consists of a nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space in which the electrons are found.

Nucleons and nuclides

Nucleons are the particles found in the nucleus: protons and neutrons. A particular combination of nucleons (a specific number of protons and neutrons) is called a nuclide. Every nuclide is defined by its nucleon number (A), proton number (Z), and neutron number (N).

AZX    with   N = A − Z

X is the chemical symbol of the element, A is the nucleon number (mass number: total number of protons plus neutrons, which determines the mass of the nucleus), Z is the proton number (atomic number: number of protons, which determines the charge of the nucleus and the identity of the element), and N is the neutron number, found by subtracting Z from A. Unknown or unidentified elements are given the generic symbol X until Z can be compared against the periodic table to identify the element.

Worked example

Lithium-7 has a nucleon number A = 7 and a proton number Z = 3. It therefore has 3 protons and N = A − Z = 7 − 3 = 4 neutrons, and is represented by the symbol 73Li. This tells us everything about the composition of the nucleus from just two numbers.

Isotopes

Isotopes are nuclides of the same element (same proton number, Z) but with different nucleon numbers (A), i.e. a different number of neutrons. Isotopes of an element have identical chemical properties (since chemistry depends on the number of electrons and protons) but different masses and, often, different nuclear stability.

Isotopes of lithium

Lithium-6 (63Li) has 3 protons and 3 neutrons; lithium-7 (73Li) has 3 protons and 4 neutrons. Both have the same proton number (3), so both are lithium and both have identical chemical behavior, but lithium-7 is heavier and the two isotopes exist in different natural abundances.

Charge of nuclide: Q = Ze;    Mass of nuclide (approx.): m ≈ Zmp + Nmn

Z is the proton number, e is the elementary charge, mp and mn are the masses of a proton and neutron, and N is the neutron number. This approximation ignores the small mass difference (mass defect) associated with nuclear binding energy, but is accurate enough for most SL calculations.

Worked example

An iron-56 nucleus has Z = 26 protons and N = 30 neutrons. Its charge is Q = Ze = 26 × 1.60 × 10-19 = 4.16 × 10-18 C. Its approximate mass is m ≈ (26 × 1.673 × 10-27) + (30 × 1.675 × 10-27) = 4.35 × 10-26 + 5.03 × 10-26 = 9.37 × 10-26 kg. This shows how the charge and mass of any nucleus can be found directly from its nuclear notation.

Mass number vs charge number

In the 1860s, chemists found that ordering elements by relative atomic mass ("mass number") produced periodic patterns in chemical behavior, but with some anomalies. Rutherford's scattering experiments (1911) showed that ordering elements instead by nuclear charge ("charge number", Z) resolved these anomalies. Since the charge number is not the same as the mass number for elements heavier than hydrogen, the nucleus must contain particles beyond just protons that add mass but no charge – leading to the prediction, and later (1932) the discovery by Chadwick, of the neutron.

Nuclear stability

The neutron-to-proton ratio

Whether a given nuclide is stable (does not spontaneously decay) or unstable (radioactive) depends largely on the ratio of neutrons to protons in its nucleus. For light elements, stable nuclei tend to have roughly equal numbers of protons and neutrons (N ≈ Z). For heavier elements, stable nuclei need proportionally more neutrons than protons, since the extra neutrons help offset the increasing electrostatic repulsion between the growing number of protons, without adding to that repulsion themselves. Plotting N against Z for all known nuclides produces a "band (or line) of stability"; nuclides that fall outside this band, with too many or too few neutrons for their proton number, are unstable and undergo radioactive decay to move toward a more stable neutron-to-proton ratio.

Electrons and the failure of the classical model

The problem with a "mini solar system"

Since the nucleus is positively charged and electrons are negative, electrons must be attracted toward the nucleus, so something must stop them from simply falling in. One idea is that electrons orbit the nucleus like planets orbiting the Sun, with circular motion providing continuous acceleration toward the center without actually getting closer. However, classical electromagnetism predicts that an accelerating (e.g. orbiting) charge must continuously radiate electromagnetic energy. An orbiting electron would therefore continuously lose energy, causing it to spiral inward and collapse into the nucleus almost instantly – which does not happen in real atoms. This contradiction shows that a purely classical model of the atom cannot be correct, and a full explanation of atomic structure requires ideas from quantum physics.

The connection between atoms and light

Light is an electromagnetic wave, and electromagnetic waves are produced by moving (accelerating) charges. Since electrons in atoms have charge and can move, the production of light by matter is closely connected to the behavior of electrons within atoms. To study this connection without interference between atoms, low-pressure gases are used, since their atoms are far enough apart to behave as isolated, independent atoms while still being numerous enough to produce a detectable amount of light.

Atomic (line) spectra

Producing and observing a line spectrum

Applying a high potential difference across a low-pressure gas in a glass discharge tube gives energy to the gas atoms, causing them to emit light. Different gases glow with different characteristic colors. Passing this light through a prism or diffraction grating spreads it out by wavelength; a spectrometer can then be used to measure the angles (and hence wavelengths) precisely. Unlike the continuous rainbow spectrum of white light, the spectrum from a low-pressure gas consists of a series of thin, discrete bright lines at specific wavelengths only – a line spectrum.

Quantized energy levels

Since light carries energy, and only certain specific wavelengths (hence specific photon energies) are emitted, the electrons in an atom must only be able to lose certain specific amounts of energy. This is only possible if the electrons themselves can only ever have certain fixed, discrete amounts of energy in the first place. We say the electron's energy is quantized, and represent the allowed values on an energy level diagram.

Photon emission and absorption

Atomic transitions

An atomic transition occurs when an electron moves from one discrete energy level to another. When an electron drops from a higher energy level to a lower one, the atom emits a photon carrying away the energy difference. When an atom absorbs a photon of exactly the right energy, an electron can be excited from a lower energy level to a higher one. Because only certain energy level differences exist in a given atom, only certain photon energies (and hence certain spectral lines) are possible – this is why every element has its own unique, characteristic line spectrum, acting like a fingerprint.

ΔE = hf

ΔE is the difference in energy between the two levels involved in the transition (J), h is the Planck constant (6.63 × 10-34 J s), and f is the frequency of the photon emitted or absorbed. Larger energy changes produce higher-frequency (bluer, more energetic) photons, while smaller energy changes produce lower-frequency (redder) photons.

Worked example

An electron falls from the −4 eV energy level to the −10 eV energy level, a change of ΔE = 6 eV = 6 × 1.60 × 10-19 = 9.6 × 10-19 J. Using ΔE = hf: f = ΔE/h = (9.6 × 10-19)/(6.63 × 10-34) = 1.45 × 1015 Hz. This corresponds to a wavelength of about 207 nm, in the ultraviolet part of the spectrum.

E = hc/λ

This combines ΔE = hf with the wave equation c = fλ to express photon energy directly in terms of wavelength λ, where c is the speed of light (3.00 × 108 m s-1). This form is useful when a spectral line's wavelength (rather than frequency) is measured directly, as is usually the case with a spectrometer or diffraction grating.

Worked example

A spectral line in helium's absorption spectrum occurs at 588 nm. The photon energy is E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10-34 × 3.00 × 108)/(588 × 10-9) = (1.989 × 10-25)/(5.88 × 10-7) = 3.38 × 10-19 J. This tiny amount of energy corresponds to exactly the gap between two of helium's electron energy levels.

Number of possible spectral lines = n(n − 1)/2

If an atom's electrons can occupy n discrete energy levels, then this formula gives the number of distinct transitions (and hence the number of possible spectral lines) between all possible pairs of levels, since a transition can start at any level and end at any lower level. This counts each pair of levels once.

Worked example

A simple atom has 4 energy levels. The number of possible transitions is n(n − 1)/2 = (4 × 3)/2 = 6. This matches the six different magnitudes of energy transition (and hence six possible spectral lines) that can occur between four energy levels.

Emission and absorption spectra

Emission spectrum vs absorption spectrum

An emission spectrum is produced when excited atoms (for example, in a discharge tube) release photons as electrons fall to lower energy levels; it appears as a series of bright lines of specific wavelengths against a dark background. An absorption spectrum is produced when white light (containing all wavelengths) passes through a cooler gas; photons with exactly the right energy to excite an electron to a higher level are absorbed, leaving dark lines at those same specific wavelengths against an otherwise continuous, bright spectrum. Because both processes involve the same set of energy level differences, the dark lines in an absorption spectrum occur at precisely the same wavelengths as the bright lines in the corresponding emission spectrum for that element.

Absorption spectra from stars

The hot, dense core of a star produces a continuous spectrum (including gamma radiation, since collisions strip electrons from atoms entirely, forming a plasma). This light passes outward through the star's cooler, lower-density outer layers, where atoms still have their electrons in place. Certain wavelengths, corresponding to the energy level differences of the elements present in the outer layers, are absorbed, leaving characteristic dark absorption lines in the star's overall spectrum. By identifying which wavelengths are missing, astronomers can determine the chemical composition of a star's outer layers.

Ionization

Ionization and ionization energy

Ionization occurs when an electron is added to or removed from a neutral atom, producing a charged ion. An electron can be removed if the atom absorbs a sufficiently high-energy photon (which is then fully absorbed, unlike ordinary excitation to a bound level), or if it is knocked out by a collision with a fast-moving charged particle, such as an alpha particle. The ionization energy from a given energy level is the minimum energy needed to completely remove an electron starting in that level, equal in magnitude to the energy of that level (measuring energy so that a free, stationary electron has zero energy).

Worked example

The ground state of a hydrogen atom has energy −13.6 eV. Removing an electron from the ground state therefore requires at least 13.6 eV of energy (raising the electron's energy from −13.6 eV to 0 eV, the level of a free electron at rest). Any photon with less energy than this cannot ionize a ground-state hydrogen atom, no matter how many such photons arrive, since each photon interacts with only one electron at a time.
