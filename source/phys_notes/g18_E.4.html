Nuclear fission

What is fission?

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large, heavy nucleus into two smaller daughter nuclei, usually along with a few free neutrons. On the binding energy per nucleon curve, heavy nuclei such as uranium sit on the downward-sloping right-hand side, below the peak near iron and nickel. If a heavy nucleus splits into two medium-sized daughter nuclei with a higher combined binding energy per nucleon, energy is released, since the products are more tightly bound (more stable) than the original nucleus.

Spontaneous fission vs induced fission

Spontaneous fission occurs naturally without any external trigger: given enough time, an unstable heavy nucleus such as uranium-236 will eventually split on its own, in the same random and unpredictable way as ordinary radioactive decay. Induced fission occurs when a nucleus is deliberately made to split by absorbing an incoming particle, typically a neutron. For example, a uranium-235 nucleus that absorbs a neutron briefly becomes uranium-236 in a highly excited, unstable state; this extra energy of excitation is enough to overcome the nucleus's stability and cause it to split. Nuclear reactors and weapons rely on induced fission, since it can be triggered and controlled on demand, unlike spontaneous fission.

Energy released in fission

Energy released = (BEper nucleon of products × Aproducts) − (BEper nucleon of reactant × Areactant)

This method uses binding energy per nucleon values read from the binding energy curve. Multiplying each nuclide's binding energy per nucleon by its own nucleon number gives its total binding energy; subtracting the reactant's total binding energy from the sum of the products' total binding energies gives the energy released (any free neutrons contribute no binding energy, since they are not bound to anything).

Worked example

Uranium-236 splits into krypton-92 and barium-141. Using approximate binding energies per nucleon of 7.6 MeV (U-236), 8.2 MeV (Kr-92) and 8.0 MeV (Ba-141): binding energy of U-236 = 236 × 7.6 = 1793.6 MeV; binding energy of Ba-141 = 141 × 8.0 = 1128 MeV; binding energy of Kr-92 = 92 × 8.2 = 754.4 MeV. Gain in binding energy = (1128 + 754.4) − 1793.6 = 88.8 MeV. Since binding energy increases, this fission releases about 88.8 MeV of energy, carried away mainly as the kinetic energy of the fission fragments.

Energy released = Δm c2 = [m(reactant) − (Σm(products))] × 931.5 MeV

This alternative method uses the actual atomic masses (in u) of the reactant and all products (daughter nuclei plus any released neutrons), applying the same mass-energy equivalence used for radioactive decay. It gives the same physical result as the binding-energy method but is used when nuclide masses, rather than binding energies per nucleon, are provided.

Worked example

Uranium-236 (236.045563 u) splits into molybdenum-100 (99.907476 u) and tin-126 (125.907653 u), releasing some free neutrons (1.008664 u each). Conserving nucleon number: 236 = 100 + 126 + (number of neutrons), so 10 neutrons are released. Total product mass = 99.907476 + 125.907653 + (10 × 1.008664) = 225.815129 + 10.08664 = 235.901769 u. Mass defect Δm = 236.045563 − 235.901769 = 0.143794 u. Energy released = 0.143794 × 931.5 = 134 MeV.

Neutron balance and the release of neutrons

Why fission releases extra neutrons

Heavy nuclei like uranium need proportionally more neutrons than protons to remain stable, compared to the medium-sized nuclei produced by fission. For example, uranium-236 (92 protons, 144 neutrons) splitting into barium-141 and krypton-92 accounts for only 141 of its 144 neutrons in the daughter nuclei; the remaining neutrons are too many for the smaller, more proton-rich-relative daughter nuclei to hold, so they are expelled as free neutrons during the fission process. These released neutrons are essential, since they can go on to trigger further fission reactions in other uranium-235 nuclei.

The chain reaction

How a chain reaction develops

When a uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and undergoes fission, it releases (on average) two or three new free neutrons. If at least one of these neutrons goes on to be absorbed by another uranium-235 nucleus, causing it to fission and release further neutrons, the process becomes self-sustaining: a chain reaction. If, on average, more than one neutron from each fission triggers a further fission, the reaction rate grows rapidly (an uncontrolled chain reaction, as in a nuclear weapon); if on average exactly one neutron from each fission goes on to trigger another, the reaction proceeds at a steady, controlled rate (as in a nuclear reactor).

Visualizing the chain reaction

Picture a single uranium-235 nucleus absorbing one neutron and splitting, releasing (for example) three neutrons. Each of these three neutrons can go on to strike a further uranium-235 nucleus, causing three more fissions and releasing up to nine further neutrons, and so on. Left completely unchecked, the number of fissions occurring would grow explosively with each generation; controlling this growth (for example with control rods, discussed below) is essential for safe, steady energy production.

Enrichment

Naturally occurring uranium is only about 0.7% uranium-235, with the remainder being uranium-238, which readily absorbs neutrons without undergoing fission and so does not sustain a chain reaction. Before uranium can be used as reactor fuel, it must be enriched (the proportion of uranium-235 increased), typically to around 3%, so that enough fissile uranium-235 is present to sustain a controlled chain reaction.

Moderation

Why neutrons must be slowed down

Neutrons released during fission travel very fast, with kinetic energies of around 1 MeV, but uranium-235 nuclei are much more likely to absorb a neutron and undergo further fission if the neutron is moving slowly, with kinetic energy of around 1 eV. Without slowing down, most fission neutrons would simply pass through the surrounding uranium without being absorbed. A moderator is a material used to slow fast neutrons down to these useful, lower speeds.

How a moderator works

A moderator (commonly water or graphite) consists of light nuclei placed between the fuel. Fast neutrons collide with these nuclei in near-elastic collisions; because the moderator nuclei (such as hydrogen in water) have a mass similar to a neutron's, a significant fraction of the neutron's kinetic energy is transferred to the moderator nucleus with each collision, progressively slowing the neutron after repeated collisions. Note that "moderation" refers to slowing the neutrons, not to slowing the chain reaction itself; removing the moderator would actually cause the reaction to slow down or stop, since the fast neutrons would then be far less likely to cause further fission.

Critical mass

Critical mass

For a chain reaction to be self-sustaining, the piece of fissile material must be large enough that neutrons are likely to be absorbed and trigger further fission before escaping from its surface. If the sample is too small, too many neutrons escape before being slowed and absorbed, and the chain reaction dies out. The critical mass is the minimum mass of fissile material required for a self-sustaining chain reaction to occur.

Components of a nuclear reactor

Layout of a nuclear power station

Fuel rods, containing enriched uranium fuel pellets, are arranged in a core surrounded by a moderator (such as graphite or water). Control rods can be inserted between the fuel rods to varying depths. A coolant (a gas or liquid) circulates through the reactor core, absorbing heat, and carries this heat to a heat exchanger, where it boils water into steam. This steam drives a turbine connected to a generator, producing electricity; the steam then cools and condenses back into water in a condenser and is recirculated. The entire reactor core is enclosed within a thick containment structure for shielding and safety.

Control rods

Control rods are made of a strongly neutron-absorbing material (such as boron). Lowering control rods further into the reactor core removes more neutrons from the chain reaction, slowing the rate of fission; raising them allows more neutrons to remain available to sustain fission, increasing the reaction rate. Unlike the moderator, control rods are specifically used to regulate (speed up, slow down, or stop) the reaction.

Energy transfers in a nuclear power station

From nuclear energy to electricity

The overall energy transfer in a nuclear power station can be represented by a Sankey diagram: nuclear energy stored in the processed fuel is converted into thermal energy as the fission fragments' kinetic energy heats the fuel and surrounding coolant; this thermal energy is used to produce steam, which turns a turbine, converting thermal energy into mechanical (kinetic) energy; the turbine drives a generator, converting mechanical energy into electricity. At each stage some energy is lost, mainly as waste heat released to the environment, and some fuel remains unused when the reactor is eventually shut down for refueling, so the overall efficiency of a nuclear power station is well under 100%, typically around 30 to 40%.

Specific energy = energy released per fission / mass of one fuel atom

This gives the energy released per unit mass of nuclear fuel (in J kg-1), allowing a direct comparison with the specific energy of other fuels such as coal or oil. Because nuclear fission releases roughly a million times more energy per atom than a chemical reaction such as combustion, nuclear fuel has an enormously higher specific energy than fossil fuels.

Worked example

Each fission of uranium-235 releases about 180 MeV = 180 × 1.60 × 10-13 = 2.88 × 10-11 J. One atom of U-235 has mass 235 × 1.6605 × 10-27 = 3.90 × 10-25 kg. Specific energy = (2.88 × 10-11) / (3.90 × 10-25) = 7.4 × 1013 J kg-1. This is roughly a million times greater than the specific energy of a fossil fuel such as coal (around 3 × 107 J kg-1).

Efficiency = useful (electrical) power output / total power generated

Only a fraction of the nuclear power generated in the reactor core is ultimately converted into useful electrical power, since energy is lost as waste heat at each stage of the energy transfer chain. Rearranging this formula lets the total nuclear power (and hence the fuel consumption rate) be found from a reactor's electrical output and its known efficiency.

Worked example

A reactor has a useful electrical power output of 1.2 GW at an efficiency of 36%. Total nuclear power required = 1.2 × 109 / 0.36 = 3.33 × 109 W. Energy required per day = 3.33 × 109 × 86400 = 2.88 × 1014 J. Using the specific energy found above (7.4 × 1013 J kg-1): mass of U-235 fissioned per day = (2.88 × 1014) / (7.4 × 1013) = 3.9 kg. This shows how little fuel mass is needed, per day, to power a large city.

Safety, meltdown and containment

Meltdown

Meltdown occurs if the fission reaction is not properly controlled (for example, due to a failure of the cooling system or control rods) and the reactor core overheats to the point that the fuel rods melt. This can damage the reactor and, in severe cases, cause the pressure vessel to rupture, releasing radioactive material. Major historical examples occurred at Chernobyl, Ukraine (1986) and Fukushima, Japan (2011).

Why a reactor cannot explode like a nuclear bomb

Although a reactor meltdown is a serious hazard, a nuclear reactor cannot explode with the destructive force of a nuclear weapon. Reactor fuel is only enriched to a few percent uranium-235, far below the very high enrichment (or the fully separated plutonium) required to sustain the extremely rapid, uncontrolled chain reaction of a weapon. The containment building, an airtight steel structure covered in thick concrete, also provides shielding that prevents most radioactive material from escaping even during a serious accident, and is designed to withstand large external impacts.

Radioactive waste management

Low-level waste vs high-level waste

Low-level waste includes materials such as protective clothing, tools and decommissioned reactor components that have picked up only small amounts of radioactivity; it is only mildly hazardous but must still be kept away from human contact for roughly 100 to 500 years, often by burial in controlled sites or encasing in concrete. High-level waste consists mainly of spent fuel rods, containing highly radioactive fission products and unused fuel; because some of these isotopes have half-lives of thousands of years (spent plutonium fuel is considered hazardous for at least 240,000 years), high-level waste requires very long-term, carefully engineered storage.

Managing high-level waste

High-level waste is generally handled in one of two ways: it is first stored underwater at the reactor site for several years to allow the most intensely radioactive short-lived isotopes to decay away, after which it may be sealed in steel cylinders for long-term storage (for example in deep geological repositories), or it is reprocessed to chemically separate out any remaining usable uranium and plutonium, leaving a smaller volume of highly radioactive fission-fragment waste whose overall half-life (and therefore required storage time) is generally much shorter than that of the original uranium or plutonium.

Advantages of nuclear power

Comparing nuclear power with fossil fuels

Because nuclear fission releases far more energy per kilogram of fuel than chemical combustion, nuclear power stations need to transport, store and replace comparatively tiny amounts of fuel for a given energy output, reducing fuel transport costs and the frequency of refueling. Since no fuel is burned, a nuclear reactor produces no carbon dioxide during operation, unlike a coal- or gas-fired power station. Because it does not depend on a supply of oxygen to generate power, nuclear energy is also particularly well suited to applications like submarines, which must operate for long periods without access to air.
