Short-run and long-run macroeconomic equilibrium

National income is equivalent to the level of output a country produces, and is a key indicator of the economic health of an economy. The actual level of output, and its corresponding price level, is determined by the interaction between aggregate demand (AD) and aggregate supply (AS). Because economists distinguish between a short-run and a long-run aggregate supply curve (Chapter 15), there is correspondingly a short-run and a long-run macroeconomic equilibrium.

What level of output represents short-run equilibrium?

Short-run equilibriumThe economy is in short-run equilibrium where aggregate demand equals short-run aggregate supply (SRAS). Because AD exactly equals AS, there is no reason for producers to change their level of output, and no upward or downward pressure on the price level – the economy is at rest as long as nothing changes AD or AS.
Figure 16.1: Short-run equilibrium outputAxes: average price level (vertical) against real output, Y (horizontal). An upward-sloping SRAS curve intersects a downward-sloping AD curve at a single point, giving equilibrium price level P and equilibrium output Y. This looks graphically very similar to a short-run equilibrium in a microeconomic market diagram, but here the axes represent the whole economy’s average price level and real output.

What level of output represents long-run equilibrium?

Because economists disagree about the shape of the long-run aggregate supply curve, the long-run equilibrium differs between the Keynesian and new classical models. Both agree that the long-run equilibrium is where aggregate demand equals long-run aggregate supply (LRAS), but they disagree on what determines the level of output at which this occurs.

The new classical perspective on long-run equilibrium

New classical viewAccording to new classical economists, the economy will always move automatically towards its long-run equilibrium at the full-employment level of output, Yf. Thus the long-run equilibrium is where AD meets the vertical LRAS curve. The word “automatically” means without any government intervention – new classical economists place great significance on the power of free markets. There may be a short-run increase or decrease in output if AD changes, but the economy will always return to its long-run equilibrium at Yf.
Figure 16.2: New classical perspective of long-run equilibriumAxes: average price level vertical, real output horizontal. A vertical LRAS line at Yf intersects a downward-sloping AD1 curve, giving equilibrium price P1 at output Yf.
Figure 16.3: New classical view – impact of an increase in AD (long run, price only)The impact of any change in AD, in the long run, falls entirely on the price level. AD shifts right from AD1 to AD2, and the price level rises from P1 to P2, but there is no increase in real output – output remains fixed at Yf because LRAS is vertical.

Adjustment from short run to long run in the new classical model (inflationary gaps)

It is valuable to trace the adjustment process from the short run to the long run, because both Keynesian and new classical economists agree on the shape of the short-run aggregate supply curve (upward sloping); they disagree only about what happens in the long run.

Figure 16.4: An inflationary gap in the new classical modelAxes: average price level vertical, real output horizontal. A vertical LRAS at Yf, an upward-sloping SRAS, and AD1 intersecting SRAS at Yf initially. AD then increases to AD2. In the short run, output rises above the full-employment level, to Y1 (Y1 > Yf), shown as the “inflationary gap”: the amount by which short-run equilibrium output exceeds Yf.
Inflationary gap – definitionAn inflationary gap exists where the economy is in short-run equilibrium at a level of output greater than the full-employment level of output. According to new classical economists this is only possible in the short run – e.g. by having existing workers do overtime – because it is not sustainable once the economy is already at full employment (there are no unemployed resources left).
Figure 16.5: New classical adjustment – short run and long runContinuing from Figure 16.4: as firms compete for increasingly scarce labour and capital to keep expanding output, they bid up factor prices. This raises firms’ costs of production, so the SRAS curve shifts left, from SRAS1 to SRAS2. The diagram shows AD2 now intersecting SRAS2 at exactly Yf again, but at a higher price level, P3 (P3 > P1). Although firms are supplying a higher level of output for the higher prices they receive, their higher costs of production mean no real gain, so they reduce output back to Yf. The economy returns to full employment, but at a permanently higher price level.

Summary of the new classical adjustment to a rise in AD: AD1→AD2 pushes output above Yf in the short run (inflationary gap) → scarce factors bid up in price → costs of production rise for all firms → SRAS shifts left (SRAS1→SRAS2) → economy returns to Yf but at a higher price level (P1→P3).

Figure 16.6: A deflationary gap in the new classical modelAxes as before. The economy starts in long-run equilibrium where AD1 intersects SRAS1 at output Yf and price level P1. AD then falls to AD2 (due to changes in any component of aggregate demand), resulting in a fall in the level of national output from Yf to Y1, and a fall in the price level. This is a deflationary (or recessionary) gap: the economy is in equilibrium at a level of output less than the full-employment level of output.
Figure 16.7: New classical adjustment – a fall in AD, short run and long runAccording to new classical economists, this deflationary gap will not persist. The fall in the price level means the prices of the economy’s factors of production also fall, so firms’ costs of production fall. This shifts SRAS to the right, from SRAS1 to SRAS2. The economy returns to its long-run equilibrium at the full-employment level of output, Yf, but now at a lower price level, P2 (lower than the original P1).
The key new classical conclusionThe long-run equilibrium level of output is always equal to the full-employment level of output, and the economy moves towards this automatically through free-market forces, without any government intervention. Since an increase in AD is purely inflationary in the long run, new classical economists see no role for government to deliberately steer the economy towards full employment via demand management – even where short-run deviations from full employment occur, they would recommend leaving the economy to market forces rather than using deliberate policies to manage AD.

The Keynesian perspective on long-run equilibrium

In both new classical and Keynesian analysis, the equilibrium level of output is where aggregate demand equals long-run aggregate supply. According to Keynesian economists, however, this equilibrium level of output may occur at different levels – significantly, they believe the economy may reach long-run equilibrium at a level of national income below the full-employment level (Yf), if the economy is operating where there is spare capacity. In this view, the equilibrium level of output depends mainly on the level of aggregate demand in the economy.

Figure 16.8: Keynesian long-run equilibrium output below full employmentAxes as before, with the Keynesian hockey-stick AS curve (flat, then upward sloping, then vertical at Yf, shown alongside a dashed vertical LRAS reference at Yf). AD intersects the flat (perfectly elastic) segment of AS at real output Y and price level P, where Y is less than Yf. The horizontal distance between Y and Yf is labelled the deflationary gap.
Deflationary gap / output gap (Keynesian)Because AS can be perfectly elastic where there is spare capacity (unemployed workers and/or underutilized capital), the equilibrium level of output can settle below the full-employment level of output. This is called a deflationary gap: aggregate demand in the economy is not sufficient to buy up the potential output that could be produced at the full-employment level. This concept may also be called an output gap, and although not easily measurable, it can be illustrated as the distance from a point inside a country’s (hypothetical) production possibilities curve to a point on the curve.
Figure 16.9: Output gap illustrated on a PPCAxes: consumer goods and services (vertical) against capital goods (horizontal). A bowed-out PPC is drawn; a point strictly inside the curve represents the economy’s actual (current) output, while a point on the curve directly “outward” from it represents the economy’s potential output. The gap (arrow) between the inside point and the curve represents the output gap / deflationary gap.

How does AD affect output at different phases of the Keynesian AS curve?

Figure 16.10: Increase in AD while operating below full employment (phase 1)AD1 shifts right to AD2, both intersecting the flat, perfectly elastic segment of the Keynesian AS curve. Real output rises from Y1 to Y2, but the price level (P) stays the same – no inflationary pressure. This is because spare capacity in the economy allows producers to employ unused factors of production to raise output with no increase in costs.
Figure 16.11: Increase in AD while approaching full employment (phase 2)AD2 shifts further right to AD3, now intersecting the upward-sloping segment of the Keynesian AS curve. The economy starts to experience inflationary pressure: as available factors of production become scarcer and are bid up in price, both real output and the price level rise (output Y2→Y3; price level P1→P2), to compensate producers for their higher costs.
Figure 16.12: Increase in AD at full employment (phase 3)The economy is already operating at Yf, where AS is vertical. AD shifts right (AD1→AD2), intersecting the vertical segment. The outcome is “purely inflationary”: there is no increase in output at all (output cannot rise further given the existing quantity/quality of factors of production), and the only change is a rise in the price level (P1→P2). The increase in AD simply reallocates the fixed level of scarce output among the competing components of aggregate demand – consumers, producers (investment), the government, and the foreign sector (net exports).
