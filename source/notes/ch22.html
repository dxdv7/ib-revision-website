Introduction: Equity, Equality, Inequality and Poverty

A goal of governments is an equitable distribution of income. Inequality and poverty are national and global problems, and this chapter's real-world "issue" article (an Oxfam report, "Reward Work, Not Wealth") highlighted several key points that run through the whole topic:

  • throughout the world there is evidence of great inequalities in income and wealth (e.g. billionaire wealth rose 13% per year 2010–2017, six times faster than the wages of ordinary workers, which rose just 2% per year);
  • poor people throughout the world are working hard but lack the opportunities to improve their well-being;
  • inequality is caused by many factors;
  • inequality can harm everyone, but it is often women who are hardest hit (e.g. a large gender pay gap, insecure/poorly-paid work);
  • it is not right that governments claim to be concerned about inequality but then implement policies that actually increase it;
  • there are a number of things governments can do, and there is widespread support for action to reduce inequality.

Are equality and equity the same thing?

Equality vs EquityEquality means economic outcomes are the same for different people or social groups — it does not mean everyone should be the same or get the same treatment.
Equity recognizes that all people are different, and is about fairness: despite differences, everyone should be given the same opportunities to succeed. Equity is about making sure that where differences exist among people due to factors such as socio-economic background, gender or race, all people are treated fairly and given the resources and opportunities they need to reach their full, healthy potential.

Most economists agree that some degree of inequality is inevitable, and even necessary, within a market economy: a gap between rich and poor gives people the incentive to work harder and gain a better education; it encourages entrepreneurship, since innovators and risk-takers who succeed can enjoy the financial rewards. These benefits matter for individual well-being but also for overall productivity and output of the economy as a whole.

However, there are many reasons to be concerned about growing income and wealth inequality. According to the World Inequality Database (WID), income inequality has increased in nearly all regions of the world in recent decades — but at very different rates: rapidly in North America, China, India and Russia; only moderately in Europe; in the Middle East, sub-Saharan Africa and Brazil inequality has remained fairly stable, but at high levels.

Inequality is not determined by income levelBecause inequality levels vary so much even between countries with similar levels of development, it is clear that inequality is NOT determined by the level of national income. It is instead the result of national policies and institutions that do or do not cater for economic growth that is "fair" or "inclusive". Where policies/institutions fail, growth does not generate higher income and better opportunities for everyone.

Inequality of outcomes vs inequality of opportunity

DefinitionsInequalities in income and wealth are considered "inequalities in outcomes". Income/wealth may be the outcome of hard work, sensible life choices or initiative — but it is often simply the outcome of having, or not having, more assets (e.g. inherited property).
"Inequality of opportunity" means a person's education, health care access, jobs available and ultimately the income they can earn are NOT based on their achievements, but on their circumstances at birth — e.g. gender, ethnicity, place born, parents' profession, socio-economic background.

SDG Goal 10 (Reduced Inequalities) is directly relevant here, as is the guiding key concept of Equity, which runs throughout this chapter, alongside Economic Wellbeing.

Each country has its own Lorenz curve based on its income data.

The Gini index (Gini coefficient)

DefinitionThe Gini index is an indicator that neatly summarizes the information in a Lorenz curve. It is derived from the Lorenz curve as a ratio of the area between the line of equality and a country's Lorenz curve (area a) to the total area under the line of absolute equality (areas a + b).
Gini index = Area (a) ÷ [Area (a) + Area (b)]

The higher the Gini index, the more unequal the distribution of income (it is sometimes expressed as a number between 0 and 1, and sometimes as a percentage/whole number between 0 and 100, as in this textbook's data). Of the four countries in Table 22.1, Bolivia has the highest income inequality with a Gini index of 58.2, while Croatia has the lowest with a score of 29.0.

Caution: Gini index ≠ level of developmentAlthough reducing income inequality may be an important development objective, one must be careful using the Gini index as a basis for evaluating a country's development progress. While low-income countries tend to have higher inequality than high-income countries on average, there is no hard and fast correlation between a country's level of development (e.g. as measured by its Human Development Index, HDI) and its Gini index. Example: the US has a high level of human development (HDI rank 13) yet a relatively high Gini index (41.5); Mauritania has a much lower level of development (HDI rank 159) but a lower Gini value (32.6). (Source: Statistical Update 2018, Human Development Indices and Indicators, UNDP.)

Income inequality vs wealth inequality

Income vs WealthIncome is a flow: all the money people earn from wages/salaries, interest from savings and bonds, dividends earned from owning stocks/shares, rent, and money gained from selling assets for more than they cost (capital gains). Because individuals must report all sources of income for tax purposes, statistical data on income and its distribution is relatively easy for governments to acquire.
Wealth (or "net worth") is a stock: the value of all of a person's assets minus their total liabilities. Assets include houses/property, money in savings accounts, investments in stocks and bonds, and retirement savings. Liabilities include all debts owed, including mortgages, student loans, car loans and credit card debt. The difference between a person's assets and liabilities is their wealth.

Wealth is substantially more concentrated than income. According to the Credit Suisse Global Wealth Report 2018, globally the bottom 50% of adults own less than 1% of total wealth, the richest 10% own 85% of total wealth, and the top 1% alone own more than half of all household wealth.

Poverty: Definitions and Measurement

How are inequality and poverty linked?

When there is a high level of income inequality, a significant portion of the population is likely to be living in some form of poverty, and this poverty is likely to perpetuate the inequalities that exist — a self-reinforcing relationship.

What is poverty?

Poverty is a global problem and a clear sign of a lack of equity. UN Sustainable Development Goal 1 (SDG1) is to "end poverty in all its forms everywhere", and has seven targets addressing different forms of poverty (e.g. income poverty, food poverty, energy poverty, child poverty, old-age poverty). This chapter focuses on two particular types: absolute poverty and relative poverty.

Absolute poverty

DefinitionAbsolute poverty occurs when the income of a person or household is not enough for them to meet even their most basic needs of shelter, food, safe drinking water, health, and education. The World Bank sets this as the "International Poverty Line" ($1.90/day as of 2015, revised upward from $1.25/day). If people earn less than this, they are said to be living in absolute (or extreme) poverty.

The $1.90 figure is converted into local currency using "purchasing power parity" (PPP) exchange rates, also called "international dollars" — not the official market exchange rate — so that the figure represents roughly the same real amount of goods and services in any country, allowing for meaningful international comparison.

Extreme poverty rates have fallen by more than half since 1990, according to the United Nations. Despite this remarkable achievement, millions and millions of people in developing regions still live on less than $1.90/day, facing extreme deprivation, and remain at risk of slipping back into extreme poverty. SDG target 1.1 aims for the elimination of extreme poverty by 2030.

What is extreme poverty? (UN Guiding Principles)"Persons living in poverty are confronted by the most severe obstacles — physical, economic, cultural and social — to accessing their rights and entitlements. Consequently, they experience many interrelated and mutually reinforcing deprivations — including dangerous work conditions, unsafe housing, lack of nutritious food, unequal access to justice, and limited political power and access to health care — that prevent them from realizing their rights and perpetuate their poverty. Persons experiencing poverty live in a vicious cycle of powerlessness, stigmatization, discrimination, exclusion and material deprivation, which all mutually reinforce one another."

Absolute poverty vs relative poverty

Absolute vs Relative povertyThe value of absolute poverty is fixed according to the cost of achieving very basic needs (though it may be raised periodically, e.g. by the World Bank). Relative poverty, by contrast, is a comparative measure based on the living standards in a particular country. A standard measure of relative poverty is earning less than 50% of median income in that country. Example: if median income in a country is $50,000, a person earning less than $25,000 would be considered in relative poverty — a level of income below $25,000 would be inadequate for an acceptable standard of living in that country. Because it is defined in relative terms, relative poverty is also, in effect, a measure of income inequality.

Absolute poverty is a much more significant problem in developing countries, where the eradication of extreme poverty and unacceptably low living standards is the priority. SDG target 1.2 aims to "reduce at least by half the proportion of men, women and children of all ages living in poverty in all its dimensions according to national definitions" — recognizing that relative poverty also matters globally, though living even close to a very low income level still involves very low living standards.

How is poverty measured?

Absolute poverty is measured using the World Bank's international poverty line, currently $1.90 PPP. Countries also set their own "national poverty lines" reflecting relative poverty, usually set at an arbitrary percentage of median income. For example, Australia sets its national poverty line at 50% of median income; by this measure, 13.2% of the population was living in poverty in 2015–2016.

Canada uses such a "market-basket measure", based on the cost of a basket of goods and services needed for a "modest, basic standard of living" for a family of four (two adults, two children). The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

Poverty means people lack basic necessities and exist in poor conditions — these are referred to as deprivations. Simply looking at income does not reveal what individual deprivations poor people actually experience. Recognizing that people experience poverty in many different forms (as SDG1 emphasizes, "in all its forms"), the UN has developed the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), a "composite indicator" that measures the many specific dimensions of poverty rather than looking at income alone.

People have basic needs in three key dimensions: health, education and standard of living. Each dimension has equal weight in measuring the extent of poverty. Within each dimension, the MPI identifies specific indicators, and notes what it means to be poor/deprived on each one.

The UNDP 2018 Statistical Update presents estimates for 105 developing countries with a combined population of 5.7 billion (77% of the world total). It found that about 1.3 billion people in the countries covered — 23.3% of their entire population — lived in multidimensional poverty between 2006 and 2016/17.

Difficulties in measuring poverty

  • There are many different types of poverty (absolute, relative, extreme, acute, income, multidimensional), and each has its own definition, but definitions vary between countries, making poverty inherently difficult to define consistently.
  • Some elements of poverty are difficult or impossible to measure — e.g. feelings of uncertainty, vulnerability, fear (where the next meal will come from, whether you can afford shoes for your child, how you'll make it to the next payday). These are valid dimensions of poverty but hard to capture, and simple income measures may miss them.
  • Measures of poverty are most often based on household surveys. While surveying techniques have improved, they require enormous resources, and survey quality may be poor in resource-constrained countries.
  • Because governments are judged on their ability to help people out of poverty, they have an incentive to adjust national poverty lines/definitions to make statistics look better — even though simply redefining the term does nothing to actually help people meet their basic needs.

Causes of Inequality and Poverty

The causes of inequality and poverty are complex and interrelated; this is not an exhaustive list.

Inequality of opportunity

People are born into vastly different circumstances and face vastly different opportunities as they grow up, get an education, find employment, and grow older. The poverty trap / poverty cycleThese examples illustrate a common reality: both economic advantages and economic disadvantages tend to reinforce themselves through a life cycle. Children born into poverty are likely to have very few opportunities to move out of poverty as they grow up — this is known as the poverty trap or poverty cycle. As the OECD notes: "The vicious confluence of poor educational opportunities, low skills and limited employment prospects can trap people in situations where they are also far more likely to be exposed to environmental hazards and violence. As a result of this multidimensional inequality, while some individuals, citizens and regions thrive, others fall further behind."
Key concept: ChangeThis section (the poverty trap / cycle of advantage and disadvantage across generations and lifetimes) illustrates the key concept of Change — specifically, the (limited) capacity for change in a person's economic position given their starting circumstances.

Social mobility

DefinitionsSocial mobility is the ability of people/households to move up or down the socio-economic "ladder". Intragenerational social mobility refers to the ability of an individual to move from one income level to another within their own lifetime. Intergenerational social mobility refers to the ability of a person to move to a higher (or lower) income level than their parents (e.g. a child born to parents who did not attend university going to university and getting a higher-median-income job would be upward intergenerational mobility).

The OECD report "A Broken Social Elevator? How to Promote Social Mobility" notes bleakly that both intergenerational and intragenerational social mobility are in decline among OECD countries. Children from disadvantaged backgrounds face too many challenges and too few opportunities to move up the ladder, and those at the top of the ladder have the tools to pass on their advantages to their children — reinforcing income and wealth inequalities. Evidence suggests that in more unequal societies (characterized by both unequal incomes and unequal opportunities), people are less likely to be socially mobile and move up or down from their level on the income scale, especially if in the top or bottom 5% of earners. This situation is even harsher in developing countries, where children in relative poverty face even fewer opportunities and greater challenges in health and education; those in absolute poverty, especially in Least Developed Countries, have virtually no opportunities to improve their standards of living.

Discrimination

Inequality may result from unfair treatment, or discrimination, against certain people due to factors including their gender, ethnicity, age, religion, sexual orientation or socio-economic status. Discrimination manifests both in the opportunities people face and in the outcomes they obtain. For example, lower-income children are discriminated against as they do not receive the same educational opportunities as higher-income children, and as a result are unable to secure the higher-paying jobs as they move into the labour market.

Wage discriminationWage discrimination is a particular type of discrimination that contributes directly to inequality: it occurs when workers in similar positions receive different wages on the basis of gender, race, ethnicity, age, sexual orientation, or any other characteristic that is not related to their skills or productivity.

Did you know? (Gender pay data, US, Joint Economic Committee 2016 report): A woman working full time, year-round, earns about $10,800 less per year than a man, based on median annual earnings — a disparity that can add up to nearly half a million dollars over a career. On a percentage basis, a woman earns only 79% of what a man earns (the "earnings ratio"). Career earnings result in even greater disparity in retirement income — women 75 and older are almost twice as likely to live in poverty as men. Women of colour face even larger gender pay gaps: African-American women are, on average, paid only 60% and Latinas only 55% of what white, non-Hispanic men are paid. On the positive side, women's increased participation in the paid labour force has been a major driver of economic growth in recent decades, and the US Council of Economic Advisers estimates the US economy is $2.0 trillion bigger today than it would have been if women had not increased their labour force participation and hours since 1970; policies that narrow the gender pay gap and help more women work full-time in paid labour would further decrease income inequality.

Differences in human capital

Basic economic theory (supply and demand) can explain why a person's income may vary by type of labour: if there is a lower supply of highly-skilled computer engineers than of unskilled manual workers, the wage of the higher-skilled worker will be higher, hence inequality. This may be justified on the grounds that the higher-skilled person should be rewarded for their own investment in human capital (e.g. years of costly education/training).

However, in recent decades, the wages of skilled workers have risen at a much higher rate than those of unskilled workers, increasing inequality. Furthermore, a reduction in trade union power in many countries has meant workers in low-skilled jobs have much less job security and protection from falling real wages.

Different levels of ownership of resources, particularly unequal ownership of capital

When people own physical capital (e.g. companies), they can earn profit; when they own financial capital (e.g. shares in companies), they can earn a share of profits through dividends. The higher a person's income, the more physical and financial capital they will tend to own. In contrast, lower-income people tend not to own capital, and the vast majority of their income comes from wages and salaries. In recent years, average profits and share prices have risen at a much higher rate than average wages and salaries, translating into even higher incomes for wealthy people with financial assets — increasing the concentration of wealth.

Globalization and technological progress affecting labour types differently

Increased global trade and the freedom of companies to locate production facilities worldwide means there is less demand for manufacturing workers in developed countries. Technological progress means machinery and computerized technologies can now do the repetitive work skilled workers used to do, compounding the fall in demand for such labour. Wages for manufacturing workers in developed countries have been depressed, and there has been rising structural (technological) unemployment. Meanwhile, labour markets still demand the skilled labour of highly-educated people in sectors such as finance, technology and electronics, so incomes for professionals in those industries have risen — increasing inequality.

"Hollowing out of the middle class"This term describes the pattern where a relatively small percentage of people earn high incomes, a notably larger percentage earn low incomes, and the numbers earning middle incomes shrink — a polarization of the income distribution associated with globalization and automation.

Market-oriented supply-side policies

In the 1980s, market-oriented, new-classical supply-side policies became very popular in the UK (under PM Margaret Thatcher) and the US (under President Ronald Reagan). There is a strong correlation between the introduction of such policies and rising inequality. Deregulation in financial markets greatly increased opportunities for wealthy people to earn more income from investments — the same deregulation is often blamed for the 2007 financial crisis, which significantly deepened inequalities. It has been argued that deregulation allowed the financial system to take much greater risks than it would have done under effective supervision.

Government tax and benefits policies

While most countries implement "progressive" tax policies, the top rate of income tax has fallen in many countries. Corporate profits are generally taxed at a lower rate than income taxes, so owners of capital benefit. Governments also tend to tax income from savings, dividends and capital gains at a lower rate than income from wages and salaries. People with higher incomes and significant wealth benefit from such policies, widening inequalities in income and wealth. There has also been a move towards "austerity" policies in many countries as governments try to reduce public debt, involving less spending in all areas, including areas specifically aimed at redistributing income, such as unemployment insurance, child benefits and housing allowances. These austerity policies have therefore tended to worsen the incomes and living standards of lower-income people.

Labour market reforms / non-standard employment

Labour market reforms to reduce the power of trade unions and make labour markets more flexible mean workers have less power in collective bargaining with employers over wages and working conditions, depressing wages. Labour market reforms have also allowed firms to offer more "non-standard employment".

Standard vs non-standard employmentStandard employment is when a person has a permanent, secure, full-time contract. Non-standard employment includes temporary workers, part-time workers, workers on zero-hours contracts, and self-employed workers — offering far less security and lower incomes. On a zero-hours contract, an employer does not have to guarantee any set number of working hours; workers are called upon as needed.

While there are benefits of non-standard employment to both firms and some workers, the International Labour Organisation (ILO) notes it poses many risks for workers, firms, labour markets and society, including: a lack of job security, lower earnings, uncertain working hours with negative implications for work–life balance, concerns over health and safety, and an inability of non-standard workers to access social security benefits such as unemployment insurance or pensions.

Unequal status and power

Where ownership of resources and income in an economy is concentrated among a small percentage of the population, wealthier people may have a disproportionate say in the development of government policies. When people running for public office finance their campaigns with donations from private individuals and companies, they are more likely, once elected, to develop policies that serve the interests of the wealthy people/businesses that supported them — such as reduced minimum wages, reduced business regulations and tax cuts for the wealthy — worsening the economic wellbeing of lower-income people. Low-income people are also less likely to become involved in the political process (lacking the background, education, social networks or time to engage), so the "voice" of low-income people may not be adequately represented in government policy.

Consequences of Inequality and Poverty

For economic growth

The link between inequality and economic growth is theoretically and empirically debated:

  • Inequality may help growth: large gaps between high- and low-income groups provide incentives for entrepreneurship and innovation. High incomes give lower-paid workers motivation to work harder and improve their education/skills so they can move into higher-paid jobs — the bigger the gap, the greater the incentive. Also, high levels of investment are needed for an economy to grow, and the pools of savings needed for that investment will not be available in a more equal society (since it is often higher-income households/individuals who save/invest most).
  • Inequality may harm growth: where inequality and poverty are high, there are increasingly fewer opportunities for lower-income members of society (poverty trap), and children from poorer backgrounds are likely to leave school without appropriate skills and training, resulting in lower productivity levels that damage economic growth. As the OECD states: "If a large swathe of the population is unable to invest in its skills, that's bad for the economy." The social and political instability often associated with high levels of poverty can also deter investment (and hence growth).
Key concept: EfficiencyThis debate about whether inequality boosts or damages economic growth/efficiency (incentive effects vs lost human capital and productivity) is a direct illustration of the "efficiency" key concept. It underlines an important point made by a wide range of economists: that high inequality is potentially damaging for everyone, not just the poor.

For living standards and social stability

Logically, wide gaps in income mean that those living below the average level of income are living in some degree of poverty. Poverty leads to unacceptably low living standards and economic vulnerability — people in poverty must make difficult choices about how to use very limited incomes (e.g. between food, fuel, school supplies and transportation). The Joseph Rowntree Foundation describes what it means to be poor in the UK: "Poverty means not being able to heat your home, pay your rent or buy the essentials for your children. It means waking up every day facing insecurity, uncertainty and impossible decisions about money. It means marginalization — and even discrimination — because of your financial circumstances. The constant stress it causes can lead to problems that deprive people of the chance to play a full part in society."

In unequal societies, neighbourhoods and areas of cities may become identified by their income groups, with "rich" and "poor" areas, and this may be self-perpetuating: if government-subsidized housing is concentrated in particular areas, this results in greater concentration of people with low incomes; low-income areas are less likely to have good schools and attract good teachers, so education prospects suffer; low-income areas may also suffer from poor infrastructure and facilities, limiting opportunities for residents.

Social stability is precarious in situations of high inequality. Where people live in extremely disadvantaged circumstances with low living standards, resentment and hostility may be high — particularly where young people feel they have no job prospects or legitimate ways of earning a living. There may be increased gang-related activity, and people in impoverished areas may feel unsafe, with law enforcement unable to deal with the dangers. Social instability may result in threats to private property. When a significant proportion of a population lives in relative poverty and perceives the system as unfair and not working for them, they are unlikely to support the political system and democratic process, giving rise to unstable social and political climates and tension between low-income people and those that govern them. This further emphasizes the conclusion that high levels of inequality are harmful for everyone.

Government Policies to Reduce Poverty and Inequality

Inequality and poverty are not simply a function of national income; they are the result of national policies and institutions that shape the way a nation's advantages and benefits are shared. Governments therefore have it within their power to shape institutions and develop policies that reduce inequalities of opportunity, income and wealth. Areas of policy inquiry include: the role of a country's tax structure in achieving equity; policies to improve people's opportunities to reach their potential; the effectiveness of transfer payments; the advantages and disadvantages of minimum wages; and Universal Basic Income as a means of reducing poverty and inequality.

Taxation

Governments at all levels (municipal, state/provincial, national) impose a huge array of taxes for many reasons: indirect taxes may reduce consumption of goods causing negative externalities, pursue environmental goals, or protect domestic producers; direct taxes may be raised or lowered to manage aggregate demand; and taxes fund government expenditure.

Progressive income taxHouseholds pay direct taxes in the form of income tax, based on all forms of income received in a year. Most governments implement a progressive income tax system as the main way of achieving greater equity: as income rises, people pay a higher percentage of that income as tax. A person earning a low income might pay a small percentage of their income to government, while a person earning a very high income pays a much larger percentage.

Many countries also have property taxes (a tax on the value of a person's home/property — higher value, higher tax). A few countries have "wealth" taxes, charging people based on the value of their financial assets, though relatively few countries impose such taxes and there is much political debate about them. In 2014, French economist Thomas Piketty's book Capital in the Twenty-First Century documented rising wealth and income inequality over time and called for a coordinated global tax on wealth — a popular demand among those concerned about growing inequalities and the concentration of global wealth among a very small percentage of the population. A small tax on the wealth of the super-rich could generate significant funds, potentially allowing government to reduce taxes on middle- and low-income earners, or finance important infrastructure.

Businesses pay taxes on their profits (corporate taxes). Rates vary by country: in some, the rate is constant regardless of profit level; in others, corporate tax is progressive (higher rates on higher profits). In reality, despite official rates, companies often take advantage of "tax breaks" that let them pay a much lower "effective" tax rate than the official corporate rate; very large multinationals are often in the media spotlight for the relatively small percentage of taxes they pay as a share of their total profits, by employing global tax experts to minimize their tax commitments. To reduce global inequalities, it is argued large multinationals need to pay appropriate taxes on their profits — the IMF has stated "a new approach to international corporate tax rules is urgent."

The "marginal rate" of taxOften (though not in all countries), a certain amount of income is untaxed. In these systems, a person earning a low income (in the first tax bracket) pays no tax at all. As income moves into the second bracket, a percentage of that additional income (not all of it) is paid as tax — this percentage is called the "marginal rate" of tax. For example, a marginal rate of 30% would apply only to income earned between $10,000 and $25,000. If income rises above $50,000, that additional income falls in the top bracket at a marginal rate of 50% — but note this 50% applies only to income over $50,000, not to the whole income.

Progressive, regressive and proportional taxes

Progressive vs regressive vs proportionalProgressive tax: takes a larger percentage of a larger income and a smaller percentage of a smaller income (most direct tax structures, e.g. income tax, are progressive).
Regressive tax: takes a larger share of income from lower-income people than from higher-income people — not a deliberate policy to harm low-income people, simply a mathematical fact. Most indirect taxes are regressive: e.g. a $1.00 tax on a litre of diesel, where people spend $50/month on petrol taxes regardless of income — for a person earning $500/month, this $50 tax represents 10% of income; for a person earning $2,500/month, the same $50 tax represents only 2% of income. The tax is regressive because it takes a larger percentage of income from the lower earner. Indirect taxes remain an important government revenue source and can discourage consumption of goods creating negative externalities, but because they are regressive, they worsen income inequality.
Proportional tax: the proportion of income paid in tax is constant across all income levels (a "flat tax"). It is argued a proportional structure removes the disincentive effect associated with progressive taxes — if people know they won't be taxed at a higher rate as they earn more, they may have more incentive to work harder.

Arguments for and against progressive taxation (Key concept: Efficiency)

Arguments FORSince equity is about fairness, it may be seen as unfair to charge all income earners the same rate of tax, since lower-income households need to use more of their income to meet basic needs and taxes could substantially reduce their economic wellbeing. A progressive tax structure narrows the gap between high- and low-income people and is a means of achieving greater equity; it also gives government the funds and the ability to make its tax structure more progressive (raising rates at higher incomes / lowering them at lower incomes), and to finance redistributive policies through transfer payments (child allowances, maternity/paternity benefits, unemployment insurance, pensions). Countries with more progressive tax structures (higher average and marginal rates) can generally afford more social services and social welfare programmes supporting lower-income households.
Arguments AGAINSTFree-market economists (see Chapter 18) who support market-oriented supply-side policies argue higher marginal taxes create disincentives: if people feel hard work simply results in paying a higher rate of tax, they have less incentive to work harder and improve their economic situation; higher taxes could discourage entrepreneurial activity and even encourage entrepreneurs to leave the country for more "favourable" tax climates; taxes on interest, dividends and capital gains (which particularly affect higher earners) could depress saving and the purchasing of stocks and shares, depriving financial markets of necessary funds. However, there is little evidence that such disincentive effects have any notable impact on the economic wellbeing of the richest within any population — economies with very progressive tax structures do not demonstrate weaker economic performance. It may be argued the benefits of greater equality far outweigh possible costs to those on very high incomes.

Transfer payments

DefinitionGovernments use tax revenues to redistribute income and provide different types of assistance to groups in the economy to improve their living standards and opportunities. These are known as transfer payments, since the assistance represents income transferred from some groups in the economy to support others. They may also be called "welfare payments" or "social security payments". Types include child support, maternity and paternity benefits, old age pensions, housing allowances, and fuel allowances to help pay for heating and other payments.

Universal transfers are given to everyone (e.g. child support given to all parents for all children they have). Means-tested transfers are given only to people whose incomes are below a certain level, or who meet eligibility criteria based on need — targeted spending aimed at reaching those who clearly need the support, seen as an efficient way to reduce poverty and inequality.

Policies to promote equal opportunities through investment in human capital

It is not enough simply to increase the quantity of government spending on education and/or health — it is the nature and quality of that spending that brings about improvements in human capital. Governments need to design policies that ensure equal opportunities for people at all income levels, requiring targeted subsidies, transfers and programmes that support lower-income households who are currently locked out of many opportunities. Regions/cities with low social mobility can learn from evidence of successes elsewhere to develop policies that help break the cycle of poverty. As the OECD's report on promoting social mobility notes, governments need to create opportunities for improved health and education at ALL age levels, from pre-school through university and adult education/retraining. Example policies:

  • Public health insurance (universal health care) so all households can access the health care system.
  • Programmes providing pre-natal and post-natal care to low-income families.
  • Improved access to good-quality childcare and preschool programmes for disadvantaged children (e.g. via subsidies to make early education affordable).
  • Conditional Cash Transfers (CCTs): payments to families given on condition that the parent(s) meet certain requirements related to their children's education and health (e.g. immunization, a minimum percentage of school attendance). These programmes reduce current poverty by providing cash while increasing the human capital of children/youths so they benefit from more opportunities later, reducing future poverty.
  • School food policies so children from low-income households have nutritious meals that help them learn effectively.
  • Health education in schools and the media to improve diet and nutrition.
  • Recruitment of quality teachers and more educational resources for schools in disadvantaged areas.
  • After-school programmes teaching non-academic skills and providing safe environments.
  • Targeted support for low-performing students to prevent school dropouts.
  • Apprenticeships for older students to prepare for the workplace.
  • Financial support, counselling and tutoring to help students from lower-income families attend and stay in higher education.
  • Training and work experience programmes to increase the employability of adults who lose their jobs.
  • Community programmes to provide parenting support.
  • More resources targeted towards elder care to help senior citizens struggling on low pensions.

Policies to reduce gender and other discrimination

In terms of basic human rights, all people in an economy should have the same access to private and public services, jobs and opportunities regardless of their gender, disabilities, race, religion, political stance or sexual orientation. It is the government's responsibility to have effective policies in place to prevent unfair treatment and thereby work towards decreasing inequalities that significantly damage people's standards of living. Governments and large companies often have "diversity" policies to ensure equal and fair treatment for all groups. Policies could include:

  • Laws related to salaries and wages to ensure equal pay for work of equal value.
  • Positive/affirmative action to encourage businesses and government bodies to have greater diversity in their workplaces and provide more opportunities for previously-discriminated-against groups.
  • Legislation making it illegal to discriminate against individuals in, e.g., education, employment, or the rental market.
  • Diversity quotas or targets to ensure discriminated-against groups make up at least a certain required percentage of people working in a particular capacity (e.g. requiring a certain percentage of women on particular government committees, giving previously disadvantaged groups greater representation and theoretically promoting their interests more effectively). To promote gender equality, governments might mandate a certain percentage of company boards of directors be women.
  • Requirements for accessibility so that a person cannot be excluded due to disabilities.

Minimum wage

Definition (ILO)A minimum wage is "the minimum amount of remuneration that an employer can legally pay a worker". According to the International Labour Organization (ILO), "the purpose of minimum wages is to protect workers against unduly low pay. They help ensure a just and equitable share of the fruits of progress to all, and a minimum living wage to all who are employed and in need of such protection. Minimum wages can also be one element of a policy to overcome poverty and reduce inequality."

While having a job is one important way of ensuring a decent standard of living, many countries face the problem of "working poverty", where people have jobs but still live below their country's national poverty line. One reason may be that in some countries the national minimum wage is not sufficient to ensure a reasonable standard of living. It may be argued it is simply not fair for a person to live in poverty while working a full-time job earning the minimum wage; an appropriate solution would be to raise the minimum wage to a level guaranteeing an income allowing for a reasonable standard of living, given the significant negative consequences of poverty and inequality.

Additional arguments FOR raising the minimum wage rate:

  • Higher wages encourage people to join the labour force, reducing unemployment.
  • Higher wages result in increased consumption by low-wage workers, increasing aggregate demand and employment.
  • Minimum wage work is generally done by low-skilled workers (retail, fast food, cleaners, carers) who were often deprived of the educational opportunities that would have given them skills for higher-paid jobs; a higher minimum wage may allow them to escape their poverty trap and provide better opportunities for their own children.

Did you know? In 2019, Spain increased its minimum wage by 22% — the largest increase in 40 years — from €736 to €900 per month, resulting in an annual income of €12,600 for most workers paid 14 times a year, as well as domestic employees. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez claimed "a rich country cannot have poor workers", and the official decree stated the rise "is a decisive factor for the creation of employment and economic recovery to be translated into a progressive real reduction of poverty and of wage inequality".

Universal Basic Income (UBI)

Welfare programmes providing transfer payments (such as unemployment benefits) are essential to reduce poverty and ensure that people who need government support are able to get it. However, many such programmes require significant paperwork, conditions and resources to make sure recipients are eligible for the benefits and to police against those who "cheat" the system.

The poverty-trap problem with means-tested benefitsConsider a person receiving a jobless payment of $900/month. If they are offered a job paying $950/month, they would lose their $900 unemployment benefit, and would clearly be left in poverty and only marginally better off (if at all) after the costs of working. An option would be to apply for a different type of income support, but this would be time-consuming and difficult, and might leave them unable to afford the basics for some time. This creates a disincentive to take the job — the opposite of the intention of the benefits system.
DefinitionUniversal Basic Income (UBI) is a welfare scheme in which every single citizen receives a guaranteed amount, each week or month, with no conditions or eligibility requirements. The amount given would be enough to keep people above the poverty line, and would be the same for everyone, regardless of their income or whether or not they are working.

UBI is nicknamed "free money for everyone", and the idea has gained popularity worldwide in recent years. Proponents of such schemes have existed for centuries and include philosophers, politicians and economists from across the ideological spectrum, including free-market economists such as Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman. The observation that technology and automation are going to continue eliminating certain forms of work, and increasing inequalities, has led many people to recognize that different solutions need to be found to ensure people can live in dignity and enjoy reasonable living standards.
