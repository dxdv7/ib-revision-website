28.1 Economic Growth vs Economic Development

Real-world issue for this chapter: Why is economic development uneven?

By the end of this chapter you should be able to: distinguish between economic growth and economic development; explain the relationship between economic growth and economic development; define sustainable development; outline the current status of the Sustainable Development Goals; Higher Level explain the relationship between sustainability and poverty; explain and give examples of common characteristics of developing countries; explain and give examples of the diversity that exists between developing countries.

National income statistics (GDP, GNI) tell us about a country's economic growth — an increase in real output over time. Traditional economic theory assumed that growth, together with the industrialization that goes with it, was equivalent to economic development. This assumption is simplistic and incorrect. Development economics grew up as its own branch of the subject precisely because economic growth is not equivalent to economic development.

Definitions Economic growth is a one-dimensional concept: a simple increase in the real output of an economy over time (an increase in real GDP/GNI). Economic development is a far more complex, multidimensional concept covering improvements in living standards and economic well-being/welfare more broadly. For growth to translate into development it must be inclusive (it must benefit all people in the economy, not just a small percentage of high-income people) and it must be sustainable (it must not come at a cost that is borne by future generations).

A very basic definition of development might be "an improvement in living standards or economic well-being". This is still simplistic, and the concept remains highly subjective — there is a wide range of possible explanations of what development actually means.

Amartya Sen and freedom as development

Development economist Amartya Sen (1933–present) makes a powerful link between development and freedom. Sen was born in West Bengal, India, and witnessed the Bengal famine as a child, which shaped his life's work on poverty and entitlement (his book Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation). His work, alongside Pakistani economist Mahbub ul Haq, led to the creation of the Human Development Index and the annual Human Development Report published by the UNDP. Sen won the 1998 Nobel Prize in Economics for his contribution to welfare economics.

Sen's view of development "Development can be seen… as a process of expanding the real freedoms that people enjoy." This contrasts with narrower views of development that identify it only with GNP growth, rising personal incomes, industrialization, technological advance or social modernization. Development requires removing major sources of "unfreedom": poverty as well as tyranny, poor economic opportunities as well as systematic social deprivation, neglect of public facilities as well as intolerance of oppressive states. Lack of substantive freedom relates directly to economic poverty — it robs people of the freedom to satisfy hunger, achieve sufficient nutrition, obtain remedies for illness, or enjoy clean water and sanitation.
Key concept: Equity Sen's freedom-based view of development is closely tied to the key concept of Equity — development is fair only when opportunities and basic freedoms are extended to everyone, not concentrated among the privileged.
Key concept: Economic well-being The multidimensional nature of development (health, education, freedom, living standards) illustrates the key concept of Economic well-being — true progress is about much more than the monetary size of an economy.

Theory of knowledge: Todaro's three core values

Economist Michael Todaro built on the work of Denis Goulet to present three "core values" or fundamental needs that must be met for development to occur (the sustained elevation of an entire society toward a "better" or "more humane" life):

  • Sustenance: the ability to meet basic life-sustaining needs for food, shelter, health and protection.
  • Self-esteem: the ability of people and communities to develop a sense of self-worth, identity, dignity and respect.
  • Freedom from servitude and the ability to choose: having a range of choices for people and societies, and freedom from oppression by external forces.

Because development is inherently subjective, it is debatable whether these values can ever be treated as universal, objective "truths".

28.2 Does Economic Growth Lead to Economic Development?

The sources of economic growth

To assess whether growth causes development, we must first understand where growth comes from. Sources of growth can be grouped under four headings:

  • 1. Natural factors: anything that increases the quantity and/or quality of land. Increasing the quantity of land is difficult and usually only has a small effect on total land area, unless the country is already very small (e.g. Singapore has increased its land area via landfill from 581.5 km² at independence in 1965 to around 721.5 km² today, a roughly 24% increase; the same absolute increase for neighbouring Malaysia would be only about 0.04% of its land area). Most countries therefore focus on improving the quality of land — fertilization, better land-use planning, improved agricultural methods, and building upwards rather than outwards (e.g. Hong Kong).
  • 2. Human capital factors: the quantity of human capital can be increased by population growth or immigration, but most developing countries are not keen to increase population size (and even those that are, like Singapore, find it a slow process). Emphasis is therefore placed on improving the quality of human capital: improved health care, improved and vocational education, retraining for the unemployed, and provision of fresh water and sanitation (which improves health and thus the quality of human capital).
  • 3. Physical capital and technological factors: growth can come from increasing the quantity and/or quality of physical capital (factory buildings, machinery, shops, offices, motor vehicles — distinct from social capital such as schools, roads, hospitals and houses). The quantity of physical capital depends on saving, domestic investment, government involvement and foreign investment. Quality is improved by higher education, R&D, and access to foreign technology/expertise. Improved extraction techniques (e.g. oil drilling, mining) effectively increase the quantity of a primary-product factor of production.
  • 4. Institutional factors: meaningful growth requires an adequate banking system, a structured legal system, a good education system, reasonable infrastructure, political stability and good international relations. These same factors are often also sources of economic development.
Capital widening vs capital deepening Capital widening occurs when extra capital is used with an increased amount of labour, so the ratio of capital per worker stays roughly the same — total production rises, but productivity (output per worker) is unlikely to change. Capital deepening occurs when the amount of capital per worker increases (often reflecting improved technology) — this usually raises labour productivity as well as total production.
Growth ≠ Development The benefits of economic growth should not be overstated, but there is significant evidence growth has helped raise millions of people out of poverty — higher incomes mean higher material living standards, and higher incomes mean higher tax revenue that governments can spend on health, education and infrastructure. However, it should never be assumed that growth always leads to development, and it is certainly not the case that growth will always be sustainable development.

28.3 Sustainable Development

IB syllabus definition "Endless economic growth, based on the consumption of finite resources, cannot continue indefinitely…"

Dr Gro Harlem Brundtland and the Brundtland Commission

Gro Harlem Brundtland (1939–present) was born in Oslo, Norway, trained as a physician, and became Norway's first woman Prime Minister (1981), serving three terms in total. In 1983 the UN Secretary General asked her to chair the World Commission on Environment and Development (the "Brundtland Commission"), tasked with evaluating critical environmental and developmental problems and proposing solutions. She later became Director General of the World Health Organization (1998–2003).

The Commission's report, Our Common Future, was released in April 1987. It was in this widely distributed report that the concept of "sustainable development" was first identified and promoted, and it led to the 1992 Rio Earth Summit, attended by almost 200 world leaders.

Definition: Sustainable development "Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." The Commission held that continued environmental degradation and use of non-renewable resources at too fast a rate cannot be sustained into the future.

As economies grow, demand for more goods and services requires more energy and resources; factories, power plants and households all consume vast amounts of resources and energy, creating earth-threatening problems (the "climate crisis") which can be examined using the economics of negative externalities of production/consumption and the threats to common access resources (see Chapter 9).

"Uneconomic growth" (Herman Daly) Economist Herman Daly defines "uneconomic growth" as occurring "when increases in production come at an expense in resources and well-being that is worth more than the items made". Economic growth based on current consumption patterns is therefore not sustainable — it is, in reality, uneconomic growth.

Climate change effects: felt most severely in developing countries

Tragically, those who will be most harmed by the climate crisis are those who contributed least to the greenhouse gas emissions that created it.

  • Access to safe water will become even more precarious — already, more than one billion people lack access to safe water.
  • Tropical diseases may spread further north.
  • Droughts will become more frequent and intense in Asia and Africa; flooding will likely worsen in temperate and humid regions.
  • Food production in the tropics and sub-tropics is likely to suffer; production could become easier in middle/high latitudes, but this will not necessarily lessen the overall risk to food security.
  • Millions will be affected by rising sea levels, including coastal areas and low-lying islands in the Caribbean and Pacific Ocean.

Economic progress must therefore be measured in terms of economic development that can be achieved in a sustainable manner.

28.4 The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

From the MDGs to the SDGs

The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) began in 2000 as a global effort to tackle the indignity of poverty, establishing measurable, universally agreed objectives on extreme poverty and hunger, deadly disease and primary education, to be met by 2015.

Key MDG achievements (2000–2015):

  • More than 1 billion people lifted out of extreme poverty (since 1990).
  • Child mortality dropped by more than half (since 1990).
  • The number of out-of-school children dropped by more than half (since 1990).
  • HIV/AIDS infections fell by almost 40% (since 2000).

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were born at the United Nations Conference on Sustainable Development in Rio de Janeiro in 2012, replacing the MDGs, with the objective of producing a set of universal goals addressing the urgent environmental, political and economic challenges facing the world. The SDGs came into effect in January 2016 and will continue until 2030. All 17 goals interconnect — success on one affects success on others (e.g. dealing with climate change impacts how we manage natural resources; achieving gender equality or better health helps eradicate poverty).

Higher Level 28.5 The Relationship Between Sustainability and Poverty

There is a vicious cycle between poverty and environmental damage. To meet basic needs, poor people rely heavily on the environment, more than richer people do, since the environment may be their direct source of food, fuel, sanitation and waste disposal. Yet their use of the environment results in further environmental problems.

Worked example: deforestation and common access resources

Low-income rural people often depend on wood as their only fuel source, using it as a common access resource. When existing trees are cut down, households must travel greater distances to acquire wood — a task most often falling to women, leaving them less time to earn income for the household. This keeps low-income households trapped in a vicious cycle, and the result is persistent overuse of the resource.

Poor people are also often unable to own their own land, so must use common-pool land poorly suited to growing food. Lack of "land tenure", insufficient knowledge of good agricultural practice, and inability to afford irrigation and fertilizers lead to low agricultural productivity and crop yields — another vicious cycle. Poverty pushes people onto more marginal land, causing greater soil erosion and land degradation, threatening not just low-income households but the long-term sustainability of the land as a natural resource.

Key concept: Economic well-being Poor people are far more vulnerable to floods and other environmental catastrophes caused by climate change, yet they contributed least to the problem — illustrating how development and well-being are unevenly distributed even when a shared global harm (climate change) is the cause.
Key concept: Equity Poor countries are also least able to afford the costs of climate-mitigation. Mechanisms exist within the Paris Agreement to support developing countries, but the consequences of the climate crisis will continue to have a disproportionate effect on poor people — an equity concern at the heart of sustainable development policy.

28.6 Common Characteristics of Developing Countries (Todaro)

Development economist Michael P. Todaro classified common characteristics of developing nations into seven broad categories. No single developing country need show every characteristic, but they recur across many developing economies.

1. Low standards of living

Characterized by low incomes, inequality, poor health and inadequate education. Indicators of low living standards include high poverty levels (very low incomes), high inequality, very poor housing, low standards of health, high infant mortality rates, and high levels of malnutrition and lack of education.

2. Low levels of productivity (output per person)

Common in developing countries; main causes are low education standards, low health levels among workers, lack of investment in physical capital, and lack of access to technology.

Higher Level 3. High rates of population growth and dependency burdens

Developing countries tend to have crude birth rates that are more than double, on average, those in developed countries (the crude birth rate is the annual number of live births per 1,000 of the population). The world average crude birth rate in 2018 was 18.2, but in some developing countries it can exceed 40 (e.g. 44.2 in Niger and Angola in 2017). Most developed countries have rates below 15 (e.g. Spain 9.2, Japan 7.7).

High crude birth rates in developing countries translate into high-dependency ratios: a large young population (under age 15) is assumed to be non-productive and must be supported by those of working age (assumed to be 15–64). Developed countries, by contrast, have larger numbers of over-64s who must be supported by the workforce.

Child dependency ratio = (% of population under 15) ÷ (% of population aged 15 to 64)
Old age dependency ratio = (% of population over 65) ÷ (% of population aged 15 to 64)

Developed countries in the data below have child dependency ratios of around 28–29% and old age dependency ratios of 24–32% (and rising). Developing countries have much higher child dependency ratios (49–91%) and significantly lower old age dependency ratios (5–11%).

Hidden groups behind the headline figure The official unemployment figures understate the true picture because three further groups are usually excluded: (1) the discouraged unemployed, who have given up searching for work and so no longer appear as "unemployed"; (2) the hidden unemployed, who work a few hours a day on a family farm or in informal trade and so do not appear as unemployed; (3) the underemployed, who want full-time work but can only get part-time or informal work. For example, Bangladesh's 4.4% headline rate hides an estimated 40% of the workforce being underemployed, working only a few hours per week at very low wages. Once all these groups are combined, it is fair to say true unemployment in many developing countries exceeds 50%, a situation worsened by high birth rates.

6. Prevalence of imperfect markets and limited information

The trend in the last twenty years has been towards a more market-oriented approach to growth, sometimes "encouraged" by bodies like the IMF and World Bank — a possibly problematic push, since many developing countries lack the necessary conditions for markets to work efficiently. They often lack a functioning banking system (to enable and encourage saving and investment), a developed legal system (to ensure business takes place fairly), adequate infrastructure (transport routes to move goods efficiently and cheaply), and accurate information systems. This leads to imperfect information, misallocation of resources, and misinformed purchasing decisions for both producers and consumers.

7. Dominance, dependence and vulnerability in international relations

In almost all cases developing countries are dominated by developed countries economically and politically. They depend on developed countries for trade, technology access, aid and investment, and it is not realistic for economically small countries to isolate themselves from world markets. This leaves developing countries vulnerable on the international stage, dominated by, and often harmed by, decisions of developed countries over which they have no control. Some argue developing countries should act as a bloc (like a trade union) to gain from "collective bargaining".

28.7 Diversity Among Developing Countries

Just as there are common characteristics, no two developing countries are the same. Notable areas of diversity:

1. Different resource endowments

It is a common but incorrect assumption that developing countries must be poorly endowed with both physical and human resources. While low-skill human capital is common, physical resource endowment varies hugely. Angola possesses oil and diamonds yet remains a developing country. Chad lacked physical resources until oil discovery/production from 2003 transformed its position. Bangladesh is very poorly endowed with physical resources (its major resource, jute, has been replaced by synthetic products), showing that a lack of physical resources doesn't preclude success. Japan and Singapore are both very poorly endowed with physical resources yet have created "economic miracles" in the last 50 years.

2. Different historical backgrounds

A large proportion of developing countries were once colonies of developed countries, but the extent to which this affected them varies greatly, depending on how long they were colonized and whether independence was granted freely or had to be fought for. Some countries arguably gained positive outcomes from colonization (e.g. Singapore, Hong Kong); others did not (e.g. Vietnam, Angola). Colonized or not, there are marked historical differences that set countries apart socially, politically and economically.

3. Different geographic and demographic factors

Developing countries differ hugely in geographical size (e.g. China, Brazil, India, DR Congo are huge) and in population (China approx. 1,420 million in 2019, India approx. 1,369 million, Indonesia approx. 270 million, versus Fiji, Guyana and Djibouti, each with populations under one million).

Did you know? Nauru is the world's smallest island nation, covering just 21 km². It is the smallest independent republic in the world and the only republican state without an official capital.

4. Differences in ethnic and religious breakdown

A wide range of ethnic and religious diversity exists between countries; high levels of diversity within a country increase the chances of political unrest and internal conflict (e.g. Rwanda, Sri Lanka, Angola and Myanmar over the last 20 years) — though this is not universal to all developing countries.

5. Different structures of industry

It is widely assumed all developing countries depend on producing/exporting primary products. While true for many (e.g. Burkina Faso, Ethiopia — see Table 28.4), others are exporters of manufactured products (Bangladesh, Nepal) or of services such as tourism (Cape Verde, Maldives — see Table 28.5).

7. Differences in political structure

Developing countries display varying political structures, including democracies, monarchies, military rule, single-party states, theocracies, and transitional political systems (often caused by conflict/civil war and so unclassifiable). Within each of these there are many sub-structures (e.g. democracies may be presidential, semi-presidential, parliamentary republics, or constitutional monarchies). Because developing countries are so diverse in governance systems, it is very difficult to establish one-size-fits-all solutions to developmental problems.

Overall conclusion While developing countries share some common characteristics (Todaro's seven categories), there are also significant differences between them. One must be very cautious about generalizations that imply all developing countries are the same.
