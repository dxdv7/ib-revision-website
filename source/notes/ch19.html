1. What is unemployment, and how is it measured?

ILO definitionUnemployment = people of working age who are without work, available for work, and actively seeking employment.
Unemployment rate = (Number unemployed ÷ Total labour force) × 100

To calculate the unemployment rate, divide the number of people who are unemployed by the total labour force (everyone employed or unemployed), then multiply by 100 to express the result as a percentage.

Worked example

A country has a total labour force of 30 million people, of whom 1.5 million are without work, available for work, and actively seeking a job. Unemployment rate = (1.5 million ÷ 30 million) × 100 = 5%. This means that 5% of the people who want to work and are available to do so currently do not have a job.

The labour force (= the workforce = the “economically active population”) is everyone of working age who is employed or unemployed. It excludes children, students, stay-at-home parents, retired people, and anyone else not seeking work.

Why is unemployment hard to measure accurately?

  • Institutional differences across countries — e.g. people registered as unemployed (Austria, Switzerland) vs. people claiming unemployment benefits (Britain, Belgium). The incentive to register depends on how generous benefits are.
  • Hidden unemployment — discouraged workers (given up looking, no longer “actively seeking”), underemployment (part-time/temporary work when full-time is wanted), overqualification (working well below one's skill level).
  • Disparities hidden by the national average — geographical (inner-city vs. suburban/rural), age (under-25s usually higher), ethnic, and gender disparities.

These are really the costs of long-term unemployment — they get worse the longer someone stays unemployed.

Cost to…Details

3. The “pool” of unemployment — a flow concept

Unemployment isn't a fixed stock — people are constantly moving in and out of the pool.

Inflows (raise unemployment)

  • People who have lost their jobs or resigned
  • School-leavers who haven't yet found work
  • People returning to the workforce (e.g. stay-at-home parents)
  • Immigrants who haven't yet found work

Outflows (lower unemployment)

  • People who find jobs
  • People who retire
  • People who go back into education
  • People who choose to stay home with children
  • People who emigrate, give up the search, or pass away
The key ruleIf more people flow in than out, unemployment rises. If more flow out than in, it falls. Governments care most about the people who get stuck — the long-term unemployed.
Figure 19.1: Inflows and outflows from the ‘pool’ of unemploymentThe stock of unemployed people is like a pool with water flowing in and out. Five inflows (job losses, resignations, school leavers, people returning to the labour force and new migrants without work) raise unemployment. Seven outflows lower it, but only finding a job is a genuine return to employment; the others remove people from the labour force, so they are no longer counted as unemployed.

4. The labour market

Aggregate demand for labour (ADL) slopes downward — higher wages mean firms want fewer workers. Aggregate supply of labour (ASL) slopes upward — higher wages attract more willing workers. Equilibrium wage and quantity of workers occur where ADL = ASL.

Figure 19.2: Equilibrium in the labour marketThe aggregate demand for labour (ADL) slopes down and the aggregate supply of labour (ASL) slopes up. They cross at the equilibrium wage We and the equilibrium number of workers Qe, where everyone willing to work at that wage has a job.

5. The four causes (types) of unemployment

① Cyclical (demand-deficient) — “Keynesian unemployment”

Linked to the business cycle: when the economy slows, AD falls, so firms need fewer workers.

Sticky wages downwardIn theory wages should fall to restore equilibrium, but firms worry about worker morale, and labour contracts and union power keep wages “stuck.” With wages stuck while demand for labour has fallen, unemployment results.
Figure 19.3(a): A decrease in ADAggregate demand falls from AD1 to AD2 against an aggregate supply curve that is flat at low output and vertical at full-capacity output. The economy moves down the curve, so real output falls from Y1 to Y2 and the price level from P1 to P2; lower output means less demand for labour.
Figure 19.3(b): Demand-deficient unemploymentThe fall in aggregate demand shifts labour demand left from ADL to ADL1. If wages fell freely the market would move from b to c at the lower wage W1. With sticky wages the wage stays at We, so employers demand only Q1 workers (point a) while Qe are willing to work (point b); the gap from a to b is demand-deficient unemployment.
CureKeynesian demand management — expansionary fiscal policy (more government spending, lower taxes) and/or monetary policy (lower interest rates, more money supply).

Downsides of demand-side policy: high opportunity cost; only effective in the longer term due to policy lags (risks becoming inflationary instead); tax cuts might just be saved, not spent; interest rate cuts might not spur borrowing; can cause crowding out.

② Structural — “the worst type”

Form 1 — a permanent fall in demand for a type of labour: technological unemployment (automation/robots), globalization (firms relocate, cheaper imports), changing consumer tastes (e.g. falling demand for coal).

Form 2 — a change in the institutional framework: strict firing laws, a minimum wage set above equilibrium, trade unions blocking non-union hiring.

Figure 19.5: Minimum wage and structural unemploymentThe free-market wage for farm workers is $8 per hour with Q workers employed. A minimum wage of $11 lies above equilibrium: employers demand only Q1 workers while Q2 want to work at that wage, so the gap between Q1 and Q2 is unemployment.
Figure 19.4: A fall in employment (structural unemployment)Automation permanently reduces the demand for call-centre workers, shifting demand left from D1 to D2 along supply curve S. The wage falls from $15 to $11 per hour and employment falls from Q1 to Q2; workers who cannot easily move to other kinds of work face structural unemployment.
Why it's “the worst”It causes long-term unemployment because workers lack occupational mobility (skills to switch jobs) and/or geographic mobility (jobs open up elsewhere).
Structural vs. demand-deficientDemand-deficient = a temporary, economy-wide fall in AD. Structural = a permanent fall in demand for one type of labour. A long recession can turn cyclical unemployment structural if skills atrophy.
Cures — interventionistEducation system reform, adult upskilling/retraining, subsidies for firm-provided training or relocation, apprenticeship programmes, job centres.
Cures — market-basedReduce unemployment benefits (increases the incentive to take a job); labour market deregulation (removes hiring/firing rules to increase flexibility).

Trade-off: cutting benefits can worsen equity, and deregulation can mean weaker job security — even as unemployment falls.

③ Frictional

Short-term and largely voluntary — someone between jobs, or waiting to start a first job after leaving education. Not generally seen as a problem in a healthy, dynamic economy.

CureMarket-based: reduce benefits (less incentive to take your time searching). Interventionist: improve the flow of information between employers and job-seekers — job sites, job centres, employment counsellors.

④ Seasonal

Demand for certain labour varies naturally by time of year (construction/farm workers in cold winters, ski instructors in summer, tourism workers off-season).

CureEncourage workers into alternate jobs in the off-season; the same tools as frictional unemployment.

6. The natural rate of unemployment

Natural rate of unemployment = Structural + Frictional + Seasonal

To calculate the natural rate of unemployment, simply add together the unemployment rates attributable to each of the three non-cyclical causes: structural, frictional and seasonal unemployment.

Worked example

Suppose economists estimate that, in a given country, structural unemployment accounts for 3.5% of the labour force, frictional unemployment for 1.5%, and seasonal unemployment for 0.5%. Natural rate of unemployment = 3.5% + 1.5% + 0.5% = 5.5%. This means that even when the economy is producing at its full-employment (potential) level of output, 5.5% of the labour force would still be unemployed, and demand management policy cannot reduce this figure.

This unemployment exists even at full-employment output. Since it isn't caused by deficient AD, demand management cannot fix it — using it at full employment just causes inflation. Only supply-side policy works here.

7. Demand-side vs. supply-side policy

  • Problem is a downturn/recession → demand-deficient unemployment rising → demand-management (fiscal/monetary) policy is appropriate.
  • Problem is the natural rate (structural/frictional/seasonal) → supply-side policy is needed.
  • In practice it's hard to tell which type is present, so governments typically use a mix of both.
Discretionary fiscal policyAutomatic stabilizers

8. Crowding out

To finance a budget deficit, government borrows by selling bonds, raising demand in the loanable funds market and pushing up interest rates. This can make borrowing more expensive for private businesses, “crowding out” private investment.

Moderate vs. extreme viewModerate: total borrowing rises, but the higher interest rate reduces private investment somewhat. Extreme (New Classical): the supply of loanable funds is drawn perfectly inelastic, so government borrowing simply replaces private borrowing one-for-one — no net rise in AD, just a much bigger jump in interest rates.
Figure 19.6: Crowding out — moderate viewGovernment borrowing shifts the demand for loanable funds right from D1 to D2. Because supply slopes upward, the interest rate rises from i1 to i2 and total borrowing rises from QLF1 to QLF3. At i2 private borrowers demand only QLF2, so private investment is partly crowded out.
Figure 19.7: Crowding out — extreme viewWith a fixed (vertical) supply of loanable funds SLF, the rightward shift of demand from D1 to D2 only raises the interest rate from i1 to i2. Total lending stays at QLF1, so the extra government borrowing displaces private borrowing, which falls to QLF2: crowding out is complete.

Keynesian economists argue crowding out won't happen if the economy is below full employment (there's slack). New Classical economists see it as a significant, potentially total, problem.
