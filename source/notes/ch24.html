What is free trade?

Free tradeFree trade takes place between countries when there are no barriers to trade put in place by governments or international organizations – goods and services are allowed to move freely between countries.

Arguments in favour of protectionism

If international trade is so beneficial to all countries, why do countries not always trade freely? Why do they often protect their economies from imports? A number of reasons are put forward, although the arguments are not always valid.

1. Protecting domestic employment

At any time some industries in an economy are in decline ("sunset industries") because they cannot compete with foreign competition. If these industries are relatively large, this leads to high structural unemployment, and governments often try to protect them to avoid this unemployment.

Weakness of this argumentIt is likely that the declining industry will continue to decline regardless, and protection simply prolongs the process. It might be better to let resources move into other areas of the economy, although in some cases the short-run social costs of a rapidly declining major industry may be so great that government feels obliged to intervene.

2. Protecting the economy from low-cost labour

It is often argued that the main reason for declining industries is the low cost of labour in exporting countries, and that the economy should be protected from imports produced where wages are very low – e.g. demands in the US to protect domestic clothing against cheap Asian imports. Job insecurity is much greater among manufacturing workers in more developed countries, and workers/trade unions may lobby vigorously for protection.

Real-world illustration (shipbuilding): in 2008, South Korea produced about 50% of the world's large commercial ships (based on low labour costs, ~30% of total ship-building cost) and China ~35%; by 2017, China had overtaken South Korea with ~45% of the world market as Chinese hourly wages fell below South Korea's.

Weakness of this argumentThis argument goes against the whole concept of comparative advantage. Protecting against low-cost imports means domestic consumers pay higher prices than they should, and production in the protected economy takes place at an inefficient (higher opportunity cost) level. The exporting country would lose trade and its economy would suffer. Comparative advantage also changes over time – e.g. the US likely had a comparative advantage in shipbuilding once, but as relative factor costs change, resources should move as freely as possible out of declining comparative-advantage industries into growing ones. Supply-side policies that keep labour markets flexible help workers adapt.

3. Protecting an infant (sunrise) industry

A newly developing industry may lack the economies of scale enjoyed by larger foreign industries, so it cannot compete against imports until it achieves those cost advantages. The argument is that the industry needs protection until it reaches a size where it can compete on an equal footing.

Possible flawsMost developed countries have highly efficient capital markets giving access to large amounts of financial capital, so there is little reason for a new domestic industry not to set up at (or rapidly reach) the most efficient size and benefit from economies of scale immediately (e.g. Saudi Arabia's petrochemical sector, built in partnership with Chevron, BP and Exxon Mobil, gained almost immediately from economies of scale). It is more plausible that developing countries, lacking access to sophisticated capital markets, can legitimately use the infant industry argument to assist diversification – though it is debatable whether they have the international political power to impose such protectionist policies without complaints/retaliation from developed countries.

4. To avoid the risks of over-specialization

Governments may want to limit over-specialization, since heavy dependence on export sales of one or two products leaves the economy vulnerable: any change in world markets for these products can have serious consequences. New products or changes in demand/supply patterns can seriously affect economies (especially developing ones) that over-specialize in primary products without choice. Example: the invention of synthetic rubber badly hurt Malaysia's rubber industry; oversupply of coffee on world markets hurt Ethiopia.

There is no real argument against this view – it does not itself promote protectionism, it simply highlights the risks that heavily-specialized countries face.

5. Strategic reasons

Certain industries (e.g. agriculture, steel, power generation) may need protection for times of war, since defence items like planes and tanks require steel, and a competitive domestic steel industry is considered strategically important.

AssessmentThis argument has some validity but is often overstated, since countries are unlikely to go to war or, if they do, to be completely cut off from all supplies. It is often really used as an excuse for protectionism.

6. To prevent dumping

DumpingDumping is the selling by a country of large quantities of a commodity at a price lower than its production cost, in another country. Example: the EU may sell surplus butter cheaply to a small developing country, ruining domestic producers there.

Where countries can prove their industries have been damaged by dumping, WTO rules allow their governments to impose anti-dumping measures to reduce the damage.

DifficultyIt is very difficult to prove that a foreign industry has actually dumped. A government that subsidizes a domestic industry may itself be accused of supporting dumping (e.g. developing countries argue the EU dumps sugar because subsidies mean the price doesn't reflect real EU production costs). Where dumping does occur, talks between governments are usually preferable to protectionism, since protectionism risks inviting retaliation, reducing the benefits available to all consumers and producers.

Real-world illustration: China accused the US, EU and Singapore of dumping rubber on China (2018); the US separately investigated Chinese aluminium sheet for being dumped/subsidized – illustrating how anti-dumping disputes can escalate into wider trade tension between major economies.

7. To protect product standards

A country may impose safety, health, or environmental standards on imports to ensure they match domestic standards. The WTO allows countries to impose such bans as long as the barrier is based on scientific evidence and does not discriminate between countries producing similar products.

Real-world illustration: the EU banned imports of US beef treated with hormones in the 1980s; a long dispute followed, eventually resulting in the EU/US agreeing quotas for hormone-free beef (20,000 tons per year, rising to 45,000 tons), with the WTO's first-come-first-served rule meaning the quota also had to be opened to other suppliers such as Argentina, Australia, Canada and New Zealand.

The catchThe protection of standards is a valid argument only as long as the underlying safety/health concerns are valid. Some bans are suspected of being subtle protectionism dressed up as a standards dispute (as US cattle farmers allege of the EU beef-hormone ban). Meeting product standards is also very costly for producers, especially in developing countries (approval and documentation costs), putting them at a disadvantage in exploiting their comparative advantage – the WTO, together with the WHO, World Bank and FAO, runs the Standards and Trade Development Facility (STDF) to help developing countries improve their capacity to analyse and implement international food safety and plant/animal health standards.

8. To raise government revenue

In many developing countries it is difficult to collect other taxes, so governments impose import taxes (tariffs) on products to raise revenue. In effect, import duties are a tax on the domestic consumers who buy the imports.

9. To correct a balance of payments deficit

Governments sometimes impose protectionist measures to reduce import expenditure and improve a current account deficit (where the country spends more on imports of goods/services than it earns from exports).

Why this is weakThis only works in the short run. It does not address the actual causes of the current account deficit, and if a country does this, other countries are likely to retaliate with protectionist measures of their own.

Key concepts illustrated across these arguments: Economic wellbeing, Efficiency, Change, and Interdependence.

Arguments against trade protection

The arguments against protectionism largely mirror the reasons why countries trade in the first place (see Chapter 23). In brief:

  • Protectionism may raise prices to consumers and to producers of the imports they buy (e.g. imported components).
  • Protectionism leads to less choice for consumers.
  • Competition diminishes if foreign firms are kept out, so domestic firms may become inefficient with less incentive to minimize costs; innovation may also fall, and export competitiveness of domestic firms may be reduced.
  • Protectionism distorts comparative advantage, leading to inefficient use of the world's resources – specialization is reduced, reducing the world's potential level of output.
  • Protectionism may provoke retaliation by other countries, risking a "trade war" with escalating tariffs.
  • For all the reasons above, protectionism may hinder economic growth.

What are the main types of protectionism?

To understand each type of protectionist measure, it is best to first look at what a free trade situation looks like in a given commodity (e.g. wheat), and then see how each protectionist measure alters that free-trade diagram.

Figure 24.1: Free trade in wheat (importing case)Axes: Price of wheat ($ per ton) vertical; Quantity of wheat (000s tons) horizontal. An upward-sloping domestic supply curve S(Domestic) and downward-sloping domestic demand curve D intersect at the autarky (no-trade) price Pc and quantity Q0. Once foreign trade is allowed, consumers can import as much wheat as they like at the (lower) world price, so the supply curve facing importers, S(World), is drawn as a horizontal (perfectly elastic) line at price Pw, which must lie below Pc or there would be no reason to trade. With free trade, the market price falls to Pw. At this lower price, domestic producers only supply OQ1 tons (less than before), while total quantity demanded rises to OQ2. The gap Q1Q2 is met by imports from foreign producers. Domestic consumers therefore get to consume Q1Q2 more wheat at a lower price than under autarky.
Figure 24.2: Exporting when the world price is above the domestic priceSame axes and same S(Domestic)/D curves as Figure 24.1. A country is able to export a product when it can produce and sell it at a price below the price the good fetches on the world market. Without trade, domestic producers produce OQ0 at price Pc. Once trade is allowed, producers find they can sell at the world price Pw, which is now drawn above Pc. They raise their price to Pw, domestic quantity demanded falls (along D) to Q1, while domestic producers expand output to Q2 (along S(Domestic)) – supplying OQ1 to the domestic market and exporting the remainder, Q1Q2, to world markets. Producers thus sell more wheat at a higher price by trading internationally.
Higher Level

Tariffs

TariffA tariff is a tax charged on imported goods. Like any tax placed on a good, it shifts the supply curve upward by the amount of the tax – here it shifts the world supply curve upward (since the tax is placed on foreign producers, not domestic ones).
Figure 24.3: A tariff on wheat importsAxes: Price of wheat ($ per ton) vertical; Quantity of wheat (000s tons) horizontal, with S(Domestic) upward-sloping, D downward-sloping, and a horizontal S(World) line at price Pw.

Before the tariff: 0Q4 tons are consumed at price Pw. Domestic production is 0Q1 (area under S(Domestic) up to Pw, region labelled g) and imports fill the rest, Q1Q4, earning foreign producers revenue of h+i+j+k.

The tariff (T) is imposed: the world supply curve shifts up by the full amount of the tariff to S(World)+tariff, and the market price rises to Pw+T. Because the price is higher, total quantity demanded falls from 0Q4 to 0Q3.

After the tariff: domestic producers expand production to 0Q2, so domestic producer revenue rises from g to g+a+b+c+h. Foreign producers now supply only Q2Q3, and must pay the tariff to the government, so their (after-tariff) revenue falls from h+i+j+k to just i+j. The government collects tariff revenue of d+e (a rectangle equal to the tariff per unit × the new quantity of imports, Q2Q3).

Two dead-weight losses of welfare occur (shaded triangles): (1) Q3Q4 tons of wheat are no longer demanded – consumers keep the money (area k) they would have spent, but lose consumer surplus equal to triangle f, because that wheat is simply no longer bought (a dead-weight loss from lost consumer surplus). (2) Q1Q2 tons are now produced by relatively inefficient domestic farmers instead of more efficient foreign farmers – foreign farmers would have supplied this quantity for a minimum revenue of h, but domestic producers require a minimum revenue of h+c. Triangle c represents this production inefficiency and loss of world efficiency, since more of the world's resources than necessary are used to produce the wheat – a second dead-weight loss.
Government tariff revenue = Tariff per unit × Quantity of imports after the tariff

Importers must pay the tariff, and the higher price is passed on to buyers further down the supply chain (e.g. millers, then bakeries/cereal companies, then consumers). If a government puts a tariff on imported automobile components, this raises costs for car-makers, who pass this to consumers as higher car prices; if the car-maker is itself an exporter, this could reduce its international competitiveness.

Tariffs are the most common anti-dumping measure: if dumping has been proven, a country may impose a tariff on the dumped goods to raise their price and remove the dumped goods' cost advantage.

Higher Level

International trade subsidies

SubsidyA subsidy is money paid by the government to a firm, per unit of output, lowering the firm's costs. Given to domestic producers to make them more competitive against imports, it shifts the domestic supply curve downward by the amount of the subsidy.
Figure 24.4: A subsidy on domestic wheat productionSame axes and starting curves as Figure 24.1 (S(Domestic), D, horizontal S(World) at Pw).

Before the subsidy: 0Q4 tons consumed at price Pw; domestic production 0Q1 earns domestic producers revenue a; imports Q1Q4 earn foreign producers revenue b+c+d.

The subsidy is granted: S(Domestic) shifts down by the amount of the subsidy to S(Domestic)+subsidy. Crucially, the market price stays at Pw (unlike a tariff) and so total demand remains at 0Q4.

After the subsidy: domestic producers increase production to 0Q2, because they now effectively receive Pw+subsidy per unit produced. Their revenue rises from a to a+e+f+g. Foreign producers now supply only the remainder, Q2Q4, so their revenue falls from b+c+d to just c+d. The government pays out the total subsidy, shown by area e+f+g in total.

Dead-weight loss: as with a tariff, Q1Q2 tons are now produced by relatively inefficient domestic farmers rather than more efficient foreign farmers. Foreign farmers would supply this quantity for a minimum revenue of b, but domestic producers require a minimum revenue of b+g. Triangle g represents this production inefficiency and misallocation of the world's resources – a dead-weight loss of welfare.

No consumer-surplus loss: because price stays at Pw, there is no dead-weight loss of consumer surplus, unlike with a tariff or quota. However, consumers are indirectly affected because government tax revenue funds the subsidy – this may mean higher taxes and involves an opportunity cost of reduced government spending elsewhere.
Total cost of subsidy to government = Subsidy per unit × Domestic quantity produced after the subsidy
Higher Level

Quotas

QuotaA quota is a physical limit on the number or value of goods that can be imported into a country (e.g. the EU imposes import quotas on Chinese garlic and mushrooms). The imposition of a quota has a distinctive effect on the free-trade diagram.
Figure 24.5: A quota on wheat importsSame starting curves as Figure 24.1.

Before the quota: 0Q4 tons purchased at price Pw; domestic supply is 0Q1; imports are Q1Q4. The government now imposes a quota limiting imports to Q1Q3 tons.

Immediately after the quota: domestic producers still supply 0Q1 at price Pw and importers supply exactly their quota, Q1Q3. But this leaves excess demand of Q3Q4 at price Pw, so the price begins to rise. As price rises, importers cannot supply any more (their quota is filled), but domestic producers are drawn in by the higher price and expand along their supply curve – in effect, above Pw the "effective" domestic-plus-quota supply curve shifts to the right. The price rises until a new equilibrium is reached at Pquota, where total quantity demanded has fallen to Q2.

At the new equilibrium: domestic producers now supply 0Q2; their revenue rises from a to a+c+d+f+i+j. Foreign producers supply their fixed quota Q1Q3 and now receive the higher price Pquota; their revenue changes from b+c+d+e to b+g+h (a fall in income, even though price is higher, because the physical quantity they can sell is capped).

No government revenue: unlike a tariff, a basic quota generates no revenue for the government – the extra amount consumers pay is captured by producers (domestic and foreign), not by the state. This is a key distinction from tariffs.

Two dead-weight losses of welfare: (1) Q3Q4 tons are no longer demanded; consumers keep the money (area e) they would have spent but lose consumer surplus equal to k – a dead-weight loss. (2) Q1Q2 tons are now produced by relatively inefficient domestic farmers rather than efficient foreign farmers: foreign farmers would supply this quantity for a minimum revenue of c+d, but domestic producers need a minimum of c+d+j. Triangle j represents this inefficiency/loss of world efficiency – a second dead-weight loss, since more of the world's resources than necessary are used to produce the wheat.
Tariff vs quota – the crucial exam distinctionBoth a tariff and a quota raise the domestic price, reduce imports, raise domestic output, and create the same two types of dead-weight loss (lost consumer surplus + production inefficiency). The key difference is who captures the revenue from the higher price: with a tariff, the government collects tariff revenue; with a quota, no government revenue is created – the gain instead goes to whichever producers (domestic and the foreign firms lucky enough to hold import licences) can sell at the now-higher price.

Administrative barriers

Three types of administrative barrier that governments may impose:

1. "Red tape"

Lengthy or complicated administrative/paperwork processes for importers act as a restriction on imports – slowing down and raising the cost of imports. Governments may also designate ports of entry that are hard to reach or more expensive, causing delays and raising costs.

2. Health and safety standards and environmental standards

Restrictions on the types of goods that can be sold, or the methods used to manufacture them, may apply to imports and restrict their entry. While it is legitimate for governments to protect the health and safety of the population from unsafe goods, it is important that such standards are not simply used as a way of keeping out imports to protect domestic producers.

3. Embargoes

EmbargoAn embargo is an extreme quota – a complete ban on imports, usually imposed as a form of political punishment (e.g. the USA's trade embargo on all products from Cuba). Complete embargoes are rare; more commonly countries impose sets of economic sanctions limiting exports/imports of one or a few key products, used as political punishment or to achieve a political objective.

Nationalistic campaigns ("moral suasion")

Governments sometimes run marketing campaigns encouraging people to buy domestic goods instead of foreign ones, to generate more demand for domestic goods and preserve domestic jobs (seen in the UK, Australia and the US). This is described as "moral suasion", where the government links the consumption of imported goods to the creation of domestic unemployment.

Higher Level

Worked numerical example: a tariff on cooking oil

This worked example (adapted from the textbook's assessment-advice worked question) shows the full method for calculating, from a diagram, the effects of a tariff on different stakeholders.

The domestic market for cooking oil has an upward-sloping S(Domestic) and downward-sloping D. The domestic equilibrium (no trade) price and quantity are shown where S(Domestic) and D cross.

  1. The world price of cooking oil is $1 per litre. Since the country can import as much as it wants at this price, the world supply curve is drawn as a horizontal (perfectly elastic) line at $1.
  2. Before free trade: domestic producers supplied 1 million litres (the no-trade equilibrium quantity).
  3. After free trade is introduced at the world price of $1: domestic supply falls to 0.5 million litres (read off S(Domestic) at price $1), and the quantity supplied by foreign producers is 1 million litres (1.5 million total demanded at $1, minus 0.5 million domestic = 1 million imported).
  4. The government then imposes a tariff of 30 cents per litre. This shifts the world supply curve up from $1.00 to $1.30, i.e. to S(World)+Tariff.
  5. Consumer expenditure before the tariff = 1.5 million litres × $1.00 = $1.5 million (i.e. total quantity demanded at $1 times the price). Consumer expenditure after the tariff = 1.2 million litres × $1.30 = $1.56 million. This is an increase in consumer expenditure of $0.06 million ($60,000), even though the quantity bought falls, because the price increase outweighs the fall in quantity.
  6. Government tariff revenue = 30 cents × 400,000 litres (the new quantity of imports after the tariff) = $120,000.
  7. Effects on stakeholders (domestic producers, foreign producers, consumers, government, and resource allocation):
    • Domestic producers can now sell more, at a higher price, increasing revenue and (probably) profit; they will also increase employment, benefiting the domestic economy.
    • Foreign producers sell less at the same price they receive net of the tariff, reducing their revenue and (almost certainly) their profit, lowering employment in the foreign (exporting) economy.
    • Consumers get to consume less of the product, at a higher price, so pay more in total; they suffer a loss of consumer surplus, some of which goes to domestic producers, some to the government, and some is a dead-weight loss (shown as the light blue triangle(s) on the diagram).
    • The government gains tariff revenue, which it may spend elsewhere; it may also benefit from reduced unemployment, though there is always a risk of retaliation from other countries.
    • Resource allocation: production of the affected quantity (e.g. 0.3 million litres of cooking oil in this example) shifts from efficient foreign producers to less-efficient domestic producers – a welfare loss and inefficient use of the world's resources, shown by the dark blue triangle on the diagram (the "efficiency loss"/production dead-weight loss), alongside the light blue "dead-weight loss of consumer surplus" triangle.
Consumer expenditure = Price × Quantity demanded
Government tariff revenue = Tariff per unit × Quantity of imports after tariff
