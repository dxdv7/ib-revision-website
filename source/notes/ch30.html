Introduction: Uneven Development and Poverty Traps

This chapter addresses the real-world issue of why economic development is uneven across countries. It examines the many domestic and international barriers that prevent developing countries from achieving sustainable economic growth and economic development.

Key concept – EquityThis chapter is fundamentally about equity: the fairness of the distribution of resources, income, and opportunity, both within developing countries and between developing and developed countries. Equity runs throughout the whole chapter.
Key concept – Economic wellbeingThe barriers discussed all ultimately reduce the economic wellbeing of populations in developing countries – their living standards, health, education, and capacity to participate fully in economic life.

What are poverty traps (poverty cycles)?

There are many barriers to economic development, but the situation is made worse when these barriers are linked together in a self-reinforcing, circular fashion – this is called a poverty trap. A poverty trap is any linked combination of barriers to growth and development that forms a circle, thus becoming self-perpetuating unless the circle is broken. Poverty traps are illustrated using a poverty cycle (also called a development trap), and are sometimes drawn as circular flow charts with arrows connecting each stage back to the start.

Figure 30.1: Examples of poverty cyclesTwo self-reinforcing poverty cycles. In the growth cycle (left), low investment causes low economic growth, which causes low incomes, which causes low savings (a high marginal propensity to consume), which leaves too little available for investment – the cycle repeats. In the development cycle (right), low incomes cause low productivity, which causes low levels of human capital, which causes low levels of education and health care, which keeps incomes low – the cycle repeats. Breaking either loop at any point can help escape the poverty trap.

Poverty cycles/traps can, in principle, be broken by external or policy intervention at any point in the circle (for example, foreign aid financing investment, or education programmes lifting human capital), which is the underlying rationale for many of the development strategies covered in the following chapter.

Domestic Economic Barriers to Development

How does rising economic inequality hinder economic progress?

All countries have income inequality, but the gap between rich and poor is generally greater in developing countries than in developed countries. High income inequality can be a barrier to growth and development for several reasons:

  • Low saving by the poor: The poor tend to save a very small proportion of their income (they have a high marginal propensity to consume just to meet basic needs), so low saving means low investment, and so low economic growth.
  • Rich dominate policy: The rich tend to dominate both politics and the economy, meaning that government policies tend to favour their interests. This means countries often fail to achieve pro-poor growth.
  • Capital flight by the rich: High income-inequality in developing countries tends to be marked by the rich moving large amounts of funds out of the economy in the form of capital flight, and a large proportion of the goods purchased by the rich are foreign-produced, so their consumption does little to help the domestic economy.
Pro-poor growthPro-poor growth occurs when economic growth leads to a fall in some agreed measure of poverty. Growth benefits the poor specifically, not just the economy in aggregate. Although income inequality is often simply seen as a consequence of low levels of development, this chapter shows it can also act as a cause (a barrier) of low growth and development – the causation runs both ways.

How does a lack of access to infrastructure and appropriate technology hinder economic progress?

1. Lack of access to infrastructure. Infrastructure can be defined as the essential facilities and services such as roads, airports, sewage treatment, water systems, railways, telephones and other utilities necessary for economic activity. Improvements in infrastructure lead to greater economic development: better roads and public transport allow children to get to school and goods to reach markets; a developed radio/TV network lets people connect with wider communities; gas and electricity enable household activities like cooking and food preservation; sewage treatment and safe water systems improve public health and well-being.

2. Lack of access to appropriate technology. Appropriate technology is technology suited to a country's existing factor endowments, applying to both production and consumption. In years gone by, development economists urged developing countries to modernize and industrialize using capital-intensive Western technology, but this was often not "appropriate". Most developing countries have a surplus of labour, so the appropriate technology makes use of this abundant labour supply – i.e. capital equipment that is cheap to make and requires labour for its use. A well-known example is the universal nut sheller: a simple hand-turned machine used to shell nuts. It is cheap to make, has a long working life, and has a one-to-one capital-to-labour ratio; because it is manual rather than automated it provides more employment than industrial machinery, adding to development.

On the consumption side, a good example of appropriate technology is the solar cooker, made from cheap aluminium foil and plastic film, and powered by sunshine, which is abundant in many developing countries. It aids development because it requires no wood (reducing deforestation), eliminates the daily search for firewood (freeing up time, especially for women, who are most commonly responsible for cooking, for other activities), improving the position of women in developing countries.

How do low levels of human capital – lack of access to education and health care – hinder economic progress?

1. Lack of access to education. Improvements in education improve the well-being of the population, both the educated individuals and society as a whole, because education provides positive external benefits. Increased levels of education mean people can read and communicate better, making discussion and debate more likely, which may lead to positive social change. Benefits include:

  • Improved role of women in society: There are high correlations between women's education and child survival rates and lower fertility rates (the annual number of live births per 1,000 women of childbearing age). Amartya Sen argued that "nothing, arguably, is as important today in the political economy of development as an adequate recognition of political, economic and social participation and leadership of women" (Sen, Development as Freedom, OUP, 1999).
  • Improved levels of health: Improved education (especially literacy) means people, especially women, communicate more and become more aware of hazards they face (e.g. HIV/AIDS, poor sanitary habits, poor dietary habits) and of opportunities that exist (e.g. inoculations, water filtering).

Progress has been made globally in the provision of education, particularly primary education (enrolment in developing countries has reached 91%), but 57 million primary-age children remain out of school. More than half of the world's out-of-school children live in sub-Saharan Africa; an estimated 50% of out-of-school children of primary school age live in conflict-affected areas; and 617 million youth worldwide lack basic mathematics and literacy skills.

Barriers to achieving universal education include: insufficient funding; disparities between urban areas (more education funding) and rural areas; family economic conditions that force children to work at home, on the farm, or as external "child labourers" rather than attend school (mostly children from poor households and from families where mothers also received little formal education); the fact that enrolment in secondary school is far lower than primary school because the opportunity cost of the child's forgone earnings rises; and glaring gender inequity in girls' access to education.

How does dependence on primary sector production hinder economic progress? (Volatile commodity prices)

While the share of manufactured goods produced by developing countries as a percentage of total world trade is growing, many developing countries remain dependent on primary commodities for a significant share of export revenues (linking back to the topic of trade in Chapter 28). Rising commodity prices can be beneficial: they raise a country's rate of economic growth, and if the revenues are used to finance education, health and infrastructure, this can set off a positive cycle of development. However, if prices fall, economies experience deteriorating economic circumstances: current account deficits are likely to increase, and it becomes very difficult to finance current expenditure and necessary imports. Unless the pattern of export trade can be changed, countries dependent on a narrow range of primary exports will find it difficult to gain much growth through international trade.

Regardless of whether the export is a commodity, a manufactured good, or a service such as tourism, dependence on a narrow range of exports creates great vulnerability and uncertainty. For example: growth reliant on the global tourist trade is damaged by terrorism or a global economic slowdown; tsunamis and natural disasters can devastate tourism-dependent economies; and countries exporting a narrow range of low-skill manufactured goods (e.g. textiles) were badly damaged when China joined the WTO and sharply increased the supply of textiles on world markets, driving down their prices.

Real-world example: Zambia's economy remains heavily dependent on copper exports, which produce relatively few jobs for the wider population – illustrating the risks of a narrow export base.
Figure 30.2: The world market for cocoaBoth demand D and supply S for cocoa are drawn steep (price-inelastic), typical of an agricultural commodity. Starting from S1 at P1 = $1,400 and Q1 = 20 million tonnes, a bumper harvest shifts supply right to S2, and price falls sharply to P2 = $1,280 for only a small rise to Q2 = 22 million tonnes; a poor harvest shifts supply left to S3, and price jumps to P3 = $1,520 as quantity falls only to Q3 = 18 million tonnes. Because both curves are inelastic, small supply shocks cause disproportionately large price swings – a key source of instability for commodity-dependent economies.

This price volatility, and the resulting export-revenue volatility, makes it very difficult for producers and governments in developing countries to plan ahead, which in turn impacts investment by companies (and thus growth), and government planning for education, health care and infrastructure (and thus development).

Combined with the short-run price volatility shown in Figure 30.2, this is a major international-facing barrier to development for primary-product dependent economies (see the "International barriers" section below).

How does market failure show up as a barrier to development?

Several of the barriers covered in this chapter are, at root, forms of market failure – markets failing to allocate resources efficiently or fairly. Examples drawn out in the chapter include: corruption causing contracts to go to the highest bidder rather than the most efficient producer (misallocating resources and shielding inefficient producers from competition, thereby hindering sustainable development); the existence of a large untaxed informal economy (below) which means government revenue and services are under-provided relative to the needs of the population; and non-existent or unenforced property rights (below), which mean assets cannot be used productively as collateral, so investment that would otherwise be profitable simply does not happen.

How does the existence of an informal economy (informal sector) hinder economic progress?

The informal sector (also called the informal or grey economy) is the part of an economy that is neither taxed nor administered by any form of government; its economic activities are not included in a country's gross national income. The International Labour Organization reported that in 2018, 2 billion people – more than 61% of the world's employed population – earned their living in the informal economy. Regional informality rates: Africa 85.8%, Asia and the Pacific 68.2%, Arab States 68.6%, the Americas 40.0%, Europe and Central Asia 25.1%.

The level of education is a key factor affecting informality: globally, as education levels rise, informality falls. People who have completed secondary and tertiary education are less likely to be in informal employment than workers with no education or only primary education. People in rural areas are almost twice as likely to be in informal employment as those in urban areas. Agriculture has the highest rate of informal employment (estimated at more than 90%). 93% of the world's informal employment is in emerging and developing countries.

Consequences of informality: workers in the informal sector lack social protection, rights at work, and decent working conditions; businesses in the informal sector suffer low productivity and difficulty accessing investment/finance; and, crucially for development, the non-payment of taxes in the informal sector reduces government revenue and the ability of governments to fund programmes such as education and health care that could otherwise drive development.

How does capital flight hinder economic progress?

Capital flight – definitionCapital flight is the movement of large sums of money out of a country as a reaction to events such as political instability or economic instability.

In developing countries there is often risk involved in holding domestic assets, including: the danger of hyperinflation, the threat of government compulsory purchase of assets, an expected devaluation of the currency, or worry regarding the security of the banking industry. Any domestic capital that leaves the country could otherwise have been used internally, so capital flight restricts growth. It may also cause a depreciation of the domestic currency, as the increased demand for foreign currencies for overseas investment/saving increases the supply of the domestic currency on international money markets.

How does indebtedness (the debt burden) hinder economic progress?

(Debt is fundamentally an international barrier, since it is owed to foreign lenders/institutions, but is treated together with domestic drivers here as the chapter presents it as a single continuous topic.)

One of the major drawbacks to growth and development in developing countries is the level of debt repayments (debt servicing) these countries must make on money borrowed previously. Developing countries incur debt mostly through government borrowing from abroad (a small amount is private borrowing). As with any loan, in the long run the principal has to be repaid along with interest at whatever rate was agreed; the repayment of interest on a loan is called debt servicing. Debt servicing represents funds leaving the country, meaning governments are unable to spend money on other areas of the economy – an opportunity cost that has a detrimental effect on two fronts: first, it slows economic growth (e.g. governments lack funds to invest in infrastructure); second, it slows development, because governments cannot afford essential services.

History of the debt crisis

Indebtedness has only really been a problem for developing countries in the last fifty years. Before the 1970s, borrowing by developing countries was low and tended to be bilateral official aid at concessionary ("soft") interest rates. In 1973, OPEC countries steeply increased oil prices, producing a massive increase in oil revenues for oil-exporting countries. These revenues, known as "petro-dollars", were deposited in Western commercial banks, and interest rates fell sharply because of the large supply of available funds. Banks needed to lend the OPEC money to third parties to make profits – there was so much money available that usual borrowers did not take the full amount, so banks offered loans to developing countries.

Developing countries began borrowing from Western banks, but these loans were made at market interest rates, not "soft" rates, and were repayable in "hard" currencies, not the currencies of the developing countries. The banks lent huge amounts of money but did not monitor what it was used for. Relatively little of the borrowed money was used for development purposes: some went into large infrastructure projects that failed; approximately 20% of the money was spent on arms; and large amounts went into the private bank accounts of dictators, generals, and corrupt politicians.

Borrowing grew alarmingly through the 1970s, and countries managed to keep up repayments. However, with a worldwide recession in the early 1980s, there was a sharp fall in demand for, and prices of, commodities, and countries found they were unable to make their loan repayments. Mexico was the first country to default on its loans in 1982, saying it could not "service their debt" (i.e. pay back the loan and its interest). Over the next few years several countries followed Mexico's inability to repay their debts; by the 1990s, this had come to be known as the "Third World Debt Crisis".

HIPC InitiativeIn 1996, the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) launched the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative. The program was reviewed and modified in 1999, and supplemented in 2005 by the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative, in order to accelerate progress towards meeting the Millennium Development Goals. As with earlier programmes, assistance under HIPC is conditional on the governments of the countries in question achieving certain criteria, committing to poverty reduction through policy changes, and demonstrating a "good track-record" over time – the conditions, the criteria, and changes to them have been the subject of much debate. Up to 2019, debt reduction packages had been approved for 36 countries (30 of them in Africa), providing $76 billion in debt relief over time, with additional countries eligible for HIPC Initiative assistance.

Even now, many developing countries still have major problems with indebtedness. Figures from the Jubilee Debt Campaign (based on IMF and World Bank databases) show that developing country debt payments increased by 60% between 2014 and 2017. Average government external debt repayments in the 126 developing countries surveyed rose from 6.7% of government revenue in 2014 to 10.7% in 2017. This rapid increase followed a lending boom after the 2008 global financial crisis: external loans to developing countries rose from $200 billion per year in 2008 to $390 billion in 2014; from 2015 to 2017 they fell back to around $325 billion per year, still well above pre-2008 levels.

Real-world example: Zambia has sacrificed social-sector spending in order to avoid a debt default, illustrating the direct opportunity-cost trade-off between debt servicing and public services.

How do geographical factors hinder economic progress?

1. Landlocked countries. Landlocked countries trade less and have slower growth rates than countries with coasts. They pay more and have to wait longer for imports to be delivered, and also face problems exporting. Transport costs are increased by additional costs such as bribery and border delays. 16 of the world's 31 landlocked developing countries are among the poorest in the world.

Real-world example: Bolivia's landlocked status is cited as a significant contributor to poor economic growth through the impact on port access and logistics costs.

2. Tropical climates and endemic diseases. Countries in the tropics tend to be less developed, and geographical location is a contributing factor. The development of production technology in the tropical zone has been far slower than in the temperate zone, for two key reasons: agriculture and health.

Key concept – EfficiencyThe economist Jeffrey Sachs estimated that, in 1995, productivity in grain production was approximately 50% higher in temperate zone countries than in tropical zone countries. Explanations include soil formation and erosion, water availability, pests and parasites, and the effects of tropical climates on plant respiration. Lower agricultural productivity leads to poorer nutrition and thus poorer health, further affecting the quality (efficiency) of human resources – illustrating how geography constrains productive efficiency.

The prevalence of disease is considerably higher in the tropical zone than in temperate zones. Vector-borne diseases, such as malaria, and helminthic infections caused by worms, are common in tropical climates and have proven hard to control, again affecting the quality of human resources. It is worth noting that countries that are both landlocked and tropical – including Bolivia, Chad, Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Central African Republic, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Lesotho and Laos – are among the poorest countries in the world, illustrating how geographical barriers can compound one another.

Domestic Political and Social Barriers to Development

How does a weak institutional framework hinder economic progress?

Legal system and property rights

An honest and fully functioning legal system is almost essential for development. In many developing countries the legal system does not function well, meaning there is no way to create and enforce contracts, and no way to uphold property rights. Social scientists consider property rights to be essentially a "basket" of legal rights, including:

  • The right to own assets, such as land or buildings.
  • The right to establish the use of our assets, e.g. an owner adding sanitation to a building.
  • The right to benefit from our assets, such as renting out land.
  • The right to sell our assets.
  • The right to exclude others from using or taking our assets.

Property rights allow people to own and benefit from private property, provided the legal and security systems can support and protect these rights. If a person cannot guarantee ownership of a property, there is no incentive to improve it, since the property (and the investment) might be lost. If property rights are not enforceable, investment and growth may be very much reduced, and development will be thwarted.

Hernando de Soto – "dead capital"Peruvian economist Hernando de Soto uses the term "dead capital" to refer to assets that cannot easily be bought, sold, valued, or used as an investment because no one actually owns them, or has the right to exchange them. De Soto points out that despite obvious poverty, people in developing countries actually have enormous assets, but they don't formally own them – they lack formal property rights. Property rights are important for three reasons: (1) private ownership lets people use initiative and entrepreneurial skill to earn an income from property; (2) when people own property, they have the incentive to improve its value, knowing that selling it will earn them the profit (capital gains); (3) when people own property such as a home, they can use it as collateral to secure a bank loan and start a business. De Soto argues that the lack of property rights traps people in developing countries in poverty – despite substantial capital, the lack of property rights means the capital is "dead" and cannot be used to improve their well-being.

Ineffective taxation structure

Tax revenue provides governments with the means to finance necessary public services (education, health care) and to improve infrastructure – but this is very difficult if governments do not earn a great deal of tax revenue. Reasons why it is difficult for developing-country governments to collect tax revenue:

  • Tax exemptions and inefficient/corrupt administration mean it is estimated that less than 3% of populations in developing countries pay income tax, compared to 60%–80% in developed countries.
  • Corporate tax revenues tend to be low, since there is relatively little corporate activity in developing countries (although growing), and governments often offer large tax incentives to encourage domestic corporate activity and attract FDI.
  • The main source of tax revenue in developing countries is export, import and excise (customs) duties, which are relatively easy to collect since they are paid when goods pass through border posts – but this only generates significant revenue if the country is heavily involved in foreign trade. (It is worth noting that the international trading system through the WTO, with its emphasis on trade liberalization, makes it harder for countries to earn revenue from tariffs.)
  • Tax systems in developing countries suffer from inefficiency, lack of information, and outright corruption, meaning people are often able to evade paying taxes owed.

The large size of the informal market (discussed above) is also relevant here: because incomes earned informally are not recorded, no tax is paid on them, and lower tax revenues make it difficult for governments to promote growth and achieve development objectives.

Real-world example: Low tax collection in developing economies has been argued to have a more devastating impact on development outcomes than is commonly appreciated.

Banking system

Developed and independent financial institutions are essential for economic growth and development, but are often underprovided in developing countries. Most developing countries have what is known as dual financial markets: the "official" financial markets are small and dominated by foreign commercial banks, which have an outward-looking emphasis and restrict lending to foreign businesses and already-established large local manufacturers; the "unofficial" markets are not legally controlled (illegal), and mainly lend money, usually at very high interest rates, to those too desperate and poor to borrow elsewhere.

Saving is necessary to make funds available for investment, and investment is necessary for economic growth. Saving is difficult where poverty is high, but even harder if there is nowhere safe to save money that gives a good return. When financial institutions are weak and untrustworthy, people with investment income tend to buy assets such as livestock, or invest their money outside the country (capital flight).

Financial services are necessary if low-income people are to manage their assets and increase their value, enabling investment in things like health care, shelter and education. The difficulties associated with saving and borrowing money are a significant barrier to growth and development, making it exceedingly difficult for poor people to escape poverty. In developing countries, poor people find it almost impossible to access traditional banking and financial systems, since they lack assets to use as collateral, are often unemployed, and lack savings. Even with entrepreneurial spirit, it is very difficult to start a business, and any money that can be borrowed often comes at exorbitant interest rates.

How does gender inequality hinder economic progress?

In many developing countries the role of women is very much subservient to that of men. Giving women more empowerment, especially through education, could be a huge factor in achieving economic development, so lack of empowerment is a significant barrier to development. Improving the welfare of women through education and social standing benefits the women themselves directly, but there are also extremely important externalities when women become more empowered:

  • The well-being of families improves, especially children's health: with better education, women are more informed about health care, hygiene, and diet, improving family welfare.
  • The education of children in the family improves: educated women pass on education and view it as more important, striving to achieve it for their children too.
  • Because of the above two points, the quality of the workforce improves over time, with significant effects on growth and development.
  • With greater empowerment women earn more money; research shows that increases in women's income in developing countries lead to greater increases in family health levels than similar increases in men's income.
  • With better education and social standing, women have more control over contraception, marry later, and tend to have smaller families, lowering the rate of population growth.

How do a lack of good governance and corruption hinder economic progress?

Figure 30.5: The elements of good governanceGood governance is a composite of eight characteristics radiating from the central concept: participatory, consensus oriented, accountable, transparent, responsive, equitable and inclusive, effective and efficient, and following the rule of law. All eight work together, so weakness in any one undermines the quality of governance overall.

A lack of good governance can have many damaging effects, including corruption, political instability, and unequal political power and status, all of which threaten economic growth and development.

1. Corruption

Corruption – definitionCorruption is defined as the dishonest exploitation of power for personal gain. It poses a huge challenge to economic growth, economic development, and sustainable development, and occurs in every country to some extent.

Corruption tends to be most prevalent where: governments are not accountable to the people (especially military governments); governments spend large amounts on large-scale capital investment projects; official accounting practices are not well formulated or controlled; government officials are not well paid; political elections are not well controlled or are non-existent (no democracy); the legal structure is weak; and freedom of speech is lacking. Many of these conditions are found in a high proportion of developing countries, which may explain their high levels of corruption. Forms of corruption include bribery, extortion, fraud, patronage, influence peddling and nepotism.

Effects of corruption that hinder growth and development:

  • Electoral corruption means the wishes of the people are not heeded; an unelected/illegitimate government is unlikely to adopt policies that benefit the electorate, and governance will not be equitable or inclusive.
  • Corruption reduces the effectiveness of the legal system; if people can "buy" their way out of trouble, there is an incentive to act illegally.
  • Corruption leads to unfair allocation of resources: if contracts go to the highest bidder rather than the most efficient producer, this is a market failure – resources are misallocated and inefficient producers are sustained by being shielded from competition, hindering sustainable development.
  • Bribes increase the costs of doing business, both in direct cash terms and in the management/negotiation time required, leading to higher prices.
  • Corruption reduces trust in an economy; countries find it harder to attract foreign investment, which is diverted to less-corrupt countries.
  • Corruption increases the risk of contracts not being honoured, deterring both internal and external investment.
  • Corruption means officials often divert public investment into capital projects where bribes are more likely, rather than important areas such as education and health care, reducing the quality of government services.
  • Corruption often means officials turn a blind eye to regulations, such as those on construction or the environment, damaging individuals and the country as a whole and hindering sustainable development.
  • Monetary gains from corruption are often moved out of the country – a form of capital flight, reducing capital available for internal investment.
  • The constant paying of small bribes reduces the economic well-being of ordinary citizens.
Corruption and equityCorruption disproportionately affects vulnerable populations and hits the poor hardest, especially women, who represent a higher share of the world's poor. In their traditional role as caregivers, women constantly experience corruption in daily interactions with school officials and health care providers. Corruption is thus a barrier for women's civic, social and economic rights and impacts both the global and local level.

Transparency International publishes a yearly Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI), which ranks 180 countries and territories by their perceived levels of public-sector corruption according to experts and business people, using a scale of 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean).

Real-world examples: Nigeria has undertaken efforts at "strengthening the anti-corruption war"; meanwhile, resource-rich DR Congo has been described as a country wracked by political instability and violence, illustrating the link between weak governance, corruption, and instability in resource-dependent states.

2. Political instability

Countries with political stability are more likely to attract foreign direct investment (FDI) and aid, and it is more likely that domestic savings and profits will stay in the country and contribute to it. Increased access to FDI may be more likely to contribute to increased growth rather than development, but increased access to aid may increase development. Where there is political stability, citizens are more likely to have input into government; government planning is likely to be more structured and long-term, and the law is likely to be more enforceable – all leading to higher living standards.

Political instability causes uncertainty and, at its most extreme, complete economic breakdown. Sudan is a case in point: civil wars from 1955 to 1972 and then from 1983 to 2005 had a devastating economic and social impact. South Sudan was given independence in 2011, but civil unrest reappeared in 2013, and in the following five years there were more than 50,000 deaths and one quarter of the population was displaced. Such extreme political instability leads to very poor economic performance and high levels of poverty and low standards of living for the majority of the population; the likelihood of attracting foreign investment (or even aid) becomes much smaller.

A number of developing countries have experienced civil wars as a result of ethnic and/or religious conflict or border conflicts. For example, since 2003 there have been ethnic and/or religious-based, internal or external conflicts in: Afghanistan, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Bahrain, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Côte d'Ivoire, Democratic Republic of Congo, India, Iran, Iraq, Israel, Kyrgyzstan, Lebanon, Libya, Mali, Mozambique, Nigeria, Pakistan, Palestine, Paraguay, Russia, Somalia, South Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, Turkey, Ukraine and Yemen. The loss of life, damage to infrastructure, loss of investment and sometimes aid, and political instability have undoubtedly hindered, if not stopped, economic growth and development in these countries.

3. Unequal political power and status

As noted elsewhere (income and wealth concentration), where ownership of resources and income in an economy is concentrated among a small percentage of the population – very much the case in developing countries – wealthier people may have a disproportionate say in government policy. Many developing countries lack democracy and their governments fail to display most, if not all, of the elements of good governance. Politicians, when in power, are more likely to develop policies in their own interests rather than in the interests of lower-income citizens. In addition, low-income people are also less likely to become involved in the political process; they may lack the background, education, or social networks, or simply lack the time to engage in political activities. As a result, the "voice" of low-income people may not be adequately represented in government policy – a significant barrier to economic development.

International Barriers to Development

Deteriorating terms of trade

As discussed above under primary product dependency, developing countries reliant on primary commodity exports face not only short-run price volatility (Figure 30.2) but also a longer-run tendency for the prices of primary commodities to grow more slowly than the prices of the manufactured goods and services they need to import. The result is deteriorating terms of trade: over time, a given quantity of commodity exports buys fewer and fewer imports, worsening the current account and reducing the real income available for development spending. This locks primary-product dependent economies into international vulnerability that they cannot control through domestic policy alone.

Protectionism in developed countries and lack of access to international markets

Protectionism is any economic policy aimed at supporting domestic producers at the expense of foreign producers, using measures such as tariffs, subsidies, quotas and non-tariff barriers. Protectionist measures by developed countries against the exports of developing countries can be very harmful, limiting developing countries' comparative-advantage-based exporting and access to foreign exchange. This is especially damaging in primary product markets.

Worked example: cotton subsidiesSince 1995, America's 25,000 cotton farmers have shared an average of $2.1 billion in government subsidies each year. This encourages farmers to over-produce, forcing down the world price, and the US exports its surplus to developing countries that do not have the benefit of such subsidies – immensely damaging developing-country cotton producers. The US does the same with maize, rice and dairy products. Similarly, protected EU farmers overproduce and export sugar, cereals and dairy produce, lowering world prices and severely damaging markets and local suppliers in developing countries. Because these subsidized products are sold at lower prices than would otherwise prevail, they are said to be "dumped" in foreign markets. Small-scale farmers in developing countries are effectively deprived of the ability to earn a living, provide for their families, and afford schooling for their children – a clear and significant barrier to development.

Many developing countries are also limited in their access to global markets simply due to being landlocked and/or having insufficient infrastructure to physically get products to markets (see geographical barriers above). Furthermore, selling in international markets requires meeting certain product standards, and the process of meeting these standards is very costly for developing-country producers.

Non-convertible currencies

Another factor preventing developing countries gaining access to international markets is that many of them have non-convertible currencies – currencies that can only be used domestically and are not accepted for exchange on foreign exchange markets. Most developing countries operate a fixed exchange rate system, where the domestic currency is pegged to a more acceptable currency (often the US dollar) at a certain rate.

Non-convertibility means that trade is less likely to occur: traders would be taking on more risk dealing in a non-convertible currency and are likely to go elsewhere to conduct business, and the same is true of foreign investment. Non-convertible currencies are often over-valued at their official, pegged exchange rate; this usually means a black market for the convertible currency arises, which can be very damaging for the economy. In some cases, the domestic currency becomes unacceptable within the country as well, damaging both local and international trade.

Debt burden (indebtedness)

See the full discussion of indebtedness under "Domestic Economic Barriers" above (history of the Third World Debt Crisis, the HIPC Initiative, Table 30.4, and Figure 30.4). Because developing-country debt is owed predominantly to foreign commercial banks and international institutions and denominated in "hard" foreign currencies (typically US dollars), debt servicing is fundamentally an international barrier: it drains foreign exchange out of the country, is worsened by movements in international interest rates and exchange rates that developing countries cannot control, and depends on internationally negotiated relief mechanisms (like HIPC) rather than purely domestic policy choices.
