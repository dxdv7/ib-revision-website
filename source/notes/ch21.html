Introduction: Economic Growth as a Macroeconomic Objective

Economic growth is the third major macroeconomic objective (after low unemployment and low, stable inflation). Importantly, achieving economic growth can help achieve the other two goals as well.

DefinitionEconomic growth is an increase in real GDP over time. Remember the distinction between real GDP (adjusted for inflation) and nominal GDP (not adjusted) — growth must always be measured using real GDP so that the figure reflects a genuine increase in output rather than just rising prices.

Rates of economic growth vary over time and from country to country. Economies experience periodic fluctuations in activity and growth rates — the business cycle. Emerging economies such as China and India have experienced rapid growth, while more developed countries (e.g. the United States, Germany) have experienced more moderate, steadier rates of growth.

Illustrating Growth With Diagrams: Short-Run vs Long-Run Growth

Demand-side factors can bring about short-term economic growth. This can be illustrated using a combined AD/AS model and PPC (production possibilities curve) model, shown side by side.

1. Starting point: a deflationary/output gap

Figure 21.2: A deflationary gap/output gapPanel (a) is a standard AD/AS diagram: y-axis "Average price level", x-axis "Real output (Y)". An upward-sloping SRAS curve and a vertical LRAS curve are drawn. AD₁ intersects SRAS to the LEFT of LRAS, at output Y₁, which is less than the full-employment/potential output Y₀ (i.e. Y₁ < Y₀). The horizontal gap between Y₁ and Y₀ is labelled the "deflationary gap". Panel (b) is a PPC diagram (axes: "Capital goods" on y-axis, "Consumer goods" on x-axis) with a bowed-out curve; a point labelled "a" sits INSIDE the curve (not on it), representing the same underemployment of resources shown by the output gap in panel (a).

An economy operating with a deflationary gap is not using its resources to the fullest extent possible — there is underemployment or unemployment of resources/factors of production.

2. Short-run growth: closing the gap via a movement toward the PPC

Figure 21.3: Economic growth to remove a deflationary gap/output gapPanel (a): AD shifts rightward from AD₁ to AD₂ along the still-unchanged SRAS and LRAS curves, moving the intersection point along SRAS from output Y₁ up to the full-employment output Y₀ (where AD₂ now meets both SRAS and LRAS). Panel (b): on the PPC, this corresponds to a movement FROM point "a" (inside the curve) TO point "b", which sits close to (just short of) the curve itself — note point "b" is NOT exactly on the PPC.
Why point "b" is not exactly on the PPCAn economy will never fully employ all of its resources to the absolute fullest extent, because there will always be some natural unemployment (frictional, structural, seasonal — see Chapters 19–20). This is why the LRAS curve is not exactly the same as the PPC in terms of representing potential output: LRAS represents output at natural/full employment, which still leaves a small amount of "spare" capacity that the PPC would represent as being available.

This growth is caused by an increase in aggregate demand and represents the economy moving toward its existing full-employment/potential level of output — i.e. making better use of existing resources rather than increasing the total quantity of resources available.

Policy linkDeflationary gaps/output gaps may result from a fall in economic growth (or negative growth) in the short run, and may be closed using demand management (expansionary fiscal and/or monetary) policies. Such policies smooth out fluctuations in economic activity shown on a business cycle diagram.

3. Long-run growth: an outward shift of LRAS and the PPC

Figure 21.4: Economic growth through an increase in potential outputPanel (a): an AD/AS diagram where the LRAS curve itself shifts rightward, from LRAS₁ to LRAS₂, increasing the full-employment/potential level of real output (GDP) from Y₀ to a higher level of potential output. Panel (b): the PPC diagram shows the entire curve shifting outward (bowing further out from the origin) from PPC₁ to PPC₂, with a point moving from "a" on the original curve to "b" on the new, larger curve. This is the diagrammatic equivalent of the LRAS shift in panel (a).

Long-run growth (the outward shift of LRAS/PPC) comes about as a result of an increase in the quantity or an increase in the quality (productivity) of an economy's factors of production, and advances in technology. It may come about through market forces, or through government supply-side policies — either interventionist or market-based.

Summing up: the trend growth line

The trend growth line shown on a business cycle diagram is influenced by the supply-side policies that generate long-run economic growth. You should be able to evaluate the consequences of both demand-side and supply-side policies in terms of: their appropriateness in different situations, their likely success in achieving their goals, the consequences for different stakeholders, and the possible trade-offs (opportunity costs) that occur in the pursuit of different economic goals.

Measuring the Rate of Economic Growth

Growth rate = [(Real GDP in year 2 − Real GDP in year 1) ÷ Real GDP in year 1] × 100

Worked example (Canada):

YearReal GDP (millions of Canadian $, base year 2002)Growth rate
20061,283,033—
20071,311,260[(1,311,260 − 1,283,033) ÷ 1,283,033] × 100 = 2.2%
20081,318,054[(1,318,054 − 1,311,260) ÷ 1,311,260] × 100 = 0.51%
20091,285,604[(1,285,604 − 1,318,054) ÷ 1,318,054] × 100 = −2.46%

Note how 2009 shows negative growth (a contraction in real GDP) — consistent with the global financial crisis of that period.

Consequences of Economic Growth

Economic growth is a policy objective of all governments because of the huge benefits it can bring — but it also carries significant negative consequences.

Positive consequences

  • Higher living standards: economic growth is an increase in national output, which is equivalent to an increase in national income. If GDP per capita rises (i.e. growth outpaces population growth), the income of the average person increases, and it is normally assumed this raises living standards.
  • Higher tax revenues: higher incomes generally generate greater tax revenues for government (even without changing tax rates), enabling more spending on merit goods and public goods, further improving living standards. Tax systems can also redistribute income and reduce inequality.
  • Improved international competitiveness: higher productivity associated with growth can improve a country's export competitiveness, increasing aggregate demand further via net exports. However, higher national income also increases demand for imports, so the net effect on trade depends on many factors.
  • Non-inflationary ("balanced") growth: see Figure 21.5 below.
  • Greater investment in human capital: as national incomes rise, there is compelling evidence of greater demand for education and human capital. Benjamin Friedman (The Moral Consequences of Economic Growth) argues growth is valuable not only for material improvements but for how it affects social attitudes, political institutions, and a society's "moral character" — e.g. greater demands for freedoms and democracy.
Figure 21.5: Non-inflationary growthAn AD/AS diagram where BOTH the LRAS curve shifts right (from LRAS₁ to LRAS₂, due to supply-side improvements) AND the AD curve shifts right (from AD₁ to AD₂) by a matching amount, so that the new equilibrium output (Y₁ to Y₂, an increase) occurs WITHOUT any rise in the average price level. This illustrates that as long as AD grows in line with the growth in LRAS (driven by supply-side improvements), GDP increases without upward pressure on prices — this is "non-inflationary growth". Because more labour is needed to produce the higher level of output, unemployment should also fall. Supply-side policies and the growth they generate can therefore result in both lower unemployment and downward/stable pressure on the price level.
Key concept: Economic wellbeingThe link between rising real GDP and improvements in material living standards (and, via tax revenue and human capital effects, wider wellbeing) is a direct illustration of the "economic wellbeing" key concept.

Negative consequences

  • Possible fall in quality of life for individuals: the drive for growth and higher incomes may come at the cost of leisure time and personal relationships. People may work longer hours, sacrificing things that would improve genuine wellbeing. A preoccupation with greater material wants may not make people happier (link to Chapter 13's international indicators of happiness/wellbeing, which are not always directly related to national income).
  • Structural change and structural unemployment: economic growth typically involves structural change — economies move from a larger share of output in the primary sector, to the secondary sector, and eventually to the tertiary sector (and structural change occurs within sectors too). As these adjustments occur, structural unemployment tends to result, meaning not everyone benefits from growth equally. Technology and globalization contribute to this, e.g. firms relocating production to lower-cost countries and technological advances reducing labour demand in many advanced countries.
  • Conflict with equity: the fact that the benefits of growth are not shared equitably is highlighted by UN Sustainable Development Goal 8, which calls to "promote inclusive and sustainable economic growth, employment and decent work for all". Growing inequalities mean the goal of economic growth can come into conflict with the goal of equity (this is examined in depth in Chapter 22).
  • Conflict with sustainability (environmental costs): rapid economic growth causes higher emissions of greenhouse gases and higher levels of waste (see Chapter 9). Higher national income also means higher levels of resource consumption, involving the depletion of non-renewable resources. At projected rates of population growth, the "equivalent of three planets" could be required to sustain current lifestyles (SDG Goal 12). Thus the goal of economic growth can come into direct conflict with the goal of sustainable development.
Key concept: EquityFlagged directly alongside SDG 8 — growth that is not shared fairly across a population undermines equity even while raising average/aggregate measures of income.
Key concept: SustainabilityFlagged directly alongside SDG 12 — growth's resource and environmental costs place it in tension with sustainable development.
