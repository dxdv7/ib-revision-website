What is fiscal policy?

“Fiscal” is defined as pertaining to government revenue and expenditure. Government (public) spending refers to total spending by all levels of government in a country – central/federal/national, regional (state/provincial) and local (municipal).

Broadly, there are three categories of public spending:

  • Capital expenditure: spending that adds to the capital stock of the economy, e.g. upgrading a national highway, or building schools and hospitals.
  • Current expenditure: ongoing spending, e.g. purchases of textbooks in schools, or wages paid to public-sector employees.
  • Transfer payments: benefits paid to people in the economy for which no goods or services are produced in return, e.g. unemployment benefits, child support payments, disability payments, pensions.

Governments receive income from several sources: income taxes and social security payments from households, social security payments and corporate taxes from firms, indirect taxes on expenditure on goods and services, tariffs on imports, income from the profits of government-owned (nationalized) businesses or the sale of nationalized industries, and income earned from renting out government-owned buildings or land.

Fiscal policy – definitionFiscal policy is the set of a government’s policies relating to its expenditure and taxation rates. Governments use expansionary fiscal policy to increase aggregate demand, and contractionary (or deflationary) fiscal policy to reduce aggregate demand.
The government budgetEach year governments issue their national budgets, laying out expected spending and revenues for the coming year – known as the “fiscal stance”. If a government expects to earn more than it spends, it runs a budget surplus. If it plans to spend more than it earns, it runs a budget deficit. If expected revenues equal expenditures, the budget is balanced. To finance a budget deficit (“run a budget deficit”), the government must borrow money, either from households and firms within the country or from abroad. It does this by selling government bonds: people buy the bonds as a form of saving, effectively lending money to the government; the government eventually pays this back, plus extra interest payments.

What are the aims of fiscal policy?

  • Maintenance of a low and stable rate of inflation
  • A low unemployment rate
  • A stable economic environment for long-term growth
  • Reducing fluctuations in the business cycle
  • Promoting an equitable distribution of income
  • Achieving an external balance between export revenue and import expenditure

What is expansionary fiscal policy?

Key concept: ChangeExpansionary fiscal policy is a form of Keynesian demand management. Keynesian economists believe that government intervention through demand management is necessary to control the economy, particularly unemployment.

Governments can use a range of fiscal measures to increase AD:

  • To encourage greater consumption, the government can lower income taxes to increase disposable income, which is likely to increase AD.
  • To encourage greater investment, the government can lower corporate taxes so firms enjoy higher after-tax profits that can be used for investment, likely increasing AD.
  • Governments can undertake major investment projects themselves, directly increasing spending to improve/increase public services, directly shifting AD to the right.

Theory of Knowledge link: paradigm shiftsThomas Kuhn’s concept of a “paradigm shift” (from the philosophy of science) describes how a discipline’s dominant theoretical framework can strongly resist change until mounting anomalies force a revolutionary shift to a new paradigm (e.g. the shift from an Earth-centred to a Sun-centred model of the universe). Economics shows a similar pattern: before Keynes, classical economics’ paradigm rested on a belief in free markets and minimal government intervention. The mass unemployment of the 1930s Great Depression was an anomaly the classical paradigm could not explain, opening the door for Keynes’s challenge that government should deliberately manage aggregate demand. Keynesian demand management then dominated policy for roughly 30 years, before a monetarist/new classical revival (from the 1970s) challenged it again. After the 2008 financial crisis, many governments reverted to Keynesian-style expansionary fiscal policy. Whether the more recent critics of demand management are “right” to challenge it is, in Kuhn’s sense, not a question with a simple yes/no answer.

How effective are fiscal policies?

Key concept: InterventionA key strength of fiscal policy is that it is effective, in the long run, at dealing with a deep recession. Historically, countries that adopted expansionary fiscal policy during the Great Depression of the 1930s recovered from the depression more quickly than those that did not. Similarly, after the 2008 world recession, many countries used Keynesian demand management via expansionary fiscal policy (increased government expenditure) to combat deflation.

A further strength is that government expenditure can be targeted at specific sectors of the economy – both increasing AD overall, and directing investment/tax cuts towards the areas or people that will benefit the most.

Case study: The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (2009)Signed by President Obama on 17 February 2009, the ARRA was a $787 billion fiscal stimulus aimed at putting money into the pockets of American families and small businesses, boosting demand and instilling confidence. It is credited with ending the Great Recession by July 2009. By 2012, the Congressional Budget Office reported Congress had added to ARRA spending in subsequent budgets, raising the total cost to $831 billion (most of the impact occurred by 2011).

ARRA had seven components:

  1. Immediate relief for families: tax cuts, tax credits and unemployment benefits – $250 billion sent to families, mostly delivered in the first two years.
  2. Modernize federal infrastructure: shovel-ready public works – $46 billion for transportation and mass transit, $31 billion to modernize federal buildings, $6 billion for water projects.
  3. Increase alternative energy production: jump-started America’s alternative energy industry and demonstrated federal support for clean energy.
  4. Expand health care: $138 billion spent, subsidizing the higher health-care costs recessions create.
  5. Improve education: $117 billion spent; education spending was rated the second-best way to create jobs (a University of Massachusetts study found $1 billion of federal spending creates 17,687 jobs).
  6. Invest in science, research and technology: $10 billion to modernize science facilities/research jobs investigating disease cures; $4 billion for rural/inner-city broadband; $4 billion for physics and science research.
  7. Help small businesses: $54 billion in tax deductions, credits and loan guarantees – relevant since small businesses drive 70% of new jobs.
Key concepts illustrated by ARRAThis case study is used to illustrate four key IB concepts: Equity (targeted relief to struggling families), Sustainability (investment in clean/alternative energy and modernized infrastructure for the future), Economic wellbeing (job creation, expanded health care and education spending), and Efficiency (support for small businesses and R&D that drives productive, competitive economic activity).

There are, however, several constraints/limitations on fiscal policy:

  1. Time lags: changing fiscal policy takes time – tax rates and government spending typically must go through democratic/legislative processes to gain approval, slowing implementation. Even once changes are made, it takes time before AD begins to shift; by the time the effects occur, the economy may already have recovered for other reasons, and the policy could become inflationary.
  2. Political pressure: government spending and taxation decisions are often driven by political rather than economic factors – e.g. a needed contractionary/deflationary fiscal policy may be politically blocked because parties fear losing votes if they raise taxes.
  3. Sustainable debt: governments may need to run budget deficits to fund expansionary fiscal policy, and over time this can accumulate into unsustainable national debt, which may in the long run prevent the government from funding necessary public spending.
  4. Effect on net exports: expansionary fiscal policy, especially if it involves increased government borrowing, may raise interest rates, which raises the exchange rate, making exports less attractive and imports more attractive (the interest rate/exchange rate link is covered in Chapter 26). This leads to a fall in net exports (X−M), which works against the intended expansionary outcome.
  5. Crowding-out effect: if government tries to increase spending through increased borrowing, it monopolizes the funds available for borrowing in the economy, meaning firms may not be able to access funds for investment, so investment falls. AD then does not shift as much as intended, since the rise in government spending (G) is partially or completely offset by a fall in investment (I). The increased demand for borrowed funds by government also tends to raise the interest rate, which itself acts like contractionary monetary policy. Higher Level Crowding out is examined in more depth as an HL topic in Chapter 19.
  6. Inability to achieve specific targets: it is very difficult, if not impossible, to finely tune fiscal policy to hit precise targets. Because government spending and tax changes are large and affect many different areas of the economy, it is possible to move the economy in the desired direction (expanding or contracting), but difficult to predict the precise outcome.

What are the costs of having high levels of government debt?

In the short term, many stakeholders in the economy benefit from deficit spending, as it drives economic growth. However, as government debt grows larger, year upon year, there will be long-term costs to bear – in particular an increase in debt servicing costs (the amount of money needed to make payments on the principal and interest on a loan in a given time period). As debt increases, debt servicing costs increase, and the government must spend more of its budget on interest costs. This can have several negative effects:

  1. It may lead to crowding out of private investment (as above): government borrowing monopolizes funds available for borrowing, so firms may not access funds for investment and investment falls.
  2. As interest payments increase as a percentage of government budget expenditure, this can have a damaging effect on other areas of spending – benefits and services provided by government may need to be cut.
  3. If the government wishes to maintain existing levels of benefits and services, it may require higher rates of taxation to fund the debt-servicing expenditure. This may in turn lead to falling output and incomes, since higher taxation is itself a deflationary fiscal policy.
  4. It may decrease the government’s ability to respond to emergencies – natural disasters, financial crises, and even military actions. If government debt is already too big, the government has fewer fiscal options available. For example, after the 2008 financial crisis, the US government had total debt of around 68% of GDP, allowing it to respond by cutting taxes and increasing spending; whether the same response would be possible with much higher debt-to-GDP ratios is a matter of debate.

Higher Level The multiplier effect

The multiplier conceptIf a government decides to fill a deflationary gap by increasing its own spending, the final increase in aggregate demand will actually be greater than the amount of spending itself. This is explained by the multiplier effect, which relies on the concepts of injections and withdrawals in the circular flow of income. Government spending and business investment are injections into the circular flow, and any injection is multiplied through the economy as people receive a share of the income and then spend part of what they receive.

Worked illustration: a government spends $100 million on a school building project. This $100 million becomes income for a vast number of people providing the factors of production – labour, such as architects, engineers, builders, electricians, plumbers and designers, as well as providers of capital and raw materials such as concrete, steel, minerals, water and electricity. So the initial $100 million ends up as income in the pockets of the people who provided the factors of production for the building project.

What do these people do with their income? Some of it goes back to the government as taxes, some of it is saved, some is spent on foreign goods and services (imports), and the rest is spent on domestically produced goods and services. The first three of these (taxes, saving, imports) are withdrawals from the circular flow. The money spent on domestic goods and services becomes income to a new set of recipients, who behave in the same way – paying some in taxes, saving some, spending some on imports, and the rest on domestic goods and services. In each successive “round”, some income is withdrawn from the circular flow, and some stays to be re-spent.

Simplified numerical example: the government spends $100 million. The average behaviour observed in the economy is: 20% of additional income goes to taxes, 10% is saved, and 10% is spent on imports. This means the remaining 60% of all additional income is spent on domestic goods and services. This proportion (60%, or 0.6) is known as the marginal propensity to consume (MPC), expressed as a decimal. In this economy, MPC = 0.6.

Tracing the rounds of spending: the government’s $100 million goes to architects, engineers, providers of raw materials, etc. They pay $20 million in taxes, $10 million leaks out as savings, and $10 million is spent on imports – so $40 million (40%) leaks from the circular flow in this round. The remaining $60 million is spent on a wide range of goods (food, clothing, entertainment, books, car repairs), and the recipients of this $60 million behave the same way, with 40% again leaving the circular flow and 60% remaining to be re-spent as other people’s income – and so on, round after round.

The final addition to national income, once all the money has been spent and re-spent, amounts to $250 million – i.e. 2.5 times the original government spending of $100 million. In this example, the multiplier is equivalent to the value 2.5: any injection into the circular flow of this economy would contribute 2.5 times its amount to national income.

Rather than working through a complicated round-by-round table every time, the value of the multiplier can be calculated directly using a formula, based on either the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) or the marginal propensity to withdraw (MPW). The MPW is the sum of the marginal propensity to save (MPS), the marginal rate of taxation (MRT), and the marginal propensity to import (MPM).

Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − mpc)
OR
Multiplier = 1 ÷ (mps + mrt + mpm) = 1 ÷ mpw
Worked example

Worked calculation (from the example above): where mpc = 0.6, the multiplier is 1 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5. Equivalently, where mps = 0.1, mrt = 0.2, and mpm = 0.1, the multiplier is 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1) = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5 (the same answer, confirming the two formulas are equivalent). This means a $100 million injection of government spending into this economy ultimately raises national income by $100 million × 2.5 = $250 million once all the rounds of re-spending are complete.

Worked example (further practice)Question (a): Calculate the multiplier for an economy where the marginal propensity to consume is 0.75.
Answer: Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4.
Question (b): By how much will national income increase in total if there is an investment of $50,000?
Answer: An investment of $50,000 will result in a final increase in national income of 4 × $50,000 = $200,000.

Any change in any of the withdrawals from the circular flow will change the economy’s multiplier. If the taxation rate increases, the value of the multiplier will fall. If the marginal propensity to import falls, the value of the multiplier will increase.

Limitation: estimating the multiplierIf a government is planning to intervene to fill a deflationary gap, it must have some idea of two things: first, an estimate of the size of the gap between equilibrium output and full-employment output; second, an estimate of the value of the multiplier, in order to judge the suitable increase in aggregate demand needed to inject into the economy to fill the gap. The practical difficulty of estimating both of these values accurately is one of the key limitations of using fiscal policy to manage aggregate demand.

Monetary policy

Monetary policy – definitionMonetary policy is the set of official policies governing the supply of money and the level of interest rates in an economy. Governments use expansionary monetary policy to increase aggregate demand and contractionary (or deflationary) monetary policy to reduce aggregate demand.

Many different interest rates exist in an economy (e.g. mortgage rates or “competitive financing” rates offered by private, profit-making commercial banks). Banks are regulated by government but are mainly free to set these rates themselves. When we talk about interest rates as a tool of monetary policy, we mean the base rate (also called the discount rate or prime rate) set by a country’s central bank. The central bank is not a private profit-making bank; it is essentially the government’s bank and has ultimate authority over the money supply. In some countries the government controls the central bank, but in most industrialized countries today the central bank is an independent body with primary responsibility for maintaining low and stable inflation. Changes in the central bank’s base rate ultimately affect all borrowing and lending in the economy, and are an important signal of a country’s monetary policy – even where the central bank is formally independent, its activities are usually treated as part of overall government monetary policy.

What are the aims of monetary policy?

  • Maintenance of a low and stable rate of inflation, usually tied to “inflation targeting”, where the central bank sets a specific medium-term target inflation rate (e.g. 2%)
  • A low unemployment rate
  • A stable economic environment for long-term growth
  • Reducing fluctuations in the business cycle
  • Achieving an external balance between export revenue and import expenditure

What is expansionary monetary policy?

Key concept: ChangeChanges in the central bank’s base rate affect the level of AD in the economy. To increase AD, the central bank might lower the base rate of interest, which ultimately reduces the cost of borrowing and can lead to increases in both consumption and investment. Alternatively, it could increase the supply of money, which lowers its price (the interest rate); a lower interest rate similarly increases consumption and investment. This is known as expansionary or “loose” monetary policy.

The policy trade-offAs with expansionary fiscal policy, expansionary monetary policy involves a trade-off between lower unemployment and higher inflation.

How effective are monetary policies?

Strengths:

  1. Quick to put into place: in most countries the interest rate is set by the central bank and can be altered quickly when felt necessary.
  2. No political intervention: because the interest rate is normally adjusted by the central bank, it does not have to go through political processes before it can be changed – the opposite of fiscal policy. Because the central bank is usually independent of the government in power, it can implement politically unpopular policies such as raising interest rates. (This strength is somewhat theoretical – in many countries, although the central bank is said to be independent, governments often still influence its decisions for political reasons.)
  3. An absence of “crowding out”: expansionary fiscal policy involving increased government borrowing may lead to higher interest rates and crowd out private investment. This is not the case with monetary policy, where interest rates are simply lowered directly.
  4. The ability to make small changes: interest rates may be adjusted by as little as one quarter of one percent, allowing more precise fine-tuning than fiscal policy and the setting of exact targets (e.g. an inflation target of 2%). This fine-tuning is helped by the speed of implementation and the lack of political involvement noted above.

Real-world illustration: a news report titled “ECB signals it will move to boost growth amid fears of low inflation” illustrates a central bank (the European Central Bank) signalling a shift towards expansionary monetary policy in response to concerns about persistently low inflation.

Limitations:

  1. Time lags: although quick to change, monetary policy still takes time to have an effect on the economy – it may take a number of months before there is a noticeable effect on aggregate demand. By that time, economic conditions may have changed and the policy may no longer be appropriate.
  2. Ineffectiveness when interest rates are low: expansionary monetary policy through cuts in the interest rate cannot be used forever – eventually interest rates approach zero and there is no room left for further cuts.
  3. Low consumer and business confidence: even when interest rates are reduced, the effect on expenditure may be dampened by low consumer and business confidence – especially if the economy is in a deep recession.

Higher Level How do commercial banks create money?

Commercial banks can actually create money through a process known as credit creation, which can have a significant effect on the money supply in an economy. Credit creation occurs when commercial banks lend money to customers (individuals or businesses) by making loans based upon the deposits customers have made with them. Crucially, it is not simply a case of lending out the exact same money that was deposited – if that were all that happened, no new money would be created. Because banks receive deposits and lend out a multiple of the deposit value, this process is sometimes called the money (or banking credit) multiplier.

The size of the money multiplier is related to the minimum reserve requirement – the percentage of their deposits that commercial banks are legally required by the central bank to hold in reserve, so they can meet the cash requirements of their depositors.

Worked example of credit creation: a new depositor places $100,000 in a bank. The central bank in that country insists upon a minimum reserve requirement of 20%. This means the bank must keep $20,000 in reserve to meet the cash requirements of the depositor. The bank now has $80,000 of the new deposit that is not being used. A business customer asks the bank for a loan of $100,000 to fund investment in a new machine. The bank decides the firm is credit-worthy and agrees the loan, crediting the firm’s bank account with $100,000 – simply a computer entry. The firm’s bank account balance increases by $100,000, and it now also has a loan/debt of $100,000. At this point, $100,000 of new money has been created.

In order to meet the reserve requirement of 20% on this new loan, the bank needs $20,000 in reserve – but this can be covered using $20,000 of the $80,000 unused new deposit, leaving $60,000 still not being used. The bank can then use this remaining $60,000 to fund a further three loans of $100,000 each, each backed by $20,000 of reserves – or, equally, it could simply lend $300,000 in total, supported by the whole $60,000. In both cases the bank meets the minimum reserve requirement of 20% of all loans held in reserve. At the end of the whole process, the original deposit of $100,000 has become deposits of $500,000 in total – a money multiplier of 5.

This confirms the credit-creation example above: an initial deposit of $100,000, with a 20% reserve requirement, can ultimately support total deposits of $100,000 × 5 = $500,000 across the banking system.

Higher Level What tools are available to control the money supply?

Key concept: ChangeThere are a number of methods the central bank may use to affect the size of the money supply, and thus the interest rate. Four main tools are covered here.

1. Minimum reserve requirements

The minimum reserve requirement is the percentage of deposits commercial banks are legally required by the central bank to hold in reserve. There is a direct relationship between the size of the minimum reserve requirement and the size of the money multiplier – the larger the minimum reserve requirement, the smaller the money multiplier (and vice versa).

Using reserve requirements as policyTo reduce the money supply (contractionary monetary policy), the central bank should increase the minimum reserve requirement. This reduces banks’ ability to create credit, reducing the money supply, which in turn increases interest rates and thus reduces AD as consumption and investment fall. To increase the money supply (expansionary monetary policy), the central bank should reduce the minimum reserve requirement, increasing banks’ ability to create credit, increasing the money supply, lowering interest rates, and thus increasing AD as consumption and investment rise.

2. Open market operations

Open market operations involve the central bank buying and selling government securities (bonds) in the open market. A government security is a bond, usually issued by the central bank, that offers interest on its nominal value and is redeemable after a given number of years. Government securities are considered very low-risk since they are guaranteed by the government.

Figure 17.4: Open market operationsAxes: interest rate (vertical) against quantity of loanable funds (horizontal). A downward-sloping demand curve for loanable funds (D) is shown alongside two supply curves, S1 and S2 (S2 lies to the left of S1, representing a reduced supply of loanable funds). An arrow labelled “buying/selling securities” links the two supply curves. If the central bank sells more government securities (contractionary monetary policy), the supply of loanable funds at the banks falls, shown as a shift from S1 to S2, and the interest rate rises from i1 to i2. If the central bank instead buys back its own securities (expansionary monetary policy), the supply of loanable funds increases, shown as a shift from S2 to S1, and the interest rate falls from i2 to i1.

Selling securities reduces the money that commercial banks have available to lend, increasing the cost of borrowing and so raising interest rates – this lowers AD as consumption and investment fall. Buying back securities increases the money commercial banks have to lend, reducing the cost of borrowing and lowering interest rates – this raises AD as consumption and investment rise.

3. Changes in the central bank minimum lending rate

The minimum lending rate is also known as the base rate, discount rate, or refinancing rate – the rate of interest the central bank charges on loans and advances to commercial banks. It operates as the base rate for the whole banking system, influencing the interest rates charged on bank loans, mortgages, and credit transactions throughout the economy. The central bank controls the level of the minimum lending rate and can raise or lower it as it wishes.

Using the minimum lending rate as policyIf the central bank implements a contractionary monetary policy, it raises the minimum lending rate. Commercial banks and other financial institutions (e.g. building societies) then raise both their lending rates and the interest rates paid to savers. This discourages consumers and businesses from borrowing, and encourages saving, reducing consumption and investment and so reducing AD. If the central bank implements an expansionary monetary policy, it lowers the minimum lending rate; banks and financial institutions lower both lending rates and rates paid to savers, encouraging borrowing and discouraging saving, thus increasing consumption and investment, increasing AD.

4. Quantitative easing (QE)

Quantitative easing involves the introduction of new money into the money supply by the central bank – the aim, obviously, is to expand the economy, so it is a form of expansionary monetary policy. QE became a common form of monetary policy following the 2008 financial crisis, after many countries found that even though interest rates had been cut very low, AD was still not increasing because low consumer and business confidence was preventing consumers and producers from borrowing more. QE was an additional means of increasing the money supply in these circumstances.

The process: the central bank injects new money directly into the economy by purchasing assets, mostly securities, from commercial banks and other financial institutions, using newly created electronic cash. This has a number of expansionary effects:

  • It increases the reserves of commercial banks as they sell their securities to the central bank, increasing their liquidity and encouraging them to lend more to households and firms, increasing consumption and investment and so increasing AD.
  • It lowers interest rates, reducing the debt burden of people and firms who have previously borrowed money; this should increase consumer and business confidence.
  • Lower interest rates cause exchange rates to fall, making exports less expensive and imports more expensive, which should increase net exports (X−M), increasing AD. (This link between interest rates and exchange rates is explained fully in Chapter 26.)

Higher Level The equilibrium nominal interest rate and the money market

Interest rates are often referred to as the price of money, but more accurately they represent the opportunity cost of holding/spending money. The nominal rate of interest is the rate of interest available in the money market, without allowing for inflation. If the rate of interest is adjusted for inflation, it is called the real rate of interest.

Demand for money

If a consumer, firm, or government holds/spends money, they forgo other uses that money could have been put to (e.g. saving or investment). If nominal interest rates are high, holding/spending money means giving up a large return on savings/investment, so people will hold/demand less money. If nominal interest rates are low, the opportunity cost of holding money is smaller, so people will hold/demand more money. This explains why the demand curve for money is downward sloping.

Figure 17.5: The demand curve for moneyAxes: nominal interest rates (vertical) against quantity of money (horizontal). A single downward-sloping demand curve, DM, is shown; at a higher interest rate a smaller quantity of money (Q1) is demanded, and at a lower interest rate a larger quantity (Q2) is demanded.

Supply of money

The supply of money in an economy is controlled by the central bank and is generally considered to be fixed at any given time; it is therefore usually shown as a perfectly inelastic (vertical) supply curve.

Figure 17.6: The supply curve for moneyAxes: nominal interest rates (vertical) against quantity of money (horizontal). The supply curve, SM, is drawn as a single vertical line at quantity QM. Because the money supply is controlled by the central bank and not affected by the nominal interest rate, the quantity supplied remains constant at QM even as the nominal interest rate varies (e.g. between i1 and i2).

Money market equilibrium

The money market is where the demand for and supply of money come together to determine the equilibrium nominal rate of interest. As in any market, equilibrium occurs where demand equals supply – here, where the demand for money equals the supply of money.

Figure 17.7: The money marketAxes: nominal interest rates (vertical) against quantity of money (horizontal). A downward-sloping demand curve for money, DM, is shown together with (at least) two vertical money-supply curves, SM1 (at a smaller quantity, Q1) and SM2 (at a larger quantity, Q2). The equilibrium nominal interest rate where SM2 equals DM is i2 (a lower rate, associated with the larger money supply Q2). If the central bank adopts a contractionary monetary policy (using any of the tools above), the money supply falls from Q2 to Q1, and the new equilibrium nominal interest rate rises to i1. If the central bank instead adopts an expansionary monetary policy, the money supply rises from Q1 to Q2, and the new equilibrium nominal interest rate falls to i2.

Nominal versus real interest rates

The nominal rate of interest is the rate available in the money market, not allowing for inflation. To find the real rate of interest, we simply subtract the inflation rate from the nominal rate.

Real rate of interest = nominal rate of interest − inflation rate
Worked example

Worked example 1: if the nominal rate of interest in the market is 5% and the inflation rate is 3%, the real rate of interest is 5% − 3% = 2%. This matters for both savers and borrowers: a person saving at a nominal rate of 5% will find their real gain is only 2%, while someone borrowing at a nominal rate of 5% is really only paying back at a rate of 2% once inflation is accounted for.

Negative real interest ratesThis becomes especially important when real interest rates turn negative. Worked example 2: if the nominal interest rate is 2% and the inflation rate is 4%, the real rate of interest is 2% − 4% = −2%. This means anyone saving money finds the real value of their savings decreasing by 2% per year, whereas someone borrowing money is paying back 2% less than they borrowed, in real terms. This is obviously a real deterrent to saving, and a real stimulus to borrowing.
