Chapter 12: Market power – Monopoly and Oligopoly Higher Level

This entire chapter is Higher Level (HL) extension content in the IB Economics syllabus. It examines the two market structures at the high-market-power end of the spectrum — monopoly (one firm) and oligopoly (a few dominant firms) — in contrast to the low-market-power structures of perfect competition and monopolistic competition. In both monopoly and oligopoly, firms may possess significant market power, meaning they can influence price and/or output to a much greater degree than firms in more competitive markets. It is the potential for abuse of this market power that provokes government intervention.

Monopoly vs oligopolyMonopoly = a single dominant firm; oligopoly = a small number of dominant firms who are aware of and react to each other’s behaviour. Both can generate abnormal (supernormal) profits in the long run if barriers to entry are strong enough to keep out competitors.

Higher Level What is monopoly?

Assumptions of the theory of monopoly

  • There is only one firm producing the product, so the firm is the industry.
  • Barriers to entry exist, which stop new firms entering the industry and so maintain the monopoly.
  • As a consequence of barriers to entry, the monopolist may be able to make abnormal profits in the long run (unlike perfect competition, where abnormal profits are competed away).
How do we define “the industry”?Whether a firm really is a monopoly depends on how narrowly the industry is defined. Microsoft may be the only producer of a particular kind of software, but not of all software. A village’s only vegetable shop is a monopoly of vegetable sales in that local area, but if the geographic area is widened (e.g. to include a nearby town), the shop loses its monopoly. The real question is not simply “is this firm a monopoly?” but how much market power the firm has: to what extent can it set its own prices without worrying about rivals, and to what extent can it keep other firms out? This depends on how many competing substitutes are available. Example: an underground railway may have a monopoly of underground travel in a city, but it still faces competition from buses, taxis, and private transport for the broader “travel” market.

Higher Level Barriers to entry – what keeps a monopoly in place?

A monopoly can only maintain its market (monopoly) power if it can stop other firms entering the industry. These obstacles are called barriers to entry.

Key concept: EFFICIENCYBarriers to entry, and in particular economies of scale, connect directly to the key concept of efficiency — large-scale, low-unit-cost production can be efficient, but the same barriers can also protect firms from having to be efficient.

1. Economies of scale

Economies of scale (definition)Economies of scale are any decreases in long-run average costs that occur when a firm increases its scale of output. Firms may gain average cost advantages as their size increases, and these unit-cost advantages act as a barrier that a small new entrant cannot easily match.
  • Specialization: In small firms there are few, if any, managers, so people take on many roles they aren’t necessarily best suited to, raising unit costs. As firms grow, management can specialize into areas of expertise (production, finance, marketing), improving efficiency.
  • Division of labour: Breaking a production process into small, repeated tasks that workers perform efficiently. As firms grow they can break down production processes, use division of labour, and cut unit costs — e.g. assembly lines for cars and televisions, where each worker adds one part as the product moves along a conveyor belt.
  • Bulk buying: Larger firms can negotiate discounts from suppliers that smaller firms cannot get, reducing the cost of inputs and therefore unit costs.
  • Financial economies: Large firms can raise financial capital more cheaply than small firms. Banks charge a lower interest rate to larger firms since they are considered less risky (less likely to default) than small firms.
  • Transport economies: Large firms making bulk orders may be charged less per unit for delivery than smaller firms. Large firms may also run their own transport fleet, which costs less than paying another firm (which would include a profit margin) to transport their products.
  • Large machines (indivisibilities): Some machinery is too large/expensive to be owned and used efficiently by a small producer — e.g. a combine harvester is uneconomic for a small farmer, who must hire the equipment (paying a supplier’s profit margin). Once a farm reaches a certain size, owning its own combine harvester becomes feasible, cutting unit costs.
  • Promotional (marketing) economies: Almost all firms promote their products via advertising, sales promotion, or personal selling. The cost of promotion tends not to rise in the same proportion as output — if a firm doubles its output, it is unlikely to need to double its promotional spending. So the cost of promotion per unit of output falls as output rises. (The same logic applies to other fixed costs, such as insurance or security costs.)

Any of these economies of scale give cost savings and lower unit costs to the incumbent monopolist. A new firm trying to enter the industry will probably have to start small, and so will not enjoy the same economies of scale as the monopolist. Even if a new entrant somehow started at the same size as the monopolist, it would still lack the economies that come from accumulated expertise — e.g. managerial economies, promotional economies, and research and development experience. Without equal economies of scale, a potential entrant knows it could not compete: the incumbent monopolist could simply cut price to the level of normal profits, at which point the new entrant (with higher average costs, since it lacks the scale economies) would be making losses. This threat of retaliation, driven by the cost gap, deters entry.

2. Natural monopoly

Natural monopoly (definition)An industry is a natural monopoly if there are only enough economies of scale available in the market to support a single firm at the lowest achievable average cost. If a second firm entered, the market would have to split between the two firms, and neither could produce at a large enough scale to achieve low average costs — both would then make losses or, at best, normal profits.
Figure 12.1: A natural monopolyAxes: Price ($) on the vertical axis, Quantity demanded on the horizontal axis. Curves drawn: MC (marginal cost, relatively low and fairly flat), a long-run average cost curve LRAC that declines steeply over a wide range of output (reflecting substantial economies of scale), and two demand/average-revenue curves: D1&AR1 (the full market demand curve, further to the right/higher, representing all industry demand going to a single monopolist) and D2&AR2 (a demand curve shifted inward/down-left, representing what each of two firms would face if industry demand were split between them). The monopolist, facing D1&AR1, can earn abnormal profits by producing at any output between q1 and q2 because AR1 lies above LRAC across that range (dashed lines mark the price p and the two quantities q1, q2 bounding this profitable range). If a second firm entered, demand would split and each firm’s demand curve would shift left to D2&AR2, which lies below LRAC at every level of output — so with two firms, neither could even earn normal profits. Conclusion: because LRAC (shaped by economies of scale) only permits an abnormal profit if one firm satisfies the entire market, the market can only ever profitably support one producer — it is a natural monopoly. Examples: utilities supplying water, electricity, and gas (huge fixed infrastructure costs mean average cost keeps falling as more customers are served by a single network).

3. Legal barriers

Key concept: INTERVENTIONLegal barriers to entry are created directly by government/legal action — a reminder that market structure is not purely a product of cost conditions, but can be deliberately shaped by law (patents, licences, nationalization).

A firm may be granted a legal right to be the sole producer in an industry. Key examples:

  • Patents: Give a firm the exclusive legal right to produce and sell a product for a fixed number of years (usually around 20 years) after invention. Patents exist to encourage invention: if individuals/firms invest time and money in R&D only to have their invention copied immediately, there would be little incentive to innovate. Knowing that a successful invention earns a protected monopoly for a number of years encourages investment in research and development. Patents, along with copyrights and trademarks, are examples of intellectual property rights — just as private property rights protect physical property, these protect the creations of the mind. The pharmaceutical industry is a classic example: developing and testing a new drug can cost from $800 million to $2 billion and take 12–15 years to bring to market, so patents let drug companies recover costs and profit before generic drugs (bio-equivalent copies, exactly the same in dosage, safety, strength, and use) can legally be produced by rivals once the patent expires. Generic drugs are far cheaper because their producers avoid the original R&D costs, though they still must meet the same health/safety standards.
  • Government-granted rights / nationalized industries: A government may grant the right to produce a product to a single firm, for example by setting up a state postal service and banning other firms from entering that industry, or by selling the right to be the sole supplier of a service to a private firm (e.g. the sole licensed mobile network provider in a country), again banning other firms.

4. Brand loyalty

A monopolist’s product may have gained such huge brand loyalty that consumers think of the product as the brand itself. Example: in the early days of the vacuum cleaner, people simply called the product a “Hoover” (the brand name), rather than by its generic product name. If brand loyalty is this strong, new firms may be deterred from entering, since they doubt they could make their product seem sufficiently different to break that loyalty.

5. Anti-competitive behaviour

A monopolist may also try to block competition through restrictive practices, which may be legal or illegal. For example, an established monopolist is in a strong financial position to start a “price war” if a new firm enters: the incumbent cuts price to a loss-making level and, because of its size/reserves, can sustain the losses for longer than the new entrant, eventually forcing the entrant out of the industry. Indeed, simply the knowledge that the incumbent could and would do this is often enough to dissuade a new firm from ever attempting entry.

Real-world case: Microsoft and the EUThe European Union Competition Commission has repeatedly fined Microsoft for anti-competitive bundling. In 2004 it fined Microsoft €497 million for bundling Windows Media Player and messaging technologies into Windows, arguing this shut out potential competitors and forced Microsoft to share technical information so rival products could be made compatible. Microsoft was fined a further €280.5 million in 2006 for failing to comply, lost its appeal in 2007, and was fined again in 2008 — €899 million, the largest fine the European Commission had ever issued. In 2009, the Commission found Microsoft guilty again, this time for bundling Internet Explorer into Windows, arguing that making IE available on 90% of the world’s PCs gave it “an artificial distribution advantage which other web browsers are unable to match,” harming both competition and the pace of innovation.

Higher Level How much market power does a monopolist have, and how is profit determined?

Because the monopolist is the industry, the monopolist’s demand curve is the industry demand curve, which is downward sloping. The monopolist can therefore control either the price or the quantity of the product — but not both simultaneously. A common misconception is that a monopolist can charge whatever price it likes and still sell any quantity it wants; in reality, to sell a larger quantity the monopolist must lower the price (movement along the downward-sloping demand curve).

Curves: a normal downward-sloping demand curve labelled D=AR, with a steeper downward-sloping MR curve lying below it (starting from the same point on the price axis, falling at roughly twice the rate), and an upward-sloping MC curve. The diagram shows the monopolist selling quantity q at price P (dashed lines from the D=AR curve at output q up to the price axis at P). Because the monopoly firm controls the entire industry’s output, it can restrict quantity in order to raise price — this is the essence of monopoly (market) power.

Profit outcomes facing a monopoly

If a monopolist can make abnormal profits in the short run and has effective barriers to entry (so rival firms cannot enter and compete the profits away), then the monopolist can go on making abnormal profits in the long run, for as long as the barriers hold.

Figure 12.3: Abnormal profits in the long run in monopolyAxes: Price and Cost ($) vertical, Output horizontal. Curves: U-shaped AC and MC, downward-sloping D=AR, and steeper MR below it. The firm maximizes profit at output q, where MC = MR. At that output, price is read off the AR curve (point P, higher) while average cost is read off the AC curve (point C, lower); since P > C, the firm earns abnormal profit, shown as the shaded rectangle PabC (bounded by price P at the top, average cost C at the bottom, and the two points a and b on the AR and AC curves directly above output q). As long as barriers to entry stop new firms from entering, this profit persists indefinitely into the long run.
Monopoly profit is not guaranteedIt is a common misconception that a monopolist will always earn abnormal profits — this is not true. If demand for the monopolist’s product is weak, it may not earn abnormal profit at all. If it makes losses in the short run, it can plan ahead to see whether changes could bring it to at least normal profits in the long run. If even that is not possible, the monopolist will close down, and — since this firm is the industry — the whole industry ceases to exist.
Figure 12.4: A monopolist making losses in the long runAxes: Price and Cost ($) vertical, Output horizontal. Curves: AC, MC, D=AR, MR as before. The profit-maximizing (loss-minimizing) output is q, where MC = MR, but here the AC curve lies above the AR curve at every level of output the firm could choose. At output q, average cost (point C, higher) exceeds price (point P, lower), so the firm makes a loss, shown as the shaded rectangle labelled Losses (bounded by C at top, P at bottom). Since there is no output level at which the firm can cover its average costs, the firm will not be willing to produce at all in the long run — there will be no industry.

Higher Level How efficient is a monopoly?

Key concept: EFFICIENCYUnlike perfect competition (which achieves both productive and allocative efficiency in long-run equilibrium), a profit-maximizing monopolist achieves neither productive nor allocative efficiency.
Figure 12.5: Productive and allocative efficiency in monopolyAxes: Price and Cost ($) vertical, Output horizontal. Curves: AC, MC, D=AR, MR. The monopolist produces at the profit-maximizing output q1 (where MC = MR), earning the shaded “Abnormal profit” rectangle. This output is restricted below both: (i) the allocatively efficient output q2, which would occur where the D=AR curve crosses the MC curve (P = MC), and (ii) the productively efficient output, which occurs at the minimum point of the AC curve. Because q1 is less than the output needed for either condition, output is restricted purely to force up the price and maximize profit — neither productive nor allocative efficiency is achieved.
Is this market failure?Yes — market failure clearly can exist in monopoly. Allocative efficiency is not achieved by a profit-maximizing monopolist, so there is a market failure whenever firms follow the profit-maximization rule. The remaining question is whether monopoly has offsetting advantages that outweigh this market failure, making government intervention unnecessary.

Higher Level Monopoly compared with perfect competition

Advantages of monopoly over perfect competition

1. Economies of scale. Monopolies may achieve large economies of scale simply because of their size (monopolies do not have to be big, but if the industry itself is large, the monopolist should gain substantial scale economies). If this pushes the monopolist’s MC curve down far enough, it is possible for the monopolist to produce at a higher output and a lower price than would occur under perfect competition. (This relative comparison is debatable and depends on how large the scale advantage really is.)

Figure 12.6: Economies of scale in monopolyAxes: Price ($) vertical, Output horizontal. Two supply-side curves are compared: the red Industry supply: perfect competition curve (upward sloping, representing the sum of many small perfectly-competitive firms’ MC curves, which lack the scale economies of a monopolist) and the blue MC: monopoly curve (which lies below and to the right of the perfect-competition supply curve, reflecting the monopolist’s lower unit costs from economies of scale). A downward-sloping D=AR curve and its associated MR curve are also shown. Under perfect competition, equilibrium occurs where industry supply meets demand, giving price P1 and quantity Q1. Under monopoly (with significant economies of scale), the monopolist produces where MC = MR, giving a greater output Q2 at a lower price P2 than under perfect competition. Conclusion: when a monopoly has strong economies of scale, it can, in principle, offer consumers a lower price and higher output than a perfectly competitive industry made up of many small firms.

2. Research and development. Firms in perfect competition are by definition small and may find it hard to fund R&D. A monopolist earning abnormal profits is better placed to fund R&D, which in the long run could benefit consumers through better products and more choice.

Disadvantages of monopoly compared with perfect competition

If significant economies of scale do not exist, a monopoly may simply restrict output and charge a higher price than would occur under perfect competition, purely to maximize profit.

Figure 12.7: Monopoly versus perfect competition without economies of scaleAxes: Price ($) vertical, Output horizontal. Here there are assumed to be no cost differences between the monopolist and a perfectly competitive industry, so a single upward-sloping cost curve is used and labelled MC (= supply under perfect competition). A downward-sloping D=AR curve is shown with its MR curve below. Under perfect competition, equilibrium is where MC (=industry supply) meets D=AR, giving price P1 and output Q1. The profit-maximizing monopolist instead produces where MC = MR, restricting output to the smaller quantity Q2 (Q2 < Q1) and charging the higher price P2 (P2 > P1). Conclusion: without a cost advantage, monopoly produces less and charges more than perfect competition would for the identical cost structure.
Key concept: EQUITYThe high profits earned by monopolists may be considered unfair, especially by competitive firms unable to earn such profits, or by low-income consumers who must pay the higher price. The scale of this equity problem depends on the size and power of the monopoly, and the extent to which it is abusing its market power.
Summary: three problems with monopoly (vs perfect competition)
  • Monopolies are both productively and allocatively inefficient.
  • Monopolies can charge a higher price for a lower level of output.
  • Monopolies can exercise anti-competitive behaviour purely to protect their monopoly power.
These problems mean monopolies can act against the public interest — risking lower output (and consumption), higher prices, and lower consumer choice. This is why virtually all governments have laws and agencies to limit the abuse of market/monopoly power where it exists.

Higher Level What is oligopoly?

Assumptions of oligopoly

Oligopoly is where a few firms dominate an industry. The exact number of firms varies, but the key feature is that a large proportion of the industry’s output is shared by just a small number of firms.

Concentration ratio (definition)A common indicator of how concentrated an industry is. Concentration ratios are expressed as CRX, where X is the number of the largest firms being considered. For example, a CR4 shows the percentage of market share (or output) held by the four largest firms in the industry. The higher the percentage, the more concentrated the market power of those largest firms. CR4 is the most commonly used ratio to link concentration to a given market structure, although interpretation of the exact dividing lines between market structures is debatable.
A concentration ratio can be misleadingA CR4 of 80% might suggest high concentration, but the underlying distribution matters: if the largest firm holds 65% and the other three hold 5% each, this is a very different competitive situation from four firms each holding 20%, even though both give a CR4 of 80%. An alternative, more detailed indicator of concentration is the Herfindahl–Herschmann Index (HHI), which accounts for the size distribution of all firms, not just the top few.
Figure 12.8: CR4 ratios in different market structuresA horizontal spectrum/scale running from 0% to 100%, labelled “Percentage market share of largest 4 firms (CR4)”. It is divided into zones, each associated with a market structure and a general concentration level: Perfect competition at 0% (labelled “Low concentration”), Monopolistic competition occupying roughly the low range up to around 50% (still “Low concentration”), Oligopoly occupying roughly 50%–80% (labelled “Medium concentration” moving into “High concentration”), and Monopoly at 100% (“High concentration”). This illustrates that market structure can be thought of as a continuous spectrum of concentration, from perfect competition (0% concentration) through monopolistic competition and oligopoly to monopoly (100% concentration), rather than four completely separate boxes.

Worked example: In the US malt beverages industry there are 160 firms, and the CR4 is 90% — the four largest firms produce 90% of industry output, indicating a high concentration of market power among the top four and classifying the industry as an oligopoly. By contrast, the frozen fish and seafood industry has 600 firms and a CR4 of only 19%, suggesting low concentration — this industry is better classified as monopolistic competition.

Characteristics of oligopoly

Oligopolistic industries can look very different from each other in terms of product type:

  • Almost identical products, e.g. petrol (the product is essentially the same; only the company name differs).
  • Highly differentiated products, e.g. motor cars.
  • Slightly differentiated products with huge marketing budgets spent persuading consumers their version is better, e.g. shampoo.

In most examples of oligopoly there are distinct barriers to entry — usually large-scale production (economies of scale) or the strong branding of the dominant firms — but this is not always the case; some oligopolies have low barriers to entry.

Key concept: INTERDEPENDENCEThe feature common to all oligopolies is interdependence. In perfect competition and monopolistic competition, firms are all too small relative to the size of the market to influence it; in oligopoly, a small number of large firms dominate the industry, so each firm must take careful notice of its rivals’ actions. Interdependence creates two opposing tendencies: firms may want to collude to avoid surprises and unexpected outcomes (and, by colluding, act like a single monopolist and maximize industry profits); but there is also a tendency for firms to want to compete vigorously with each other to gain a greater market share.
Price rigidity in oligopolyOverall, prices in oligopoly tend to change much less than in more competitive markets. Even when production costs change, oligopolistic firms often leave their prices unchanged, out of fear of how competitors might react to a price change.

Higher Level Collusive vs non-collusive oligopoly

Collusive oligopoly (definition)Exists when firms in an oligopolistic market collude to charge the same prices for their products, in effect acting as a single monopolist, and so dividing up any monopoly profits that are made.

Two types of collusion

  • Formal collusion: Firms openly agree on the price they will all charge (or sometimes agree on market share or marketing expenditure instead). A collusive oligopoly of this kind is often called a cartel. Because this results in higher prices and less output for consumers, formal collusion is against the interests of consumers and is generally banned and illegal in most countries: if a country’s anti-trust authority finds firms engaged in such anti-competitive behaviour, the firms will be penalized with fines or other punishments. Collusion between governments, however, may be permitted — the prime example is OPEC (the Organisation for Petroleum Exporting Countries), which sets production quotas and prices for the world oil market.
  • Tacit collusion: Firms charge the same prices without any formal agreement. A firm may simply charge the same price as a dominant firm in the industry, or as its main competitors, by observing their prices — it is not necessary to communicate directly in order to end up charging the same price.

In both formal and tacit collusion, the outcome is the same: firms behave like a monopolist (single producer), charge the monopoly price, and share the resulting profits according to market share.

The colluding oligopolists jointly restrict output to q (where MC = MR) and charge price p (read off the AR curve). Incentive to collude vs incentive to cheatCollusive oligopoly offers one explanation of price rigidity in oligopoly: if firms are colluding (formally or tacitly) and are already receiving their share of long-run monopoly profits, they will try to keep prices stable in order to maintain that situation — this is the incentive to collude (i.e. to protect long-run monopoly profits). However, there is always an incentive to cheat on the agreement. Consider a collusive duopoly (an oligopoly of just two firms): the first firm to break the agreement and lower its price could gain a greater market share (until the other firm responds), potentially earning greater revenue and profit.
The danger of a price warIf one firm breaks the collusive agreement by cutting price, this risks triggering a “price war”: the other firm must lower its own price below the price-breaker’s in order to win back its market share. In turn, the first firm, having lost market share and revenue to the second firm’s undercutting, will need to lower its price again to compete. This process can spiral downward, with each firm repeatedly undercutting the other, ultimately harming both/all firms involved through much lower prices and profits than under stable collusion.
Non-collusive oligopoly (definition)Exists when firms in an oligopoly do not collude, and so must be very aware of the reactions of other firms when making pricing decisions. The behaviour of firms in a non-collusive oligopoly is called strategic behaviour, because firms must develop strategies that take account of all possible actions of their rivals. Economists use game theory to explain and predict how firms behave in these strategic situations.

Higher Level Game theory and the prisoner’s dilemma

Game theory considers the optimum strategy a firm could adopt given the different possible decisions of rival firms. For simplicity, economists often analyse a duopoly — a market made up of just two firms — assuming the firms have equal costs, produce identical products, and initially share the market evenly (so demand for each firm’s good starts out the same). These are, admittedly, quite strong simplifying assumptions.

Key concept: CHOICEThe payoff matrix above frames a genuine strategic choice facing each firm — keep price unchanged, or lower it — where the best choice depends entirely on what the rival firm chooses to do.
Key concept: INTERDEPENDENCEGame theory is the formal tool economists use to model interdependence: neither firm can decide its best price in isolation, because the payoff of each of its choices depends on what the rival does.

Working through the logic

Assume both firms currently charge $5.50 and are both making $6 million profit. They are not colluding, but are separately considering whether to lower their price to $5.00. Firm A has two choices: leave its price unchanged, or lower it.

  • Pessimistic/cautious approach (worst-case reasoning): If A leaves its price unchanged, the worst outcome (B lowers its price) gives A profit of $2 million. If A lowers its price, the worst outcome (B also lowers its price) gives A profit of $4 million. The best of these two worst outcomes is to lower price ($4m > $2m). The firm is maximizing its minimum possible profit — this is known as a “maximin” strategy: adopting the policy that has the least-bad worst outcome.
  • Optimistic approach (best-case reasoning): If A leaves price unchanged, the best outcome (B also doesn’t lower price) gives A $6 million. If A lowers its price, the best outcome (B doesn’t lower price) gives A $8 million. The best of these two best outcomes is again to lower price ($8m > $6m). This is known as a “maximax” strategy: trying to make the maximum profit available.

Both the maximin and the maximax strategies point Firm A toward the same decision: lower its price. By identical logic (the situation is symmetric), Firm B’s maximin and maximax strategies both also point toward lowering its price.

The prisoner’s dilemma outcomeIf both firms adopt the seemingly logical strategy of lowering price, they both end up making only $4 million each — less than the $6 million each they were earning (and could still be earning) by both keeping prices at $5.50. The price-cutting exercise, though individually rational, is actually harmful to both firms collectively. They would have been better off leaving prices unchanged, and would have benefited from the ability to collude — if only they could trust each other to do so.

Limitations of game theoryGame theory is useful for firms if they can predict fairly accurately the outcomes that will follow any set of decisions — but this is not necessarily easy. The more firms there are in an industry, the more difficult it becomes to estimate all the possible combinations of decisions and outcomes. So game theory is mostly useful when there are only a few firms in an industry (i.e. in oligopoly), where there is only a small number of possible options and outcomes can be predicted with reasonable accuracy.

The prisoner’s dilemma (Theory of Knowledge link)

The concept of the “prisoner’s dilemma” was developed by Flood and Dresher and formalized by Albert Tucker. It uses the example of two men arrested by police, who lack enough evidence and so separate the men, offering each the same conditions: (1) testify against your partner and go free (while your partner gets 10 years) — but only the first to confess gets this offer; (2) if both stay silent, each gets only 5 years. Each prisoner must choose between cooperating (staying silent) or betraying the other, raising questions about trust, cooperation, and whether it is human nature to save oneself at the expense of others.

Real-world illustration: cyclingThe same logic appears in competitive cycling. When two riders break away from the main group (the peloton), if they cooperate — each taking a turn at the front to shelter the other from the wind — they can stay ahead and finish first and second. If they don’t cooperate, the peloton catches them. Often, one rider (the “cooperator”) does all the hard work at the front while the other (the “betrayer”) sits in the leader’s slipstream, conserving energy — and the betrayer usually wins. Theorists have adapted the prisoner’s dilemma into game theory, and economists apply it to firm behaviour, particularly in oligopoly, where cooperating (collusion) can benefit both firms jointly, but each has an individual incentive to betray (undercut) the other.

Higher Level Non-price competition in oligopoly

Key concept: CHOICEBecause oligopoly firms tend not to compete on price (price rigidity), the concept of non-price competition becomes central — firms compete by offering variety and differentiation rather than lower prices.

Types of non-price competition include: use of brand names, packaging, special features, advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, publicity, sponsorship deals, and special distribution features such as free delivery and after-sales service. Oligopoly is characterized by very large advertising and marketing expenditures, as firms try to build brand loyalty and make demand for their product less price-elastic. This can be criticized as a misuse of scarce resources on persuasion rather than production — but it could also be argued that competition among large companies for consumer attention actually results in greater consumer choice.

Branding can disguise concentrationMany branded consumer goods that appear to come from a wide variety of competing companies are actually produced by just a small number of giant firms. A supermarket aisle of washing powders might suggest a vast number of competing brands, when in reality the majority are produced by just two multinational companies (e.g. Unilever and Procter & Gamble), who compete with each other across many product categories — home care, personal hygiene, health care, and beauty products.

Higher Level Market failure in oligopoly

As with any firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve, oligopoly firms will not produce at the allocatively efficient level of output if they are profit-maximizing — so there is a market failure. The existence of market power always creates risks in terms of output, price, and consumer choice.

If there is collusive oligopoly (formal or tacit) with barriers to entry, the firms collectively behave like a monopolist: they produce the profit-maximizing output q1 (where MC = MR), earning the shaded “Abnormal profit” rectangle, and then split this profit according to market share. As in monopoly, neither the allocatively efficient output qa (where AR = MC) nor the productively efficient output qp (minimum point of AC) is achieved — output is restricted below both, purely to force up price.
Non-collusive oligopolyThe same type of inefficiency (restricted output, price above marginal cost, no productive efficiency) occurs in non-collusive oligopolies too, although the degree of market power — and so the degree to which output is restricted — may be somewhat lower than in a fully collusive oligopoly.

Higher Level Government intervention to restrict market power

Why intervene? Five key problems

Key concept: INTERVENTION
  • Restriction of output, higher prices, and distorted resource allocation: firms with market power restrict/control output to force up prices; consumers get less of the good and pay a higher price, and there can be knock-on effects for resource allocation (e.g. employment levels), plus a loss of consumer surplus.
  • Lower consumer choice: fewer firms (or a single firm) dominating an industry means fewer available brands/varieties for consumers.
  • Productive inefficiency: production does not take place at the lowest possible unit cost, wasting resources.
  • Allocative inefficiency: there is an under-allocation of resources to the product, since the value consumers place on it (shown by demand/price) exceeds the cost of producing it (marginal cost).
  • Abnormal profits and inequity: higher prices in oligopoly or monopoly can exploit consumers, including low-income consumers, transferring purchasing power to the owners/shareholders of firms in the form of higher profits — this can worsen the distribution of income (reduced equity).

How might governments intervene?

Key concept: INTERVENTIONWhether market power counts as “abused” is a subjective judgement that varies from country to country, but where abuse is judged to exist, intervention is considered justified given the benefits of competition and the dangers of monopoly power.
Competition authoritiesMost governments set up an agency to promote competition and prevent abuse of monopoly power — variously called a “Competition Commission,” “Commerce Department,” or informally a “monopoly watchdog.” Typical powers/actions include:
  • Laws restricting the ability of firms to grow through mergers (two similarly-sized firms agreeing to combine into one larger firm) or takeovers (usually a larger firm acquiring a smaller one, sometimes “hostile” — i.e. without the target company’s consent). Regulators may block mergers/takeovers that would give a firm more than a set percentage of the market (e.g. 25%), or that would let a specified number of the largest firms control more than a set percentage of the market (e.g. the top 4 firms controlling more than 60%).
  • Laws against price fixing, making it illegal to collude over prices — illegal in the majority of the world’s countries.
  • Powers to stop firms insisting on a fixed retail price, or refusing to supply retailers, in order to control the market.
  • The ability to insist on average price levels that set a “fair rate of return,” based on the profit levels that might be expected in a competitive market.
  • The ability to make firms “unbundle” their products, making it easier for other firms to compete. Example: Microsoft selling Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Access as a single bundled “Office” package.
  • The (rarely used, extreme) ability to break up a monopoly into separate businesses.
  • The ability to set quality-of-service standards for an industry.
  • In extreme cases, taking an industry into public (government) ownership — producing goods/services through a nationalized industry owned by the state; this tends to be a highly publicized, politically contentious step.
Sector-specific regulatory bodiesGovernments may also create regulatory bodies for particular industries, tasked with representing consumer interests by promoting competition — for example, the Office of Gas and Electricity Markets (Ofgem) in the UK. Such bodies may have powers including: setting price controls (price capping); imposing fines for anti-competitive behaviour; insisting on a fair rate of return; forcing unbundling; breaking up a monopoly into separate businesses; setting quality-of-service standards; and bringing an industry into public ownership.

“Dominant companies have a special responsibility not to abuse their powerful market position by restricting competition.” — European Commission

Country example: New Zealand’s Commerce ActUnder New Zealand’s Commerce Act, any business must promote competition for the long-term benefit of consumers. Competitive markets keep prices low, maintain quality, offer consumers a range of choices, and give firms incentives to innovate, invest, and operate efficiently; anti-competitive behaviour jeopardizes all of this, as well as a firm’s own ability to win new customers. The Act prohibits anti-competitive agreements between businesses, such as agreements to fix prices, allocate markets, or restrict output. New Zealand’s Commerce Commission can take enforcement action, and courts can impose significant penalties on both businesses and the individuals responsible for breaches.
Real-world headlinesRegulatory scrutiny of large firms is an ongoing, global phenomenon — e.g. reports of the “Big Four” accounting firms being accused of anti-competitive behaviour, and the EU fining Google $2.4 billion for anti-competitive practices.
