What is an exchange rate?

An exchange rate is the value of one currency expressed in terms of another currency, for example €1 = US$1.12. Currencies are exchanged (traded) on the foreign exchange market (the Forex market), the largest market in the world in terms of cash movements. The market includes the trading of foreign currencies between governments, central banks, private commercial banks, MNCs and other financial institutions.

Did you know? – the Forex marketThe international foreign exchange market is known as the Forex market. There are five main centres of Forex trading: London, New York, Zurich, Frankfurt and Tokyo. On any given day, total trading on the Forex market can exceed US$1.5 trillion. Individuals and importers who need foreign currency generally do not buy directly from the Forex market – they buy from commercial banks, which charge a higher price (their commission) than is found on the Forex market. This is one way commercial banks compete with each other, by charging different rates of commission.

Exchange rates are usually expressed as a comparison of the values of two single currencies (e.g. Australian dollars for Japanese yen). It is also possible to express one currency against a collection (basket) of other currencies; when this is done the outcome is known as an exchange rate index. The exchange rate index is a weighted average index, like the consumer price index – it shows the exchange rate of one currency, expressed as an index, relative to a weighted basket of other currencies. The currencies chosen are usually the country’s major trading partners, and the weighting is usually based on the relative value of trade with each country (an example is Australia’s “trade weighted index”, TWI).

Exchange rate systems

The way a country manages its exchange rate is its exchange-rate regime. There are three main types – a fixed exchange rate, a floating exchange rate, and a managed exchange rate.

How does a fixed exchange rate system operate?

Key concept – InterventionA fixed exchange rate exists only because of continual government/central bank intervention in the foreign exchange market.

A fixed exchange rate is an exchange-rate regime where the value of a currency is fixed, or pegged, to the value of another currency, to the average value of a selection of currencies, or to the value of some other commodity, such as gold. As the value of the variable that the currency is pegged to changes, so does the value of the currency. Deciding upon, and then maintaining, the fixed value of the currency is carried out by the government or the central bank.

Revaluation vs devaluationIf the value of the currency in a fixed exchange-rate regime is raised, this is called a revaluation of the currency. If the value is lowered, this is called a devaluation of the currency. These terms are very specific and must be used only when referring to changes in fixed exchange rates (contrast with appreciation/depreciation, used for floating rates – see below).

Worked example – the Barbadian dollar (Bds$): fixed against the US dollar at a rate of 2 Bds$ = 1 US$ since 1975. So, 1 Bds$ = 50¢ US.

Figure 26.1: An increase in the supply of EC$Worked example: the EC$ is pegged at EC$1 = US$0.40, with demand D and supply S1 crossing exactly at that rate at Q1 = 100 million. If Eastern Caribbean residents buy more imports, they must supply more EC$ on the forex market to pay for them, shifting supply rightward from S1 to S2. At the fixed rate of 0.40 this creates excess supply: quantity supplied on S2 rises to Q2 = 150 million while quantity demanded stays at 100 million. To hold the peg, the central bank must buy up the 50 million surplus EC$ using its reserves of foreign currency.
Figure 26.2: An increase in the demand for EC$Starting from the same peg of EC$1 = US$0.40 with D1 and S1 crossing at Q1 = 100 million, suppose more tourists want EC$ to spend on holiday: demand shifts rightward from D1 to D2. At the fixed rate this creates excess demand of 40 million (quantity demanded rises to 140 million while supply stays at 100 million). To defend the peg the central bank itself sells EC$, shifting supply from S1 to S2 until it meets D2 exactly at the fixed rate, at the new equilibrium Q2 = 140 million – a sale that adds to the country’s foreign currency reserves.

Alternative (illegal-trade) method: a fixed exchange rate can also be maintained by making it illegal to trade the currency at any other rate. This is hard to enforce unless the government has an effective monopoly over currency conversion. It was carried out with some success by the Chinese government in the 1990s to keep the yuan pegged to the US$. The danger of this sort of control is that a black market may emerge in the currency, operating at a different rate.

How does a floating exchange rate system operate?

A floating exchange rate is an exchange-rate regime where the value of a currency is allowed to be determined solely by the demand for, and supply of, the currency on the foreign exchange market. There is no government intervention to influence the value of the currency.

Figure 26.3: A floating currencyThe market for Australian dollars (A$) in terms of Singapore dollars (S$). Demand for A$ (from holders of S$ who want Australian goods, assets or currency) slopes downward; supply of A$ (from Australians who want to buy things priced in S$) slopes upward. The equilibrium price, where the two curves cross, is the floating exchange rate: A$1 = S$0.90.
Appreciation vs depreciationIf the value of a currency in a floating exchange-rate regime rises, this is an appreciation of the currency. If the value falls, this is a depreciation of the currency. These terms must not be confused with revaluation/devaluation, which apply only to fixed-rate regimes.
Figure 26.4 – Changing values of the Canadian dollar and the euroTwo line (time-series) graphs covering the same roughly 30-day period (10 July to 7 August). The top graph plots Canadian dollars per 1 euro, fluctuating between a minimum of about 1.4170 and a maximum of about 1.4532. The bottom graph plots euros per 1 Canadian dollar over the identical period, fluctuating between a minimum of about 0.6881 and a maximum of about 0.7057. Because these are reciprocal exchange rates of the same currency pair, the two lines are mirror images of one another: whenever the Canadian-dollar-per-euro line rises (the Canadian dollar depreciating against the euro), the euro-per-Canadian-dollar line falls by the corresponding reciprocal amount (the euro appreciating against the Canadian dollar), and vice versa. This illustrates that appreciation of one currency in a pair is always simultaneously depreciation of the other – “two sides of the same coin”.

How do you calculate changes in exchange rates and the price of a good in different currencies?

In exam calculations you may be asked to compute exchange rates and changes in exchange rates, and their effect on the price of goods in different currencies.

Rate for €1 in US$ = 1 ÷ (US$ per €1 rate)

Exchange rates are always quoted as “how many units of one currency for one unit of another”. If you are given the rate one way round but need it the other way round, simply divide 1 by the quoted rate — the result tells you how many units of the first currency one unit of the second currency is worth.

Worked example

Suppose the British pound is quoted at £1 = US$1.30. To find how many pounds one US dollar buys, calculate 1 ÷ 1.30 = £0.77 (to 2 decimal places). This means a tourist arriving in the UK with US$500 could expect to receive approximately 500 × 0.77 = £385 when exchanging their dollars, before any bank commission is deducted.

Worked example 1: the US dollar is trading against the euro at a rate of US$1 = €0.8. What is the rate for €1 in US$?

If US$1 = €0.8, then €1 = 1 ÷ 0.8 = US$1.25
Worked example

Since US$1 = €0.8, one euro is worth 1 ÷ 0.8 = US$1.25. In other words, exchanging one euro would get you one dollar and twenty-five cents, confirming that at this rate the euro is worth more than the dollar.

Worked example 2: with the exchange rate above (US$1 = €0.8), what would be the cost in euros of a good selling for US$75?

If the good sells for US$75, then it will cost 75 × 0.8 = €60

To convert a price given in one currency into another, multiply it by the exchange rate showing how many units of the target currency each unit of the original currency is worth. Here, because US$1 is worth €0.8, multiplying a dollar price by 0.8 converts it into the equivalent euro price.

Worked example

A good priced at US$75 therefore costs 75 × 0.8 = €60 in the eurozone at this exchange rate.

Worked example 3: if the exchange rate changes from US$1 = €0.8 to US$1 = €0.9, explain what would happen to the euro price of a US-manufactured dress shirt exported to Europe from the USA at a cost of US$150.

Original: 150 × 0.8 = €120
New: 150 × 0.9 = €135
Worked example

With the original exchange rate of US$1 = €0.8, the dress shirt would cost €120 (150 × €0.8). With the new exchange rate, the euro has depreciated – it now costs more euros to buy the same amount of dollars – so the price of the shirt in euros increases to €135 (150 × €0.9).

What causes a change in the value of a country's currency in terms of another currency?

We need to consider what factors will shift the demand and supply curves for a currency. Using the example of the demand for US dollars by people in the EU: people in the EU will need to buy US dollars on the foreign exchange market in order to:

  • buy US exports of goods and services, and to travel in America
  • invest in US firms (foreign direct investment or portfolio investment)
  • save their money in US banks or other US financial institutions
  • make money by speculating on the US dollar

Thus the demand for the US dollar will rise if:

  • there is an increase in the demand for US goods and services. This could be caused by: US inflation rates being lower than EU inflation rates (making US goods relatively less expensive than EU goods); an increase in incomes in the EU, so people there increase their demand for all things including US imports; a change in tastes in the EU in favour of US products
  • US investment prospects improve, due to (for example) strong economic growth or new business-friendly policies in the US
  • US interest rates increase, making it more attractive to save there than in EU financial institutions
  • speculators in the EU think the value of the US dollar will rise in the future, so they buy it now – if correct, they can later sell those dollars when they are worth more, making a financial gain
Figure 26.5: An increase in the demand for the Australian dollarSupply of A$ stays fixed while demand shifts rightward from D1 to D2 (e.g. more foreign appetite for Australian exports or assets). The equilibrium price of the Australian dollar rises from P1 = S$0.90 to P2 = S$1.00, an appreciation, and the equilibrium quantity traded rises from Q1 = 100 million to Q2 = 125 million. Each A$ now buys more Singapore dollars.

Now consider the supply curve for US dollars. Americans supply US dollars on the foreign exchange market when they wish to:

  • buy EU goods and services and to travel in Europe
  • invest in EU firms (FDI or portfolio investment)
  • save their money in EU banks or other EU financial institutions
  • make money by speculating on the euro

In each case, Americans need euros and so must exchange dollars to get them, increasing the supply of dollars on the foreign exchange market. Thus the supply of the US dollar will rise if:

  • Americans increase their demand for EU goods and services, exchanging more dollars for euros. This could be caused by: US inflation being higher than EU inflation (making US goods relatively more expensive than EU goods); an increase in incomes in the USA, so Americans increase their demand for all things including EU imports; a change in tastes in the USA in favour of EU products
  • EU investment prospects improve
  • EU interest rates increase, making it more attractive to save there than in US financial institutions
  • speculators in the US think the value of the dollar will fall in the future, so they sell it now and buy euros – if correct, they can buy dollars back more cheaply later, making a financial gain
Figure 26.6: An increase in the supply of the Australian dollarDemand for A$ stays fixed while supply shifts rightward from S1 to S2 (e.g. higher Australian interest in imports). The equilibrium price of the Australian dollar falls from P1 = S$0.90 to P2 = S$0.80, a depreciation, and the equilibrium quantity rises from Q1 = 100 million to Q2 = 125 million. Each A$ now buys fewer Singapore dollars.

The managed exchange rate system

Key concept – InterventionA managed float sits between the two extremes: the currency is largely left to the market, but the authorities intervene selectively.

In reality, no currency in the world is allowed to be completely freely floating. Even where governments try to be as non-interventionist as possible, there will be times when the currency is subject to extreme fluctuations and the government, or central bank, will feel it must intervene. In the same way, frequent changes in the exchange rate, if it is completely freely floating, may cause uncertainty for businesses, which is not good for trade, so governments will be forced to intervene to stabilize the exchange rate.

Because of this, most exchange rate regimes in the world are managed exchange rates – regimes where the currency is allowed to float, but with some element of interference from the government. The most common system is where a central bank sets an upper and lower limit (a “band”) and then allows the currency to float freely so long as it does not move outside that band. If the exchange rate approaches the upper or lower limit, the central bank intervenes in the foreign exchange market. Central banks do not usually make these upper and lower limit values public, for fear of speculation.

Advantages and disadvantages of high and low exchange rates

The actual level of the exchange rate has marked economic effects upon a country. Understanding these helps explain why governments intervene to influence the value of the exchange rate.

Advantages of a high exchange rate

Key concept – EfficiencyA strong currency can force domestic firms to become more internationally efficient in order to remain competitive.
  • Downward pressure on inflation: with a high exchange rate, the price of finished imported goods will be relatively low. Cheaper imported raw materials and components reduce firms’ production costs, potentially leading to lower prices for consumers. The lower price of imported goods also pressures domestic producers to remain competitive by keeping their own prices low.
  • More imports can be bought: a high-value currency buys more foreign currency per unit, and so more foreign goods and services – both visible imports (e.g. technology) and invisible imports (e.g. foreign travel).
  • Forces domestic producers to improve efficiency: a high exchange rate threatens firms’ international competitiveness, forcing them to lower costs and become more efficient to remain competitive. While this might result in worker lay-offs, there are other means of raising efficiency that increase overall economic productivity.

Disadvantages of a high exchange rate

  • Damage to export industries: a high exchange rate makes it harder to sell goods and services abroad because they are relatively more expensive, which could lead to unemployment in export industries.
  • Damage to domestic industries: with imports relatively cheap, domestic producers may see increased competition reduce demand for their own goods/services, potentially causing further unemployment as firms cut back.

Advantages of a low exchange rate

  • Greater employment in export industries: with a low exchange rate, exports become relatively less expensive and more competitive abroad, potentially leading to more employment in export industries.
  • Greater employment in domestic industries: a low exchange rate makes imports more expensive, encouraging domestic consumers to buy domestically produced goods instead of imports, which may also raise employment.

Disadvantages of a low exchange rate

  • Inflation: a low currency value makes imported final goods and services, and imported raw materials and components (a cost of production for firms), more expensive. This is likely to push up prices throughout the economy – a serious risk of inflation.
The main pointsA high value of a currency may be good for fighting inflation, but may worsen unemployment problems, whereas a low value of a currency may be good for solving unemployment problems, but may create inflationary pressure in a country.

Why do governments intervene in the foreign exchange market and how do they do it?

Key concept – InterventionGovernment/central bank intervention in the currency market is a recurring theme linking fixed, floating and managed regimes.

There are a number of reasons why governments may wish to intervene in the foreign exchange market to influence the value of their currency. They may wish to:

  • lower the exchange rate in order to increase employment
  • raise the exchange rate in order to fight inflation
  • maintain a fixed exchange rate
  • avoid large fluctuations in a floating exchange rate
  • achieve relative exchange-rate stability in order to improve business confidence
  • improve a current account deficit, which is where spending on imported goods and services is greater than the revenue received from exported goods and services (see Chapter 27)

Whatever the reason for intervention, the government is seeking to manipulate the exchange rate. There are two main methods:

Method 1 – using reserves to buy or sell the currencyIf the government wishes to increase the value of its currency, it can use its reserves of foreign currencies to buy its own currency on the foreign exchange market – this increases demand for its currency and forces its price up. If the government wishes to lower the value of its currency, it simply sells its own currency for foreign currencies on the foreign exchange market, increasing supply of its own currency (and increasing its foreign currency reserves in the process), lowering its exchange rate.
Method 2 – changing interest ratesIf the government wishes to increase the value of its currency, it can raise domestic interest rates relative to those abroad. This makes domestic financial investment more attractive to foreign investors, who must buy the country's currency to invest, increasing demand for it and its exchange rate. If the government wishes to lower the value of its currency, it can lower domestic interest rates relative to abroad, making financial investment abroad relatively more attractive; domestic investors must buy foreign currency to invest abroad, increasing the supply of the domestic currency and lowering its exchange rate.

Higher Level Advantages and disadvantages of fixed and floating exchange rates

Whatever exchange rate regime a country chooses to operate, there are bound to be advantages and disadvantages, and we need to look at these in turn.

Advantages of a fixed exchange rate

  1. A fixed exchange rate should reduce uncertainty for all economic agents in the country – businesses can plan ahead knowing their predicted costs and prices for international trading agreements will not change.
  2. If exchange rates are fixed, inflation may have a very harmful effect on the demand for exports and imports (making exports uncompetitive/imports cheap), so the government is forced to take measures to keep inflation as low as possible in order to keep businesses competitive on foreign markets. Thus fixed exchange rates encourage sensible government policies on inflation.
  3. In theory, the existence of a fixed exchange rate should reduce speculation in the foreign exchange markets. (However, in reality, this has not always been the case, and there are often attempts to destabilize fixed exchange rate systems for speculative gains.)
  4. A country that fixes its exchange rate at an artificially low level may create international disagreement, because a low exchange rate makes that country's exports more competitive on world markets and may be seen as an unfair trade advantage, potentially leading to economic disputes or retaliation.

Disadvantages of a fixed exchange rate

  1. The government or central bank is compelled to keep the exchange rate fixed – mainly through manipulating interest rates. This can have negative consequences for the domestic economy: for example, if the exchange rate is in danger of falling, the central bank must raise interest rates to increase demand for the currency, but this has a deflationary effect on the economy, lowering domestic demand and increasing unemployment – a domestic macroeconomic goal (low unemployment) may have to be sacrificed to keep the exchange rate fixed.
  2. To keep the exchange rate fixed and instil confidence on the foreign exchange markets, a country with a fixed exchange rate must maintain high levels of foreign currency reserves, to make it clear that it is able to defend its currency by buying and selling foreign currencies.
  3. Setting the level of the fixed exchange rate is not simple – there are many possible variables to take into account, and these will also change over time. If the rate is set too high, export firms may find they are not competitive in foreign markets, requiring devaluation; but finding the exact right level is very difficult.

Advantages of a floating exchange rate

  1. Because the exchange rate does not have to be kept at a certain level, interest rates are free to be employed as domestic monetary policy tools, such as controlling inflation.
  2. In theory, the floating exchange rate should adjust itself to keep the current account balanced. For example, if there is a current account deficit, the demand for the currency is low (export sales are relatively low) and supply of the currency is high (demand for imports is high). This should mean the exchange rate falls, making exports relatively more attractive and imports relatively less so, correcting the current account itself. (This self-correction actually depends on the Marshall–Lerner condition being satisfied – see Chapter 27, Higher Level only.)
  3. Because reserves are not used to control the value of the currency, it is not necessary to keep high levels of reserves of foreign currencies and gold.

Disadvantages of a floating exchange rate

  1. Floating exchange rates tend to create uncertainty in international markets, making it hard for businesses to make accurate predictions about likely future costs and revenues. Investment is harder to assess with volatile exchange rates, since it is difficult to gauge the true level of return and risk, reducing international investment.
  2. In reality, floating exchange rates are affected by more factors than simply demand and supply, such as government intervention, world events and speculation. Because of this, they do not necessarily self-adjust to eliminate current account deficits.
  3. A floating exchange-rate regime may worsen existing inflation. If a country has high inflation relative to others, this makes its exports less competitive and imports relatively less expensive, so demand for its currency falls and its exchange rate falls to correct the situation – but this could lead to even higher import prices for finished goods, components and raw materials, causing further cost-push inflation, fuelling the overall inflation rate even more.
