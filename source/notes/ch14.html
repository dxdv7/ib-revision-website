What is aggregate demand (AD)?

DefinitionAggregate demand (AD) is the total spending on goods and services in a period of time, at a given price level.

On a diagram AD looks similar to a microeconomic demand curve, in that it is downward sloping – but the underlying concepts are different.

Figure 14.1(b): Microeconomic demand curve (for comparison)Axes: Price ($) on the vertical axis, Quantity (units) on the horizontal axis. A single downward-sloping curve is drawn, labelled “Demand”.
Micro demand vs macro aggregate demandThe microeconomic demand curve shows the relationship between the price of one good (e.g., soccer balls) and the quantity demanded of that one good – it describes a single market. Macroeconomics considers the working of the whole economy, i.e., all goods and services demanded together. Where the micro demand curve has the price of one good on the y-axis, the macro AD curve has a measure of the average price level of all goods and services in the economy. Where the micro demand curve has the quantity of one good on the x-axis, the macro AD curve has the total quantity of all goods and services, i.e., national output.

Given that national output is equivalent to national income and national expenditure (Chapter 13), the x-axis of the AD diagram is commonly labelled “real output” (the value of national output/expenditure adjusted for inflation) or national income (Y). Different textbooks may use different x-axis labels (national income Y, national expenditure, national output, or real output) – whichever term is used, it must be distinct from a simple “quantity” or Q label, which would indicate a single market (a microeconomic diagram).

The AD curve illustrates the Law of Demand at the aggregate (whole-economy) level: at a lower average price level, a higher quantity of real output is demanded. The word “aggregate” means “total”. When constructing an aggregate demand curve, we look at the demand from all possible sectors of the economy – giving the components of aggregate demand.

The components of aggregate demand

There are four components that make up aggregate demand.

1. Consumption (C)

Consumption is the total spending by consumers on domestic goods and services. Consumer demand for goods is split into two categories:

  • Durable goods: goods such as cars, computers, mobile phones and bicycles that are used by consumers over a period of time (usually more than one year).
  • Non-durable goods: goods such as rice, toilet paper and newspapers that are used up immediately or over a relatively short period of time.

2. Investment (I)

DefinitionInvestment is the addition of capital stock to the economy. It is carried out by firms.

There are two types of investment:

  • Replacement investment: firms spend on capital in order to maintain the productivity of their existing capital (e.g., replacing worn-out machinery).
  • Induced investment: firms spend on capital to increase their output in order to respond to higher demand in the economy.

An economy’s capital stock includes all goods that are made by people and are used to produce other goods or services, such as factories, machines, offices or computers.

Investment vs savingInvestment (in the economic sense) is not to be confused with buying shares or putting money in a bank – in everyday English we tend to call this “investment”, but economically it is, in fact, saving, as it is a leakage from the circular flow (not spending on new capital goods).

3. Government spending (G)

Governments at a variety of levels (federal, state/provincial, municipal/city) spend on a wide variety of goods and services, including health, education, law and order, transport, social security, housing and defence. The amount of government spending depends on the policies and objectives of the government.

4. Net exports (X − M)

Exports are domestic goods and services that are bought by foreigners – when firms in a country sell exports to foreigners, this results in an inflow of export revenues to the country. Imports are goods and services that are bought from foreign producers – when imports are bought, this results in an outflow of import expenditure.

The net trade component of AD is actually export revenues minus import expenditure, simplified as exports minus imports, (X − M). This figure can be either positive, whereby export revenues exceed import expenditure (adding to AD), or negative, whereby import expenditure exceeds export revenues (reducing AD).

Aggregate demand = Consumption + Investment + Government spending + Net exports
AD = C + I + G + (X − M)

To calculate aggregate demand for a given period (e.g. a year), simply add together the total value, usually in a currency such as dollars, of each of the four components: household consumption spending (C), business investment spending (I), government spending (G), and net exports (X − M, which is exports minus imports and can itself be positive or negative).

Worked example

Suppose that in a given year a country records consumption of $600 billion, investment of $150 billion, government spending of $200 billion, exports of $90 billion and imports of $110 billion, so net exports (X − M) = $90bn − $110bn = −$20 billion. Aggregate demand = $600bn + $150bn + $200bn + (−$20bn) = $930 billion. This $930 billion is the total planned spending on the country’s goods and services at the current price level — a single point on that year’s AD curve.

This is equivalent to GDP calculated using the expenditure method.

The shape of the AD curve

Aggregate demand can be presented as a formula, C + I + G + (X − M), and as a diagram.

A single downward-sloping line is drawn, labelled “AD = C + I + G + (X−M)”. Dashed reference lines show that at price level PL1, the quantity of real output demanded is Y1; at the lower price level PL2, the quantity of real output demanded is the higher Y2.

When the average price level in the economy falls from PL1 to PL2, the level of output demanded by consumers (C) plus firms (I) plus governments (G) plus the net foreign sector (X−M) increases from Y1 to Y2 – a movement along the AD curve.

What causes changes in AD? Movements vs shifts

Movement along AD vs shift of ADA change in the (average) price level results in a movement along the AD curve, from one level of real output to another. A change in any of the components of aggregate demand (C, I, G, or X−M) causes a shift of the whole AD curve.
Figure 14.3: Shifts in aggregate demandAxes: Average price level ($) on the vertical axis, Real output on the horizontal axis. Three parallel downward-sloping lines are drawn: AD3 (furthest left), AD1 (middle, the original position) and AD2 (furthest right).

An increase in any of the components of aggregate demand results in an increase in aggregate demand and a shift of the AD curve to the right, from AD1 to AD2. A decrease in any of the components of aggregate demand results in a fall in aggregate demand and a shift of the AD curve to the left, from AD1 to AD3.

What causes changes in consumption?

1. Changes in income taxes

The most significant determinant of consumption is income. As incomes rise, people have more money to spend on goods and services, so consumption increases. If there is an increase in direct taxes (taxes paid on income by individuals), then people will have less disposable income – the income that people have remaining for spending and saving after income taxes have been paid. With less disposable income, consumption will decrease and there will be a fall in AD. A reduction in income tax levels will lead to an increase in disposable incomes and thus an increase in consumption and AD. Government manipulation of income taxes is an element of fiscal policy (covered in more detail in Chapter 17).

2. Changes in interest rates

Spending on non-durable goods is carried out with day-to-day income that people earn. But some of the money used to buy durable goods comes from money that people borrow from the bank. When people borrow money, they must pay interest to the bank for the price of the borrowed money. If there is an increase in interest rates, the price of borrowed money rises, so there is likely to be less borrowing (because it is more expensive to borrow). Therefore consumption will fall, resulting in a fall in AD.

For example, borrowed money is usually used to buy houses via a loan called a mortgage. If interest rates increase, monthly mortgage repayments may become more expensive, leaving people with less money to spend on other goods and services, so consumption falls. Also, a rise in interest rates makes saving more attractive; people would prefer to put extra income in the bank to earn money rather than spend it on goods and services.

Overall, an increase in interest rates leads to a fall in consumption. Conversely, a fall in interest rates leads to an increase in consumption, ceteris paribus, as it becomes more attractive to borrow money to spend on durable goods and services, and it becomes less appealing to save money in the bank (since the interest earned on savings is relatively low).

3. Changes in wealth

Income vs wealthIt is important not to confuse “income” and “wealth”. Income is the money that people earn. Wealth is made up of the assets that people own – this includes physical assets, such as houses, art, jewellery, and monetary/financial assets, such as shares in companies, government bonds or bank savings.

There are two main factors that can change the level of wealth in the economy:

  • A change in house prices: when house prices increase across the economy, consumers feel more wealthy and are likely to feel confident enough to increase their consumption, either by saving less or by borrowing more.
  • A change in the value of stocks and shares: many consumers hold shares in companies. If the value of those shares increases, people may feel wealthier, which might encourage them to spend more. Alternatively, they might sell those shares and use the proceeds to increase consumption.

4. Changes in consumer confidence/expectations

If people are optimistic about their economic future, they are likely to spend more now. For example, if they think they will get a promotion in the future due to a booming economy and strong sales, they will feel more confident about taking up a loan or using savings. High consumer confidence is thus likely to lead to increased consumption. However, if people expect economic conditions to worsen, they are likely to reduce their consumption today in order to save for the future. Economists regularly measure consumer confidence and publish the information as a “consumer confidence index” or “consumer sentiment index”. An increase in the index indicates that confidence is rising; if this is the case, consumer spending is likely to rise as well. (Real-world example noted: political uncertainty has been shown to weigh on consumer confidence, e.g., in Thailand.)

Consumer expectations regarding the future price level will also affect consumption. If consumers expect the price level to increase in the future (i.e., inflation), they will increase consumption now, especially of durable consumer goods, to buy before prices rise. In the same way, if they expect the price level to fall (i.e., deflation), they will put off consuming now, believing that products will be less expensive in the future. Again, this especially relates to durable consumer goods.

5. Levels of household indebtedness

The extent to which households are willing and able to borrow money affects consumption. If it is easy to borrow money (easy credit) and interest rates are low, then households will take on more debt, and spending on goods and services will rise. However, if interest rates rise, households will need more money to repay their loans and mortgages (the original amount borrowed plus interest). In the short run households might simply continue to borrow, but ultimately the debt will have to be repaid, and this may well leave consumers with less money to spend on goods and services – leading to falling AD.

Did you know? (Canadian household debt, 2019)A 2019 survey of 1,515 Canadians found that one-in-five Canadians with debt said they would need to liquidate assets (e.g., cash in pension plans, take a second mortgage, sell a vehicle) to pay down debt in 2019 – this was significantly higher among men (24%) than women (14%), and among those with children (23%) than those without (16%). Almost two-thirds of Canadians with debt anticipated taking on new debt in 2019 (new/increased credit card balances, lines of credit, vehicle loans/leases, or mortgages), with those under 55 much more likely to anticipate new debt (67%) than those 55 and older (50%). Canada’s national debt at the time was over $691 billion (about $18,700 per Canadian), and almost half of Canadian households were within $200 of being unable to pay their bills – illustrating just how sensitive consumption can be to household indebtedness.

Economics in action: in most countries, consumption makes up the largest share of aggregate demand/GDP – this is typical of developed economies generally, not just any one country studied in isolation.

What causes changes in investment?

1. Changes in interest rates

In order to invest, firms need money. The money firms use for investment comes from several sources, most importantly either their “retained profits” or money they borrow. Both of these are affected by the interest rate. If the money is to be borrowed, an increase in the cost of borrowing will lead to a fall in investment. If interest rates are high, firms may prefer to put their retained profits in the bank to earn higher returns as savings, rather than use them to invest. Therefore, there is an inverse relationship between interest rates and the level of investment.

Figure 14.4: The relationship between investment and the interest rateAxes: Interest rate (i) on the vertical axis, with two levels marked, 7% (higher) above 4% (lower); Level of investment on the horizontal axis, with I1 (lower) and I2 (higher) marked. A single downward-sloping curve is drawn (similar in shape to a demand curve). A decrease in the interest rate from 7% to 4% is shown as a movement down along the curve, from I1 to I2 – decreasing the incentive to save and decreasing the cost of borrowing, so borrowing increases, resulting in an increase in the level of investment. An increase in the interest rate would have the opposite effect (a movement up the curve to a lower level of investment).

2. Changes in business taxes

If the government increases taxes on business profits, this reduces firms’ post-tax profits, meaning firms have less money to invest, so we would expect to see a fall in AD. In the same way, if the government lowers taxes on business profits, firms retain more profit, more investment can take place, and there would be an increase in AD (i.e., a shift of the AD curve to the right). Government manipulation of corporate taxes (taxes on the profits of firms) is an element of fiscal policy, covered in more detail in Chapter 17.

3. Technological change

In any dynamic economy there is likely to be a quick pace of technological change. In order to keep up with advances in technology and remain competitive, firms will need to invest. This will increase AD.

4. Changes in business confidence/expectations

Businesses make decisions about the amount of investment they should undertake largely based on their expectations for the future and their confidence in the economic climate. There would be little point in investing to increase the potential output of a firm if consumer demand were expected to fall in the future. If businesses are very confident about the future of the economy and expect consumer demand to rise, then they will want to be ready to meet the increases in consumer demand by investing to increase potential output and productivity. Economists regularly measure the confidence of businesses and publish this data in the form of a “business confidence index”. (Real-world example noted: Brexit-related uncertainty has been reported as reducing UK business investment.)

5. Levels of corporate indebtedness

The extent to which businesses are willing and able to borrow money affects investment. If it is easy to borrow money (easy credit) and interest rates are low, then businesses will take on more debt and investment will rise. However, if interest rates rise, then businesses will have to spend more to repay their loans (the original amount borrowed plus interest). In the short run they might simply continue to borrow, but ultimately the debt will have to be paid, and this may well leave businesses with less money to spend on investment – leading to falling AD.

What causes changes in government spending?

The amount and nature of government spending depends on the political and economic priorities of the government. For example, if a government has made a commitment to financially support a given industry (a subsidy), government spending will rise. If governments are obliged to spend to correct market failure, government spending will rise. A new education or health policy might require increased public spending on schools or hospitals. Put quite simply: if government spending increases, then AD will shift to the right; if government spending falls, then the opposite will happen (AD shifts to the left).

What causes changes in net exports?

Net exports are the difference between export revenues and import expenditure over a given period of time, expressed as (X − M). A positive (X−M) is a trade surplus, which will lead to a shift of AD to the right; a negative (X−M) is a trade deficit, which will shift AD to the left.

1. Changes in the level of exports

Exports are goods or services that are bought by foreigners. If foreign incomes rise, foreigners’ consumption of imported goods (i.e., a country’s exports) will rise. For example, as Chinese national income rises, Chinese people are more willing and able to buy imported goods and services from Europe – so European exports to China rise. Similarly, as China grows, investment in China expands, likely involving imports of capital equipment; as China grows, German exports of capital equipment may rise.

Other than changes in the national income of trading partners, changes in the value of a country’s currency (its exchange rate) can also affect a country’s exports. If a country’s exchange rate becomes stronger, this makes the country’s exports relatively more expensive to foreigners; by the Law of Demand, this will cause the quantity of exports to fall (the effect on export revenues depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports, addressed in a later chapter). Conversely, if the exchange rate falls in value, exports become more competitive and may result in an increase in export revenues.

Changes in a country’s trade policies may affect its exports. If a country adopts a policy of more liberalized (free) trade, reducing the tariffs it charges on imports, this effectively allows other countries to export more to it. On the other hand, if a country adopts more protectionist policies to reduce the level of imports, this will reduce the exports of other countries.

The last broad factor affecting export revenues is the relative inflation rate among trading partners. For example, if inflation in the US were relatively higher than in Canada, US goods would be less competitive in Canada and this may reduce the export revenues the US earns from its exports to Canada. On the other hand, if the inflation rate in the US were relatively lower than the Canadian rate, American goods would be more competitive in Canadian markets, and so American export revenues might be expected to rise, ceteris paribus.

2. Changes in the level of imports

It has already been established that when a country’s national income is growing there is likely to be an increase in consumption. As people consume more goods and services, it will necessarily be the case that some of these goods and services will be imported. Similarly, as national income rises, investment is likely to be greater, and part of the capital goods purchased will be imported (imported capital goods and/or components). Thus, as national income rises, so does spending on imports. If national income falls, spending on imports will be reduced.

Using the same analysis as for exports, changes in a country’s exchange rate would be expected to change the level of spending on imports. An increase in the exchange rate would make imported goods less expensive and, depending on the elasticity of demand for imports, could increase import expenditure. A decrease in the exchange rate would make imported goods more expensive, and thus would also affect the level of import expenditure.

The type of trade policies that a country adopts will also affect its level of import spending. If a country decides to adopt a more liberalized trade policy (e.g., reducing tariffs on imports), then import expenditure would be expected to rise, ceteris paribus. If a more protectionist set of policies were adopted instead, import expenditure would be expected to fall.

If the inflation rates of trade partners were to vary notably, this might also affect the level of import spending, as noted above (relative inflation rates).

Summary: determinants of net exportsNet exports depend on: domestic national income (affecting demand for imports); trading partners’ national incomes (affecting demand for exports); changes in exchange rates; changes in trade policies; and relative inflation rates between trading partners. These are discussed in much more depth in later chapters on trade and exchange rates.

Adding up the four components for a given time period gives the total value of aggregate demand: take the currency value of consumption, investment and government spending, then add net exports (exports minus imports, which can be negative if imports exceed exports).

Worked example

Consider a small open economy where, over a year, consumption is $40 billion, investment is $8 billion, government spending is $12 billion, exports are $15 billion and imports are $10 billion, so net exports = $15bn − $10bn = $5 billion. Aggregate demand = $40bn + $8bn + $12bn + $5bn = $65 billion. Because net exports are positive here, the foreign sector is adding to, rather than subtracting from, this economy’s total demand for goods and services.
