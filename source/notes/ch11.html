1. What is market power?

Market powerMarket power is the ability of a firm to raise the market price of a good or service above marginal cost. The firm is able to influence market outcomes by restricting output in order to increase price.
  • In perfectly competitive markets, firms have no market power, because they cannot raise price — if they try, demand falls to zero. Because firms cannot raise price, they are said to be “price takers”.
  • In imperfect markets, firms can raise prices without losing all of their customers. A firm with complete market power could raise prices without losing any customers at all. Firms with some degree of market power are said to be “price makers” or “price setters”.

Price makers experience a normal, downward-sloping demand curve, and so have the ability to alter price, or the quantity supplied, but not both. Increases in price will lead to a fall in quantity demanded.

Why market power is a concernReducing supply in order to increase price is considered bad for society, since it moves the market away from the socially-efficient level of output (where marginal social cost, MSC = marginal social benefit, MSB), creating a reduction in community surplus and a market failure. Because of this, many countries impose legislation to limit the market power that firms might develop and utilize.

Economists have identified four market forms: perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly and monopoly. This chapter covers the first two; oligopoly and monopoly are covered in the next chapter.

2. What is perfect competition? Assumptions

Perfect competition is a model used as the starting point to explain how firms operate. It is a theoretical model, based on very precise assumptions. Although purely theoretical, it is important because once we have built the model of a perfectly competitive market, we can begin to relax the assumptions and move towards more realistic models of markets.

Assumptions of perfect competition
  • The industry is made up of a very large number of firms.
  • Each firm is so small, relative to the size of the industry, that it is not capable of altering its own output to have a noticeable effect upon the output of the industry as a whole. A firm cannot affect the supply curve of the industry, and so cannot affect the price of the product. Individual firms must sell at whatever price is set by demand and supply in the industry as a whole. Individual firms are “price-takers”.
  • Firms produce exactly identical (“homogeneous”) products. It is not possible to distinguish between a good produced by one firm and a good produced by another. There are no brand names and no marketing to try to make goods different from each other.
  • Firms are completely free to enter or leave the industry — there are no barriers to entry or exit.
  • All producers and consumers have perfect knowledge of the market. Producers are fully aware of market prices, costs in the industry, and how the market works. Consumers are fully aware of prices, quality, and availability of goods.

Real-world example: wheat growing in the EU is used as a near-example. Some wheat farms are large but very small relative to the whole wheat-growing industry, so no single farm can affect the EU wheat price (cannot shift the industry supply curve) — it has to sell at the existing price. Wheat is homogeneous (wheat is wheat), so there is no way to differentiate one farm’s wheat from another’s. However, although firms are relatively free to enter or leave the wheat industry, there are significant costs to doing either, which may affect decisions, and information is not perfectly open, so producers and consumers do not have truly “perfect knowledge”. The EU wheat industry is close to perfectly competitive but not precisely so.

How much market power exists in perfect competition?

Figure 11.1: The demand curves for the industry and the firm in perfect competitionIn the industry graph, supply S and demand D determine the market price P and quantity Q. The individual firm is a price taker: at that price it can sell any quantity it likes, so its demand curve D = AR = MR is a horizontal line at P (note the firm’s quantity axis is on a much smaller scale).

Because firms are price-takers, they must sell at the industry price P. If a firm tried to sell at a higher price, consumers would simply buy from another firm instead, since the goods are homogeneous and there is no difference in looks or quality. If a firm sells at the industry price, it can sell as much as it wants, because as it increases output this does not affect the industry supply curve and so does not alter the industry price. The firm derives its price of P from the equilibrium price in the industry, where industry supply equals industry demand. The firm faces a perfectly elastic demand curve at price P and has no market power.

3. Profit maximization in perfect competition — short run

As in Chapter 10, firms maximize profits by producing at the output where MC = MR.

Figure 11.2: The profit-maximizing level of output in perfect competitionThe firm takes the market price P from the industry graph, so P = D = AR = MR. Profit is maximised at output q, where MC = MR. Firm output q is only a tiny fraction of industry output Q; if it were large enough to shift industry supply the firm would have market power.

In the short run in perfect competition there are two possible profit/loss situations.

Short-run abnormal profits

Figure 11.3: Short-run abnormal profits in perfect competitionThe firm produces at q, where MC = MR = P. At q the average cost C read from the AC curve is below the price P, so the firm earns an abnormal profit of (P − C) per unit, shown by the shaded rectangle of height (P − C) and width q. The MC curve cuts AC at its minimum.

Short-run losses

Figure 11.4: Short-run losses in perfect competitionThe firm sells at the market price P and maximises profit (here, minimises loss) at q where MC = MR. At q the average cost C is greater than P, so it makes a loss of (C − P) per unit, shown by the shaded rectangle. It still produces because P covers average variable cost, so producing loses less than closing.

4. From short-run to long-run equilibrium in perfect competition

Key concept: CHANGEThe adjustment process from short-run abnormal profit or loss to long-run normal profit is flagged as illustrating the key concept of Change (twice: once for the entry process, once for the exit process).

1. Short-run abnormal profits → long-run normal profits

Figure 11.5: The movement from short-run abnormal profit to long-run normal profitIn the short run the firm earns the shaded abnormal profit at price P and output q. Profit attracts new firms, so industry supply shifts right from S to S1, lowering the price to P1 and raising industry output to Q1. The firm’s demand line falls to D1 = AR1 = MR1, which just touches the bottom of AC where MC = MR at q1; P1 = C1 and only normal profit remains.

Since there is perfect knowledge and no barriers to entry, firms outside the industry that could also produce the good are attracted by the chance to make abnormal profits, and start to enter the industry. At first, because the entering firms are relatively small, this has no real effect. However, as more and more firms enter, attracted by the abnormal profits, the industry supply curve starts to shift to the right (from S towards S1).

As industry supply shifts right, industry price starts to fall (from P towards P1). Because firms in the industry are price-takers, the price they can charge falls, and their (horizontal) demand curves shift downwards. This means the abnormal profits they had been making start to be “competed away”.

This process continues as long as there are abnormal profits in the industry. Eventually the industry supply curve reaches S1, where price is P1. At this point firms are “taking” the price of P1, and the demand curve is D1 = AR1 = MR1. Firms are now making normal profits, with price per unit equal to cost per unit (P1 = C1). Entrepreneurs are satisfied because they are exactly covering their opportunity costs. There is no reason to leave the industry, and no reason for new firms to enter (no abnormal profit remains to attract them). The industry is in long-run equilibrium: no one enters, no one leaves. The outcome is a bigger industry producing Q1 units, with more, slightly larger (smaller, per original text says “slightly larger”) firms each producing q1 units.

2. Short-run losses → long-run normal profits

Figure 11.6: The movement from short-run losses to long-run normal profitIn the short run the firm makes the shaded loss at price P and output q. Losses drive firms out, so industry supply shifts left from S to S1, raising the price to P1 and cutting industry output to Q1. The firm’s demand line rises to D1 = AR1 = MR1, which touches the bottom of AC where MC = MR at q1; P1 = C1 and the loss disappears.

The reverse process: some firms in the industry, after a time, start to leave (unable to cover their costs). At first this has no real effect, since the firms leaving are relatively small. But as more and more firms leave, the industry supply curve starts to shift to the left (from S towards S1). As industry supply shifts left, industry price starts to rise (from P towards P1). As price-takers, the remaining firms find the price they can charge rises, and their demand curves shift upward. This means the losses they had been making start to get smaller. This process continues as long as there are losses in the industry, until eventually the industry supply curve reaches S1, price is P1, and the firms in the industry are making exactly normal profits (P1 = C1), so no more firms leave and no new ones enter.

Long-run equilibrium in perfect competition

Long-run equilibrium resultIn the long run, firms in perfect competition will always make normal profits. This is because, even if firms are making short-run abnormal profits or short-run losses, the industry will adjust — with firms entering or leaving — until a normal profit situation is reached.
Figure 11.7: Long-run equilibrium in perfect competitionIn long-run equilibrium the price P set by the industry equals the minimum of the firm’s AC curve. The firm produces at q where MC = MR, and there AC = P, so it earns exactly normal profit. There is no incentive for firms to enter or leave until demand or costs change.

5. Efficiency in perfect competition

Key concept: EFFICIENCYThis whole section is flagged as illustrating the key concept of Efficiency. Two measures of efficiency are considered: productive efficiency and allocative efficiency.

1. Productive efficiency

Productive efficiencyA firm is productively efficient if it produces its product at the lowest possible unit cost (average cost).
Figure 11.8: Productive efficiencyMC cuts AC exactly at the lowest point of AC. At that output q the cost per unit is at its minimum, c, so q is the productively efficient level of output (MC = AC).
Productively efficient level of output is where: MC = AC

MC (marginal cost) is the extra cost of producing one more unit of output (the change in total cost divided by the change in quantity), while AC (average cost) is total cost divided by the number of units produced. To find the productively efficient output from a table of cost data, work out MC and AC at each output level and identify the output at which the two figures are equal — this will also be the output at which AC is at its lowest value.

Worked example

A firm's total cost (TC) schedule is: 4 units, TC = $60; 5 units, TC = $75; 6 units, TC = $96. Average cost at 4 units = 60 ÷ 4 = $15; average cost at 5 units = 75 ÷ 5 = $15; average cost at 6 units = 96 ÷ 6 = $16. Marginal cost of the 5th unit = 75 − 60 = $15; marginal cost of the 6th unit = 96 − 75 = $21. At 5 units, MC ($15) equals AC ($15), and this is also the lowest point AC reaches before rising again — so 5 units is this firm's productively efficient output, produced at the lowest possible unit cost of $15.

This follows from Chapter 10: MC always cuts AC at AC’s lowest point. Productive efficiency matters because if a firm produces at the productively efficient output, it is combining its resources as efficiently as possible and resources are not being wasted through inefficient use.

2. Allocative efficiency

Allocative efficiencyAllocative efficiency (sometimes called the socially optimum level of output) occurs where suppliers are producing the optimal mix of goods and services required by consumers.

Price reflects the value that consumers place on a good and is shown on the demand curve (average revenue). Marginal cost reflects the cost to society of all the resources used in producing an extra unit of the good, including the normal profit required to keep the firm in business. If price were greater than marginal cost, consumers would value the good more than the cost to make it, so output should expand until price equals marginal cost. Similarly, if marginal cost were greater than price, society would be using more resources to produce the good than the value it gives to consumers, so output should fall.

Allocatively efficient level of output is where: MC = AR (price)

AR (average revenue) is simply the price of the good, since AR = Total Revenue ÷ Quantity = (Price × Quantity) ÷ Quantity = Price. To find the allocatively efficient output from a set of cost data, compare marginal cost at each output level with the price the good sells for, and identify the output at which the two are equal.

Worked example

A firm sells its product at a fixed market price of $20 per unit. Its marginal cost schedule is: 8th unit, MC = $16; 9th unit, MC = $20; 10th unit, MC = $25. At 9 units, MC ($20) equals AR/price ($20), so 9 units is the allocatively efficient output. This makes economic sense: the 9th unit costs society $20 of resources to produce and is valued by consumers at exactly $20, so it is worth producing; the 10th unit, however, would cost $25 to make but is only valued at $20, so producing it would waste $5 worth of resources.

Figure 11.9: Allocative efficiencyLeft: a firm with a downward-sloping D = AR (and steeper MR). The allocatively efficient output q1 is where MC cuts AR. Right: a firm with perfectly elastic demand, where allocative efficiency is at q2, where MC cuts the horizontal D = AR = MR line. At these outputs the cost to producers (MC) equals the value to consumers (AR).

Allocative efficiency is important because at the allocatively efficient output there is a situation of “Pareto optimality”, where it is impossible to make one person better off without making someone else worse off (met previously in Chapter 9).

Efficiency with short-run abnormal profit

Figure 11.10: Productive and allocative efficiency with short-run profit in perfect competitionThe profit-maximising output q (where MC = MR) equals q1, the allocatively efficient output, because AR = MR = P here. But q is not q2, the productively efficient output at the minimum of AC (MC = AC), so the firm earns abnormal profit (shaded) while being allocatively but not productively efficient.

If a firm is making abnormal profits in the short run in perfect competition, although it is producing at the profit-maximizing output q (MC=MR) and at the allocatively efficient output q1 (MC=AR), it is NOT producing at the productively efficient output q2 (MC=AC).

Efficiency with short-run losses

Figure 11.11: Productive and allocative efficiency with short-run losses in perfect competitionThe loss-minimising output q (where MC = MR) equals q1, the allocatively efficient output (MC = AR = P), but is smaller than q2, the productively efficient output at the minimum of AC. The shaded rectangle is the short-run loss.

Once again, while making a short-run loss, the firm is producing at the allocatively efficient level of output q1 (MC=AR) but not at the productively efficient level q2 (MC=AC).

Efficiency in the long run

Figure 11.12: Productive and allocative efficiency in the long run in perfect competitionThe price line is tangent to the minimum of AC, so a single output satisfies all three conditions: q (MC = MR), q1 (MC = AC, productive efficiency) and q2 (MC = AR, allocative efficiency). The firm earns normal profit only.

In the long run in perfect competition, all profit-maximizing firms produce at the lowest point of their long-run average cost curves. Because there is perfect knowledge in the industry, all firms face the same cost curves, so they are all selling at the same price and minimizing their average costs by producing where MC = AC. All the profit-maximizing firms in the long run are also producing at the allocatively efficient level of output, because they produce where MC = AR.

Is there a market failure in perfect competition?

No market failure in the long runBecause in the long run all firms in perfect competition are simultaneously productively efficient (MC=AC) and allocatively efficient (MC=AR), there is no market failure involved in perfect competition. This means there is no need for government intervention in perfectly competitive markets to rectify market failure. The market power of perfectly competitive firms is non-existent, since they are price-takers.

6. What is imperfect competition? (general market failure diagram)

A market is considered to be imperfect if it fails to equate marginal social cost (MSC) and marginal social benefit (MSB). This applies to all markets where firms face a normal, downward-sloping demand curve. Monopolists and other imperfect market firms may restrict output to push up prices and maximize profits, because they have market power. Because of this, they do not produce at the socially efficient level of output.

Figure 11.13: Imperfect competition (market failure diagram)The socially efficient output Q* is where D = MSB meets S = MSC, at price P*. A profit-maximising imperfect competitor produces where MR = MC, at the smaller output Q1, and charges P1 read from the demand curve. The darker triangle is lost consumer surplus and the paler one lost producer surplus; together they are the welfare loss, because units between Q1 and Q* have marginal benefit above marginal cost but are not produced.

The more market power available to firms — the greater the market power, the greater the market failure that needs to be addressed. In perfect competition, there is no market failure. We now look at monopolistic competition, and later at monopoly and oligopoly, to assess levels of market power, market failure, and the intervention needed.

7. What is monopolistic competition? Assumptions

The theory of monopolistic competition was developed by Edward Chamberlin (1899–1967), an American economist, who was dissatisfied with the two extreme theories that existed at the time (perfect competition and monopoly) and wanted to devise something more realistic sitting between them. In simple terms, a monopolistically competitive market is one with many competing firms where each firm has a little bit of market power — firms are “mini-monopolies”, hence the term “monopolistic”.

Assumptions of monopolistic competition
  • The industry is made up of a fairly large number of firms.
  • The firms are small, relative to the size of the industry, so the actions of one firm are unlikely to greatly affect any of its competitors. Firms assume they can act independently of each other.
  • Firms all produce slightly differentiated products — it is possible for a consumer to tell one firm’s product from another’s.
  • Firms are completely free to enter or leave the industry — there are no barriers to entry or exit.
The one key difference from perfect competition: product differentiationProduct differentiation exists when a good or service is perceived to be different from other goods or services in some way. Products may be differentiated by brand name, colour, appearance, packaging, design, quality of service, skill levels, and many other methods. Examples of monopolistically competitive industries: nail (manicure) salons, car mechanics, plumbers, jewellers.
Figure 11.14: The demand curve for a firm in monopolistic competitionBecause there are many close substitutes, the firm’s D = AR curve is downward sloping but relatively elastic (fairly flat). MR lies below it and falls twice as steeply. The firm produces at q where MC = MR and charges P, read from the AR curve.

Although this may appear to be a small difference from the assumptions of perfect competition, it leads to a markedly different market structure: because products are differentiated, there is some extent of brand loyalty. Consumers become loyal to a product and continue to buy it even if the price goes up a little (e.g. a customer might stay with “their” plumber when she raises prices above local rivals, believing she is slightly more skilled than competitors). This brand loyalty means producers have some element of independence when deciding on price. They are, to an extent, price-makers, and face a downward-sloping demand curve — but demand will be relatively elastic since there are many, only slightly different substitutes.

How much market power exists in monopolistic competition?

Individual firms in monopolistic competition are price-makers, since they face downward-sloping demand curves — a firm can raise prices, so long as it is prepared to accept a fairly large fall in quantity demanded (demand is relatively elastic). However, the firm has very little market power, since it is so small relative to the size of the industry. Hence, it cannot increase the industry price by reducing its own output. (Larger firms in oligopoly and monopoly, covered in Chapter 12, are able to do this and so may develop significant market power from their size relative to the industry.)

8. Profit maximization in monopolistic competition — short run

Short-run abnormal profits

Figure 11.15: Short-run abnormal profits in monopolistic competitionThe firm maximises profit at q where MC = MR and charges price P from the AR curve. At q the average cost is C, below P, so it earns an abnormal profit of (P − C) × q, shown by the shaded rectangle.

Short-run losses

Figure 11.16: Short-run losses in monopolistic competitionThe firm produces at q where MC = MR and charges P from the AR curve, but here the whole AR curve lies below AC. At q the average cost C is above P, so it makes a loss of (C − P) × q, shown by the shaded rectangle.

Just as in perfect competition, firms in monopolistic competition can make either abnormal profits or losses in the short run, depending on where the AC curve sits relative to the AR curve at the profit-maximizing output.

9. From short-run to long-run in monopolistic competition

Whether firms are making abnormal profits or losses in the short run, because of the freedom of entry and exit there will be a long-run equilibrium, where all firms in the industry are making normal profits.

Key concept: CHANGEThe adjustment mechanism (entry of new firms competing away abnormal profit) illustrates the key concept of Change.
Key concept: CHOICEThe differentiation/loyalty dynamics driving the adjustment also illustrate the key concept of Choice.

If firms are making short-run abnormal profits, the industry will attract new entrants, since there are no barriers to entry. As new firms enter, they take business away from the existing firms, so existing demand curves start to shift to the left. If firms are making short-run losses, some firms will leave; the remaining firms will find their demand curves shift to the right, as they pick up the trade from the leaving firms.

Illustrative example: imagine a new sushi restaurant opens in a district and is so popular there is a line outside every evening. Other catering entrepreneurs are attracted by the possibility of doing well too, and it is likely another sushi restaurant will open in the area. This may not happen immediately, but eventually is likely to result in a fall in demand for the original sushi restaurant as some of its customers switch. If demand continues to be strong, even more restaurants will open. Each restaurant will try to distinguish itself from the others — perhaps by staying open longer, offering a “Happy Hour”, special theme nights, or free children’s meals. This product differentiation is also known as non-price competition.

Figure 11.17: Long-run equilibrium in monopolistic competitionIn the long run entry and exit shift each firm’s demand until the AR curve is just tangent to AC. The firm produces at q where MC = MR, and at q the price P equals the average cost C, so there is no abnormal profit rectangle and no incentive to enter or leave. The tangency lies to the left of the minimum of AC.

Each firm exactly covers its costs, including its opportunity costs, so there is no incentive for firms to leave, and outside firms will not enter since they would be aware that entry would lead to losses for everyone (the market is already saturated with firms earning only normal profit).

10. Efficiency in monopolistic competition

Key concept: EFFICIENCYThis section is flagged as illustrating the key concept of Efficiency.

Productive efficiency is achieved at the level of output where a firm produces at the lowest possible cost per unit — the point where MC crosses AC (at AC’s minimum). Allocative efficiency is achieved at the level of output where the MC curve crosses the AR curve: the socially optimum level of output.

Figure 11.18: Productive and allocative efficiency in the short run in monopolistic competitionIn both panels the firm produces at q where MC = MR. Neither output equals q1, the productively efficient output at the minimum of AC (MC = AC), nor q2, the allocatively efficient output where MC = AR. Left: q is followed by q1 then q2 and the shaded rectangle is abnormal profit. Right: the firm makes the shaded loss, and q2 lies to the left of q1.
Figure 11.19: Productive and allocative efficiency in the long run in monopolistic competitionIn long-run equilibrium AR is tangent to AC on its falling part, so the firm produces at q (MC = MR), left of the minimum of AC. The allocatively efficient output q2 (where MC cuts AR) is larger than q, and the productively efficient output q1 (the minimum of AC, where MC = AC) is larger still, so neither efficiency condition is met.

The firm produces at the level of output where profits are maximized, q (MC=MR), as opposed to the productively efficient level of output, q1 (MC=AC), or the allocatively efficient level of output, q2 (MC=AR). This is the case in both the short run and the long run: unlike perfect competition, where in the long run firms are profit-maximizers, productively efficient, and allocatively efficient all at once, in monopolistic competition, although maximizing profits, firms in the long run are neither productively nor allocatively efficient.

Is there a market failure in monopolistic competition?

Figure 11.20: Market failure in monopolistic competitionThe firm produces at q where MC = MR, less than the allocatively efficient output q1 where MC = AR. Between q and q1 the value to consumers (AR) exceeds the marginal cost (MC), so the shaded wedge bounded by AR and MC shows the welfare loss, a market failure.

Unlike perfect competition, there is a small market failure in monopolistic competition, since firms are not allocatively efficient (shown by the shaded area in Figure 11.20). However, even though monopolistic competition is not allocatively efficient, the inefficiency is not due to the firm’s ability to restrict output and increase price — it is, in fact, the result of consumers’ desires for variety. Although allocative efficiency does not occur, it is hard to argue that consumers are worse off with monopolistic competition than with perfect competition, because the difference is due entirely to consumer desire to have differentiated products.

Choice vs efficiency trade-offRather than having a perfectly competitive situation, where consumers would in theory pay lower prices but are only able to purchase a homogeneous product, monopolistic competition gives consumers the opportunity to make choices. This is why they are prepared to pay slightly higher prices for the products.
Key concept: CHOICEThis trade-off (variety and choice vs. a small allocative inefficiency) is flagged as illustrating the key concept of Choice.
Little need for interventionBecause of the very small level of market power found in monopolistic competition, governments do not intervene in these markets to curb that market power. Governments might, however, intervene to correct other market failures — for example, correcting imperfect information by setting standards for trades such as plumbers, and then giving authorization to people who reach those standards. In this way, consumers can get information on which firms are approved, reducing previous imperfect information.

Economics in action: the book suggests investigating a monopolistically competitive market in the student’s local area (number/size of firms, product differentiation, entry/exit costs, information availability) and any government intervention (e.g. health and safety legislation, qualification requirements) that exists in it.
