Why Do Governments Intervene in Markets?

Governments may intervene in individual, microeconomic markets for a number of reasons: to support households, to support firms, to influence consumption and/or production, to protect consumers from the problems associated with monopoly power, to promote well-being and equity, or simply to earn government revenue for other purposes. This chapter considers four different types of government intervention in markets: indirect taxes, subsidies, and minimum and maximum (floor and ceiling) prices, and assesses how effective each method may be in achieving the desired aims.

Key concept: InterventionThe entire chapter illustrates the key concept of Intervention – the idea that when free markets do not produce optimal outcomes for consumers, producers or society, governments can and do act directly to change market outcomes (through taxes, subsidies, and price controls).

Indirect Taxes

What is an indirect tax?

DefinitionAn indirect tax is a tax imposed upon expenditure (i.e. on spending, not on income). Different countries use different names for indirect taxes, including “goods and services tax” (GST), “value added tax” (VAT), sales tax, consumption tax and excise tax.

Indirect taxes matter for two main reasons:

  • Government revenue: indirect taxes provide governments with revenue needed to carry out their many responsibilities. Other tax revenue comes from direct taxes (household income tax, corporate tax) and property tax and social insurance tax. Indirect taxes are a very important revenue source in many countries.
  • Discouraging consumption: a government may place a “standard” indirect tax on all goods and services, but place an extra tax on goods considered undesirable (e.g. cigarettes, alcohol), in order to discourage their consumption.

An indirect tax is placed upon the selling price of a product, so it raises the firm’s costs and shifts the supply curve for the product vertically upwards by the amount of the tax. Because of this shift, less of the product will be supplied at every price.

Two types of indirect tax

Specific tax vs Percentage (ad valorem) tax
  • A specific tax is a specific, fixed amount of tax imposed upon a product regardless of its price – for example, a tax of $1 per unit. This shifts the supply curve vertically upwards in parallel by the amount of the tax at every quantity.
  • A percentage tax (also known as an ad valorem tax) is a tax that is a percentage of the selling price. Because the tax is a fixed percentage, not a fixed amount, the gap between the original supply curve and the after-tax supply curve gets bigger as the price of the product rises. For example, if the percentage tax is 20%, then at a price of $5 the tax is $1, but at a price of $10 the tax becomes $2 – so the gap between the curves widens at higher prices/quantities.
Figure 8.1: The effect on a supply curve of different types of indirect taxesTwo small diagrams, each with Price ($) on the vertical axis and Quantity on the horizontal axis. (a) A specific tax: the original supply curve S and a new curve S+tax lie parallel to each other, with a constant vertical gap of $1 between them at every quantity – a straight, parallel upward shift. (b) A percentage tax: S and S+tax start together near the origin but diverge as quantity/price increases – the vertical gap between them (the tax) is small at low prices (e.g. $1 at a price of $5) and grows larger at higher prices (e.g. $2 at a price of $10), so S+tax is steeper/rotated relative to S rather than a pure parallel shift.

The impact of a specific tax: price, quantity, government revenue and employment

When an indirect tax is imposed on a product, we add the demand curve to the diagram and ask four questions:

  • What will happen to the price that consumers pay?
  • How much will the amount received by the producer change?
  • How much tax will the government receive?
  • What will happen to the size of the market, and so to employment?

Take a normal demand and supply curve and assume the government imposes a specific tax. The market starts in equilibrium, with quantity Qe supplied and demanded at price Pe. After the tax is imposed, the supply curve shifts vertically upwards by the amount of the tax. Producers would like to raise the price to cover the full tax and pass all of the cost onto consumers, but at that price there would be excess supply (quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded), so the price must fall until a new equilibrium is reached. At the new equilibrium, price P1 (higher than Pe) and a smaller quantity Q1 (lower than Qe) are both demanded and supplied.

Figure 8.2: The imposition of a specific tax on a productThree related diagrams, each with Price ($) on the vertical axis and Quantity on the horizontal axis, showing the original supply curve S, the after-tax curve S+tax (shifted vertically up in parallel by the tax), and a downward-sloping demand curve D. In all three, the original equilibrium is at price Pe and quantity Qe (where S and D cross), and the new equilibrium after the tax is at price P1 and quantity Q1 < Qe (where S+tax and D cross). Producers receive a net price of C per unit (C is below Pe), since they must hand the tax per unit over to government; the vertical gap between P1 and C at quantity Q1 equals the specific tax per unit.

(a) Producer revenue: shows the rectangle for producer revenue before the tax (price Pe × quantity Qe) shaded, and a smaller rectangle for producer revenue after the tax (price C × quantity Q1) shaded – producer revenue clearly falls.

(b) Government tax revenue: shades the rectangle bounded by price P1 (top), price C (bottom) and quantity Q1 (width) – this rectangle is the total tax revenue collected by government, equal to (tax per unit) × Q1.

(c) The tax burden: splits that same government-revenue rectangle horizontally into two shaded portions – an upper portion between Pe and P1 labelled “tax burden for consumers” (the amount consumers pay above the original price) and a lower portion between C and Pe labelled “tax burden for producers” (the amount producers give up below the original price they used to receive).

Working through Figure 8.2: the price for consumers rises from Pe to P1 (their share of the tax, in this illustration about half the whole tax). Producers now receive C per unit after paying the tax to government, so they contribute the rest of the tax (Pe − C per unit). Producer revenue falls from (Pe × Qe) to (C × Q1). Government tax revenue equals the tax per unit × Q1, and the market shrinks in size from Qe to Q1 units – this may have implications for the level of employment in the market, as firms may need fewer workers to produce less output. In this illustration, the burden of the tax is shared fairly evenly between consumers and producers.

Government tax revenue = tax per unit × new (after-tax) equilibrium quantity
Consumer burden of tax (per unit) = new price paid by consumers − original equilibrium price
Producer burden of tax (per unit) = original equilibrium price − new net price received by producer

Who pays what share of the indirect tax? The role of elasticity

Key concept: InterdependenceThe size of the tax burden on consumers versus producers is not fixed – it depends on the interdependent relationship between the price elasticity of demand (PED) and price elasticity of supply (PES) for the product.

Two contrasting cases illustrate this:

Figure 8.3: A specific tax where PED is greater than PES (demand relatively elastic, supply relatively inelastic)Price ($) on the vertical axis, Quantity on the horizontal axis; a relatively flat (elastic) demand curve D and a relatively steep (inelastic) supply curve S, shifting up in parallel to S+tax after the tax. Original equilibrium at Pe, Qe. After the tax, the new equilibrium price P1 is only slightly above Pe (a small rise), while the price producers actually keep, C, is far below Pe (a big fall). The diagram shades a small rectangle between Pe and P1 (consumer burden) and a much larger rectangle between C and Pe (producer burden), showing the tax burden falls mostly on producers.

Because demand is very elastic, consumers would stop buying the product if the price rose much, so producers cannot pass on much of the tax – they must absorb most of the burden themselves. The price to consumers rises just a little (Pe to P1); producers keep only C per unit after paying the tax, so their revenue falls a great deal (from Pe×Qe to C×Q1). Government still receives tax revenue equal to (P1 − C) × Q1, and the market shrinks from Qe to Q1, again with possible implications for employment. Here, the burden of the indirect tax is much heavier on producers than on consumers.

Figure 8.4: A specific tax where PED is less than PES (demand relatively inelastic, supply relatively elastic)Price ($) vertical axis, Quantity horizontal axis; a relatively steep (inelastic) demand curve D and a relatively flat (elastic) supply curve S, shifting up in parallel to S+tax. Original equilibrium Pe, Qe. After the tax, the new price P1 is far above Pe (a substantial rise for consumers), while producers’ net price C is only slightly below Pe (a small fall). The diagram shades a large rectangle between Pe and P1 (consumer burden) and a small rectangle between C and Pe (producer burden), showing the tax burden falls mostly on consumers.

Because demand is fairly inelastic, consumers are not very responsive to the price rise, so producers can pass on most of the tax. Consumer price rises substantially (Pe to P1); producer income falls only a small amount (from Pe×Qe to C×Q1). Government again receives high tax revenue, and the market shrinks from Qe to Q1. Here, the burden of the indirect tax is much heavier on consumers than on producers.

Three rules on tax incidence
  • Where PED = PES for a product, the burden of any tax imposed will be shared equally between consumers and producers.
  • Where PED > PES for a product, the burden of any tax imposed will be greater on producers than on consumers.
  • Where PED < PES for a product, the burden of any tax imposed will be greater on consumers than on producers.
Why governments tax inelastic-demand goodsThis is one reason governments tend to place indirect taxes on products with relatively inelastic demand, such as alcohol and cigarettes. Because demand changes by a proportionally smaller amount than the change in price, the government gains high tax revenue and yet does not cause a large fall in the quantity traded (and so does not cause large job losses in that industry).

Higher Level In HL Paper 3 you may be asked to calculate the effects on stakeholders of an indirect tax from a diagram or data, in terms of price, quantity, consumer expenditure, producer revenue, government revenue, consumer surplus or producer surplus. Typical calculations include: consumer expenditure = new price × new quantity; producer revenue = new net price × new quantity; government revenue = tax per unit × new quantity; and the loss of consumer/producer surplus shown as the relevant triangular areas that disappear once the market shrinks from Qe to Q1.

Economics in actionReal-world cigarette tax investigations (e.g. into how much tax is charged per packet, how the tax has changed, and who benefits/suffers from it) are a good way to link tax incidence theory to the nine key concepts.

Subsidies

DefinitionA subsidy is an amount of money paid by the government to a firm, per unit of output.
Key concepts: Intervention and EquitySubsidies are a direct form of government Intervention in a market, and are often justified on Equity grounds (e.g. making essential goods affordable, or protecting jobs/industries).

Reasons governments grant subsidies

  • To lower the price of essential goods (such as rice) to consumers – the government hopes that lower prices will encourage greater consumption of the product.
  • To guarantee the supply of products the government thinks are necessary for the economy – this may be because the good is essential (e.g. a basic food supply or a power source like coal), or because the industry creates a lot of employment that would otherwise be lost, causing economic and social problems.
  • To enable producers to compete with overseas trade, thus protecting the home industry (protectionism – covered in more detail in Chapter 24).

If a subsidy is granted to a firm on a product, the supply curve shifts vertically downwards by the amount of the subsidy, because it reduces the firm’s costs of production, and more will be supplied at every price. As with indirect taxes, how much of the subsidy is passed on to consumers as lower prices, and how much is retained by producers, depends on the relative elasticities of demand and supply.

Specific subsidyAlthough percentage subsidies are sometimes granted, they are rare. Textbook analysis focuses on the specific subsidy – a fixed amount of money given for each unit of the product (e.g. $2 per unit).
Figure 8.5: The effect on a supply curve of a specific subsidyPrice ($) on the vertical axis, Quantity supplied on the horizontal axis. The original supply curve S1 and the after-subsidy curve S1−subsidy lie parallel to each other, with S1−subsidy shifted vertically downward by a constant amount (e.g. $2) at every quantity – the mirror image of the specific tax shift, but downward instead of upward.

The impact of a specific subsidy: price, quantity, revenue and government cost

Take a normal demand and supply diagram and assume the government grants a specific subsidy. The market starts in equilibrium with quantity Qe supplied and demanded at price Pe. After a subsidy per unit is granted, the supply curve shifts vertically downward. Producers lower their prices and increase output until a new equilibrium is reached at a lower price P1 and a larger quantity Q1 (Q1 > Qe), which is both demanded and supplied.

Figure 8.6: The granting of a specific subsidy on a productThree related diagrams, Price ($) vertical axis, Quantity supplied horizontal axis, showing supply curve S1, the after-subsidy curve S1−subsidy (shifted down in parallel by the subsidy amount), and downward-sloping demand curve D. Original equilibrium at Pe, Qe; new equilibrium (where S1−subsidy meets D) at a lower price P1 and larger quantity Q1. Producers actually receive a higher net price (their price P1 plus the subsidy per unit) for each of the Q1 units.

(a) Increase in producer revenue: shades the rectangle for original producer revenue (Pe × Qe) and a larger rectangle for new producer revenue (net price received, which is higher than Pe, × Q1) – showing producer revenue rising.

(b) Change in consumer expenditure: shades the area representing consumers’ saving on the original Qe units bought more cheaply (the price fall × Qe), and separately the extra spending on the additional units now purchased (the new lower price × the extra quantity Q1 − Qe) – total consumer expenditure may rise or fall overall depending on the balance between these two effects.

(c) Amount of government subsidy: shades the full rectangle bounded by the net price received by producers (top), the price P1 paid by consumers (bottom) and quantity Q1 (width) – this is the total cost of the subsidy to government, equal to the subsidy per unit × Q1.

The price to consumers falls from Pe to P1, but not by the whole amount of the subsidy (a full pass-through would require a bigger fall). Producer income rises from the original level (Pe × Qe) to a higher level (net price × Q1). Consumers now buy the original Qe units more cheaply, saving money on those units, but they also purchase extra units (the increase from Qe to Q1), so they spend more on those additional units – total consumer expenditure may increase or decrease overall, depending on the relative sizes of the saving and the extra spending.

Total cost of subsidy to government = subsidy per unit × new (after-subsidy) equilibrium quantity
Opportunity cost of a subsidyThe money spent by government on a subsidy has to come from somewhere. There is an opportunity cost: the government must either take money away from other areas of expenditure (such as infrastructure or public amenities), or raise taxes elsewhere.

Evaluating subsidies

  • Opportunity cost: a government granting a subsidy is making a choice, since there are always other projects on which that money could have been spent instead.
  • Loss of efficiency incentive – if firms do not have to compete with foreign producers in a “free market” because they are protected by a subsidy, they may lack the incentive to become more efficient.
  • Who really pays? Although a subsidy allows consumers to buy products more cheaply, the subsidy is ultimately funded by taxpayers – raising the question of who is paying the taxes that fund it.
Key concept: EfficiencySubsidies can remove the competitive pressure that normally forces firms to cut costs and innovate, since protected firms do not have to compete on equal terms with unsubsidized foreign rivals.
Key concept: EquityLarge agricultural subsidies paid by high-income-country governments to their farmers are a major source of international debate. These subsidies can lead to overproduction, which has sustainability implications, and can be highly damaging to small-scale farmers in developing countries, who do not receive similar subsidies themselves yet must compete with the artificially low prices charged by subsidized farmers. High-income country farmers have been accused of dumping – selling products in developing countries at prices beneath their own costs of production. This is a major issue of contention at the World Trade Organization (WTO).
Economics in actionAs part of commitments to the Sustainable Development Goals, many governments use subsidies to encourage the development of green/renewable energy, illustrating how subsidy policy can be linked to the key concept of Sustainability.

Price Ceilings (Maximum Prices)

Key concept: InterventionAlthough it may seem an optimum situation, the free market does not always lead to the best outcomes for all producers and consumers, or for society in general. Governments often choose to intervene directly to achieve a different outcome, using two key types of price control: maximum prices and minimum prices.
DefinitionA price ceiling (maximum price) is a situation where the government sets a maximum price, below the equilibrium price, which then prevents producers from raising the price above it. These are sometimes called “ceiling prices” since the price cannot go above the “ceiling” put in place by government.
Key concept: EquityMaximum prices are usually set to help consumers, and are normally imposed in markets where the product in question is a necessity and/or a merit good (a good that would be underprovided if the market were allowed to operate freely). Examples include maximum prices in agricultural/food markets during times of food shortages to ensure affordable food, or maximum prices on rented accommodation to try to ensure affordable housing for those on low incomes.

The market effects of a price ceiling

Figure 8.7: The market for breadPrice of bread ($) on the vertical axis, Quantity of bread (000s of loaves) on the horizontal axis. A downward-sloping demand curve D and an upward-sloping supply curve S intersect at the free-market equilibrium, price Pe and quantity Qe. The government imposes a maximum price Pmax, shown as a horizontal dashed line below Pe. At Pmax, the quantity demanded (read off the demand curve, further right, call it Q2) is greater than the quantity supplied (read off the supply curve, further left, call it Q1) – the horizontal gap between these two points at price Pmax is labelled “excess demand”.

Without government interference, the equilibrium quantity demanded and supplied would be Qe, at price Pe. The government imposes a maximum price Pmax to help consumers of bread. However, a problem arises: at Pmax, quantity demanded rises (because price is lower) but only a smaller quantity is supplied (because producers are less willing to supply at a lower price) – there is excess demand (a shortage). If the government does not intervene further, actual consumption of bread falls to the lower quantity that is supplied, even though the price is lower than the free-market price.

Consequences of the shortageExcess demand creates problems. Shortages may lead to the emergence of a black market (an illegal market) in which the product is sold at a higher price, somewhere between the maximum price and the equilibrium price. Queues may develop in shops, and producers may start to decide who is going to be allowed to buy – since these outcomes are not really “fair” for consumers, the government may need to try to eliminate, or at least reduce, the shortage.

How can the government try to remove the shortage?

The government essentially has two options:

  • Shift the demand curve to the left until equilibrium is reached at the maximum price – but this would limit the consumption of the product, which goes against the whole point of imposing the maximum price in the first place.
  • Shift the supply curve to the right until equilibrium is reached at the maximum price, with more being both supplied and demanded. There are several ways to do this:
    1. The government could offer subsidies to firms in the industry to encourage them to produce more.
    2. The government could start to produce the product itself, thus increasing supply. This is known as direct provision.
    3. If the government had previously stored some of the product, it could release some of the stocks (stored goods) onto the market. However, if the product were perishable (like bread), this would not be possible.
Figure 8.8: Government action to solve the problem of excess demandPrice of bread ($) vertical axis, Quantity of bread (000s of loaves) horizontal axis. Shows the original D and S curves plus a new supply curve S2 shifted to the right of the original S (via subsidy, direct provision, or releasing stored bread). S2 intersects D exactly at the maximum price Pmax, at a new, larger quantity Q3, which is now both demanded and supplied – the shortage is eliminated.

If the government is able to shift the supply curve to the right (via subsidy, direct provision or using stored bread), equilibrium will be reached at Pmax, with more of the product being demanded and supplied. However, it is fair to say this may well mean the government incurs a cost, especially in the case of a subsidy, and this will have an opportunity cost – the government may have to reduce expenditure in some other area, such as education or health.

Economics in actionRent controls (legal maximum rents on rental accommodation) are a widely used real-world example of the maximum-price policy, implemented in many cities around the world to try to keep rental accommodation affordable for people on low incomes.

Higher Level Calculating the effects of a price ceiling – worked example

In HL Paper 3 you may be asked to calculate the possible effects of a price ceiling, including the resulting shortage and changes in expenditure. Worked example: the market for cooking oil in a country, where the equilibrium price is $1.50 per litre and the equilibrium quantity is 1 million litres. The government imposes a legal price ceiling of $1 per litre.

  • Equilibrium (no ceiling): price $1.50, quantity 1 million litres.
  • At the ceiling price of $1: 1.5 million litres of cooking oil will be demanded, but only 0.5 million litres will be supplied. This means there will be an excess demand (shortage) of 1 million litres.
  • Change in consumer expenditure: consumer expenditure before the maximum price was 1 million × $1.50 = $1.5 million. Consumer expenditure after the maximum price (based on the quantity actually transacted, i.e. the quantity supplied of 0.5 million) was 0.5 million × $1.00 = $0.5 million. Therefore, consumer expenditure on cooking oil fell by $1 million.
  • Measures to eliminate the excess demand: (a) direct provision of cooking oil by the government, i.e. setting up government-owned cooking oil manufacturers; or (b) granting subsidies to cooking oil producers in order to encourage greater supply.
Excess demand (shortage) at a price ceiling = Quantity demanded at Pmax − Quantity supplied at Pmax

Price Floors (Minimum Prices)

DefinitionA price floor (minimum price) is a situation where the government sets a minimum price, above the equilibrium price, which then prevents producers from reducing the price below it. These are sometimes called “floor prices” since the price cannot go below the “floor” put in place by the government.

Reasons for setting minimum prices

Key concept: InterventionMinimum prices are mostly set for one of two reasons.
  • To attempt to raise incomes for producers of goods and services that the government thinks are important, such as agricultural products. Their prices may be subject to large fluctuations, or there may be a lot of foreign competition. When equilibrium prices are low, the economic well-being of producers may be harmed.
  • To protect workers by setting a minimum wage, to ensure that workers earn enough to lead a reasonable existence. Very low equilibrium wages may be seen as unfair to workers who receive them, raising issues of equity.
Key concept: EquityBoth agricultural price supports and the minimum wage are framed by the textbook as Equity-driven interventions – trying to ensure producers and workers receive a “fair” payment.

The market effects of a price floor

Figure 8.9: A minimum price in the market for wheatPrice of wheat ($) on the vertical axis, Quantity of wheat (000s of tonnes) on the horizontal axis. Downward-sloping demand curve D and upward-sloping supply curve S intersect at the free-market equilibrium, price Pe and quantity Qe. The government imposes a minimum price Pmin, shown as a horizontal dashed line above Pe. At Pmin, quantity demanded (read off D, a smaller quantity, Q1) is less than quantity supplied (read off S, a larger quantity, Q2) – the horizontal gap between these two points at price Pmin is labelled “excess supply”.

Without government interference, the equilibrium quantity demanded and supplied would be Qe, at price Pe. The government imposes a minimum price Pmin in order to increase the revenue of wheat producers. However, a problem now arises: at Pmin, only the smaller quantity Q1 will be demanded because the price has risen, but Q2 will now be supplied – there is excess supply (a surplus). If the government does not intervene further, consumption of wheat actually falls from Qe to Q1, albeit at a higher price.

Consequences of the surplusExcess supply creates problems. Producers will find themselves with surpluses and will be tempted to try to get around the price controls and sell their excess supply for a lower price, somewhere between Pmin and Pe, undermining the policy.

How does the government maintain the minimum price?

In order to maintain the minimum price, it is likely that the government will have to intervene, typically by buying up the surplus itself.

Figure 8.10: Government action to solve the problem of excess supplyPrice of wheat ($) vertical axis, Quantity of wheat (000s of tonnes) horizontal axis. Shows the original D and S curves, the minimum price Pmin as a horizontal line, and a new demand curve labelled “D + government buying” shifted to the right of the original D. This new demand curve intersects S exactly at price Pmin, at the full quantity Q2 (the original quantity supplied at Pmin), which is now entirely demanded (by private consumers plus the government) and supplied – the surplus is eliminated.

The government would normally eliminate the excess supply by buying up the surplus products at the minimum price, thus shifting the (effective) demand curve to the right and creating a new equilibrium at Pmin, with the full quantity Q2 now demanded and supplied. The government could then store the surplus, destroy it, or attempt to sell it abroad. However, storage tends to be rather expensive, and destroying products is considered wasteful. Selling abroad is always an option, but it often causes angry reactions from foreign governments involved, who claim their domestic industries are being harmed by products being “dumped” on their markets.

Opportunity costThere is bound to be an opportunity cost whenever governments spend money in any given area. Here, the cost of buying up and storing surpluses must be paid for, so the government may well have to cut back on expenditure in some other area, such as funding for teacher training, or raise taxes.

Alternative ways to maintain a minimum price

  • Quotas: producers could be limited by quotas, restricting supply so that it does not exceed the quantity demanded at the minimum price (Q1). This keeps the price at Pmin, but means that only a limited number of producers would receive that price.
  • Increase demand: the government could attempt to increase demand for the product by advertising, or, if appropriate, by restricting supplies of imported competing products through protectionist policies, thus increasing demand for domestic products.
Figure 8.11: A quota to maintain a minimum pricePrice of wheat ($) vertical axis, Quantity of wheat (000s of tonnes) horizontal axis. Shows D and S curves and the minimum price Pmin above the free-market equilibrium. A vertical “Quota” line is drawn at quantity Q1 (the quantity demanded at Pmin), physically restricting the amount that can legally be supplied to that level, so that the price is held at Pmin without government purchases – but only the limited number of producers permitted to sell up to the quota actually benefit from the higher price.
Efficiency cost of guaranteeing pricesIf governments protect firms by guaranteeing minimum prices, problems are likely to occur. Firms may think that they do not have to be as cost-conscious as they should be, and this may lead to inefficiency and a waste of resources. It may also lead to firms producing more of the protected product than they should, and less of other products that they could produce more efficiently.

Are minimum prices only implemented to help producers or workers?

No. Typically, minimum prices are imposed to help certain producers who would struggle to survive by selling at the equilibrium price, or to help workers who might not be able to afford a decent standard of living if paid the equilibrium wage. However, there are also examples of governments setting minimum prices to prevent people from consuming too much of a product, in order to make them make better choices – two examples being alcohol and cigarettes.

Minimum prices used to reduce consumptionCertain types of alcohol can be very affordable (i.e. very inexpensive), resulting in overconsumption and problems for those people who drink too much alcohol. Overconsumption of alcohol can result in larger problems for society – this is an example of “market failure” (addressed in much more detail in the next chapter). To prevent suppliers, such as supermarkets, from selling alcohol at low prices, governments might set a minimum price above the market equilibrium price, thereby forcing suppliers to sell at a higher price.

Such a policy might be very important from society’s point of view, but consumers who enjoy the product, and producers who profit from its sale, will certainly be against such government intervention.

So Why Do Governments Intervene in Markets? (Chapter Summary)

This chapter looked at four types of government intervention in markets: indirect taxes, subsidies, and maximum and minimum prices. There is much controversy surrounding the issue of government intervention, with free market economists often opposed to the types and extent of government intervention. It is valuable to summarise the main reasons for government intervention in the context of key IB concepts.

Key concept: ChoiceTo help consumers make better choices:
  • Indirect taxes reduce the quantity of a product sold in a market, and can make certain goods less attractive – helping achieve a government’s goal of reducing the consumption of goods which may be harmful.
  • Minimum prices increase the price of goods that are harmful to consumers, discouraging them from buying as much of the product.
  • Subsidies on healthy products make them more affordable and give consumers the incentive to increase their consumption.
Key concept: SustainabilityTo promote sustainability:
  • Indirect taxes may be imposed on production processes or products that threaten sustainability. Taxes such as carbon taxes, which raise the costs of production, may encourage firms to produce using more sustainable practices.
  • Subsidies may be given to producers whose production methods, or final goods, are favourable to the environment.
Key concepts: Equity and Economic WellbeingTo promote equity and economic well-being:
  • Minimum wages are one way of trying to ensure that workers earn a fair payment for their labour.
  • Minimum prices are one way of trying to ensure that producers earn a fair payment for their products.
  • Maximum prices on necessities and rental accommodation may make such things affordable for low-income households.
