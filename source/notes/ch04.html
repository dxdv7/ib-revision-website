What is Elasticity of Demand?

Elasticity of demandElasticity is a measure of how much something changes when there is a change in one of its determinants – in other words, a measure of responsiveness. Elasticity of demand measures how much the demand for a product changes when there is a change in one of the factors that determine demand.
Key concept: ChangeElasticity is fundamentally about measuring the size and sensitivity of Change – how far one variable changes in response to a change in another.

This chapter looks at two elasticities of demand:

  • Price elasticity of demand (PED)
  • Income elasticity of demand (YED)

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

Definition: PEDPrice elasticity of demand measures how much the quantity demanded of a product changes when there is a change in the price of the product.
PED = Percentage change in quantity demanded of the product ÷ Percentage change in price of the product

To calculate PED, first find the percentage change in quantity demanded, using (change in quantity ÷ original quantity) × 100, and the percentage change in price, using (change in price ÷ original price) × 100. PED is then the first percentage divided by the second. Because price and quantity demanded normally move in opposite directions, this calculation usually gives a negative number, which economists conventionally report as a positive value.

Worked example: a monthly magazineA publishing firm lowers the price of its magazine from $5 to $4.50, and monthly sales rise from 200,000 to 230,000.
  1. Price has fallen by 50 cents from $5, a change of −10%: (−50 ÷ 500) × 100 = −10%.
  2. Quantity demanded has risen by 30,000 from 200,000, a change of +15%: (+30,000 ÷ 200,000) × 100 = +15%.
  3. PED = +15% ÷ (−10%) = −1.5.
The negative value shows the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded predicted by the Law of Demand. By convention, economists ignore the negative sign and report PED as a positive number, so PED for this magazine = 1.5.

The Theoretical Range of Values for PED

In theory, PED can range from zero to infinity. In practice, actual PED values for real products lie somewhere in between these two extremes.

Figure 4.1: A perfectly inelastic demand curve (PED = 0)A perfectly inelastic demand curve is a vertical line at quantity Q. Whether the price is P1 or P2, quantity demanded stays at Q, so the percentage change in quantity demanded is always zero and PED = 0.
Figure 4.2: A perfectly elastic demand curve (PED = infinity)A perfectly elastic demand curve is a horizontal line at price P1. At P1 consumers will buy any quantity, but at any price above it quantity demanded falls to zero, so PED is infinite.

These two extremes are purely theoretical – no real product has a PED of exactly zero or exactly infinity. Real, normal products are classified into three categories based on where their PED falls.

1. Inelastic demand: 0 < PED < 1

A change in price causes a proportionately smaller change in quantity demanded. If price is raised, quantity demanded falls by relatively little, so total revenue (quantity sold × price) increases.

Worked example: strawberry yoghurtWhen the price of a carton of strawberry yoghurt is raised from $1.00 to $1.20 (a 20% rise), quantity demanded per week falls from 12,000 to 10,800 cartons (a 10% fall).
PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price = 10% ÷ 20% = 0.5
PED = 0.5, which is less than one, so demand for the yoghurt is inelastic. Before the price rise, total revenue = 12,000 × $1.00 = $12,000. After the rise, total revenue = 10,800 × $1.20 = $12,960. The firm's revenue has increased by raising the price.
Figure 4.3: The demand for bus passes (inelastic, revenue rises)The price of a monthly bus pass rises from $50 to $60 and quantity demanded falls from 10,000 to 9,000, so PED = −10% ÷ +20% = −0.5 (inelastic). Area c ($50 × 1,000 = $50,000) is lost, but area a ($10 × 9,000 = $90,000) is gained. Because a is larger than c, total revenue rises by $40,000, from $500,000 to $540,000.

2. Elastic demand: 1 < PED < infinity

A change in price causes a proportionately greater change in quantity demanded. If price is raised, quantity demanded falls by relatively more, so total revenue falls.

Worked example: hot dogsWhen the price of a hot dog is raised from $2.00 to $2.10 (a 5% rise), quantity demanded per week falls from 200 to 180 hot dogs (a 10% fall).
PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price = 10% ÷ 5% = 2
PED = 2, greater than one, so demand is elastic. Before the price rise, total revenue = 200 × $2.00 = $400. After the rise, total revenue = 180 × $2.10 = $378. Raising the price has caused total revenue to fall by $22.
Figure 4.4: The demand for streaming subscriptions (elastic, revenue falls)The monthly price of a streaming subscription rises from $10 to $12 and subscribers fall from 50,000 to 35,000, so PED = −30% ÷ +20% = −1.5 (elastic). Area c ($10 × 15,000 = $150,000) is lost while area a ($2 × 35,000 = $70,000) is gained. Because a is smaller than c, total revenue falls by $80,000, from $500,000 to $420,000.

3. Unit elastic demand: PED = 1

A change in price causes an exactly proportionate, opposite change in quantity demanded – if price rises by a given percentage, quantity demanded falls by that same percentage, so total revenue does not change at all.

Figure 4.5: A rectangular hyperbola where PED = 1 at every pointA demand curve for taxi rides with P × Q = 600 at every point. Moving from $30 and 20 (thousand) rides to $12 and 50 (thousand) rides, the revenue lost (area a, $360 thousand) exactly equals the revenue gained (area c, $360 thousand). Total revenue never changes along the curve, so PED = 1 at every point.

Why PED Changes Along a Straight-Line Demand Curve

Common student mistakeIt is a common mistake to assume that elasticity is simply a measure of the slope of the demand curve, and that its value stays the same at every point on the curve. This is not true. For a straight-line, downward-sloping demand curve, the value of PED falls as price falls (i.e. as you move down and to the right along the curve), even though the slope of the line itself stays constant.
Figure 4.6: PED values for a straight-line demand curveOn the straight-line demand curve P = 25 − Q/10, moving from a (50, $20) to b (70, $18) gives a 40% rise in quantity for a 10% fall in price, so PED = 4.0 (elastic). Moving from c (150, $10) to d (170, $8) gives a 13.3% rise in quantity for a 20% fall in price, so PED = 0.67 (inelastic). Elasticity falls as we move down the same straight line.
Worked example

Points a to b: price falls from $20 to $18, quantity demanded rises from 60 to 80 units.

PED = %ΔQD ÷ %ΔP = 33.3% ÷ 10% = 3.3 (elastic)

Points c to d: price falls from $10 to $8, quantity demanded rises from 160 to 180 units.

PED = %ΔQD ÷ %ΔP = 12.5% ÷ 20% = 0.625 (inelastic)

Conclusion: the value of PED falls as we move down a straight-line demand curve. This makes intuitive sense: low-priced products tend to have more inelastic demand than high-priced products, because consumers are less concerned about a given proportional price rise on an inexpensive item than on an expensive one.

The Determinants of Price Elasticity of Demand

1. The number and closeness of substitutes

This is the single most important determinant of PED. The more substitutes available for a product, and the closer those substitutes are, the more elastic demand will be. Example: with many brands of butter on the market, a rise in the price of one brand causes many customers to switch to another brand – demand for that brand is elastic. Products with few substitutes (e.g. oil) tend to have relatively inelastic demand, since quantity demanded falls relatively little as price rises.

2. The necessity of the product, and how widely it is defined

Food is a necessity – without it we die – so demand for "food" overall is very inelastic. But if we narrow the definition to "meat", demand becomes less inelastic (more elastic), since alternatives like vegetables exist. Narrow it further to a specific type of meat (chicken, beef, lamb, pork), and demand becomes even more elastic, since a consumer can easily switch from one meat to another if its price rises. Narrow it further still, to a specific, identical branded product, and demand becomes even more elastic again.

Figure: Level of definition (Fig 4.7)A branching diagram starting from "Food" at the top, splitting into broad categories such as meat and vegetables; "Meat" then splits further into specific types (beef, chicken, lamb, pork); a specific meat type then splits again into competing identical branded products ("Brand A markets" vs "Brand B markets"). At each step down the hierarchy, the product is defined more narrowly and demand becomes progressively more elastic.
Necessity is subjectiveWhat counts as "necessary" changes from consumer to consumer, since tastes differ. For example, chicken is very popular (and closer to a necessity) in Malaysia, giving it less elastic demand there than in Italy, where it is valued less highly. Some goods are habit-forming or addictive (cigarettes, alcohol, chocolate) and so have inelastic demand regardless of whether they are "objectively" necessary.

3. The proportion of income spent on the good

If a good costs very little and makes up a very small part of a person's budget, a change in its price will cause very little change in quantity demanded, i.e. demand will be quite inelastic. Example: a person earning a good salary who buys a coffee for $1.50 is unlikely to be put off by a 10% price rise to $1.65. (Behavioural economists would also explain this persistence in terms of the status quo bias.)

4. The time period considered

As the price of a product changes, it often takes time for consumers to adjust their buying and consumption habits. PED tends to be more inelastic in the short term, and becomes more elastic the longer the time period considered.

Worked example: heating oil in AustriaWhen heating oil prices rose sharply one winter in Austria, demand fell by a proportionately smaller amount than the price change (relatively inelastic), since people had no immediate alternative way to heat their homes. However, over the following years, demand for heating oil fell considerably more as people gradually switched their heating systems to gas, coal, or wood – PED measured over the longer time period was much more elastic.

Why Knowledge of PED Matters for Firms and Governments

For firms

Knowledge of PED lets a firm predict the effect of its pricing decisions on quantity demanded and, crucially, on total revenue (as shown in the worked examples above).

For governments

Governments must be aware of the likely consequences of indirect taxes (e.g. sales taxes) on a number of economic variables. If a tax is imposed on a product, its price will rise, and quantity demanded is likely to fall – with consequences for the tax revenue the government actually collects. There are also consequences for employment in the affected industry: if demand for the product is very elastic, a tax-driven price rise causes a relatively large fall in demand, which is likely to cause a relatively large fall in demand for the workers who produce it, potentially raising unemployment significantly.

Policy implicationSince governments generally do not want to increase unemployment, they tend to place higher taxes on products with relatively inelastic demand, so that demand for the product – and hence employment in that industry – does not fall significantly. That said, real-world decisions about which products to tax, and by how much, depend on more than elasticity alone.
Higher Level

PED for Primary Commodities vs. Manufactured Goods

"Primary commodities" are raw materials, such as cotton or coffee. They tend to have inelastic demand because: (a) they are necessities to the industries that buy them (their "consumers" are typically manufacturing firms, not everyday households), and (b) there are few or no substitutes for them as production inputs.

Worked example: green coffee beansCoffee-processing companies that make instant coffee must buy green (raw) coffee beans. If the price of green coffee rises, the processing companies have little choice but to keep buying it, since they need it to meet fixed production targets and have no substitute input – quantity demanded falls only a little (inelastic). Likewise, if the price of green coffee fell, processing companies would not proportionately increase their purchases, since their production targets are already set and they have no further use for extra input regardless of price.

Manufactured goods, by contrast, tend to have more elastic demand, since there are usually many more substitutes available to consumers, as products can be differentiated by different producers/brands. Example: if the price of one branded vacuum cleaner rises significantly, consumers can easily switch to one of many other brands. This ties directly back to "the number and closeness of substitutes" as a determinant of PED – for most consumer goods, producers differentiate their products in many ways, giving consumers lots of choice.

Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

Definition: YEDIncome elasticity of demand measures how much the demand for a product changes when there is a change in the consumer's income.
YED = Percentage change in quantity demanded of the product ÷ Percentage change in income of the consumer

To calculate YED, first find the percentage change in quantity demanded and the percentage change in income, each worked out as (change ÷ original value) × 100. YED is then the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income. Unlike PED, the sign of the result is kept rather than dropped, since it shows whether the good is normal (positive) or inferior (negative).

Worked example: holiday spendingA person's annual income rises from $60,000 to $66,000 (a $6,000 increase), and their annual holiday spending rises from $2,500 to $3,000 (a $500 increase).
  1. Income change: (+6,000 ÷ 60,000) × 100 = +10%.
  2. Quantity demanded (holiday spending) change: (+500 ÷ 2,500) × 100 = +20%.
  3. YED = +20% ÷ +10% = 2.

The range of values for YED

Unlike PED, the sign of YED (positive or negative) matters – it tells us whether the good is a normal good or an inferior good.

Normal goods: YED positiveDemand rises as income rises.
  • If the % increase in quantity demanded is less than the % increase in income, YED is between 0 and 1: demand is income-inelastic. These are "necessity goods". Example: demand for bread does not increase significantly as income rises, because people already feel they have enough bread.
  • If the % increase in quantity demanded is greater than the % increase in income, YED is greater than 1: demand is income-elastic. These are "superior" or "luxury" goods. Example: demand for foreign holidays tends to be income-elastic.
Inferior goods: YED negativeDemand falls as income rises. As income increases, people switch expenditure away from the inferior good toward higher-priced goods they can now afford. Example: demand for inexpensive, unbranded jeans falls as income rises, as people switch to more expensive branded jeans.

Demand peaks at income Y* and then falls as households can afford superior alternatives, so beyond Y* instant noodles behave as an inferior good.

Why Knowledge of YED Is Important

1. For decision-making by firms

Knowledge of YED helps firms decide which markets to enter and which products to sell. Products with high YED will see large increases in demand as income levels rise in a country, so their markets grow quickly – firms want to be aware of the YED of their products and of changing incomes among their target consumers, so they can take full advantage of growth opportunities. Example: as incomes in a country rise, demand for smartphones is likely to rise proportionately more (high YED), so producers look to expand sales in rapidly growing economies. Producers often manufacture several versions of a product at different price points to appeal to consumers across different income levels, and want to anticipate the effect of a recession (falling national income): if a recession is expected, producers will want to increase production of inferior goods (whose demand rises as income falls) and decrease production of normal/luxury goods (whose demand falls as income falls).

2. For explaining sectoral changes in the structure of the economy

Production in an economy is usually separated into three sectors:

  • Primary sector – agricultural and fishing industries, and extraction industries such as forestry and mining. Their output is known as primary products.
  • Secondary (manufacturing) sector – industries that take primary products (raw materials) and use them to manufacture producer goods (e.g. machinery) or consumer goods (e.g. electronic goods, clothing); also includes construction.
  • Tertiary (service) sector – industries producing services or intangible products, such as financial services, education, information technology, and mass media.
Sectoral changeSectoral change refers to the shift in the relative share of national output and employment attributed to each production sector as an economy develops over time. This does not mean a sector is shrinking in absolute size, only that its relative share of the economy is changing.

YED explains this pattern. As economies grow and incomes rise, demand for primary products (e.g. agricultural goods) does not increase greatly, because such products have income-inelastic demand. Extra income is instead spent on manufactured goods (mobile phones, electronics etc.), which have income-elastic demand. So output in the primary sector still grows, but output in the secondary (manufacturing) sector grows faster – the relative proportions of output coming from each sector change.

As a country develops further, the same YED logic explains growth of the tertiary (service) sector: services tend to have high YED, so as income grows further there is faster growth in this sector, reflecting its high income-elasticity (entertainment, education, healthcare and similar industries growing especially quickly). Therefore, tertiary sectors of an economy tend to grow at a faster rate as a country develops and living standards rise, since demand for tertiary products grows more rapidly.

A globalized caveatIn an increasingly globalized world, where it is easier to import goods and services from other countries, it is not necessarily the income of an individual country alone that determines how its own sectors grow. It is more accurate to say that rising global incomes lead to increased demand for manufactured goods, and even greater increases in demand for services, sourced from many different countries – so a country's secondary or tertiary sector can grow by meeting rising global demand, not only domestic demand.
