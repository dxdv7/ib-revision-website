1. What is market failure? The baseline of social efficiency

In a perfectly working ("real") market, resources are allocated efficiently and community (social) surplus is maximized. However, real-world markets are very often not perfect — they suffer from problems that prevent them from allocating resources optimally. When this happens, community surplus is not maximized, and we say that there is a market failure. When markets fail, governments are often expected to intervene to try to correct the failure and move the economy back towards the optimal allocation of resources.

Key concept: InterventionThe whole chapter revolves around this idea: whenever a market fails to allocate resources efficiently, there is a case (in theory) for government intervention to correct it.

Marginal social cost (MSC) and marginal social benefit (MSB)

Marginal social cost (MSC) is equal to marginal private cost (MPC) plus or minus any external cost or benefit of production. If there are no externalities of production, then MSC = MPC. The MPC is essentially the "private" supply curve, based on the firm's own costs of production.

Marginal social benefit (MSB) is equal to marginal private benefit (MPB) plus or minus any external cost or benefit of consumption. If there are no externalities of consumption, then MSB = MPB. The MPB is essentially the "private" demand curve, based on the utility or benefits to consumers.

MSC = MPC ± external cost/benefit of production
MSB = MPB ± external cost/benefit of consumption

To calculate MSC, start with the firm's marginal private cost (MPC) of producing one more unit and add any extra cost imposed on third parties (a negative production externality), or subtract any extra benefit enjoyed by third parties (a positive production externality). To calculate MSB, start with the marginal private benefit (MPB) enjoyed by the consumer of one more unit and add any extra benefit enjoyed by third parties (a positive consumption externality), or subtract any extra cost imposed on third parties (a negative consumption externality). The external amount is measured in the same units as the private figure, typically dollars per unit.

Worked example

Consider a paint factory whose marginal private cost (MPC) of producing the 1,000th litre of paint is $4.00. That litre of production also creates $1.50 of external cost for nearby residents, in the form of health problems caused by fumes. MSC = MPC + external cost = $4.00 + $1.50 = $5.50. Now consider a measles vaccine: the marginal private benefit (MPB) to the person being vaccinated is $30 (reduced personal risk of illness), but vaccination also creates an external benefit of $20 to the rest of the community (a lower risk of the disease spreading). MSB = MPB + external benefit = $30 + $20 = $50. In both cases, a free market that only responds to MPC or MPB (ignoring the $1.50 external cost or the $20 external benefit) will misallocate resources relative to the true social cost or benefit.

If no externalities exist in a market at all, then MSC = MSB and we have social (allocative) efficiency, with community surplus maximized — this is shown in Figure 9.1. If, however, externalities exist in a market, then MSC does not equal MSB, and so we have a market failure and an inefficient allocation of society's resources.

Figure 9.1: Social efficiencyAxes: Price ($) on the vertical axis; Quantity (000s) on the horizontal axis. Two curves are drawn: S = Marginal Social Cost (MSC), sloping upward from left to right; D = Marginal Social Benefit (MSB), sloping downward from left to right. The two curves intersect at the market equilibrium. The area above the equilibrium price and below the MSB (demand) curve, up to the equilibrium quantity, is consumer surplus. The area below the equilibrium price and above the MSC (supply) curve, up to the equilibrium quantity, is producer surplus. Consumer surplus plus producer surplus is community surplus, which is at its maximum at this point because MSB = MSC for every unit produced up to the equilibrium quantity. This is the "no externality, no market failure" benchmark against which every diagram in this chapter is compared.

Externalities may be split into four types: positive externalities of consumption, positive externalities of production, negative externalities of consumption, and negative externalities of production. An externality occurs when the production or consumption of a good or service has an effect upon a third party. If the effect is harmful, we talk about a negative externality — there is an external cost that must be added to the private costs of the producer or consumer to reflect the full cost to society. If the effect is beneficial, we talk about a positive externality — there has been an external benefit to add to the private benefits of the producer or consumer.

2. Positive externalities of consumption and merit goods

There are certain goods or services which, when consumed (used), are beneficial to those who consume them but which will also provide external benefits to third parties. For example, when people "consume" health care, they aim to improve their own health, but they also create a positive externality for society: if people are healthier, they will not pass on illnesses to others. A healthier workforce also means a higher level of human capital, meaning the economy will be more productive, benefiting the whole population. Thus, the marginal social benefit of consuming health care is greater than the marginal private benefit enjoyed by the consumers themselves.

Figure 9.2: A positive externality of consumption (health care)Axes: Price of health care ($) vertical; Quantity of health care horizontal. Three curves: MSC (=S, the ordinary upward-sloping supply curve — there is no production externality here); MPB (=D, the private/free-market downward-sloping demand curve); MSB, drawn above and to the right of MPB, also downward sloping, with the vertical gap between them at any quantity labelled "positive externality". The free-market equilibrium occurs where MPB meets MSC, giving quantity Q1 at price P1 (this is what actually happens in a free market, since consumers only respond to their own private benefit). The socially efficient point occurs where MSB meets MSC, giving a larger quantity Q* at a higher price P*. Because Q* is greater than Q1, the market underallocates resources — too little health care is consumed. The "potential welfare gain" is shown as a shaded triangle between the MSB and MSC curves, bounded by Q1 and Q*: for every unit between Q1 and Q*, MSB exceeds MSC, so society could gain extra welfare if consumption rose to Q*. This gap is also referred to as a "welfare loss", because society is currently losing the welfare that would be achieved if the market operated at the socially efficient level.
Key concept: Equity / Economic wellbeingUnder-consumption of goods like health care and education has significant implications for both the equity of outcomes in society and overall economic wellbeing, since the third-party benefits (a healthier, better-educated population) are missed when the market under-produces these goods.
Merit goodsMerit goods are goods that are beneficial to consumers, but people might not consume them enough — either because they underestimate the potential benefits, or because they choose to ignore the potential benefits. That is, consumers have imperfect information about the potential benefits. Therefore, the demand for the good is lower than it "should" be from society's perspective.
Key concept: ChoiceMerit goods illustrate how imperfect information distorts the choices consumers make, leading them to under-value goods that are actually highly beneficial to them and to society.

Governments aim to bring about an increase in the quantity of merit goods consumed. This benefits the consumers themselves, but more importantly, higher consumption brings higher benefits to society as a whole. Some examples of merit goods and their possible private and social benefits:

ProductPossible private benefitsPossible benefits to society as a whole
A university education in engineeringThe joys of pursuing a career in a chosen field. Better job prospects.More people educated in engineering at university may contribute to important technological innovations.
Measles vaccinesLess risk of contracting measles. Better health.If a certain percentage of the population is immunized against measles, then it reduces the risk of a measles outbreak.
Sports facilitiesEnjoyment of physical activity. Better health.A "fitter" population is a more productive population.

It is worth drawing a link to the Production Possibilities Curve (PPC) model (Chapter 1). Any improvement in the quality and quantity of an economy's factors of production will shift out the PPC, increasing the economy's production possibilities. By this reasoning, improvements in the health and education of an economy's population will improve the quality of labour, thereby shifting the PPC outwards, with potential benefits for the wellbeing of the whole economy.

Key concept: EfficiencyImprovements in merit good consumption (health, education) raise the quality of the labour factor of production, shifting the economy's PPC outward — connecting a microeconomic market failure to macro-level productive potential.

In this essential-theory presentation, the examples of health care and education are broad. Each category could be broken into a large number of smaller sub-markets — e.g. health care could be split into measles vaccines, flu injections, anti-malaria tablets, prostate cancer screening or dementia screening; education could be split into kindergarten, high school, tertiary, girls' education, or adult education. Each of these sub-markets creates benefits both for the individuals concerned and for society as a whole.

Government responses: promoting merit goods

Key concept: InterventionIf a government wishes to increase the consumption of merit goods that create positive externalities of consumption, it has several possible policies (examined here in the context of health care, but applicable to any merit good).
Figure 9.3: Measures to promote positive externalities of consumptionStarting from the curves of Figure 9.2 (MSB above MPB, with MSC the supply curve), two possible government solutions are shown together on one diagram. Solution 1 (subsidy): a government subsidy shifts the supply curve downward from MSC to "MSC + subsidy". This new, lower curve now intersects MPB at the socially efficient quantity Q*, and the price paid by consumers falls to P2 (below the original free-market price P1). Solution 2 (positive advertising): instead of subsidizing supply, the government shifts the MPB curve rightward (raising private demand) through an awareness campaign, so that the new, higher MPB curve intersects the original MSC curve at the same efficient quantity Q*, but via a different price path than the subsidy solution. Price labels on the vertical axis are P* (the fully socially-optimal MSB=MSC price), P1 (original free-market price) and P2 (post-subsidy consumer price); quantity labels on the horizontal axis are Q1 (original free-market quantity) and Q* (socially efficient quantity, reached by either policy route).
Subsidies / direct provisionThe government could subsidize the supply of health care (Figure 9.3). A subsidy shifts the MSC curve downwards so the socially efficient level of consumption Q* can be reached, at a lower price to the consumer, P2. The government may deem the good so important that it subsidizes it all the way to being free of charge to the consumer, or supplies it itself at no direct cost to the consumer. Problem: cost and opportunity cost. Governments have limited budgets and must choose how to allocate resources; any increase in health spending likely requires cutting spending elsewhere. Political considerations matter too — different parties may not see health spending as an equal priority, and government policy can be slow to change (especially around elections).
Improving information / public awareness campaignsSince consumers might have imperfect information about the potential benefits of a merit good, the government could use public awareness campaigns to increase awareness of the benefits, shifting the MPB curve to the right towards MSB, increasing welfare. Problem: advertising can be costly, and although the effect may be beneficial in the long run, it takes a long time to have an effect, so short-run benefits may be minimal.
LegislationThe government could pass laws requiring citizens to have vaccinations against certain diseases, or regular health checks. This will only be successful if the government provides the service free of charge, and people often resent this type of law as an infringement of their civil liberties.

The extent to which government intervenes depends on the external benefits involved, but these are always difficult to measure accurately. For merit goods like health care and education, the positive externalities are significant, and economic growth is heavily dependent on the productivity of labour, which depends on education and health. It is therefore a priority for governments to have an effective system for providing education and health care — though the extent to which this is done through direct provision or through the private sector varies by country, highlighting one of economics' central themes: the extent to which governments should intervene in the allocation of resources.

Economics in action: the debate over whether governments should make it compulsory for all children to be immunized against measles illustrates the trade-off between maximizing the positive externality benefits of a merit good and respecting individual freedom of choice.

3. Positive externalities of production

These occur when the production of a good or service creates external benefits that are favourable to third parties. For example, if a large printing firm provides high-quality training for its employees, this is a cost to the firm. When employees leave the printing firm and go to other firms, those other firms benefit because they do not have to spend money training their new workers. This is a positive externality of production — society gains from the training given by the printing firm, even though the firm itself has not. Thus, the marginal private cost of the firm is greater than the marginal social cost.

Figure 9.4: A positive externality of production (printing firm)Axes: Price of printing ($) vertical; Quantity horizontal. Curves: MPC (top, the firm's actual private marginal-cost/supply curve); MSC, drawn below MPC (since the production externality lowers the true social cost); MSB (=D, ordinary downward-sloping demand, since there is no consumption externality here). The free-market equilibrium is where MPC meets MSB, giving quantity Q1 at price P1. The socially efficient point is where MSC meets MSB, giving quantity Q*, which is greater than Q1 (Q* > Q1) — i.e. underproduction. A "potential welfare gain" triangle sits between the MPC and MSC curves, spanning the output range from Q1 to Q*.

The printing firm produces where its marginal private cost (in effect, the supply curve) meets marginal social benefit (in effect, the demand curve) at output Q1. This is below the socially efficient level Q*. Between Q1 and Q*, there is a potential welfare gain: if output increased to Q*, welfare would be gained because for all units from Q1 to Q*, MSB is greater than MSC. This is also a "welfare loss" in the sense that society is losing out on welfare relative to the socially efficient outcome. Again, this is a market failure because in a pure free market there would be an under-allocation of resources to this market — firms will not want to engage in as much training as would be desirable from society's point of view.

SubsidiesThe government could subsidize firms that offer training. This shifts the MPC curve downwards; if a full subsidy were given, then MPC would equal MSC and the socially efficient point would be reached. Two main problems: (1) it is very difficult for the government to estimate the level of subsidy deserved by each individual firm; (2) the cost of the subsidies implies an opportunity cost — the government must choose to cut back spending elsewhere.
Direct provisionThe government could provide vocational training through the state, by setting up training centres for workers in certain industries. Although this is possible, costs would be high, the trainers may lack the expertise found in the firms themselves, and it may dissuade firms from offering their own training.

Regardless of who pays, economies benefit enormously from the training and retraining of labour, a factor of production — any improvement in the quality of labour can shift out an economy's PPC. As technological change rapidly changes the nature of employment, replacing many workers with machinery, it is extremely important that workers are given retraining opportunities. Without such systems, there is a grave risk that people who lose their jobs due to changing technologies will remain unemployed, reducing their economic wellbeing.

Key concept: EfficiencyTraining and retraining is a productive-efficiency issue at the level of the whole economy — better-quality labour shifts the PPC outward, increasing the maximum possible output of the economy.

4. Negative externalities of consumption and demerit goods

There are many things that, when consumed by individuals, adversely affect third parties. Examples include cigarettes and "second-hand smoke", petrol or diesel for driving cars and air pollution, and alcohol and anti-social behaviour. In each case, the marginal social benefit is less than the marginal private benefit, because the private utility enjoyed by the consumer is diminished by the negative externality suffered by third parties.

Figure 9.5: A negative externality of consumption (cigarettes)Axes: Price of cigarettes ($) vertical; Quantity horizontal. Curves: MPB (top, the private demand curve, downward sloping); MSB, drawn below MPB (the vertical gap between them at any quantity is labelled "negative externality"); MSC (=S, the ordinary upward-sloping supply curve, since there is no production externality here). The free-market equilibrium is where MSC meets MPB, giving quantity Q1 at price P1 — consumers maximize their own private utility and ignore the externality they create. The socially efficient point is where MSC meets MSB, giving a smaller quantity Q* at price P*, with Q* < Q1. A "welfare loss" triangle, labelled at the top of the diagram, sits between the MPB and MSB curves, spanning the output range from Q* to Q1: for every unit produced beyond Q*, MSC exceeds MSB, so those units represent pure waste to society.

People who smoke choose to do so because they enjoy some private benefits of smoking. However, this creates costs (private utility losses) for other people through passive/second-hand smoking, as well as simple discomfort at the smell of cigarettes. Costs to others are significant and include lung cancer, bronchial illnesses and asthma. Because there is a free market, consumers will maximize their private utility (benefit) and consume at the level where MSC = MPB, ignoring the externality they create. This means they over-consume, at Q1 rather than the socially efficient Q*. Since MSC is greater than MSB for these units, there is a welfare loss to society and a market failure: too many resources are allocated to this market, and the good is over-produced.

Key concept: SustainabilityMany products whose consumption creates significant environmental problems also create threats to sustainability — the waste created and the greenhouse gases emitted from consuming goods and services are negative externalities of consumption. This may be seen as a situation of inter-generational inequity, since today's actions damage the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
Demerit goodsDemerit goods are goods that are harmful to consumers, but people who consume them are either unaware of the possible harm, or they ignore the possible risks. That is, consumers have imperfect information about the potential costs to themselves, and to others. Therefore, the demand for the good is higher than it "should" be from society's perspective. Demerit goods also create negative externalities when consumed.
Not every externality-causing good is a demerit goodIt is worth noting that not all goods that create negative externalities of consumption are demerit goods. For example, the petrol consumed when a car is driven causes external costs for third parties as a result of emissions, but it does not cause a particularly harmful effect to the driver themselves. Therefore, petrol is not considered a demerit good.

Governments aim to bring about a reduction in the quantity of demerit goods consumed. This will benefit the consumers themselves, but more importantly lower consumption will reduce the welfare loss to society as a whole. Examples of demerit goods:

ProductExamples of the possible harm to the consumerExamples of the possible harm to third parties
CigarettesRespiratory illnesses, cancer, death, damage to unborn children.Unpleasant smell (small cost). All the same problems as consumers as a result of the harm to consumers as a result of passive smoking.
AlcoholHealth consequences, such as hangovers, liver disease, high blood pressure.Problems to society as a result of excessive (binge) drinking, such as aggressive behaviour, domestic violence, costs of medical care.
Unhealthy food (eg sugary drinks, food with high sugar or fat content)Possible health consequences, such as high blood pressure, problems associated with being overweight.Health care costs are borne by society.

Government responses: reducing demerit goods

Key concept: InterventionThe government will act to reduce or eliminate the negative externality, with several possible policy options, examined here in the context of the cigarette market.
Figure 9.6: Measures to reduce negative externalities of consumptionStarting from Figure 9.5's curves (MPB top, MSB below it, MSC the original supply curve), two solutions are shown: Solution 1 (indirect/Pigouvian tax): a tax shifts the supply curve upwards from MSC to "MSC + tax", which now intersects MPB at the socially efficient quantity Q*, but at a higher consumer price P3 (above the original P1). The government collects tax revenue equal to the tax per unit multiplied by Q*. Solution 2 (negative advertising / education): shifts the MPB curve to the left (reducing private demand), so that a lower MPB curve intersects the original MSC nearer the efficient quantity, without needing a price rise via tax.
Market-based approach: indirect (Pigouvian) taxA Pigouvian tax is an indirect tax imposed on any market that creates negative externalities, in order to eliminate the externality (Figure 9.6). If the government imposes an indirect tax, this shifts the MSC curve upwards to MSC + tax, reducing consumption to the socially efficient level Q*, but the price to consumers becomes P3. The government gains significant tax revenue which may be used to correct some of the negative externalities caused by smoking.
Limits of indirect taxesThe inelastic demand for cigarettes (and other demerit goods) means that taxes do not reduce quantity demanded very much, so quantity demanded does not fall all the way to the socially efficient level. If taxes are raised too much, people may look for cheaper sources of supply elsewhere (e.g. neighbouring jurisdictions with lower taxes), so consumption is not reduced but government tax revenue is lost.
Key concept: EquityA significant criticism of indirect taxes is that they are regressive: their impact is greater on lower-income people than on higher-income people, because although higher- and lower-income people pay the same amount of tax, this takes up a larger proportion of income for lower-income people. Thus, they are criticized for being inequitable.
Legislation / regulation ("command and control")The government could create laws and regulations to alter consumer behaviour, such as age restrictions on cigarette purchases, bans on smoking in public places, and laws about advertising and packaging of tobacco products. Increased regulations may be met with opposition from those who argue that it removes people's right to choose for themselves (see the term "nanny state"). When regulations are effective, they have a negative effect on producers, who may lobby the government to prevent new legislation. Regulations may also be difficult and costly to enforce, requiring resources to ensure compliance and punish non-compliance.
Education / raising awarenessSince consumers do not necessarily have perfect information about the damage demerit goods might cause, governments could increase available information via the education system (a longer-term solution) or public awareness campaigns (funded by demand), shifting the MPB curve to the left as shown in Figure 9.6. Revenue from taxes placed on cigarettes could ideally finance this spending on awareness campaigns. There is some doubt about the effectiveness of education and awareness campaigns in reducing consumption — many teenagers seem little affected by measures to put them off. Behavioural economics offers explanations: consumers may be aware of the risks but convince themselves they will not be affected, or they may reason that the consequences will be felt too far in the future to worry about.

Higher LevelConsumer nudges

Key concept: ChoiceBehavioural economics has shown that consumers do not always have perfect information and do not always make rational choices. They may be poor at judging the utility of a product when the benefits are enjoyed in the present, while the consequences might not occur for some time. Consumers are also subject to peer pressure, enjoy conforming to things that appear to be enjoyed by others, and have limited willpower. In short, consumers do not always have perfect information and do not always make rational choices. Governments have much work to do in encouraging people to make better choices, so that their own wellbeing, as well as the wellbeing of society as a whole, is improved.

The work of behavioural economics has been prominent in getting governments to use "consumer nudges" to encourage consumers to reduce their consumption voluntarily. In the case of cigarettes, research showed that cigarette companies were using innovative design of cigarette packages as a form of advertising to get around advertising bans. As a result, laws have been put into place requiring cigarette packs to be a plain, dull colour, with graphic images (rather than simple warnings) showing the dangers of smoking. This is designed to nudge consumers to choose not to smoke. The idea behind nudge theory is that consumers still have their freedom of choice, but they are nudged to choose differently. Not surprisingly, the cigarette industry has mounted massive campaigns against governments choosing this approach, arguing that it is taking away their freedom to pursue profits.

5. Negative externalities of production

These occur when the production of a good or service creates external costs that are damaging to third parties. This relates mainly (but not exclusively) to environmental problems, where economic activity results in clear threats to sustainability. In the absence of government intervention, profit-maximizing producers only take into account their private costs of production and are unconcerned about the costs that may impact upon other people, now and in the future.

Key concept: SustainabilityNegative production externalities are the core mechanism by which economic activity today can damage the environment and the wellbeing of future generations.

Consider a paint factory that emits fumes harmful to people in the area: there is a cost to the community that is greater than the costs of production paid by the firm. The firm's marginal private costs are below marginal social cost because there is an extra cost to society (e.g. respiratory problems for nearby residents) caused by the pollution. The firm will only be concerned with its private costs and will produce at Q1. It is not producing at the socially efficient output Q*, where marginal social cost equals marginal social benefit, so there is a market failure. There is a misallocation of society's resources: too much paint is being produced at too low a price. There is a welfare loss to society of the extra units from Q1 to Q*, because MSC is greater than MSB for those units — shown by the shaded triangle.

6. Common pool resources and the tragedy of the commons

Common pool resourcesCommon pool resources are typically natural resources, such as fishing grounds, forests and pastures, where it is very difficult, or very expensive, to exclude people from using them. Thus, they are considered to be non-excludable. In addition, if one person uses the good, it reduces the value of that resource to others — in this sense, common pool resources are said to be rivalrous. Because they are non-excludable and rivalrous, the main consequence is that, in the absence of effective management, common pool resources are inevitably degraded.
Key concept: Sustainability / ChoiceThe theory of common pool resources connects individual rational choice to society-wide sustainability outcomes — showing how rational self-interested behaviour at the individual level can generate collectively destructive outcomes.

In 1968, biologist Garrett Hardin wrote a paper entitled The Tragedy of the Commons, examining the consequences of the non-excludability of common resources. Hardin challenged the notion that rational decisions by producers and consumers acting in their own self-interest would result in the best possible outcomes for society as a whole.

The tragedy of the commons is best explained with Hardin's example. There are a number of cattle herders who can all take their cattle to graze on a large area of fertile pasture land that is open to everyone (open access). Acting rationally and wanting to maximize their own self-interest, each cattle herder will want to graze increasing numbers of cattle on the land, since every extra cattle will earn them more money. However, when all the other cattle herders think and behave in the same way, the number of grazing cattle grows, and overgrazing will result in soil degradation. The previously fertile land will become infertile and be of no use to any of the herders. No individual herder would take into account the eventual cost of the total destruction of the resource. The cumulative effect of all rational producers acting in their own self-interest results in a fall in social welfare, and is therefore a "tragedy of the commons".

Hardin's own words (1968)“Each man is locked into a system that compels him to increase his herd without limit – in a world that is limited. Ruin is the destination toward which all men rush, each pursuing his own best interest in a society that believes in the freedom of the commons. Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all.”

Some producers might see that this was happening and could voluntarily reduce their production to prevent the degradation of the resource. Yet this would give rise to the "free-rider problem": those who do not change their behaviour would benefit from those who do. In the absence of intervention, a profit-seeking producer would not do this if others were going to continue to reap the benefits.

There are ample examples of the tragedy of the commons. Commercial fishers, taking into account only their own private costs of production, have the incentive to catch as many fish as they can, ignoring the external costs. These might include, for example, the collapse of fishing stocks and the ultimate loss of livelihood to fishers in the future, along with many other ecological problems. Profit-seeking forestry companies want access to as much timber as possible, at the lowest private costs, ignoring the external (social and ecological) costs associated with deforestation.

Key concept: EquityThis is essentially the theory of negative externalities of production applied to common pool resources: producers, seeking to minimize their private costs of production, impose external costs on others. Not only do the actions of such groups hurt others in the present, they are damaging the ability of future generations to meet their needs, posing threats to sustainability (an issue of inter-generational equity).

7. International cooperation and agreements on common pool resources

Key concept: SustainabilityBecause problems such as climate change degrade genuinely global common pool resources (the atmosphere, oceans), international cooperation is essential to tackling them.

The Kyoto Protocol was the first main agreement made under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Its objective was to cut global emissions of greenhouse gases (GHGs). The treaty was negotiated in Kyoto, Japan in 1997 and came into force in February 2005. The goal was to cut GHG emissions by 5% between 2008 and 2012, relative to 1990 levels. The agreement mainly targeted advanced industrialized nations, with developing countries being asked to comply voluntarily. While the Kyoto Protocol had some success in reducing emissions from signatory countries, it was not successful for reducing global emissions. Nonetheless, the process created a foundation for the next international agreement, known as the Paris Agreement.

In December 2015, all countries of the UNFCCC met in Paris and agreed to an historic commitment "to combat climate change and to accelerate and intensify the actions and investments needed for a sustainable low-carbon future." The Paris Agreement's central aim is to strengthen the global response to the threat of climate change by keeping a global temperature rise this century well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, and to pursue efforts to limit the temperature increase even further, to 1.5 degrees Celsius.

Along with targets to reduce emissions, the Paris Agreement also aims to "increase the ability of countries to deal with the impacts of climate change". Within the agreement is a recognition of the interrelationship between human rights and climate change. Human rights include the rights to shelter, access to clean water and nutritious food. Global warming is threatening these human rights for millions of people, particularly in developing countries. The agreement includes means to financially support developing countries and the most vulnerable.

To meet the goal of limiting the increase in global temperature, countries have to reduce the amount of emissions released into the atmosphere, and increase the current concentration of carbon dioxide by increasing "carbon sinks" such as forested areas. Reducing emissions and increasing sinks is known as "mitigation".

Each country sets its own Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), which establish its mitigation goals and plans. Unlike the Kyoto Protocol, which set binding targets, the Paris Agreement gives each country the freedom to set its own NDCs. Each country updates its NDCs every five years, and each new target is supposed to show a higher level of commitment to mitigation, i.e. a lower level of emissions.

On the record“Climate change is a result of the greatest market failure the world has seen. The problem of climate change involves a fundamental failure of markets: those who damage others by emitting greenhouse gases generally do not pay.” — Nicholas Stern, Stern Review Report on the Economics of Climate Change, 2006. This links directly to the SDG 13 (Climate Action) global goal.

8. Government responses to negative production externalities and common pool resource threats

Tradable permits

Key concept: InterventionTradable permits are a market-based approach to reducing negative externalities of production and reducing the ability of people to degrade a common pool resource.
Tradable permitsIn a tradable permit scheme, a governing body sets a limit on the ability of users to access a resource. For example, if the common pool resource is a fishery, the governing body would set a maximum amount of fish that could be caught (the "allowable catch") that would allow the fish stock to remain sustainable. If the resource is a water supply, there would be a maximum amount of water that could be extracted. In the case of the atmosphere, there is a maximum amount of greenhouse gases that can be emitted. The governing body divides up and allocates the total amount into individual amounts, which are essentially "permits" to use or access the resource. Owners of the permit can use it themselves, or exchange it in a market — e.g. a fishing company allocated a certain catch could sell any remaining allowance to another fishing company if it did not need to reach its maximum.

A well-known example is the Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) set up under the Kyoto Protocol, also known as "cap and trade". Each member country accepted a target for limiting or reducing emissions. The level of allowed emissions was divided into "Assigned Amount Units" (AAUs), also known as carbon credits. If countries do not use up all their units — i.e. if they emit fewer gases than they are permitted — they can sell these excess units to countries that are over their targets. This is a market-based response because it creates a market for the emissions, and gives producers an incentive to adopt cleaner technologies so they do not have to buy carbon credits.

The Paris Agreement also includes mechanisms whereby nations can trade in "carbon credits" to offset their own carbon emissions — this offers developing countries the opportunity to enhance their own carbon sinks (e.g. forests) in their own countries and sell carbon credits to developed countries, financing developing countries' mitigation plans. Many individual states, countries or regions have also set up their own domestic emissions trading systems.

Note: what is actually being tradedCarbon dioxide is the main GHG, so people speak of "trading in carbon" and the "carbon market". Of course, no one is actually buying or selling carbon itself — they are buying or selling the right to emit carbon.

The effectiveness of an ETS depends on many factors, including:

  • the inclusiveness of the scheme — how many participants are committed;
  • whether or not the original allowances are sufficient to bring about the desired change;
  • the way in which the allowances are allocated, which may be problematic;
  • the possibility that companies may simply absorb the extra costs of emitting and not change their behaviour;
  • the ability of the administrators to accurately monitor emissions;
  • the strength of the "punishments" for excess emissions;
  • the possibility that the system may be manipulated by stakeholders.

Carbon taxes

Key concept: EquityA carbon tax follows the "polluter pays" principle, which argues that those who create pollution should be forced to pay for it — a way of internalizing the externality.
Carbon taxA "carbon tax" is a tax imposed when fossil fuels are burned. When users of fossil fuels are taxed for their carbon emissions, it is a way of making them pay for the external costs that are not included in the market price. A carbon tax is a market-based approach: a higher tax raises the price, but polluters retain freedom of choice — they can choose to reduce emissions, or carry on polluting and pay the price. A carbon tax is a Pigouvian tax aimed at eliminating the negative externalities associated with emitting carbon dioxide and forcing the producer to pay the full costs of production. To completely eliminate the negative externality, the government would have to be able to accurately calculate the external cost and set the tax to reflect it exactly. In practice, a carbon tax will not completely eliminate the welfare loss, but is more likely to move the market closer to the socially efficient level of output. Most carbon taxes are set at a relatively low rate to begin with, and then increased gradually over time to allow producers and consumers time to change their patterns of production and consumption.
Figure 9.8: The effect of a carbon tax (electricity)Axes: Price of electricity ($ per kWh) vertical; Quantity of electricity horizontal. Curves: MSC (top, the true full social cost including the carbon externality); MPC + carbon tax (e.g. +$10 per tonne of CO2), shown below MSC since the tax is typically set below the true external cost; MPC (the firm's original private marginal cost, below MPC + carbon tax); MSB (=D, ordinary downward-sloping demand). The original free-market equilibrium is at the intersection of MPC and MSB, giving quantity Q1 at price P1. Once the carbon tax is imposed, firms' costs rise from MPC to MPC + carbon tax, and the new equilibrium is at the intersection of (MPC + carbon tax) and MSB, giving a smaller quantity Q2 at a higher price P2. The true socially efficient quantity Q* (where MSC would intersect MSB) is smaller still than Q2, because the tax under-states the true external cost. A residual "welfare loss" triangle therefore remains between Q* and Q2, bounded by the MSC and MSB curves, and is labelled near the top of the diagram. On the horizontal axis, the three quantities appear in the order Q*, Q2, Q1 (left to right).

Benefits of a carbon tax:

  • To maintain profitability, electricity producers are incentivized to invest in newer, cleaner technologies that allow them to emit less carbon.
  • At higher prices, consumers have an incentive to reduce their consumption of electricity, e.g. by switching to more energy-efficient appliances.
  • Consumers may seek different sources of energy, such as electricity from renewable sources.
  • A carbon tax generates government revenue, which may be used to subsidize renewable energies to make them more affordable for consumers. Alternatively, many governments have promised to make carbon taxes "neutral", compensating people by reducing taxes in other areas, such as indirect taxes.

Despite the fact that additional taxes people are asked to pay are generally deeply unpopular, carbon taxes are favoured by many economists because they are a market-based approach and directly tackle a problem that all countries are committed to resolving.

Legislation and regulations ("command and control")

Key concept: Intervention / SustainabilityWhere governments can clearly see that economic activity creates negative externalities of production, they can enact legislation and set up regulatory agencies to prevent such impacts.

Whereas tradable permits and taxes are market-based responses, regulations are known as a command-and-control solution, because they strictly mandate the way producers behave. In some cases, an activity needs to be severely restricted or banned, because there is no amount of output that is acceptable; in others, output needs to be reduced, so either a market-based approach or command-and-control approach could be used (e.g. for single-use plastic bags, a government could ban them outright — command-and-control — or charge a fee for them — market-based).

Examples of regulations include: emissions regulations, laws to protect endangered species, recycling regulations, laws concerning the disposal of waste, regulations of pollutants discharged into rivers, and laws to limit the amount of plastic waste. To meet the standards, firms have to spend money, thus increasing their private costs — graphically, this is the same as Figure 9.8, but replacing "MPC + carbon tax" with "MPC + increased costs". The expectation is that such laws will incentivize producers to make different choices about how they produce their goods and services.

Concerns about the use of regulations include:

  • Establishing environmental regulations requires access to so much information that it is impossible for governments to realistically gather and process it all.
  • Regulations need to take into account the vast number of different circumstances facing different industries and ecosystems, inevitably resulting in over-regulation in some areas and under-regulation in others.
  • Increased regulation raises the price of firms' products and makes firms less competitive against foreign producers.
  • Regulations and standards have to be strictly monitored to ensure compliance, which is time-consuming and expensive.
  • Excessive environmental regulations concerning new technologies may discourage the development of technologies that could potentially achieve environmental benefits.
  • Governments are slow-moving and resistant to change and so cannot adapt when circumstances change.
  • Governments are subject to intense lobbying by stakeholders and may favour some areas of the environment over others because of the power of lobbies, or the desire to win votes. There may also be a problem of "regulatory capture", where government agencies responsible for regulating a particular industry are heavily influenced by representatives of the industry they are supposed to be regulating.

Subsidies for renewable energy

Key concept: InterventionGreater use of renewable energy sources, such as solar or wind power, will greatly reduce GHG emissions. If the costs of renewable power are very high, this may make its widespread use unaffordable. Furthermore, with rapidly growing populations in developing countries, there will be increasing demands for energy; if commitments to emissions reductions are to be met, this will require much greater use of renewable energies. Therefore, it is argued that governments should provide subsidies to producers, reducing their costs of production and making renewables more accessible — and international cooperation is needed to ensure these technologies are affordable to developing countries too (linking to SDG 7, Affordable and Clean Energy). There is much evidence that, as a result of technological improvements, the costs of renewable energy have fallen significantly; if this continues, it may help countries reduce GHG emissions without needing further government subsidies.

Collective self-governance (Elinor Ostrom)

The tragedy of the commons suggests that when users of a resource act in their own self-interest, the result will be destruction of the resource, harming everyone. Two possibilities are traditionally suggested to prevent this: the government takes over management of the resource and sets strict regulations for its use, or the land is privatized, with management determined by the private owner.

Key concept: InterdependenceElinor Ostrom, who won the Nobel Prize for Economic Sciences in 2009 for her work analysing the management of common pool resources, offered a different approach. Based on a tremendous amount of research into current and historical examples of community management of common access resources in Maine, Nepal, Indonesia and Kenya, she demonstrated that individuals and communities could work together without top-down regulation to develop rules and institutions to manage their shared resources in a sustainable and equitable manner.

One of the key points of Ostrom's work is that there is no "one size fits all" approach to managing a particular resource, and that since the communities who use the resource have the greatest incentive to ensure its sustainability, they should be the ones developing the rules for its management. Furthermore, the users of the resource are more likely to implement, monitor and enforce those rules, since they were involved in establishing the rules and are the ones to gain when the rules are followed.

Ostrom's insight“There is no reason to believe that bureaucrats and politicians, no matter how well-meaning, are better at solving problems than people on the spot, who have the strongest incentive to get things right.” — Elinor Ostrom, Nobel Prize for Economic Sciences, 2009.

Why is international cooperation so important?

Key concept: InterdependenceThreats to sustainability are global threats because they degrade common pool resources including the atmosphere, oceans, deserts, forests and climate. The global nature of the problem underlines the interdependence among countries and emphasizes the need for cooperation.

The Paris Agreement and the UN Sustainable Development Goals represent significant progress on how countries can and should cooperate to address sustainability problems. However, challenges remain:

  • Despite progress in reducing emissions through carbon pricing methods (emissions trading schemes and carbon taxes), the OECD reports that "governments need to raise carbon prices much faster to meet their commitments on cutting emissions and slowing the pace of climate change under the Paris agreement."
  • Not all countries are equally committed to the process, and at least one country has said that it will remove itself from the agreement on the grounds that the requirements will damage its economic growth. Other governments may behave similarly.
  • Short-sighted governments may be preoccupied with achieving high rates of economic growth and short-term political popularity, without considering the long-run impacts, weakening the political will to follow through on past commitments.
  • Both the Paris Agreement and the SDGs contain detailed information about how developed countries are to support developing and least developed countries (LDCs), both financially and technologically. However, the mechanisms for ensuring this support is provided are not binding, which may make it difficult for developing countries and LDCs to meet their own mitigation goals.
  • While countries have the freedom to establish their own Nationally Determined Contributions, there are concerns that there are insufficient means of monitoring and enforcing the extent to which countries meet their commitments.

Economics in action: the fast-fashion industry is now seen as a major contributor to the global waste problem and therefore a threat to sustainability — inexpensive clothing creates significant negative externalities of production and consumption, even though it is not classed as a demerit good in the same way as cigarettes or unhealthy food.

9. Public goods and the free-rider problem

Public goods are goods that would not be provided at all in a free market. Since they are of benefit to society, the lack of public goods in a free market is considered a market failure. Examples of public goods include national defence, flood barriers and streetlights on motorways.

Non-excludability and non-rivalryPublic goods will not be provided at all in a free market because they have two characteristics: they are non-excludable and non-rivalrous. If a good does not have both of these characteristics, it is not a public good. If a good is completely non-excludable and non-rivalrous, such as national defence and flood barriers, it is called a pure public good.
Non-excludableA good is non-excludable if it is impossible to stop other people consuming it once it has been provided. If a private individual erects a flood barrier to protect their house, other people in the area also gain the benefit, even though they have paid nothing. This is known as the free-rider problem: logically, no one will pay for a flood barrier, in the hope that someone else will do it. The good will not be provided at all by the free market. No profit-seeking private producer would have any incentive to finance the construction of a flood barrier without the ability to charge people for its use to recover the costs of production and make a profit.
Non-rivalrousA good is non-rivalrous when one person consuming it does not prevent another person from consuming it as well. If a person eats an ice cream, another person cannot consume that ice cream as well (rivalrous). However, if one person is protected by a flood barrier, it does not stop other people from being protected at the same time (non-rivalrous). The private benefit from a flood barrier would be very small relative to the cost, although the social benefit to all the people who were protected by it would be huge and probably greater than the cost. Thus, there is no incentive for a private individual to erect a flood barrier — a clear market failure despite a positive net social benefit.
Quasi-public goodsThere is much debate over what constitutes a public good. There is a category of goods known as "quasi-public goods", which have some, but not all, of the characteristics of public goods. For example, a "public" area such as a beach or a park might appear to be non-rivalrous, in that if one person is enjoying the area, up to a point others can also enjoy the area. However, one could argue that the space is only partly rivalrous; once the space becomes overcrowded, it is no longer possible for other people to enjoy the space.

Advancements in technology are making it increasingly possible to charge for things that were formerly difficult to charge for, which might make more goods "private goods" produced by private producers. Consider the case of roads, highways or motorways, which may be considered quasi-public goods. It is difficult to exclude people from driving on roads, so private producers would not have the incentive to build roads. Toll booths that prevent people getting onto a road can be used, but these clog up traffic, worsening other problems. Technology is now making it much easier to charge people all around the world to use roads through electronic road pricing schemes.

Government response: increasing the supply of public goods

Key concept: InterventionGovernments may try to reduce this market failure by intervening in a number of ways.
Direct provisionGovernments may provide the public good themselves. This is usually the case with things such as national defence, flood barriers, pavements, roads, street lighting and lighthouses. The use of taxpayers' money to fund provision spreads the cost over a large number of people, including those who would not be prepared to pay individually.
Public-private partnershipThe government might work in partnership with the private sector, providing the financing for the establishment of the good, and then allowing the private producer to run it. It may be assumed that private producers will only engage in such a partnership if profits are to be earned, which makes the issue of such partnerships rather controversial. Economics in action: public-private partnerships may be very important in enabling an economy to enjoy more and better infrastructure, but they are not without their criticisms.

10. Higher LevelAsymmetric information

Key concept: EfficiencyFor markets to function perfectly — that is, for there to be no market failure — we assume that all parties in every transaction have what is known as perfect information or perfect knowledge. In other words, if buyers and sellers come together in a market, there will be allocative efficiency if they are all fully aware of what products are available and the range of prices. In reality, this will very often not be the case, and so economic decisions are frequently made based on incomplete information, resulting in market failure.

The most common example of imperfect information is known as asymmetric information. This is where one party in an economic transaction has access to more information, or better information, than the other party. There are two types of asymmetric information market failure: adverse selection and moral hazard.

Higher LevelAdverse selection

Adverse selectionAdverse selection occurs when one party in an economic transaction has better information than the other — it may be that buyers have better information than sellers, or that sellers have better information than buyers. In both cases, the result is that poor choices are made from society's point of view.

Health insurance example: a common illustration of adverse selection occurs in health insurance markets, where the people buying insurance have better information about their own health than the insurance company. Consider the case of a heavy smoker buying health insurance: the insurance company does not know the person is a heavy smoker, and charges them the market price for the insurance. When the person becomes ill and requires the insurance company to pay the medical costs, the insurance company then has to charge other policy-holders higher prices to cover itself, which inevitably means that some people will choose not to buy insurance. In countries where the state does not provide health care, this is a significant problem: many cannot afford insurance because insurance companies charge higher prices to insure themselves against the higher-risk customers, and many people — particularly young, healthy people — do not buy insurance, perhaps foolishly assuming they will not need it. This is said to be a situation of "missing markets", as those people who should buy insurance would not be able to buy it, because the market price is distorted by the higher-risk customers. It is a market failure because the price of the good is influenced by the imperfect information held by one of the parties.

ScreeningScreening is one solution to the problems that occur as a result of adverse selection. Screening occurs when the less-informed party finds a way to get the other party to reveal the relevant information. In health insurance, the insurance companies are the "less informed party" and get customers to reveal information concerning risk factors by asking questions about, for example, age, weight, whether the consumer smokes, how much alcohol they drink, how active they are, and whether they have any pre-existing conditions. They can then offer different insurance plans according to the risk factors. The principle is that higher-risk people should pay higher prices, so that insurance companies can sell lower-priced policies to people whom they know face lower risks. Governments have a role to play in transactions between sellers and buyers, setting regulations to ensure that insurance companies do not violate the rights of consumers.

Used car ("market for lemons") example: adverse selection can also occur when sellers have more information than buyers. A commonly cited example is the used car market. Economist George Akerlof won a Nobel Prize in Economics in 1971 for his publication, "The Market for Lemons: Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism." For used cars, there are inevitably some good-quality used cars and some poor-quality cars (poor-quality used cars have the nickname "lemons"). However, uninformed consumers are unlikely to be able to tell the difference between the higher-quality and the lower-quality cars. Therefore, used car sellers have to charge an average price. Sellers who get an average price for their "lemon" are happy with such a transaction, as the true value of the car would be lower. However, sellers who possess high-quality used cars would make a loss, as people would not be prepared to pay a higher price for a car that might be a lemon! The end result is that there would be no market for good, quality used cars, and therefore a market failure. It is argued that the adverse selection results in "bad drives out the good" (this is similar to the health insurance market, where the adverse selection results in the unhealthy buyers, the "bad", driving out the healthy buyers, the "good", by causing the health insurance companies to charge higher prices).

SignallingIn cases where the seller has better information than the consumer, signalling is a possible solution. Signalling takes place when the party with more information can provide reliable information to the party with less information. In the case of used-car sellers, they can provide accurate signals to consumers about the quality of the used car, for example by giving warranties or guarantees to potential buyers, with a promise to take back the car or fix any problems. This signals to the consumer that the seller knows the car is worth the price.

Higher LevelMoral hazard

Moral hazardMoral hazard occurs when people have an incentive to alter their behaviour and take more risk when they know that the negative consequences of their risky decision-making will be borne by others.

Car insurance example: consider a previously safe driver who buys full insurance for their car, then changes their behaviour by starting to take risks while driving, knowing that if there is any damage to the car it will be paid for by the insurance company.

DeductiblesInsurance companies try to get around this moral hazard by implementing "deductibles". If the reckless driver in the previous situation causes $1,000 of damage to the car, they might have to pay the first $500 themselves as a "deductible". This acts as a disincentive to risky behaviour.

Labour market example: moral hazard can also be seen in the labour market. If a previously very good worker is promoted and guaranteed a fixed salary on a permanent contract, the worker might choose to do the minimum amount of required work, knowing the salary will be given to them regardless of the quality or quantity of work they do. This is why firms offer bonuses or performance-related pay to incentivize workers to work hard. Limited-term contracts are also a way of ensuring that workers maintain their productivity.

Banking / 2008 financial crisis example: one of the reasons for the financial crisis in 2008 was said to be the irresponsibility of the profit-seeking commercial banking sector in extending far too many loans to people whom they knew would not be able to pay back their debts, packaging these loans in other financial instruments and then selling them onto other people, knowing that there was a high risk that the loans would not be repaid. The inability of the people who had been encouraged to take out mortgages to pay back thousands and thousands of dollars of debt should really have resulted in the collapse of those banks that had lent money carelessly. However, many banks were "bailed out" by their governments — the argument being that the banks were "too big to fail", as their collapse would have led to an even greater economic crisis. Knowing that they will be bailed out, banks can take bigger risks than they would if they would be fully responsible for the consequences. This is a moral hazard.

Solutions to banking moral hazardOne solution would be for the government to set more regulations to prevent the risky behaviour in the first place; however, businesses often object when regulations are implemented that might damage their opportunities to earn profits. Alternatively, the government can bail out the banks, but impose heavy fines on those people responsible for the excessively risky behaviour; prior knowledge of the potential fines would ideally act as a disincentive.
Krugman's definitionMoral hazard is "any situation in which one person makes the decision about how much risk to take, while someone else bears the cost if things go wrong." — Paul Krugman, Economics Nobel Prize recipient.
Adverse selection vs moral hazardIn the case of adverse selection, the market failure occurs before the economic transaction has been made — one party has more information than the other, and this gives that party an advantage going into the transaction. In the case of moral hazard, the market failure occurs after the economic transaction has been made — the party with greater knowledge of the risk has the advantage following the transaction.
