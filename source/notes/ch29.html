29.1 Measuring Development: Single Indicators

Real-world issue: Why is economic development uneven?

By the end of this chapter you should be able to: explain the multidimensional nature of economic development; compare and contrast GDP and GNI per capita figures (including at PPP prices); compare and contrast health and education indicators; explain economic/social inequality indicators, energy indicators and environmental indicators; explain and give examples of composite indicators (HDI, GII, IHDI, HPI); discuss the strengths and limitations of approaches to measuring development; and discuss possible relationships between economic growth and economic development.

Because development is multidimensional, measuring it is extremely difficult. Single indicators are solitary measures used to assess development; they tend to be financial, health, education, or institutional measures.

Financial single indicators

GDP per capita vs GNI per capita GDP per capita = total economic activity taking place within a country (regardless of who owns the productive assets), divided by population. This includes the income of foreign companies producing within the country, but excludes the country's own companies' activity abroad.

GNI per capita = total income earned by a country's own factors of production, regardless of where those assets are located, divided by population. This excludes the income of foreign companies operating domestically, but includes the country's own companies' activity abroad.

Foreign direct investment (FDI) into developing countries has grown roughly 250% between 2000 and 2017. In 2017, FDI inflows to developing economies were US$671 billion (nearly double their outflows of US$381 billion); developed economies, by contrast, generate more FDI than they receive (2017 inflows US$712 billion, outflows US$1 trillion). Asia and Oceania accounted for more than two-thirds of all developing-economy FDI inflows.

Why GNI is preferred for developing countries If a developing country has a large amount of inward FDI, its GDP figure will be significantly higher than its GNI figure, because GDP includes profits that may well be repatriated abroad. Since repatriated profits do not remain in the economy, they cannot contribute to the country's progress — this is the main reason GNI figures tend to be used to measure the status of developing countries. For developed countries whose firms are heavily involved in outward FDI, the GDP figure will typically be significantly lower than the GNI figure.

Purchasing power parity (PPP)

Comparing national income figures across countries is problematic because goods and services do not cost the same everywhere — the purchasing power of the same amount of currency differs by country (e.g. a loaf of bread costs less in India than in Austria). To solve this, economists calculate a PPP exchange rate: an exchange rate that attempts to equate the purchasing power of currencies in different countries by comparing the prices of identical goods and services. The most widely used PPP rate is calculated by the World Bank.

Economics in action: The Big Mac Index ("Burgernomics") The Economist magazine calculates its own informal PPP measure based on the price of Big Macs in different countries, publishing it as a horizontal bar chart of "Price for a Big Mac in selected countries (in US Dollars)". Approximate figures shown: Switzerland 6.76, Sweden 5.28, USA 5.14, France 5.14, Italy 4.84, Spain 4.77, Germany 4.41, UK 3.96, Argentina 3.81, UAE 3.43, Japan 3.29, China 3.17, Russia 2.29, Ukraine 1.64. The chart illustrates that identical goods cost very different amounts in different countries when converted at market exchange rates — the basis for PPP adjustment.

Worked example: India's GNI per capita converted to US dollars at the official/market exchange rate is US$1,790 (2017 figure). But because prices are lower in India than in Vienna/the US, that $1,790 has much higher real purchasing power in India than it would abroad. Converting the Indian GNI per capita at the PPP exchange rate instead gives approximately US$6,950 (2017 figure) — still a relatively low income, but a far more realistic measure of the ability of people to meet their basic needs than the $1,790 official-rate figure. Typically, GNI converted at official exchange rates is considerably lower than GNI converted at PPP rates for lower-income countries.

29.2 Health, Education and Other Single Indicators

Key concept: Economic well-being Health and education indicators capture dimensions of well-being that pure income measures miss entirely.

Health measures

  • Life expectancy at birth: the average number of years a person may expect to live from birth, sometimes split into male and female figures. It is one of the three components of the HDI. It is influenced by quality of health care, provision of clean water/sanitation, nationwide education, adequate food supply, healthy diets/lifestyles, low poverty, and the absence of conflict/war.
  • Infant mortality rate: the number of deaths of babies under age one per thousand live births in a given year (excludes stillbirths). Like life expectancy, it is affected by health care quality, clean water/sanitation, food, and poverty levels.

Education measures

  • Expected years of schooling: the number of years of schooling a child of school-entrance age may expect to receive.
  • Mean years of schooling: the average years of schooling received by people in the economy aged 25 years and over.

Other single indicators

  • Economic/social inequality indicators: measures such as income and wealth distribution, pay inequality, asset ownership and access to credit.
  • Energy indicators: there is no clear agreement on defining "energy poverty" — broadly, the inability to maintain a home at an adequate temperature or to provide essential energy services for decent living conditions. Useful measures include access to electricity, the impact of energy bills on household budgets, and the ability to maintain an adequately heated home.
  • Environmental indicators: there is a huge range, depending on which environmental issue is considered.

29.3 The Human Development Index (HDI)

The UNDP's Human Development Index is a composite index bringing together three variables representing three basic goals of development: (1) a long and healthy life, measured by life expectancy at birth; (2) improved education, measured by mean years of schooling (adults 25+) and expected years of schooling (children of school-entering age); and (3) a decent standard of living, measured by GNI per capita converted at PPP US$.

HDI scale The three indicators are combined to give an index value between 0 and 1, with higher values representing a higher level of human development.

How useful is HDI information?

Before the HDI was established (1990), GDP per capita was the yardstick for measuring development, under the assumption that higher national income directly translated to higher development. The UNDP now regards GNI per capita as a more accurate income measure. Comparing a country's GNI-per-capita ranking with its HDI ranking gives useful insight into how well a country translates national income into development.

Worked comparison: Norway has the highest HDI value in the world but is ranked only 6th for GNI per capita — suggesting Norway converts its national income into development unusually effectively. The United States is ranked 11th for GNI per capita but only 13th for HDI. South Africa is ranked 90th for GNI per capita but only 113th for HDI (18 places below Tunisia's HDI rank), suggesting relatively weaker translation of income into development — a discrepancy that invites hypotheses about national policy choices (health, education spending, inequality) that affect development outcomes independently of income.

UNDP's own caveat The UNDP observes that one purpose of the HDI is to "re-emphasize that people and their capabilities should be the ultimate criteria for assessing the development of a country, not economic growth alone."

Is the HDI sufficient on its own?

Limitations of the HDI The HDI is far more effective than a simple GDP figure, but it is not sufficient alone: there are many aspects of development beyond the three included in the HDI, and a country's HDI is only an average figure that can mask significant inequalities within the country — between rural and urban citizens, between men and women, and between different ethnic groups. This is why several other composite indicators and a vast number of single indicators exist, each measuring one thing or attempting to capture a different dimension of development.

29.4 Other Composite Indicators

1. The Gender Inequality Index (GII)

Key concept: Equity Women and girls are all too often discriminated against in health, education, political representation and the labour market, which has a negative impact on their capabilities and freedom of choice — a central Equity concern in development.

The GII measures gender inequalities in three important aspects of human development:

  • Reproductive health, measured by maternal mortality ratio and adolescent birth rate.
  • Empowerment, measured by the proportion of parliamentary seats occupied by females and the proportion of adult females/males with at least some secondary education.
  • Economic status, measured by labour force participation rate of female and male populations aged 15 and older.

The GII is built on the same framework as the IHDI. It considers the position of women in 160 countries. The higher the GII value, the greater the disparity between females and males, and the greater the loss of human development.

Higher Level 2. The Inequality-adjusted Human Development Index (IHDI)

Key concept: Equity The IHDI is basically the HDI, but adjusted downward to take into account the human development costs of inequality.

Each of the three HDI components (life expectancy, years of schooling, income) is adjusted downward by its level of inequality. If a country had perfect equality, its IHDI value would equal its HDI value. Without perfect equality, the IHDI value will always be below the HDI value — the gap between the two represents the loss to human development caused by inequality. The IHDI is calculated for 151 countries.

5. The Inclusive Development Index (IDI)

Key concepts: Economic well-being, Equity and Sustainability The IDI is a project of the World Economic Forum's System Initiative on the Future of Economic Progress. It is an annual assessment of 103 countries' economic performance measuring how countries perform on eleven dimensions of economic progress, in addition to GDP.

The IDI has three pillars: Growth and Development, Inclusion, and Intergenerational Equity and Sustainability.

29.5 Beyond GNI: The Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI)

Since rising GNI does not automatically equate to rising welfare, economists have developed the Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI), used mainly in more developed countries, to measure whether a country's growth (an increase in the output of goods and services) has actually led to an improvement in people's welfare.

How the GPI is built Starting from GNI, the GPI adds a measure of non-monetary benefits, such as the benefits of household work, parenting and volunteer work. Because economic growth also generates many costs, an indicator of genuine progress must then deduct such costs rather than only adding to GNI. These deductions include estimates of:
  • Environmental costs such as air, water and noise pollution; loss of farmland, wetlands and forests; resource depletion; ozone depletion; pollution abatement.
  • Social costs such as family breakdown, crime, personal security (e.g. home security systems), and loss of leisure time.
  • Commuting costs.
  • Costs of automobile accidents.
Difficulty of measurement While such variables are, of course, difficult to measure precisely, the recognition that rising GNI does not equate with rising welfare means that welfare economists are constantly looking for ways to measure the true consequences of growth, so that both developed and developing countries can aim for growth that is equitable and sustainable.
