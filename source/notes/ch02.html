Classical Economics (18th Century)

Mercantilism (the pre-classical view)

Before the Industrial Revolution and the birth of capitalism, the prevailing view (mercantilism) held that a nation’s wealth was measured by the amount of gold and silver it possessed. Rulers aimed to accumulate wealth by maximizing exports (to earn gold/silver) and using trade barriers (customs, tariffs) to restrict imports and prevent gold/silver leaving the economy. Colonies abroad were one important way that countries accumulated more gold and silver, via controlled production and export of goods.

Adam Smith and The Wealth of Nations (1776)

In 1776, the “father” of classical economics, Adam Smith (a Scottish moral philosopher), published An Inquiry Into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (commonly The Wealth of Nations) — often called the first book on modern economics. It transformed how economic activity was understood and became the backbone of classical economic theory into the nineteenth century.

Smith’s central insightContrary to mercantilist theory, a nation’s wealth/prosperity is not based on its accumulation of gold and silver, but on the value of the goods and services it produces (in modern terms, its Gross National Product). Smith argued that government policy should prioritise maximizing the country’s output, focusing on production and productivity (output per worker).

Division of labour (the pin factory example)

One of Smith’s major contributions was identifying the benefits of specialization and the division of labour. He illustrated this with a famous story of a pin factory employing ten workers, involving about eighteen separate steps to manufacture a pin. Working alone, each worker could make between 10 and 20 pins a day. But when the eighteen steps were divided up, with each worker specializing in one or two steps, the factory could make 48,000 pins a day — an average of 4,800 pins per worker, about fifty times more productive than working individually. As output increases through further division of labour, productivity rises across the economy, leading to higher profits, accumulation of capital, better production technologies, and more output — and thus, contrary to earlier thought, wealth is increased by producing more (not merely by trade or by acquiring more gold/silver).

The invisible hand and laissez-faire

The invisible handSmith observed that when private producers are left alone to decide what and how to produce, they are guided by an “invisible hand”. Producers are not told what to produce by government or authority — they choose based on what consumers want. In pursuing their own self-interest (higher profits), producers benefit consumers too, because competition between producers gives them the incentive to develop better and cheaper products. The satisfaction/pleasure consumers get from consuming products is utility; when producers supply the goods that give consumers the most utility, they create jobs and wealth for the nation as a whole. Because the system is self-regulating, it reaches an optimum outcome without government intervention.
Laissez-faireSmith’s conclusion — that society as a whole prospers from the forces of competition and the invisible hand — is the basis of the laissez-faire theory. Laissez-faire translates literally from French as “let do”, and refers to the capitalist economic system where production, consumption and trade take place in free markets with as few regulations and as little government intervention as possible. Note: Smith did NOT advocate zero government intervention — in his view, government responsibilities include defence, universal education, providing essential infrastructure (roads, bridges), establishing legal rights, and punishing crime.

The labour theory of value

Labour theory of valueIntroduced by Smith and developed by other classical economists: the value, or price, of a good is the sum of the value of all the labour used in producing it. E.g. the price of a bushel of corn is determined by all the labour costs of the inputs used to produce it. Contrary to later theories, value was seen as determined primarily by factors relating to the supply of the product (production/labour costs), not by consumer demand.

Classical Economics Develops in the 19th Century

Classical economics was developed further through the work of David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus, John Stuart Mill and Jean-Baptiste Say.

David Ricardo — comparative advantage

Ricardo is well known for his work on international trade. Like Smith, he agreed countries should specialize in producing different goods and trade freely to increase global output. Where Smith focused on what became known as the “absolute advantage” of one country over another, Ricardo developed the law of “comparative advantage”. This theory remains the basis of most international trade theory taught today, and is developed further later in the course.

Jean-Baptiste Say — Say’s Law

Say was a Frenchman strongly influenced by, and very much in favour of, the writings of Adam Smith — supporting free markets and free trade.

Say’s Law“Supply creates its own demand” (a paraphrase of Say’s original writing) — the production of goods is itself the source of all demand in an economy. This links to the circular flow of income model: the economic activity of production creates incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) equivalent to the value of output; these incomes are then used to consume other goods and services. By supplying goods, producers effectively create the purchasing power for consumers to demand other goods; total demand in the economy comes about as a result of production.

Say’s theory was used to argue that there could not be sustained overproduction of goods within the economy, and that economic growth is achieved by focusing on increasing production as a means of generating further demand for products. Consistent with classical economics generally, Say’s theory (with its focus on the supply side of the economy) rejects government interventions that restrict the operation of free markets.

The Neoclassical School and the Marginal Revolution (from 1870)

The neoclassical school emerged in 1870 through the work of William Jevons, Léon Walras and Carl Menger — three economists working independently in three different countries, yet reaching similar conclusions.

Neoclassical vs classical: the value/price debateA key difference concerns what determines the value (price) of a good. Classical economists (Smith and others) supported the labour theory of value — value determined by costs of labour and other production inputs (a supply-side focus). Neoclassical economists rejected the labour theory of value, arguing instead that the value of a good is determined by the utility that consumers place on it — a demand-side focus. Neoclassical economists believed utility could be measured and given a monetary value, reflecting the school’s increasing use of mathematical analysis.

The marginal revolution and diminishing marginal utility

The work of Jevons, Menger and Walras introduced “marginal decision-making” (the “marginal revolution”): consumers decide whether to consume the “next” unit of a good depending on how much extra utility it brings them; producers decide whether to produce the “next” unit of a good depending on the extra cost of producing it.

Law of diminishing marginal utility (worked example)Imagine finishing a sports event on a very hot day. The first bottle of water someone offers you gives huge pleasure/satisfaction — high utility. After finishing it, you are offered a second bottle: still hot and thirsty, you drink it, but the extra (“marginal”) utility it brings is somewhat less than the first. Offered a third bottle shortly after, you might still drink it, but the additional satisfaction is considerably less than from the first or second bottle. This illustrates diminishing marginal utility: as successive units of a good are consumed, the extra utility gained from each additional unit falls — an important neoclassical concept.

This marginal revolution marked a significant shift from the classical school’s largely literary/philosophical approach to the much more mathematical, scientific approach of neoclassical economics. Although classical economists considered both supply and demand, they treated them separately and placed more weight on the supply side (due to their belief in the labour theory of value); neoclassical economists placed more emphasis on the demand side and developed complex mathematical functions to explain the interaction of supply and demand in determining prices and resource allocation in individual markets.

Demand and supply (preview)Demand is the quantity of a good/service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at different prices in a given period (the “demand side” = behaviour of consumers). Supply is the quantity of a good/service that producers are willing and able to supply at different prices in a given period (the “supply side” = behaviour of producers). These are developed in full later in the course.

Alfred Marshall and the first supply-and-demand diagram

Perhaps the most notable contribution to the neoclassical school came from Alfred Marshall, regarded as the actual founder of the neoclassical school. In his Principles of Economics (1890), Marshall was the first to present the visual supply-and-demand graphical model still used today to illustrate how prices are determined in a market.

Figure 2.1: The “first” supply and demand diagram (Marshall)Vertical axis: price. Horizontal axis: quantity, with points labelled O (origin), M, and H marked along it. A downward-sloping demand curve (labelled DD’) runs from upper-left to lower-right; an upward-sloping supply curve (labelled SS’) runs from lower-left to upper-right. The two curves intersect at an equilibrium point (labelled p). The area between the demand curve and the equilibrium price, above the price line, is labelled “Consumer’s Rent” (i.e. consumer surplus — the extra value consumers get beyond what they pay). Below the equilibrium price, areas are labelled “Producer’s Rent” and “Producer’s Expenses”, distinguishing the profit-like surplus producers earn above their costs from their actual costs of production. This diagram, still recognisable as the standard supply-and-demand cross used throughout modern microeconomics, was a landmark break from the classical school’s pages of prose — illustrating theories and models visually and mathematically rather than purely verbally.

Rational choice theory: the assumptions behind the neoclassical model

To build their models of producer and consumer behaviour, neoclassical economists made key assumptions, which continue to underpin much of contemporary economic theory: both producers and consumers are assumed to be optimizers seeking the best outcome for themselves — consumers are assumed to want to maximize utility, and producers are assumed to want to maximize profits.

  • Consumers are assumed to behave rationally: they act in a self-interested (in some interpretations, “selfish”) way.
  • Consumers are assumed to have full information about their options, and to be able to judge, instantly, the marginal utility of consuming a product.
  • Producers are assumed to be rational, aiming to maximize profits, and are assumed to be able to accurately calculate the marginal cost of producing an extra unit of a good.

Collectively, these assumptions are referred to in modern terminology as rational choice theory. This model is consistent with Adam Smith’s invisible hand: when producers rationally maximize profit (act in self-interest) by competing with each other, they are driven to produce the best possible products at the lowest possible prices, giving consumers choice — and, the model concludes, producing the best possible outcome from society’s point of view.

Political economy becomes “economics”Before the marginal revolution and the neoclassical school, the discipline was formally called “political economy” and its approach was largely philosophical. With its heavy reliance on mathematical models, equations and diagrams, neoclassical economics effectively allowed the discipline to be viewed as a science, and from this point the discipline became known by its modern term, “Economics” — part of a move to remove subjective, normative questions from the study and give it more objectivity and rigour compared with the earlier, more philosophical classical approach.

Karl Marx (1818–1883)

Best known not as an economist or philosopher but as a revolutionary communist whose works inspired the foundation of many communist regimes in the twentieth century, with major influence on the shape of the world in that century. Although originally trained as a philosopher, Marx increasingly turned towards economics and politics. He attempted to prove his theories using mathematics, feeling this was important since he claimed his book, Das Kapital, was a “scientific description” of the course history would take.

Marx saw capitalism as the free market system, being only one of a series of modes of production through history. He predicted there would be an inevitable breakdown of capitalism (for economic reasons), and that communism would be the natural end result — he issued the rallying cry, “the proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains… working men of all countries, unite!” Marx predicted that, eventually, society’s ability to produce would grow faster than its ability to consume, causing growing unemployment; he argued the free market could not be depended upon to serve the best interests of workers, and there would be a need for government to take over the means of production, in a system known as communism. Marx’s ideas continue to have a prominent role in economic and political debate today, and his views are typically associated with the “left” of the ideological spectrum.

The Early Twentieth Century: Neoclassical Dominance

In the first few decades of the twentieth century, the neoclassical faith in the free market’s ability to bring about the best outcomes prevailed. Since individual markets moved towards equilibrium when left alone to the forces of demand and supply, it was believed that the economy as a whole would reach a general equilibrium with full employment of resources, without government intervention. If there was ever over-supply in the economy (“gluts”), prices would drop, encouraging consumers to buy and eliminating the surplus — up-and-down cycles of economic growth would self-correct. The “orthodox”/generally accepted view remained that governments need not intervene — laissez-faire.

This theory was also applied to labour markets to explain why unemployment would not be a long-run problem. If there was unemployment (an over-supply of labour), it would be a short-run phenomenon: like any other market, if there were too much labour, the price of labour (wages) would fall, and producers would want to hire more workers at the lower wage — automatically eliminating the unemployment, with no need for government intervention.

Keynesian Macroeconomics

John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946)

Born in Cambridge, England, into a highly intellectual family, and highly educated at Eton and Cambridge. Although highly intelligent, Keynes did not focus exclusively on academics but found ample time for literary pursuits and political activities; he was well known for his involvement with the progressive literary Bloomsbury Group in London, which included intellectuals such as Bertrand Russell and Virginia Woolf. He joined the British civil service in 1906 — ironically, despite entering with a mediocre result in his economics exam, he later remarked, “I evidently knew more about economics than my examiners.”

After a short period with the civil service, Keynes returned to Cambridge and then went to work at the British Treasury (the government department responsible for government spending and taxation). He was a key representative of the British Treasury at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919, but was very much against the conclusions of the Conference, which required Germany to make massive reparation payments to the Allied countries after World War I. As a result, he resigned from the Treasury and wrote The Economic Consequences of the Peace, arguing it would be impossible for Germany to pay the amounts demanded and predicting the consequences would be very damaging — he turned out to be right.

The General Theory (1936) and the case for intervention

Keynes’ most well-known and relevant contribution to this course was published in 1936 in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money. The governing orthodoxy of the time was laissez-faire, holding that government intervention in the economy should be carefully limited and focused only on the supply side — i.e. creating the conditions necessary for maximum production (education to create skilled workers, infrastructure, laws to guarantee stability), but otherwise leaving the economy to demand and supply.

Key concept: InterventionKeynes changed the focus, putting forward the radical idea that it was demand, rather than supply, that determined the overall level of national income — and, crucially, that governments had a key role to play in managing the level of total demand (“aggregate demand”) in the economy. This ran directly counter to the neoclassical notion that markets automatically self-stabilize; leaving the economy alone, Keynes argued, could see market forces eventually restore full employment, but this could take an unacceptably long time, with governments expected to accept high and prolonged unemployment along the way — an obligation Keynes felt governments should not accept.

Keynes’ General Theory grappled with the problem of mass unemployment during the Great Depression. He observed that the persistent high unemployment of the 1920s was not going to disappear if left to market forces, as laissez-faire economists believed it would.

Keynes’ diagnosis and solutionContrary to earlier economists’ theorizing, Keynes argued the problem during the Great Depression was one of insufficient demand in the economy. Demand from consumers and businesses was not enough to buy up the total output of goods and services being produced — a “general glut”. With excess supplies, firms would lay off workers, who then had even less purchasing power to buy goods and services, resulting in further falls in demand for goods/services and even less demand for workers — a vicious downward spiral. Then, if workers demanded higher wages, producers would charge higher prices to cover their higher costs. Keynes’ solution: the government should intervene to increase total demand in the economy, by spending more money itself and lowering taxes to allow businesses and households to spend more. Increasing aggregate demand was advocated as the means of bringing the economy out of depression.

This ran counter to neoclassical thinking in two ways. First, it rejected the idea that markets automatically stabilize; Keynes argued it was an obligation for governments to intervene rather than leaving the economy alone. Second, at the time it was felt that all economic agents (households, firms, government) should live within their means and not spend more than they earned. Keynes instead proposed that, to stimulate the economy, governments should go into debt and “run budget deficits” — spending more than they earn from taxation to make up any shortfall — on the assumption that in good times, when the economy was growing well, government would take in more in taxes and spend less, so the debts could be repaid.

Demand management and the business cycle

Keynesian economics is also called “demand-side theory” or “demand management”. Observing that economies typically pass through business cycles fluctuating between rapid growth (low unemployment, rising inflation) and slow growth/recessions (high unemployment), Keynes advocated “counter-cyclical” government policies:

  • During a downturn/recession (high unemployment): governments should increase aggregate demand using expansionary fiscal policy (increasing government spending and decreasing taxes) and expansionary monetary policy, operated by the central bank (increasing the money supply and decreasing interest rates).
  • During a rapidly growing/booming economy (risk of rising inflation): governments should decrease aggregate demand using contractionary fiscal policy (decreasing government spending and increasing taxes) and contractionary monetary policy (increasing interest rates and decreasing the supply of money).

The rise and fall of Keynesian dominance

Keynesian economics gained widespread acceptance and became the dominant school of economic thought until the 1970s, when economic realities could not so easily be explained by Keynesian theories. Up until the late 1960s/early 1970s, Keynesian demand-management policies seemed to work, and countries employing them enjoyed strong economic growth with low unemployment and low inflation. However, around this time, many economies began to face the simultaneous problem of high unemployment and high inflation (known as stagflation), which Keynesian theory could not adequately explain. In fact, it was argued that governments using Keynesian policies were making the economic problems even worse. Keynesian theory had implied economies would either face the problem of high unemployment during a downturn or the problem of high inflation during an upturn — not both at once — and that by managing the level of aggregate demand, governments could minimize both problems. Stagflation cleared the way for a new school of macroeconomic thought.

Monetarism and New Classical Economics

Monetarism (Milton Friedman)

Monetarism emerged as the main challenge to Keynesianism in the late 1960s, made famous by economist Milton Friedman, who received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1976. Monetarists believe the main determinant of economic growth is the total amount of money in the economy, so their focus is mainly on monetary policy.

Monetarist view of inflation“Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon” (Friedman, The Counter-Revolution in Monetary Theory, 1970). Monetarists were most concerned with inflation, which they observed was caused by too much growth of the money supply. In their view, a country’s central bank should not use monetary policy to try to deliberately increase aggregate demand by increasing the money supply, as this would simply lead to higher and higher inflation — central banks should instead increase the money supply by a strictly controlled steady amount, consistent with the rate of growth of national income. Growing the money supply faster than output growth leads to “too much money chasing too few goods”, and prices rise rapidly.

In monetarists’ view, the best way to achieve economic growth is for the government to steer clear of demand management, and for the central bank to control the growth of the money supply.

New classical economics and rational expectations

New classical economics builds on the work of the neoclassical school and similarly argues that the economy will move automatically to a level of national income where all resources are fully employed. It revives the notion of rationalism through its theory of “rational expectations”. Applied to macroeconomics, this assumption leads new classical economists to conclusions about inflation similar to the monetarists: when governments employ expansionary policies, households and businesses will anticipate that inflation will occur and, acting rationally, will behave in a manner that actually causes wages and prices to rise. For example, if governments use expansionary policy, workers will rationally expect inflation to occur and so will demand higher wages in advance; if workers demand higher wages, producers will then charge higher prices to cover their higher costs — making the expansionary policy self-defeating and simply inflationary.

Economist Robert Lucas Jr. received a Nobel Prize in Economics in 1995 “for having developed and applied the hypothesis of rational expectations, and thereby having transformed macroeconomic analysis and deepened our understanding of economic policy.”

Economists in the new classical school of thought reach similar conclusions to the monetarists: governments should not try to manage the level of demand in the economy. Both schools go back to the classical and neoclassical economists in arguing that the only way government can promote economic growth is by using supply-side policies — policies creating incentives, such as tax cuts, for businesses to become more efficient and for workers to work harder.

Higher Level Behavioural Economics (Economics Meets Psychology)

The neoclassical approach makes the assumption that consumers behave rationally — that whenever a consumer chooses to consume a good or service, it is assumed they have carried out an internal cost–benefit calculation of the utility the product gives them relative to what it costs, in an intelligent, logical, and self-interested way.

Challenging rational choice theoryIn the real world, humans do not necessarily behave in this fully rational way and are not able to make instant cost–benefit calculations on every day-to-day choice. We do not necessarily make intelligent and logical decisions, we do not have perfect information about relative prices and the utility each product will give us, and we do not act in a purely self-interested way — we actually care about how our choices affect others. Challenges to the assumptions of consumer rationality gave rise to a branch of economics called behavioural economics, which incorporates insights from psychology and recognizes that consumer choices are governed by many factors inconsistent with neoclassical assumptions.

Richard Thaler is regarded as a pioneer in behavioural economics and won a Nobel Prize for his work in 2017. Given that humans are not necessarily rational, they do not always make the best choices about what to consume (or not consume). Behavioural economics aims to understand the decision-making process of actual human beings, rather than the so-called “rational” human beings assumed by neoclassical models — and uses this understanding to help consumers make better choices and to help governments design policies that will encourage consumers to make better choices.

Nudge theory

Nudge theoryIn the terminology adopted by Richard Thaler and his colleague Cass Sunstein in their book Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth and Happiness, consumers can be “nudged” to make choices voluntarily that are better for them and, indeed, better for society — without removing their freedom of choice. Example: when governments legislate that processed foods must have nutritional values printed on their labels, this “nudges” consumers towards making healthier eating choices, without banning any product outright.

Nudge theory has had a big impact on governments and organisations worldwide, which now nudge people in countless ways to help them make “better” decisions — contributing to improvements in people’s standards of living, health, communities, and the environment.

Criticisms of nudge theoryNudge theory is not without its critics. Any form of government intervention may be accused of taking away individual rights. There is also the concern that governments do not actually know what is best for people, and so cannot be trusted to choose how to nudge people. This is a good example of the wider debate between those who believe governments have an important role to play in the functioning of markets and those who believe market forces operate efficiently on their own. However, behavioural economists argue the very belief that markets operate efficiently on their own is itself based heavily on the assumption that consumers act rationally — and this assumption can easily be challenged. Their experimental work (drawing on insights from psychology) shows how carefully designed and tested interventions can be used to nudge consumers in the right direction, without taking away their rights to choose.

Reframing Economics in the Twenty-First Century

The linear economy vs the circular economy

There is increasing awareness that tremendous challenges to sustainability result from the fact that economic activity tends to take place within what is described as a “linear economy”. This has led to calls for a different approach — a shift from a linear economy to a “circular” economy.

Figure 2.2: The take-make-waste (linear) approachA simple horizontal flow diagram running left to right: an icon of the Earth/natural resources, an arrow labelled “Take”, leading into a stage labelled “Make”, leading into a stage labelled “Dispose”, ending at an icon representing waste/landfill. The diagram illustrates the traditional linear economic model: natural resources are extracted (“take”), turned into products (“make”), used, and then thrown away (“dispose”) into landfill sites or incinerated. This type of economy is responsible for the overexploitation and degradation of natural resources, and results in unmanageable accumulations of waste with accompanying health and environmental risks — a fundamental threat to sustainability.

Did you know? The Olusosun landfill site in Lagos, Nigeria, is the largest landfill site in Africa, with approximately 10,000 tons of garbage ending up there every day — much of it electronic waste (computers, printers, phones, TVs) arriving from around the world on container ships, driven by rapid technological change and constant demand for new products.

Figure 2.3: The circular economyA circular flow diagram with several segments arranged around a ring, connected end to end: “Take” → “Make” → “Use” → “Resell / Refurbish” → “Remanufacture” → back to “Take”, illustrated with icons of electronic devices moving around the loop. In contrast to the linear model, the circular economy is described as a “regenerative” and “restorative” approach: products are specifically designed to be long-lasting, and materials for new products come from reusing and recycling old products. There is much greater emphasis on the design, maintenance, repair, refurbishment and remanufacture of products, rather than disposal.

The principles of the circular economy are consistent with many of the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Governments worldwide are increasingly encouraging, or even requiring, the adoption of circular economy principles — e.g. in 2019 the European Union released a comprehensive report on the implementation of its Circular Economy Action Plan. From a business perspective, there are great potential gains from adopting circular economy models in terms of cost savings and compliance with national environmental regulations; many companies have adopted circular economy principles.

Kate Raworth and Doughnut Economics

Kate Raworth’s “embedded economy” model (introduced in Chapter 1, Figure 1.6) is another depiction of a circular economy, and its key feature is the interdependence that exists between the economy, society, and the environment. Economic activity that does not appreciate this interdependence will increase the challenges confronting the globe today.

Key concepts: Interdependence, Equity, SustainabilityRaworth argues that economics must take a radically different approach to deal with the challenges of the twenty-first century. She shows that policies based on traditional economic models have resulted in a narrow preoccupation with economic growth and a careless disregard for the negative consequences of “extreme inequalities of income and wealth coupled with unprecedented destruction of the living world”. According to Raworth, the goal must be changed: it cannot simply be to achieve economic growth or increases in economic output. “For the 21st century, a far bigger goal is needed: meeting the human rights of every person within the means of our life-giving planet. Instead of pursuing ever-increasing GDP, it is time to discover how to thrive in balance.” This is only possible, in her view, if one sees the economy as operating within society and within the Earth’s ecosystem.
Figure 2.4: The doughnut economy (Raworth, 2017)Two concentric rings forming a doughnut/torus shape. The inner ring boundary is the “Social foundation” — the basics of life that should be available to everyone: sufficient food; clean water and decent sanitation; access to energy and clean cooking facilities; access to education and healthcare; decent housing; a minimum income and decent work; and access to networks of information and social support. In addition, the model shows people should be entitled to gender equality, social equity, political voice, peace and justice. Falling short of the social foundation (i.e. being in the hole in the middle of the doughnut) represents human deprivation. The outer ring boundary is the “Ecological ceiling”, representing the Earth’s environmental limits/planetary boundaries (e.g. climate change, ocean acidification, chemical pollution, nitrogen and phosphorus loading, freshwater withdrawals, biodiversity loss, land conversion, air pollution, ozone layer depletion). Moving beyond this outer ring means putting the Earth’s ecosystems, and humanity’s ability to live within them, under great threat. The green doughnut-shaped band between the two rings is the “safe and just space for humanity” — where human needs and rights are met without exceeding the planet’s ecological limits. The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), with a target date of 2030, aim to achieve all the social foundation goals shown in the inner ring.

Economics in action: are we inside the doughnut?

Using data assembled by Raworth, humanity is currently falling short of the social foundation in many areas while simultaneously overshooting at least four planetary boundaries (ecological ceilings) — climate change, land conversion, nitrogen and phosphorus loading, and biodiversity loss. Selected statistics (as reported in Raworth’s 2017 book) illustrating shortfalls in the social foundation:

  • Worldwide, one person in nine does not have enough to eat.
  • One in four lives on less than $3 a day.
  • One in eight young people cannot find work; one in three has no access to a toilet.
  • One in eleven has no source of safe drinking water.
  • One child in six aged 12–15 is not in school, the vast majority of them girls.
  • Almost 40% of people live in countries where income is distributed highly unequally.
  • More than half of the world’s population live in countries where people severely lack political voice.

Despite many improvements against these metrics over time, at the time of writing the world was still not operating within the safe and just space of the doughnut — falling short of the social foundation in some areas while simultaneously exceeding the ecological ceiling in others. It is hoped progress towards the UN SDGs will move the world in the right direction.

Conclusion: Towards a Heterodox Economics

There have always been debates among different branches and schools of economic thought. Many students of economics have grown increasingly frustrated with the focus and assumptions of “mainstream” economics (the theories most commonly taught in high schools and universities). It has been argued that growing real-world crises — such as climate change and tremendous socioeconomic inequities — mean businesses and governments have been using narrow economic theories as the basis for their decisions.

A global movement of students calling for change in how economics is taught has grown, demanding more questioning of assumptions and inclusion of a wider variety of schools of thought. In this brief look at the origins of economic thought, only a few schools that have gained “mainstream” status were covered in depth; many other schools and approaches exist, including Developmentalist Economics, Feminist Economics, Complexity Economics, Institutional Economics, Islamic Economics, Cooperative Economics, and many more. It is important to be aware this multitude of approaches exists, and that economics is not just new classical economics. With growing awareness of the importance of circular/doughnut economics, this may represent the beginning of the “next paradigm” in economic thought.

Circular / Doughnut economicsKate RaworthEconomy is embedded in society and the environment (not separate from it); goal should be meeting human needs within planetary/ecological boundaries (the “safe and just space”), not maximizing GDP; circular economy replaces the linear “take-make-waste” model.Policy should be redesigned around social foundation and ecological ceiling goals, not growth alone.
