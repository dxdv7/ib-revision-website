How is national income measured?

National income can be measured in three theoretically equivalent ways. Because national output flows around the economy between households, firms, governments and the foreign sector (the circular flow of income model), the same total value can be captured at three different points in that flow.

Figure 13.1: The circular flow of income (referenced diagram)The textbook illustrates the three calculation methods using a numbered circular flow of income diagram (households, firms, and the wider economy). The arrows referred to in the text are: arrow (2) – the flow of factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) paid by firms to households, which is what the income method measures; arrow (3) – the flow/value of output produced by firms, which is what the output method measures; and arrow (4) – the flow of spending by all sectors (households, firms, government, foreign sector) on goods and services, which is what the expenditure method measures. In the standard circular flow model, households supply factors of production to firms (a real flow) and receive factor incomes in return (a money flow); firms supply goods and services to households (a real flow) and receive expenditure in return (a money flow). Because these three flows are simply different points at which the same total value of economic activity is measured, in theory all three methods should give the same final figure.

1. The output method

This measures the actual value of the goods and services produced in the economy. It is calculated by summing the value added by all firms in the economy. “Value added” means that at each stage of production, the cost of inputs is deducted so that inputs are not double-counted. Data are usually grouped by production sector: agriculture and mining (primary sector), manufacturing (secondary sector) and services (tertiary sector).

2. The income method

This measures the value of all incomes earned in the economy (wages, rent, interest and profit paid to the factors of production).

3. The expenditure method

This measures the value of all spending on goods and services, by summing spending across all sectors of the economy:

  • spending by households, known as consumption (C)
  • spending by firms, known as investment (I)
  • spending by governments (G)
  • spending by foreigners on exports minus spending on imports, known as net exports (X − M)
GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

Regardless of which method is chosen, in theory all three should give the same final figure, because they are all measuring the same underlying economic activity from a different angle. In practice, because the data used for each method come from many different and varied sources, inaccuracies creep in and the three figures do not perfectly match. Some of this is due to the timing of data gathering – figures often have to be revised at later dates once fuller information is collected.

DefinitionNational output = national income = national expenditure. These three totals are, in theory, always equal, because they are three different ways of measuring the same flow of economic activity.

GDP vs GNI, and nominal vs real values

Gross domestic product (GDP) vs gross national income (GNI)

GDP is the total value of all economic activity (output, income or expenditure) within a country’s borders, regardless of who owns the productive assets used. GNI is the total income earned by a country’s factors of production, regardless of where in the world those assets are located.

Example: if an Indian multinational company (MNC) operates and earns profits in Canada, this income is included in Canadian GDP (because the production takes place on Canadian land) but not Indian GDP. However, because the profits belong to an Indian-owned asset, they are included in India’s GNI, not Canada’s GNI (because Canada does not own the productive asset).

GDP vs GNIGDP is based on where production happens (location of the assets). GNI is based on who owns the assets that generate the income, wherever in the world they are located.

Property income from abroad is income earned by a country’s factors of production/assets operating in other countries. Net property income from abroad is the difference between income earned by a country’s assets operating abroad and income paid out to foreign-owned assets operating domestically.

GNI = GDP + net property income from abroad

Nominal vs real GDP/GNI

When comparing GDP across years, rising prices (inflation) will make GDP appear to rise even if there has been no real increase in economic activity. Nominal GDP is GDP measured at current prices (not adjusted for inflation). Real GDP adjusts nominal GDP for inflation, using a “GDP deflator”, giving GDP at constant prices. Comparisons over time must use real values so that price changes do not distort the picture.

Real GDP = Nominal GDP adjusted for inflation
Real GNI = Nominal GNI adjusted for inflation
Higher Level

Calculating real GDP/GNI using a price deflator

Price deflator = (Nominal GNI ÷ Real GNI) × 100

If you are given the inflation rate (rather than the deflator itself), the deflator fraction is calculated as 100 ÷ (100 + inflation rate), and this fraction is multiplied by the nominal figure to give the real figure.

Real GNI = Nominal GNI × [100 ÷ (100 + inflation rate)]
Worked example (Assessment advice box)The nominal GNI for country X in 2009 was US$750 billion, and in 2010 it rose to US$780 billion. In the year in question, country X experienced an inflation rate of 4%.

Q1: Calculate the real GNI for 2010.
Real GNI for 2010 = 780 billion × price deflator = 780 billion × (100 ÷ 104) = 750 billion.

Q2: Explain why the real GNI figure for 2010 is not the same as the nominal figure.
Because there was inflation of 4% in the year, the prices of everything in the economy, on average, went up by 4%. This means the value of goods and services produced in the economy in nominal terms would appear 4% greater than the previous year, even without any real increase in output. Once this inflationary effect is stripped out, the real GNI figure for 2010 turns out to be exactly the same as the 2009 figure (750 billion). This means the economy produced the same real volume of goods and services in both years – the real rate of economic growth was 0%.

GDP/GNI per capita

GDP or GNI per capita are the easiest national income statistics to use for comparing living standards, since they adjust for differences in population size.

GDP per capita = Total GDP ÷ population size
GNI per capita = Total GNI ÷ population size
Worked exampleThe projected GDP of China for 2019 was US$13,457 billion, significantly higher than that of Canada, with a projected GDP of US$1,733 billion – China’s total output is roughly seven-and-three-quarter times larger than Canada’s. However, once population is taken into account: China’s GDP per capita is US$9,633, while Canada’s GDP per capita is US$46,733. Thus, output per person in Canada is almost five times that of China, even though China’s total GDP is far larger. (Source: IMF World Economic Outlook Database, March 2019.)

In the case of GNI per capita, it is simply the total GNI divided by the size of the population – using GNI per capita rather than GDP per capita better reflects the income actually available to a country’s residents/citizens, rather than just what is produced on its territory.

Why are national income statistics gathered, and how are they used?

Compiling accurate national accounts is complicated and expensive. Every country has an organization responsible for calculating and reporting its national accounts, and the United Nations provides guidelines through the System of National Accounts (SNA) to help standardize this work internationally.

  • National income statistics act as a “report card” for a country – economic growth is a stated macroeconomic objective of governments, so people use the statistics to judge whether a government has been successful in achieving that objective.
  • Governments use the statistics to develop policies.
  • Economists use the statistics to build models of the economy and forecast the future.
  • Businesses use the statistics to forecast future demand.
  • The performance of an economy over time can be analysed (given sufficient historical data).
  • Because rising national income is often equated with rising living standards, national income accounts are used as a basis for evaluating a country’s standard of living/quality of life – although the textbook flags that the truth of this equation is something to question repeatedly throughout the course.
  • National income statistics are used as a basis for comparing different countries.

Limitations of national income statistics

National income data have three broad types of limitation: accuracy of the data itself, appropriateness for making comparisons between countries, and appropriateness for drawing conclusions about living standards (economic well-being).

Accuracy problems

  • Inaccuracies: data come from a wide range of sources (tax claims by households and firms, output data, sales data) and inevitably contain inaccuracies, leading to imbalances between the output, income and expenditure totals. Figures become more accurate with a time lag as they are revised with additional data. Statisticians in more developed countries can generally be assumed to produce more reliable data; the UN SNA works with all countries to improve data-gathering methods and thereby improve the validity of international comparisons.
  • Unrecorded/under-recorded economic activity – informal markets: national accounts can only record economic activity that is officially recorded.
    • Do-it-yourself work: if you paint your own house, this is not included in GDP; but if you pay a company to do it, the identical output raises GDP. This especially affects developing countries where subsistence farmers produce and consume their own food, which is unrecorded or hard to value – GDP is likely undervalued and comparisons become difficult.
    • The hidden/shadow economy: unrecorded activity that is illegal (e.g., drug trafficking), or legal activity done illegally/without permits (e.g., unlicensed foreign workers cleaning, building or working in restaurants), or activity deliberately hidden to evade tax. High indirect taxes on cigarettes give smokers an incentive to buy them illegally to avoid tax. High direct (income) taxes give people an incentive to under-declare income – e.g., a lawyer might declare some official income but do extra work for cash and not declare it.

For example, Norway’s official GDP might be underestimated by 18.7% due to its hidden economy, while the US is underestimated by only 8.6%. It is argued that countries with higher tax burdens tend to have a higher amount of hidden economic activity. Because the size of the hidden economy differs across countries, and because the figures are only estimates, comparing GDP between countries on this basis may be problematic.

Appropriateness for measuring living standards / economic well-being

  • External costs: GDP does not account for the costs of resource depletion. Cutting down trees increases GDP (through the value of timber sold) but there is no deduction for the loss of the trees themselves. GDP figures also make no deduction for negative externalities such as air/water pollution or traffic congestion, even though these costs almost certainly reduce quality of life as GDP rises.
  • Other quality-of-life concerns: GDP can grow because people are working longer hours or taking fewer holidays – higher incomes may not translate into a higher standard of living. GDP accounting does not include free activities such as volunteer work or people caring for the elderly or children at home – activities that could improve society but might even be discouraged in the pursuit of GDP growth.
  • Composition of output: a large share of a country’s output might be goods that do not directly benefit consumers, such as capital goods or military goods. If so, it is hard to argue that a higher GDP automatically means higher living standards.
Key concept: SustainabilityFlagged where GDP fails to deduct the depletion of natural resources or environmental damage – growth measured this way may not be sustainable.
Key concept: Economic well-beingFlagged throughout this limitations discussion – the central tension is that GDP measures the size of economic activity, not whether that activity actually improves people’s well-being.

Economics in action: New Zealand became one of the first countries to explicitly move away from using GDP alone as its measure of success, instead building its national budget around wellbeing and happiness indicators.

The business cycle

DefinitionThe business cycle (or trade cycle) is the periodic fluctuation in economic activity, measured by changes in real GDP, that developed economies generally experience – periods of rising growth followed by periods of slowing and even falling growth.
Figure 13.2: The standard business cycleAxes: Real GDP on the vertical axis, Time on the horizontal axis. The curve is a repeating wave shape rising from a trough, through a recovery phase, up to a boom (peak), then falling through a recession (contraction) back down to another trough, and so on. The phases marked along the wave are, in order: Recovery → Boom → Recession → Trough → Recovery again. The rising portions of the wave are also labelled “Expansion” and the falling portions “Contraction”.

The four phases

  • Recovery: economic expansion occurs, with GDP increasing at a rising rate. This is largely driven by an increase in aggregate (total) demand, as households and businesses are encouraged to spend more. To meet rising demand, firms increase output and take on more workers, so unemployment falls. The newly employed workers spend their incomes on new goods and services, so household spending increases even more. As increasing aggregate demand pushes an economy towards “booming”, this is likely to result in an increase in average prices. As an economy’s rate of growth of GDP exceeds its potential output, inflationary pressure builds up, and economic policy makers are likely to react by trying to slow the growth of the economy – this may cause a fall in total demand, marking the beginning of the recession part of the cycle.
  • Recession: defined as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth, i.e., actually falling GDP. During a recession, falling aggregate demand leads firms to lay off workers, so unemployment rises. As more people become unemployed, there is even less spending, resulting in lower rates of inflation, or even deflation.
  • Trough: the point at which the contraction comes to an end. Output cannot fall forever, because there will always be some people with jobs maintaining a given level of consumption, foreigners will continue to demand some exports, governments will continue to spend (running budget deficits), and people will use savings to finance consumption. Additionally, low demand for money for investment will result in lower interest rates, making borrowing for investment more attractive. This stimulates a pickup in aggregate demand, and the economy enters the recovery phase – the cycle repeats.
Note: a fall in GDP vs a fall in GDP growthA decrease in GDP (the economy actually shrinking) is not the same as a decrease in the rate of GDP growth (the economy still growing, just at a slower rate). Many students confuse the two. Worked example: Luxembourg’s year-on-year GDP growth rate fell from 3.5% in 2002 to 2% in 2003 – this does not mean output was smaller in 2003 than in 2002, only that GDP grew more slowly than the year before. From 2003 to 2004 the growth rate increased sharply to about 4.2%, then slowed slightly the following year to about 4%. In every one of these years, Luxembourg’s actual GDP was still rising.

Long-term growth trend and output gaps

Economies tend to go through periodic fluctuations in real GDP around their long-term growth trend, or long-term potential output. This is shown by plotting the periodic fluctuations (the actual output line) against the economy’s long-term trend (a steady increasing line).

Figure 13.3: Long-term output and output gapsAxes: Real GDP on the vertical axis, Time on the horizontal axis. Two lines are drawn: a straight, steadily rising line labelled “Long-term trend” (this represents the growth rate the economy can sustain over time, i.e., potential output – not to be confused with sustainable development), and a wavy line labelled “Actual output” that oscillates above and below the trend line, crossing it repeatedly. Point A is marked where actual output is below the trend line (a negative output gap) and point B is marked where actual output is above the trend line (a positive output gap).

The difference between actual and potential output is the output gap. At point A there is a negative output gap: the economy is producing below its trend and unemployment is likely to be a problem. At point B there is a positive output gap: the economy is producing above its trend, i.e., beyond capacity, and inflation is likely to be a problem.

The short-run inflation/unemployment trade-offThis illustrates an important feature of economies: in the short run there can be a “trade-off” between inflation and unemployment. When operating below potential, unemployment tends to be the problem; when operating above potential, inflationary pressure (rising rate of inflation) tends to be the problem.

Economists have long studied the causes of business cycles and hypothesised about the length and magnitude of a “typical” cycle, though there are no straightforward answers. One theory is that a country’s business cycle may be linked to its electoral cycle: a government might use expansionary policies to stimulate the economy and lower unemployment just before an election, then put less popular contractionary policies in place after being re-elected. A criticism of such cycles is that they can widen the magnitude of the cycle, producing higher levels of unemployment and inflation than there would be if the economy were simply left on its own.

Other measures of economic well-being

Because GDP has significant limitations as a measure of well-being, several composite indicators have been developed as alternatives or supplements.

1. OECD Better Life Index

The OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) was established in 1961; its mission is to promote policies that improve the economic and social well-being of people around the world. It has 35 member countries, comprising most of the world’s developed economies, plus “key partners” such as Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Russia and South Africa.

The OECD Better Life Index allows comparison of well-being across countries based on 11 topics the OECD identifies as essential to well-being, covering material living conditions (housing, income, jobs) and quality of life (community, education, environment, governance, health, life satisfaction, safety and work-life balance).

2. Happiness Index (World Happiness Report)

Compiled by the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network and published annually in the World Happiness Report. The 2018 report ranked 156 countries by happiness levels, and 117 countries by the happiness of their immigrants. Data are drawn from the Gallup World Poll and other sources such as the World Values Survey. The main variables considered in 2018 were: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity and perceptions of corruption.

3. Happy Planet Index (HPI)

Key concept: SustainabilityThe HPI measures sustainable well-being – how well countries achieve long and happy lives while taking sustainability (efficient use of environmental resources) into account.

The HPI combines four elements to show how efficiently residents of different countries are using environmental resources to lead long, happy lives.

HPI = (Wellbeing × Life expectancy × Inequality of outcomes) ÷ Ecological Footprint
  • Wellbeing: how satisfied residents of each country say they feel with life overall, on a scale from 0 to 10, based on data collected as part of the Gallup World Poll.
  • Life expectancy: the average number of years a person is expected to live in each country, based on UN data.
  • Inequality of outcomes: the inequalities between people in a country in terms of how long they live and how happy they feel, based on the distribution in each country’s life expectancy and wellbeing data; expressed as a percentage.
  • Ecological Footprint: the average impact each resident of a country places on the environment, based on data from the Global Footprint Network; expressed using a standardized unit – global hectares (gha) per person.
