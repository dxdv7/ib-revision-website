What is a Market, and What is Demand?

This chapter looks at the "market mechanism" – the way that producers and consumers interact with each other to direct resources to meet the needs and wants of people in an economy. The standard models used in this analysis (demand and supply) are based on the neoclassical school of thought, though the assumptions behind these models can be, and are, questioned (see the Higher Level sections on behavioural economics below).

What is a market?

Definition: MarketA market is where buyers and sellers come together to carry out an economic transaction. Markets may be physical places where goods and services are exchanged for money, but transactions increasingly happen in other ways too – online, through credit cards, or via money transfers.
  • Product markets – where goods and services are bought and sold.
  • Factor markets – where factors of production are bought and sold, such as the labour market.
  • Stock markets – where shares in companies are bought and sold.
  • International financial markets – where international currencies are traded, such as the foreign exchange market.

At the core of standard market theory are the concepts of demand and supply.

What is demand?

Definition: DemandDemand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at different prices in a given time period. For example, a group of people may buy 150 cans of a soft drink at $1.20 each, each afternoon – their demand for soft drinks at a price of $1.20 would be 150 units per afternoon.
Willingness AND abilityThe important phrase in the definition is "willingness and ability". It is not enough for consumers to be willing to purchase a good or service – they must also have the financial means to buy it, i.e. the ability to buy. This is known as effective demand. Wanting a motorcycle but not having the money to buy one is not effective demand.

Reminder: throughout demand and supply analysis, economists use the assumption of ceteris paribus ("all other things being equal"). This means that when we look at the effect of a change in one determinant of demand, we assume all the other determinants stay unchanged, so we can isolate the effect of the one factor we are interested in.

The Law of Demand

The Law of DemandAs the price of a product falls, the quantity demanded of the product will usually increase, ceteris paribus. This is often expressed simply as "the demand curve normally slopes downwards".

Figure: A demand curve for soft drinks (Fig 3.1)Vertical axis: Price of soft drinks ($). Horizontal axis: Quantity of soft drinks (units). The curve, labelled D (Demand), slopes downward from upper-left to lower-right. Demand curves are technically convex to the origin, but for ease of analysis economists usually draw them as straight lines (while still calling them "curves"). A fall in the price of soft drinks from $1.20 to $0.80 causes an increase in the quantity demanded of soft drinks from 150 to 225 cans – this is shown as a movement along the existing curve, not a shift of it.
Change in quantity demanded vs change in demandA change in the price of the product itself causes a change in the quantity demanded – a movement along the existing demand curve. This is different from a change in demand, which refers to an actual shift of the whole demand curve to the left or right, caused by a change in one of the non-price determinants of demand.

Why does a fall in price raise quantity demanded? Common sense: this is what drives promotions and sales – more consumers buy a product when the price drops. Also, since additional units of a product bring consumers less extra utility (satisfaction) as they consume more (the law of diminishing marginal utility), the price has to fall to give them the incentive to buy more.

The Non-Price Determinants of Demand (Shifts of the Demand Curve)

Whenever one of the determinants below changes (with everything else held constant – ceteris paribus), the whole demand curve shifts to the left or right. This is called a change in demand.

1. Income

Normal goods: As income rises, demand for most goods also rises – the demand curve shifts right. The size of the shift depends on the good itself: a small increase in income may cause only a small shift in demand for salt, but a much larger shift in demand for cinema tickets.

Figure: The demand for air travel (Fig 3.2)Vertical axis: Price of air travel ($). Horizontal axis: Quantity of air miles. Two parallel downward-sloping lines, D and D1, with D1 to the right of D. An increase in income shifts the whole demand curve for air travel to the right, from D to D1, so more air travel is demanded at every price level.

Inferior goods: If a product is "inferior", demand for it falls as income rises, because consumers switch to higher-priced substitutes. Examples: cheap wine, "own brand" supermarket detergents. As income rises, the demand curve for the inferior good shifts left. Beyond a certain income level, the consumer buys only the higher-priced goods and demand for the inferior good may disappear altogether.

2. The price of related goods

Products can be substitutes, complements, or unrelated to each other.

Substitutes: a change in the price of one leads to a change in demand for the other (in the same direction).

Figure: The demand for chicken and beef (Fig 3.3)Two separate diagrams, each with Price ($) on the vertical axis and Quantity (kg) on the horizontal axis. Chicken panel: a fall in the price of chicken from p to p1 causes a movement along the chicken demand curve, increasing quantity demanded from q to q1. Beef panel: the price of beef stays at p (unchanged), but because chicken is now cheaper, some consumers switch away from beef – the whole demand curve for beef shifts left from D to D1, so demand for beef falls from q to q1 at the unchanged price p. Conclusion: an increase in the price of a substitute would instead shift the other good's demand curve to the right.

Complements: products often purchased together, e.g. printers and ink cartridges, or games consoles and games.

Figure: The demand for games consoles and games (Fig 3.4)Two diagrams, Price ($) vertical, Quantity horizontal. Consoles panel: a fall in the price of consoles from p to p1 is a movement along the console demand curve, increasing quantity demanded from q to q1. Games panel: the price of games has not changed, but because consumers now own more consoles they need more games to use with them – the whole demand curve for games shifts right, from D to D1, increasing demand from q to q1 at the same price. Conversely, a rise in the price of one complement shifts demand for the other complement to the left.

Unrelated goods: a change in the price of one good has no effect on demand for the other (e.g. toilet paper and pencils).

3. Tastes and preferences

Consumer tastes have a powerful influence on demand. When tastes shift in favour of a product, more is demanded at every price (demand curve shifts right); when a product becomes less popular, demand falls (curve shifts left). Forces behind changing tastes include marketing and advertising, peer pressure, and media influence.

Figure: The demand for skateboards (Fig 3.5)Price of skateboards ($) on the vertical axis, quantity of skateboards on the horizontal axis. An advertising campaign or a televised world skateboarding championship increases the popularity of skateboarding, shifting the whole demand curve to the right, from D to D1 – more skateboards are demanded at every price.
Economics in action: Blue Planet 2 and plasticsThe BBC's 2017 documentary Blue Planet 2, narrated by David Attenborough and watched by 62% of the UK population, highlighted the damage of plastic pollution to oceans. Increased public awareness reduced demand for single-use plastic water bottles (a leftward shift) and increased demand for reusable bottles (a rightward shift) – a real-world example of a taste-driven demand shift.

4. Future price expectations

If consumers expect a product's price to rise in the future, they demand more of it now, taking advantage of the current lower price – demand shifts right in the present. Example: an announced future tax increase on cigarettes from a certain date typically causes bulk-buying of cigarettes beforehand, shifting the current demand curve for cigarettes to the right.

If consumers expect price to fall in future, they demand less now, postponing purchases – demand shifts left in the present. Example: "Black Friday" – in the weeks before Black Friday, demand for electrical products falls as people postpone their purchases to benefit from the anticipated lower prices, shifting the current demand curve for those products to the left.

5. Number of consumers

An increase in the number of consumers of a product shifts demand to the right. This often relates to population size and demographic change – if a country's population is growing, demand for most products increases and their demand curves shift right. Changes in a population's age structure also matter: as the proportion of older people rises in many European countries, demand curves shift right for products targeted at senior citizens, such as certain holidays, walking frames, and mobility scooters.

An exception to the Law of Demand: Veblen goods

Theory of Knowledge: are economic "laws" like scientific laws?In the physical sciences, laws (like gravity) have no exceptions – if an exception appeared, the law would be scrapped. Economic "laws", such as the Law of Demand, are not this rigid: economist Thorstein Veblen identified a situation in which quantity demanded rises as price rises – a genuine exception, not a flaw that invalidates the whole law.

In The Theory of the Leisure Class, Veblen described how some products become more popular as their price rises, driven by conspicuous consumption: people gain satisfaction from being seen to consume expensive products by other people. Veblen wrote that "failure to consume in due quantity" becomes "a mark of inferiority and demerit". As the price of a Veblen good rises, it can gain "snob value" (e.g. a luxury handbag), so people with high incomes buy more of it – it becomes "a good of ostentation".

Figure: The demand for a Veblen good (Fig 3.8)Price ($) on the vertical axis, quantity of the Veblen good on the horizontal axis. At low prices the curve behaves normally, sloping downward (quantity demanded falling as price rises). But as price continues to rise, the curve bends and starts sloping upward – quantity demanded now rises as price rises – because the product is achieving "snob value status", and further price rises attract more buyers seeking to be seen consuming it.

Background: Thorstein Veblen (1857–1929), American economist and sociologist of Norwegian immigrant descent, argued that human nature is shaped by culture rather than simple rational self-interest, challenging the simplicity of "homo economicus" well before behavioural economics became mainstream. His key works were The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899) and The Theory of Business Enterprise (1904). Unlike Marx, Veblen believed the working class did not want to overthrow the upper class, but rather aspired to move up the class structure, with the upper class setting the aspirational example.

Higher Level

How Economists Explain the Law of Demand: Income and Substitution Effects

When the price of a product falls, there are two reasons why quantity demanded increases:

1. The income effectA fall in price increases people's "real income" – the amount their income will actually buy. With more real income to spend, people are likely to buy more of the product, partly explaining the rise in quantity demanded.

Worked example: someone buys 14 caffe lattes a week at $2.95 each, spending $41.30 per week. If the price falls to $2.45, they save $0.50 per cup – $7.00 per week. Their "real income" has effectively increased by $7.00 per week, which they may now spend on buying more coffees, increasing the quantity demanded.

2. The substitution effectConsumers receive a certain amount of satisfaction (utility) from consuming a product. When the price of a product falls, people gain the same utility as before but pay less for it, so their ratio of satisfaction to price improves. The product becomes relatively more attractive than other products whose prices have not changed, so consumers substitute towards it, buying more of it in place of other goods.

Worked example: a cup of caffe latte gives 10 utils (a unit of satisfaction) and costs $1.95 – a ratio of 1 util to $0.195. If the price falls to $1.45, the ratio improves to 1 util to $0.145. The consumer now pays less for the same satisfaction, making the coffee "a better deal", so they buy more of it.

The overall effect of a fall in price on quantity demanded is, in the end, a combination of the income effect and the substitution effect.

Higher Level

The Assumptions Behind the Theory of Demand: Rational Consumer Choice

Economic models rest on assumptions that must be made to generate testable hypotheses. In the standard neoclassical model, consumers ("agents") are assumed to behave rationally: they always seek to maximize their own utility, they act only in their own self-interest (not considering others), and they have access to all the relevant information about their choices (perfect information). This idealized rational consumer is known as homo economicus. When faced with choices, homo economicus makes intelligent, logical, well-considered decisions that give them the most utility.

Key concept: ChoiceThe question of how realistic the "rational consumer" model of choice actually is – and how humans really make choices – is a central illustration of the IB key concept of Choice.

How realistic are these assumptions?

Perfect information vs. imperfect informationThe assumption of perfect information implies all economic agents have equal, complete access to information about the price and quality of all products at the same time. In reality: (1) information asymmetries exist – consumers, producers and governments each have different levels of information; (2) even where all information is technically available, humans face cognitive limits on how much they can process (worsened today by "information overload" from the internet's near-infinite information). In the real world, consumers make decisions based on imperfect information.
Bounded rationalityConsumers' rationality is limited by the time they have and their cognitive ability to weigh up every option – a concept known as bounded rationality.
Bounded selfishnessThe standard model assumes consumers act only in their own self-interest. In reality, humans do care about others – we volunteer, donate to charities, and buy Fairtrade products because we care about producers' wellbeing. Bounded selfishness is the term used to explain that humans do not always act purely in self-interest, as the neoclassical model assumes.
Bounded self-controlRational agents are assumed to have perfect willpower to act on their rational decisions. In reality, consumers are often unable to do this, giving in to temptation – a tendency captured by the term bounded self-control.

Behavioural Economics

Richard Thaler, a pioneer of behavioural economics who won the Nobel Prize in 2017, refuted the idea that consumers are "rational maximizers" – mathematical machines carefully calculating every decision to maximize utility. He observed that standard economics studies the behaviour of "homo economicus", a person who does not actually exist – we are all "homo sapiens". Thaler uses the term "Econs" for the idealized homo economicus, and "Humans" for real people.

The Dual System Model

Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky developed the "dual system model" to explain how humans actually make decisions, using two different systems of thinking. Richard Thaler (in his book Nudge) calls System 1 the "Automatic System" and System 2 the "Reflective System".

Figure: The Automatic System and the Reflective System (Fig 3.10)Two contrasting word-clouds. System 1 (Automatic): described by the words gut instinct, uncontrolled, intuitive, effortless, fast, immediate, impulsive. Used for tasks like answering "what is three plus four?", walking a familiar route home, speaking your mother tongue, or routine grocery shopping. System 2 (Reflective): described by the words controlled, calculating, deliberate, slow, logical, unemotional. Used for tasks like answering "what is 1989 times 31?", choosing IB subjects, or holding a conversation in a second language you are not fluent in.

Neoclassical economists assume rational consumers always use reflective (System 2) thinking, but this is not realistic. For important, complex decisions, people do tend to use elements of the Reflective System and take their time. However, at other times the Automatic System takes over, which can lead to poor decision-making – particularly when a short-term decision is made too quickly without considering long-term effects (e.g. accepting a doughnut while trying to diet: the short-term benefit is not worth the longer-term cost, but System 1 wins out).

On a day-to-day basis we cope with thousands of decisions using mental "rules of thumb", also called heuristics – mental shortcuts that let us make decisions and solve problems quickly and efficiently. However, some of these shortcuts are problematic: they are known as cognitive biases, and letting impulsive, automatic thinking take over an important decision can produce choices we later regret (e.g. impulse buying).

Cognitive biases affecting consumer choices

Key concept: ChoiceThe study of cognitive biases, and how behavioural economics uses them to help consumers make better choices, illustrates the key concept of Choice.
  • Availability bias – recent information and examples over-influence decision-making. Consumers are poor at judging real risk and probability, relying on memorable recent examples rather than careful data. Example: news of a salmonella outbreak in one region can make consumers nationwide change their food choices, even where there is no real local risk. Similarly, a young person who sees an older long-term smoker who is healthy may delude themselves into thinking smoking is not dangerous.
  • Anchoring bias – being given a value as a reference point ("anchor") that then influences future choices; once an anchor is set, people rely on it too heavily. Supermarkets exploit this by anchoring high prices in consumers' minds, so a subsequent "sale" price feels like a great deal, encouraging people to buy more than they need. Salespeople exploit it by opening with a very high price then "accepting" a lower one that is still above the item's true worth.
  • Framing bias – the way information is presented (positively or negatively) changes how people react to identical facts. Example: a label reading "90% fat free" is far more appealing than an identical product labelled "contains 10% fat", even though the two are the same – a technique widely used in marketing and advertising.
  • Social conformity / herd behaviour – the natural desire to fit in exerts a powerful influence on choices; it can be rewarding to be like others. A clear example is changing clothing fashions, where producers persuade people to buy new clothes to keep up with new styles even when their existing clothes are perfectly fine. This is also known as the "bandwagon effect" – joining a perceived majority even when it goes against one's own best interest.
  • Status quo / inertia bias – faced with a bewildering array of choices, consumers often prefer to do nothing and maintain the status quo. Example: when a mobile phone contract expires, it would be a good time to shop around, but consumers usually just stick with their existing provider even if a better deal exists elsewhere.
  • Loss aversion bias – experiments show humans feel losses about twice as strongly (psychologically) as equivalent gains. This makes people reluctant to change the status quo for fear of losing something, even when the fear is not well-founded (linked to status quo bias). Sellers exploit this with messages like "buy now before stocks run out!", making consumers feel they will lose out if they do not act.
  • Hyperbolic discounting – the tendency to prefer smaller short-term rewards over larger rewards that come later. Example: with an assignment due tomorrow and not yet started, a student may choose to go out and enjoy the evening, convincing themselves they will do a better job in the morning – sacrificing the larger, longer-term reward for a smaller immediate one. This bias grows stronger the further away the larger reward is.
Higher Level

Choice Architecture and Nudge Theory

Given the many cognitive biases that lead consumers to make poor System-1 decisions, behavioural economics aims to help people make better choices by altering the choice architecture they face.

Choice architectureThe theory that the decisions we make are heavily influenced by the way choices are presented to us. The person or entity presenting the choices is called the "choice architect".

Example: supermarkets deliberately place tempting products (chocolate bars, candy) right at the checkout to encourage "impulse buying" – this is not accidental, but a choice-architecture decision, and is especially effective on children waiting with a parent.

Default choiceChoice architecture often works through the default choice – either (1) the pre-set option a decision-maker gets automatically if they do nothing (e.g. Google as the default search engine in many browsers), or (2) an option that is always followed out of habit unless a deliberate decision is made to change it (e.g. always ordering the same coffee).

Defaults do not always lead to good outcomes, but they are popular because consumers often lack the time, resources, cognitive skill, or courage to research and switch to alternatives, and are generally comfortable with, and prefer, the easier option (linked to status quo bias).

Case study: organ donation defaultsThere is a chronic shortage of organs for transplant. Under an opt-in system, people must actively register as donors, or their organs cannot be used. Under an opt-out system, organs are automatically available for donation unless a person specifically requests otherwise before death. Simply switching the default from opt-in to opt-out substantially increases the number of organ donors – a clear application of choice architecture.
Mandated choiceAn alternative approach is mandated choice, where people are required by law to make a choice in advance. Example: some governments require people to tick a box indicating whether or not they wish to be an organ donor when renewing their driving licence, which increases the number of people who record a decision, compared with a purely voluntary register.

Nudge theory

Nudge theoryDeveloped by behavioural economist Richard Thaler, this theory suggests choice architects can carefully design choices to gently encourage ("nudge") people to voluntarily choose the option that is genuinely better for them. It became widely known through Thaler and Cass Sunstein's book Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth and Happiness.

The key to nudge theory is that it preserves consumer sovereignty (the right to choose) while encouraging better decisions. Example: placing healthy food in a convenient, easy-to-reach, easy-to-see place in a school cafeteria increases consumption of healthy food – students are not forced to eat healthily, but are subconsciously "nudged" toward it while still making the choice themselves.

When designing positive nudges, choice architects must essentially work around certain cognitive biases – if consumers fall back on System 1 rules of thumb that lead to poor decisions, a nudge may be needed to steer them toward better ones.

Case study: pension savings and the "Save More Tomorrow" (SMT) schemeMany governments run pension schemes to ensure a decent standard of living in retirement, but these schemes are under threat as people live longer and governments struggle to finance them. A rational "Econ" could calculate exactly how much to save each month, but real Humans do not save enough because of (1) hyperbolic discounting – saving now means giving up rewards now, and retirement always feels far away, so people keep saying "I'll start later"; and (2) loss aversion – cutting current spending to save is felt as a loss, which people are reluctant to accept.

Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi's "Save More Tomorrow" (SMT) scheme in the US nudges people to save more for retirement: employees start with small pension contributions, and whenever they receive a pay rise, their pension contribution automatically increases too ("automatic escalation"). This addresses hyperbolic discounting (starting small, later) and loss aversion (the extra saving comes from a pay rise, not a cut to current spending, so it isn't felt as a loss). Workers can opt in or opt out at any time, preserving consumer sovereignty – which is what makes it a "nudge" rather than a mandate. Research shows such schemes have been very successful at raising pension contributions.
Theory of Knowledge: "libertarian paternalism"Nudge theory has been described as libertarian paternalism – a seemingly contradictory term, since "libertarian" suggests freedom of choice while "paternalism" suggests being told what to do. Proponents of nudge theory are comfortable with the label because it captures the balance nudges strike: guiding people toward better outcomes without removing their freedom to choose otherwise.
Criticism of nudge theoryAny form of government intervention can be accused of taking away individual rights. Critics also argue governments cannot really know what is "best" for people and so should not be trusted to decide how to nudge them. This reflects a wider debate between those who believe markets operate efficiently on their own (so government should not intervene) and behavioural economists, who argue that the belief in efficient, self-regulating markets itself rests on the assumption that consumers behave rationally – an assumption their experimental work directly challenges.
Economics in action: ethical / belief-driven consumption

Consumers increasingly "vote with their dollars" by buying, switching away from, avoiding, or boycotting brands based on the brand's stance on societal issues – known as "ethical consumption" or "belief-driven buying". The 2018 Edelman Earned Brand Report, surveying consumers across Brazil, China, France, Germany, India, Japan, the UK and the US, found that a majority of consumers across most of these markets choose, switch, avoid or boycott brands based on their societal stances, with year-on-year growth in most markets surveyed. For example, consumer concern about single-use plastics has pushed coffee chains to promote reusable cups and stop offering plastic straws to maintain demand – producers pay close attention to consumer demand and adjust their behaviour, in this case helping to reduce plastic waste.

Figure: Belief-driven buying around the world (Fig 3.11)Bar chart data from the 2018 Edelman Earned Brand Report. The percentage of consumers who are belief-driven buyers rose in every market shown between 2017 and 2018, from an eight-market average of 51% to 64%, with the largest single-year jumps in Japan (+21 points) and the U.K. (+20 points). This is the underlying evidence behind the "voting with their dollars" trend described above – direct data showing belief-driven consumption becoming mainstream rather than a niche behaviour.
Key concepts: Choice and ChangeBelief-driven buying is flagged in the textbook as an example of both the Choice concept (consumers choosing which brands to support) and the Change concept (shifting consumption patterns driving producers to change their behaviour, e.g. toward sustainability).
Does the producer's motive matter?A cynic might say producers only respond to social/environmental issues to protect or grow their profits, not because they genuinely care. But if the outcome (e.g. less plastic waste) is positive either way, does the underlying motive actually matter? This is a genuinely open, debatable question with no single right answer.
Theory of Knowledge: UtilitarianismUtilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill) is the philosophy that a person should act to produce the best consequences for the greatest number of people. It underpins the economic idea of utility: consumption of a good can be measured in "utils", and the marginal utility gained from an extra unit is assumed to decrease as consumption increases (linking back to the law of diminishing marginal utility and the Law of Demand).
