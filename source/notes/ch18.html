Supply-side policies — overview and goals

DefinitionSupply-side policies are policies designed to increase the long-run aggregate supply in the economy by increasing the quantity and/or quality of the factors of production. They are intended to shift the AS/LRAS curve to the right (and, in real terms, to push the economy’s production possibilities curve, PPC, outward).

The main goals of supply-side policies are to:

  • Achieve long-run economic growth by increasing the productive capacity of the economy.
  • Improve competition and efficiency.
  • Reduce labour costs and unemployment through increased labour market flexibility.
  • Reduce inflation to improve international competitiveness.
  • Increase firms’ incentives to invest in innovation by reducing costs.

Market-based supply-side policies

Market-based policies focus on allowing markets to operate more freely with minimal government intervention. They are advocated by economists who believe in the efficiency of free markets in allocating resources. The word “incentives” is often used with such policies, as they are designed to increase the incentive for labour to work harder and more productively and to increase the incentive for firms to invest and increase productivity. These policies may also be described as institutional changes, since they affect the structures, institutions and “rules” that govern economic stakeholders.

Reduction in household income taxes

Policy: lower income taxWhen people work harder and earn more money, they face higher taxes on the higher levels of income. Higher taxes can act as a disincentive to work, because people may prefer to substitute more leisure time rather than pay higher taxes on the extra income earned. If income taxes are reduced, people are not taxed more heavily for working more, so they may have a greater incentive to work harder and become more productive, thus increasing the potential output of the economy.

Reductions in corporate taxes

Policy: lower corporate taxIf businesses can keep more of their profits, they have more money available for investment. Since investment is the addition to the capital stock of the economy, greater investment means more capital — a factor of production. Firms also use profits to fund research and development (R&D), which leads to advances in technology. If businesses know they can keep a larger share of profits rather than give it to the government in taxes, they have more incentive to produce efficiently. Given the importance of the profit motive in stimulating competition, the potential for firms to earn higher profits is likely to generate positive supply-side benefits.

Labour market reforms

Reduction in trade union powerTrade unions are responsible for protecting workers’ well-being, rights and incomes, but it may be perceived that they raise the cost of labour above the level that would exist in a non-union environment. Reducing (or eliminating) trade union power reduces unions’ ability to negotiate higher wages, lowering firms’ costs of production and increasing the number of workers firms are willing to hire.
Reduction or elimination of minimum wagesBecause a government-set minimum wage keeps the price of labour above its free-market level, reducing or abolishing the minimum wage would decrease the cost of labour and thus increase aggregate supply.
Reduction in unemployment benefitsIf unemployed people receive generous government benefits, it can be argued they have less incentive to find and take available jobs. Market-oriented, supply-side economists recommend reducing unemployment benefits to encourage unemployed people to take available jobs. This policy only works if jobs are actually available.

Deregulation

DeregulationIf governments place many regulations on business operations, this increases firms’ costs of production, reducing potential output. Such regulations include environmental laws, health and safety regulations, and laws concerning working hours, leave and holidays. A reduction in the number and/or severity of regulations — deregulation — helps increase aggregate supply.

Privatization

PrivatizationPrivatization is the sale of public, government-owned (nationalized) firms to the private sector. Free-market economists argue that privately-owned, profit-maximizing firms will be much more efficient than nationalized firms and will therefore increase the potential output of the economy. Nationalized firms tend to have different goals from private firms, such as maintenance of employment or provision of a service to an isolated market, and this means they may operate inefficiently.

Policies to increase competition

Competition policyCompetition encourages greater efficiency. Therefore, any policies that increase competition, such as enforcing strict anti-monopoly laws, will increase efficiency and improve the productive potential of an economy.

Trade liberalization

Trade liberalizationThis is another measure designed to increase competition, but internationally. The elimination of subsidies, tariffs and quotas would reduce government intervention and lead to free trade. This would mean exporting firms need to be more efficient in order to compete with foreign firms — they need to improve their efficiency and increase investment.

Limitations of market-based, supply-side policies

How effective market-based, supply-side policies are in achieving specific macroeconomic objectives (such as low unemployment and a low, stable rate of inflation) is considered later in the course. All market-based policies emphasize a reduced role for government and greater freedom for markets (especially labour markets) to operate without interference. This should improve resource allocation at no significant cost to the government. However, there are limitations related to their effectiveness and/or equity.

  1. Reduction in household income taxes may not lead to a great increase in the incentive to work. When people have an increase in their disposable income, it is possible that they may choose to work less instead.
  2. Reduction in household income taxes may benefit higher wage earners more than those on lower wages, so there may be an increase in income inequality.
  3. Reducing corporate taxes and thus increasing net profits will benefit wealthy shareholders, so may once again lead to increased income inequality.
  4. Labour-market reforms may lead to a possible reduction in living standards for low-income or unionized workers, leading to increased income inequality.
  5. Deregulation may have a number of negative effects:
    • Negative consequences for the environment if environmental regulations are relaxed, so there are more negative externalities of production.
    • A potential reduction in worker safety if health and safety regulations are relaxed.
    • A worsening in working conditions if regulations concerning working hours are changed.
  6. Privatization may not have the desired results if the process is not carried out transparently. There have been cases in some countries where nationalized industries have been sold at prices below their true value to individuals with connections to the government. These industries have then enjoyed monopoly power, meaning there has been no incentive to reduce costs, reduce prices or offer more choice. Indeed, in some cases there are increases in price and a reduction in supply.
  7. In all market-based, supply-side policies, there will be time lags before the effects of the policies are filtered through to increased potential output.
Key concept — EquityPoints 1–4 above illustrate the key concept of equity: market-based supply-side policies (income tax cuts, corporate tax cuts, labour market reforms) can widen income inequality even while raising potential output.
Key concept — Economic wellbeingLabour market reforms and reduced benefits/regulation can reduce the economic wellbeing of low-income or unionized workers, even as aggregate potential output rises.
Key concept — SustainabilityDeregulation, particularly of environmental laws, risks negative externalities and undermines the sustainability of economic activity, even as it boosts short-term productive capacity.

Interventionist supply-side policies

Key concept — InterventionInterventionist supply-side policies are based on the idea that government has a fundamental role to play in actively encouraging growth, rather than simply removing itself from markets.

Investment in human capital

Education and trainingTo constantly increase the quality/productivity of a country’s labour force, education and training need to be available. Education and training create positive externalities — the benefits are felt not just by the people who receive the education, but across the economy, as a more educated and better-trained workforce increases the potential output of the economy.

Although education and training would be provided in a pure market system, they would be underprovided, so it is the responsibility of government to ensure that a country’s education facilities provide the necessary skills and knowledge for a dynamic economy. This involves both the skills young people need to enter the labour force and the retraining of workers to help them adjust to changing economic circumstances. Such training can take place in schools, universities, public training institutions and apprenticeship programmes. Governments can also support private schools, private universities and private training institutions with subsidies or tax benefits, and support firms that provide training and apprenticeship programmes. All these methods should help raise the level of human capital of an economy.

In addition to education and training, the quality of human capital may also be improved by investment in the quality and quantity of health care, as well as improving people’s access to health care.

Research and development (R&D)

Support for R&DIt is important that an economy’s firms stay up-to-date with modern developments, develop new production techniques and seek improved methods of production. All of this may increase the economy’s potential output, but it involves extensive spending on research and development. Governments can actively encourage R&D by firms by offering tax incentives — for example, allowing firms not to pay taxes on the retained profits used for R&D (known as a tax credit). Firms may be reluctant to spend on R&D if they think they will not be able to reap the full benefits of their spending, so governments can encourage R&D by guaranteeing intellectual property rights, such as patents and copyrights. Alternatively, governments themselves could finance R&D in public research facilities and universities.

Provision and maintenance of infrastructure

InfrastructureInfrastructure is the large-scale capital, usually provided by government, that is necessary for economic activity to take place. It includes capital such as roads, electricity, water supply, sanitation, waste management, railways, airports, ports, telecommunications, Internet access and public transportation. Other institutions, such as the education and health systems or the legal and security framework of a country, can also be considered a type of infrastructure, since they too are necessary for economic activity. Since infrastructure is necessary for economic activity to take place, the productive potential of an economy (as illustrated by its LRAS) will be enhanced by improved infrastructure.

Direct support for businesses / industrial policies

Direct support/industrial policyGovernments have agencies or ministries responsible for developing policies that support and encourage the development of industry (for example, a Ministry of Industry, Ministry of Industry and Trade, or Commerce Department). Their mandates may include: improving the competitive nature of industries through the maintenance of anti-monopoly laws, helping small and medium enterprises to become established and to grow, supporting export companies in accessing markets abroad, and advising government on how education can meet the needs of businesses. In addition to tax credits, governments may also support certain industries directly through financial subsidies.
Economics in actionThe book cites the idea of looking up the government agency responsible for supporting industry in an OECD country (e.g. a Ministry of Industry or Commerce Department) to see its real-world mandate and achievements in encouraging industrial development.

Limitations of interventionist, supply-side policies

All the interventionist policies described above are necessary in an economy and are therefore not considered to be controversial in principle. They have the characteristics of merit goods, in that while they could be provided by the market system, they would be underprovided. The economy would not benefit from the potential welfare gain in terms of the greatest possible increase in long-run aggregate supply without government intervention. However, there are limitations related to their implementation:

  1. There is a significant monetary cost involved in all interventionist policies. This creates two problems:
    • There is always an opportunity cost when governments spend money — for example, increased expenditure on energy infrastructure may mean inadequate expenditure on the health care system.
    • If the increased expenditure is funded through borrowing, it may increase the levels of government indebtedness and have negative effects in the future.
  2. There will be time lags before the effects of interventionist policies are filtered through to increased potential output.
  3. In any democracy, the extent to which different interventionist policies are provided inevitably depends on the ideological aims of the government in power and the influence of various interest groups. Changes in government can lead to significant changes in emphasis on different policies.
  4. Controversies may arise concerning the provision and funding of education — for example, how many resources should be allocated to science education versus arts education, or disagreement over which examinations should be used in the education system. Similar disputes may arise over health care funding.
Economics in actionIn reality, all governments employ a mixture of market-based and interventionist supply-side policies; the book suggests investigating the actual mix of policies used in a chosen OECD country.

How supply-side policies and demand-side policies are connected

Some supply-side policies will have demand-side effects, and some demand-side policies will have supply-side effects.

Demand-side effects of supply-side policies

Supply-side policies are, by definition, long run in their intended effect. However, when they are implemented, many of them will also have short-run, demand-side effects.

Market-based policies → demand-side effectsReducing household income taxes and corporate taxes are also expansionary fiscal policies. Thus, in the short run, they can be expected to increase aggregate demand, leading to economic growth and lower unemployment — but at the cost of increased inflationary pressure.
Interventionist policies → demand-side effectsInterventionist policies all have a demand-side implication, since they all involve government spending. The increased spending by government is itself an addition to aggregate demand, and so also has expansionary fiscal effects.

It should be noted that, while the demand-side effects may be felt quite quickly, for the most part there is a fairly lengthy time lag between the implementation of supply-side policies and their effect on potential output.

Supply-side effects of demand-side policies

Demand-side, fiscal policies can also have supply-side effects. An expansionary fiscal policy involving lower household income taxes may increase the incentive to work harder and to become more productive, thus increasing the potential output of the economy. In the same way, an expansionary fiscal policy that reduces corporate taxes may lead to increased investment and/or increased research and development, increasing the quality and quantity of factors of production.

Expansionary fiscal policies involving an increase in government expenditure may have strong supply-side effects depending on the direction of the spending. Any government expenditure aimed at the provision of infrastructure, investment in human capital (such as education or health care), or increasing research and development will not only shift aggregate demand, but will also, in the long term, increase the quantity and quality of factors of production, increasing the potential output of the economy.
