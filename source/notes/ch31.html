Introduction: Growth vs Development Strategies

Chapter 30 covered the barriers to economic growth and economic development. This chapter considers the strategies that might reduce or eliminate those barriers so that developing countries can achieve economic progress.

Growth is not developmentEconomic growth is a one-dimensional concept relating only to an increase in real GDP. Economic development is a multidimensional concept relating to a myriad of improvements in wellbeing. The importance of growth is not in question — the Sustainable Development Goals note that “economic growth can lead to new and better employment opportunities and provide greater economic security for all”. The word ‘can’ is key: growth will not contribute to development if the benefits are not shared equitably. The priority must therefore be to achieve inclusive growth and adopt ‘pro-poor’ strategies that directly confront poverty.

The strategies in this chapter fall into two broad categories that recur throughout the course: market-oriented (free-market) strategies, which rely on reducing government intervention and trusting price signals/competition, and interventionist strategies, where government (or NGOs) deliberately target the poor to make growth inclusive. Most real countries use a blend of both.

Trade Strategies for Growth and Development

Key concept: InterventionTrade strategies illustrate the concept of Intervention because the central debate is how much government should intervene in trade (protecting infant industries, managing exchange rates, subsidizing exporters) versus leaving trade to free-market forces.

International trade can contribute to economic growth and, when well-regulated, to economic development. There are three trade-based approaches developing countries may adopt.

1. Import Substitution (Import Substitution Industrialization, ISI)

DefinitionImport substitution industrialization (ISI) is an inward-oriented strategy stating that a developing country should, wherever possible, produce goods domestically rather than import them. Domestic industries grow as the economy grows, eventually becoming competitive on world markets through economies of scale. It is the opposite of export-led growth and is rejected by economists who favour free trade based on comparative advantage.

Necessary conditions for the strategy to work:

  • The government organizes the selection of goods to produce domestically — historically labour-intensive, low-skill manufactured goods such as clothing.
  • Subsidies are made available to encourage domestic industries.
  • The government implements a protectionist system with tariff barriers to keep out foreign imports.
Advantages of ISI
  • Protects jobs in the domestic market since foreign firms are prevented from dominating.
  • Protects local culture and social habits by isolating the economy from foreign influence.
  • Protects the economy from the power, and possibly bad influence, of multinational corporations (MNCs).
Disadvantages of ISI
  • May only protect jobs in the short run; in the long run, growth may be lower and the lack of growth may lead to a lack of job creation.
  • The country does not enjoy the benefits of comparative advantage and specialization, so it produces relatively inefficiently when it could import from efficient foreign producers.
  • Leads to inefficiency in domestic industries because a lack of competition does not encourage research and development.
  • May lead to high rates of inflation due to domestic aggregate supply constraints.
  • May cause other countries to take retaliatory protectionist measures.

Main adopters: Latin American countries (Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, Chile, Uruguay); former colonies after independence (India, Nigeria, Kenya) in the 1960s and 1970s. Policies showed some success initially but started to fail in the early 1980s and were abandoned. ISI does not seem to be a chosen option for economic growth at present.

2. Export Promotion (Export-led Growth)

DefinitionExport promotion (export-led growth) is an outward-oriented strategy based on openness and increased international trade. Growth is achieved by making increasing exports and export revenue a leading factor in aggregate demand. Rising exports raise GDP, which raises incomes and eventually generates growth in both domestic and exporting markets. The country concentrates on producing and exporting goods in which it has comparative advantage, and may try to keep its exchange rate low against other currencies to make exports more attractive.

The theoretical “package” of policies associated with export-led growth includes:

  • Liberalized trade — opening domestic markets to foreign competition in order to gain access to foreign markets.
  • Liberalized capital flows — reducing restrictions on foreign direct investment.
  • A floating exchange rate.
  • Investment in infrastructure to enable trade.
  • Deregulation and minimal government intervention.

In reality, countries adopting an outward-oriented strategy do not necessarily adopt all of these policies.

Developing countries may export either primary products and/or manufactured products (some have tried to export services, usually tourism).

Primary products as the engine of growthMany developing countries depend on primary product exports for revenue. However, the overall trend in primary product prices, with the exception of oil and some metals, has been downward for many years, due to increasing supply and relatively insignificant increases in demand for many commodities. This, combined with increased protectionism by developed countries, means export-led growth based on primary products is unlikely to be achieved.

The focus of export-led growth is usually on increasing manufacturing exports. The historic success of the “Asian tigers” — Japan, South Korea, Hong Kong, Singapore and Taiwan — is usually used to illustrate the effectiveness of this strategy. These countries exported products in which they had comparative advantage, usually based on low-cost labour, and were extremely successful. Over time, the type of product exported shifted from labour-intensive, low-skill goods to more sophisticated products using capital-intensive production and highly skilled workers — improvements in education systems were essential for this.

Problems with export-led growth
  • The success of the Asian tigers (since around 1965) and the emergence of China as an exporting powerhouse led to increased protectionism in developed countries against manufactured imports from developing countries. Trade unions and workers argued this competition was unfair and lobbied for tariffs and quotas, removing the comparative-advantage price benefit of the exporting countries. Tariff escalation also reduced the ability of developing countries to export processed and assembled goods, forcing many to export primary products and low-skilled manufactured goods instead.
  • Certain assumptions about the necessary conditions for export-led growth were not necessarily met. Many economists argue the role of the state was vital in successful export-led growth and that minimizing government intervention was not actually the way it happened. In the Asian tiger countries, governments played an important role providing infrastructure, subsidizing output through cheap credit via central banks, and promoting savings and technological improvement. Governments protected industries not yet ready to compete with foreign firms while promoting industries ready for export competition — this is the “infant industry” argument for protection, and is a subject of great debate among development economists (some say intervention was vital; others say state intervention actually slowed growth rates).
  • If countries attract MNCs to kick-start export-led growth, there is always a fear that the MNCs become too powerful within the country.
  • Some economists argue free-market, export-led growth may increase income inequality — if so, economic growth may be achieved at the expense of economic development.
Key concept: InterventionThe debate over whether state intervention (infant-industry protection, subsidized credit) or minimal intervention explains Asian tiger success is a direct illustration of the Intervention concept.

3. Economic Integration

Developing countries have long been involved in preferential access schemes and regional free-trade agreements, offering both opportunities and challenges for growth and development. The extent of opportunities/challenges depends on the extent of the integration — whether it is simply preferential access for particular goods, a free-trade area, a customs union, or a common market.

What is “aid-for-trade”?Recognising the vast opportunities that trade offers for growth and poverty reduction, the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development calls for increasing “aid-for-trade” support for developing countries. The Aid for Trade initiative was launched in 2006 through the World Trade Organisation, and aims to provide: technical assistance to help countries negotiate trade strategies and implement negotiation outcomes; transport infrastructure connecting domestic and international markets and storage facilities; support for agriculture, energy generation and supply; investment assistance so countries can diversify and build on comparative advantage; and adjustment assistance to help with the costs of tariff reductions and loss of preferential treatment. There is no single trade SDG, but around 20 targets across the SDGs relate directly to international trade — Target 8.a specifically calls to “increase aid-for-trade support for developing countries, in particular least developed countries”.
Possible advantages/opportunities of integration
  • Larger export markets may allow producers to gain economies of scale.
  • Larger markets may encourage diversification and reduce dependence on a narrow range of commodities.
  • Integration is likely to encourage regional cooperation in areas such as infrastructure, transport and telecommunications.
  • For landlocked countries, regional integration may offer vital links to ports and infrastructure networks in neighbouring countries.
  • Further stimulus for inward FDI, as foreign companies benefit from the larger market size.
  • Free movement of labour gives workers opportunities to work in other member countries and send remittances home.
  • Free movement of capital gives companies opportunities to invest in other member countries.
  • Greater political stability and cooperation, which can result in higher levels of investment.
  • Greater efficiency as domestic producers must compete with lower-priced imports from other member countries.
  • As part of a trading bloc, individual countries may increase their bargaining power in multilateral trade negotiations.
  • Consumers may access less expensive imported goods and services (though this may be limited if trade diversion occurs and the new common external tariff makes some imports more expensive — Higher Level).
  • Trade creation will benefit producers who can import inputs without tariffs (Higher Level).
Possible disadvantages/challenges of integration
  • The role of the WTO might be undermined, since integration allows member countries to look inwards.
  • Trade can become more complicated when agreements exist with other trading blocs.
  • Unemployment may arise as less efficient companies (with higher labour costs) fail to compete with lower-priced imports from other member countries.
  • Trade diversion may be encountered (Higher Level).

Diversification

A major problem for many developing countries has been over-dependence on exporting a limited range of primary commodities — in many cases dependence on export earnings from just one or two commodities.

Export diversificationCountries pursue export diversification to move away from producing and exporting primary commodities and towards producing and exporting manufactured and semi-manufactured products. The aims are to protect against volatile primary product prices, to stabilize or increase export revenue, to stabilize or increase employment, and to raise the use of technology and demand for more highly skilled workers.
Barriers to diversification
  • Tariff escalation: the rate of import tariffs rises the more a good is processed. The importing country protects its own processing/manufacturing industries by placing lower tariffs on raw materials and components and higher tariffs on processed and finished goods. There is little incentive for developing countries to diversify into processing, since the resulting tariffs would make their processed goods uncompetitive.
  • Need for a more highly qualified workforce to produce relatively sophisticated products. Many developing countries have relatively low education standards and find it difficult to fund an improving education system — a poverty trap: low education leads to production of low-profit commodities and components, which leads to low incomes for governments and individuals, which perpetuates the low ability to fund education.

Market-Based Supply-Side Policies

Three market-based supply-side policies are most often put forward as a means of achieving growth and development.

1. Trade Liberalization

Key concept: EfficiencyTrade liberalization is the removal, or at least reduction, of trade barriers that block the free trade of goods and services between countries — eliminating tariff barriers, quotas, export subsidies and administrative legislation.

The belief is that trade liberalization will increase world trade and enable developing countries to concentrate on producing goods and services in which they have a comparative advantage.

LimitationsMany developing countries, and particularly the least developed countries, lack the infrastructure and institutions necessary to gain the full benefits from trade liberalization. Protectionist policies by developed countries that block developing countries from exporting higher-value manufactured goods reduce the effectiveness of liberalization for developing countries. Subsidies given by developed-country governments to their own producers can also damage the ability of developing-country producers to compete in developed markets. These challenges underscore the need for international cooperation through aid-for-trade strategies.

2. Privatization

Key concept: EfficiencyPrivatization is the sale of publicly (government) owned firms — nationalized firms — to the private sector. Free market economists argue privately owned, profit-maximizing firms will be more efficient than nationalized firms and will therefore increase potential output.

Nationalized firms tend to have different goals from private firms, such as maintaining employment or providing a service to an isolated market, meaning they may operate inefficiently.

LimitationsPrivatization will only generate economic gains if the process is carried out and managed well. It requires careful design and sequencing, creation of a regulatory infrastructure, assessment of possible poverty and social impacts, and transparent public communication — often challenging in developing countries. It may also be necessary for some goods and services to be provided by nationalized firms so that they remain accessible and affordable to everyone, particularly low-income people. If privatized, certain operations might close (because they are not profit-making) or the price of the product might become unaffordable.

Worked example: water and sanitation. Access to safe and affordable drinking water and sanitation is a human right and essential to any fight against poverty. Given the huge expense of the necessary infrastructure, governments in developing countries have often turned to the private sector (sometimes even required to privatize in order to receive support from international agencies). Private companies have the financial resources for large infrastructure investments. However, left purely to market forces, a lack of competition and regulation means the development goal of increased access to water at affordable prices is unlikely to be met. Therefore, if the water industry is to be privatized, government involvement in establishing and enforcing regulations is necessary to ensure the goal of safe, affordable water is reached.

3. Deregulation

Key concept: EfficiencyIf governments place many regulations on business operations (environmental laws, health and safety regulations, laws on working hours/leave/holidays) this increases firms’ costs of production and reduces potential output. A reduction in the number and/or severity of regulations — deregulation — increases aggregate supply and generates economic growth.

In many developing countries there is much “red tape” involved in setting up a business, which deters both domestic and foreign investors. Reducing the difficulty of the process may increase investment.

LimitationsDeregulation has its drawbacks. If deregulation of labour laws damages the safety and rights of workers, any benefits of growth will not be inclusive. If deregulation of the banking system leads to debt-driven growth, growth is achieved at the cost of development. If deregulation relates to the relaxing of environmental laws, any growth will be at the cost of sustainable development.

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

DefinitionForeign direct investment (FDI) is long-term investment by private multinational enterprises/corporations (MNEs/MNCs) in countries overseas. It usually occurs either as greenfield investment (MNCs build new plants or expand existing facilities abroad) or as MNCs merging with or acquiring (buying) existing firms in foreign countries.

Why MNCs are attracted to developing countries

  • Countries may be rich in natural resources such as oil or minerals — MNCs have the technology and expertise to extract them (e.g. top FDI recipients in Africa include resource-rich Nigeria and South Africa).
  • Some developing countries (Brazil, China, India) represent huge and growing markets; locating directly in these markets gives better access to rising consumer demand.
  • Labour costs are much lower than in developed countries, allowing lower final prices and higher profits.
  • Government regulations are often much less severe, making it easier and cheaper to set up and reducing costs of production; many governments also offer tax concessions, and over the last 15 years many countries (developed and developing) have adopted policies more favourable to FDI, often reduced corporate tax rates.

Worked example: China. Since 1978, China has actively tried to attract foreign investment to stimulate economic growth. A significant proportion of China’s exports are produced by foreign firms; through joint ventures, Chinese firms have grown rapidly and successfully, and China itself is now the source of a large outflow of Chinese FDI (as China grows, its demand for raw materials grows, and much Chinese FDI abroad is investment in natural resources).

Advantages of FDI
  • A necessary condition for growth is increased savings; developing countries tend to suffer from a savings gap, and FDI helps fill this gap (Key concept: Efficiency).
  • MNCs provide employment and, in many cases, education and training, improving workforce skill levels and managerial capability.
  • MNCs give developing countries greater access to research and development, technology and marketing expertise, enhancing industrialization.
  • Increased employment and earnings may have a multiplier effect on the host economy, stimulating growth.
  • The host government may gain tax revenue from MNC profits, usable for infrastructure, health or education investment to promote development.
  • If MNCs buy existing companies, they inject foreign capital and increase aggregate demand.
  • MNCs may improve physical or financial infrastructure, or spur governments to do so to attract them.
  • MNCs may provide more choice and lower prices for consumers, including essential goods not otherwise available domestically.
  • MNC activity combined with liberalized trade can lead to a more efficient allocation of world resources.
Disadvantages of FDI
  • MNCs often bring their own management teams, providing only inexpensive, low-skilled jobs with no education or training, limiting host countries’ ability to acquire new technologies.
  • MNCs may have too much power (due to their size), gaining large subsidies or reducing potential government income; their income/size may allow too much influence over policy internationally (e.g. the WTO).
  • Transfer pricing: MNCs sell goods/services from one division of the company to another division in a different country to exploit different corporate tax rates. Developing countries with low tax rates gain little tax reward, and developed countries lose potential revenue too. Since roughly one-third of international trade is intra-firm, this represents a potentially large loss of government revenue. Rules exist to prevent abuse of transfer pricing, but they are difficult to monitor and enforce, particularly for developing-country governments.
  • MNCs may situate themselves where pollution legislation is weak, reducing their private costs while creating external (environmental) costs for the host country. Similarly, they may exploit weak labour laws through low wages and poor working conditions.
  • MNCs may enter a country purely to extract particular resources, then strip those resources and leave — causing unrest as nationals see profits sent abroad.
  • MNCs may use capital-intensive production methods to exploit abundant natural resources, which does little to improve employment; it is argued MNCs should use appropriate (labour-intensive) technology aligned to the resources available, given developing countries’ large supply of cheap labour.
  • Where MNCs buy domestic firms, the previous owners are usually paid in shares/stock of the MNC, so the actual money is likely never used in the developing economy.
  • MNCs may repatriate profits, transferring them out of the host country back to the MNC’s country of origin.
Key conceptsFDI illustrates several key concepts at once: Efficiency (filling the savings gap, resource allocation), Sustainability (environmental exploitation concerns), Equity (bargaining power imbalance between MNCs and host governments), and Economic Wellbeing (worker exploitation, child labour concerns).
OECD Guidelines for Multinational EnterprisesThe OECD Guidelines provide a detailed, extensive list of “non-binding principles and standards for responsible business conduct” to all multinationals operating in, or from, any of the 48 adherent countries. They are the only multilaterally agreed and comprehensive code of responsible business conduct that governments have committed to promoting. Each member country has a National Contact Point (NCP) agency to promote/implement the Guidelines and mediate disputes. MNEs are expected to: contribute to economic, environmental and social progress with a view to sustainable development; respect internationally recognized human rights; encourage local capacity building; encourage human capital formation through employment and training; and provide full financial and operating information.

There are always concerns about MNC activity: exploitation of workers, use of child labour, the inability of workers to form trade unions, and business practices causing environmental damage. With fast information flow through media/internet and active public interest groups, it is increasingly difficult for MNCs to conceal problematic activities, so firms increasingly publicize Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) policies (through annual reports, websites, advertising) to show they act responsibly and ethically. The extent to which such policies are actually followed, and their real effect on workers, communities and the environment, is uncertain — but it is usually regarded as a step in the right direction.

Social Enterprise

DefinitionSocial enterprises are organizations that have specific social objectives as their primary goal. They may be for-profit or non-profit, but profit is a secondary goal. Most social enterprises aim for the creation of social wealth via a viable, sustainable business model with environmentally responsible operation.
Key conceptsSocial enterprise touches on four key concepts at once: Sustainability (long-term, self-sustaining business model rather than a one-off donation), Equity (targeting the poor and gender-sensitive design), Efficiency (using market mechanisms rather than pure charity), and Change (transforming local economic opportunity).

The principal objective is to overcome or alleviate a global or local issue — poverty, lack of health care, lack of education, lack of technology access, or an environmental problem. As with any business, the financial figures and cash flow must be capable of providing a sustainable, long-term existence.

Worked example: Sunny Money. An enterprise providing solar-powered products (solar torches, lights, battery chargers) to rural/off-grid communities in Africa at very low prices. Selling is done through local agents, creating employment in the areas where products are sold. Rather than giving products away (e.g. via a charity), the managing director argues a market-based approach is more sustainable: “Giving away products runs the risk of destroying a market which responds to the needs of the people — how can an entrepreneur in Kenya make a living out of selling solar lights if a nearby NGO is busy giving them away for free?”

Economics in action: Solar Sister (solarsister.org) is a similar real-world social enterprise that sells affordable solar lighting products while also creating economic opportunities for local women entrepreneurs across Africa.

Institutional Change

Key concept: ChangeIf the institutional barriers identified in Chapter 30 (weak property rights, corruption, poor governance, lack of financial access) can be reformed, development becomes more achievable — a direct illustration of the Change concept.

Four strategies for favourable institutional change are considered.

1. Improved Access to the Banking System

Developing countries face enormous challenges from weak financial systems. Two billion people worldwide still lack access to regulated financial services. This limits the ability of households and businesses to save, buy and sell goods and services, and — crucially — limits the ability of entrepreneurs and firms to gain credit to start and expand businesses.

MicrofinanceMicrofinance is a financial service geared specifically to the poor: the provision of financial services such as small loans, savings accounts, insurance and even cheque books. The provision of small loans is known as micro-credit. Original micro-credit schemes originated in developing countries in the mid-1970s: Opportunity International (1972), ACCION International (1973), Muhammad Yunus/Grameen Bank (1974/76), FINCA International (1985) and the SEEP Network (1985). Loans typically fund very small-scale “micro-enterprises” — roadside kiosks, bicycle repair, market stalls, rice wine making, knitting, woodworking — giving protection against unexpected events and seasonal problems, helping families build wealth and escape poverty.

Women tend to be the main recipients of micro-credit: they are considered a better credit risk (more likely to repay loans), and since women are usually responsible for children, reductions in a woman’s poverty translate directly into improvements for her children (more schooling, etc.). When women take loans and begin to earn an income, their social and economic status is raised and economic development is enhanced.

Mobile phone bankingAccess to banking has been transformed by mobile phone banking/mobile money: a sender texts an amount to the receiver’s phone number, and the receiver collects cash from an authorized local shop. Africa — known as the “unbanked continent” — has been transformed: in Kenya and Tanzania, British operator Vodafone has over 33 million customers using its M-Pesa system, and formal financial inclusion in Tanzania rose from 44% (2009) to over 65% today. In Pakistan, Norwegian company Telenor has offered mobile banking since 2008 with over 40 million subscribers who can withdraw cash at over 11,000 shops — compared to only 8,000 banks in the whole country. Real-world impacts: Tanzanian hospitals receive bus-fare payments from women via mobile money; the Afghan government pays policemen by mobile phone; East African coffee plantation owners pay workers by text; migrant workers send remittances home more cheaply, quickly and securely.

2. Increasing Women’s Empowerment

DefinitionEmpowerment is the process by which women gain power and control over their own lives and acquire the ability to make strategic choices. Important positive externalities arise when women become more empowered (see Chapter 30).

Quote (SDG Goal 5): “Providing women and girls with equal access to education, health care, decent work and representation in political and economic decision-making processes will fuel sustainable economies and benefit societies and humanity at large.”

A strategy for women’s empowerment should include
  • Increasing support for the education of females.
  • Increasing access to healthcare for women.
  • Creating a safe environment in the home, the workplace and society.
  • Establishing the right for women to own property and other assets.
  • Increasing female involvement in decision-making, both within and outside the home.

3. Reducing Corruption

Key concept: EquityCorruption is a significant barrier to development — no country, developed or developing, is immune. The abuse of public office for personal gain destroys trust in institutions, makes public policy less effective and fair, and diverts taxpayers’ money away from merit goods and public goods such as schools, roads and hospitals.

Corruption limits a government’s ability to grow the economy in a way that benefits all citizens: corrupt governments receive lower tax revenue because people pay bribes to avoid tax, and if people believe the government itself is corrupt, they avoid paying tax believing the money will be misspent. An IMF report estimated that the least corrupt governments can collect 4% more of GDP than similarly placed countries with higher corruption. Anti-corruption reforms can generate large gains: in Georgia, tax revenues more than doubled, rising 13 percentage points of GDP between 2003–2008; in Rwanda, reforms since the mid-1990s raised tax revenues by 6 percentage points of GDP.

IMF-suggested measures to reduce corruption
  • Invest in high levels of transparency and independent external scrutiny — e.g. Colombia, Costa Rica and Paraguay use online platforms letting citizens monitor investment projects.
  • Reform institutions from all angles — e.g. simplifying tax laws and reducing officials’ discretion increases the payoff of tax administration reform.
  • Build a professional civil service — transparent, merit-based hiring and pay reduce opportunities for corruption; leaders must set a clear ethical tone.
  • Keep pace with new challenges — focus on high-risk areas (procurement, revenue administration, natural resource management) and strengthen internal controls; e.g. Chile and Korea’s electronic procurement systems curtailed corruption by improving transparency and competition.
  • More international cooperation against corruption — joint efforts against cross-border corruption and money laundering, and against opaque financial centres hiding corrupt money.
Corruption and genderCorruption disproportionately affects the poor and, in particular, women. Transparency International recommends: collecting and disseminating gender-desegregated data; recognizing gendered forms of corruption such as “sextortion” (where sex is the currency of a bribe); including women in anti-corruption decision-making; empowering women (who are less likely to report abuse and more often unaware of their rights); and creating safe, gender-sensitive reporting mechanisms.

4. Promoting Secure Property Rights and Land Tenure Rights

Key concepts: Equity & ChangePeruvian economist Hernando de Soto divides the world into those with defined property rights and those without. About 2 billion people lack such rights to the property they live in or land they farm. Their assets become “dead capital” that cannot generate income or growth, and de Soto argues the poor are trapped in a “tragedy of the commons” — because they don’t own their land, their unregistered assets are effectively appropriated by more powerful entities.

Poor people often have the ability to use land for subsistence agriculture, grazing or firewood, but lack the statutory/legal right to determine its use, sell it, use it as collateral, or bequeath it. De Soto argues legally protected property rights are the key source of the developed world’s wealth, and their absence is why many nations remain trapped in poverty — he estimates that titling the world’s poor for their land, homes and unregistered businesses would unlock $9.3 trillion in assets.

The World Bank’s seven reasons legal/land reforms matter for growth and development
  • Agriculture: secure land titles give farmers the incentive to invest in and borrow against their land.
  • Urban development: unclarified land rights cause slums/informal settlements; effective urban planning requires guaranteed land rights.
  • Environment: secure rights give people incentive to look after their land and ensure its sustainability.
  • Private sector development and job creation: land/property rights provide collateral to finance business operations and expansion.
  • Empowering women: many legal systems require a male guardian to own property, or fail to register land in a wife’s name, or deny inheritance rights in practice — legal reform reduces these vulnerabilities.
  • Indigenous peoples’ rights: recognizing land rights allows more sustainable resource use and improves social/economic status.
  • Keeping peace: without legally protected property rights, displaced people cannot return home after conflict, potentially triggering further conflict.

Quote (Oxfam): “Land is vital to our livelihoods. Whether we use it for building a home, operating a business, growing food, or for mere enjoyment, land sustains us all.”

Interventionist Strategies: Overview

Key concept: InterventionMany strategies aim to grow the economy in the belief that growth will lead to development. Interventionist strategies instead try to ensure that growth is inclusive — directly improving the assets and capabilities of poor people and involving them directly in the process. They may be implemented by governments or by NGOs.

To be effective, such strategies need to be focused directly on the poor. They should be aimed at:

  • the sectors of the economy in which the poor work (e.g. agriculture, the informal economy);
  • the areas in which the poor live (e.g. town ghettos or underdeveloped rural regions);
  • the factors of production which the poor possess (e.g. unskilled labour);
  • the products which the poor consume (e.g. food).

Redistribution Policies (Fiscal Policy)

Key concept: EquityIn developed countries, fiscal policy is an effective tool for redistributing income — governments earn money from taxes and redistribute through transfer payments. Developing countries have far less scope to do this because their ability to raise tax revenue (their “fiscal capacity”) is quite limited, which in turn limits their ability to provide transfer payments and finance merit goods such as health services, education and infrastructure.

Transfer Payments and Conditional Cash Transfers (CCT)

Governments in developing countries face the challenge of allocating very limited revenue between investment in merit goods/public goods (education, health, infrastructure — which has a greater effect on future poverty) versus spending on transfer payments (which has a greater impact on current poverty). The complexities and costs of administering transfer payments are also a challenge.

Conditional Cash Transfers (CCTs)CCTs are transfer payments targeting low-income people that aim to reduce poverty by making welfare payments conditional on specific behaviours — e.g. children’s school attendance, up-to-date vaccinations, or regular pregnancy health-care visits. CCTs have direct effects on poverty (raising the recipient’s immediate income) but also a positive impact on the socio-economic wellbeing of the recipient and their family, alleviating poverty while improving the quality of human capital.
Limitations of CCTsCCTs do not themselves create jobs, but by permitting children more access to education and health care, they should make those children more productive in future, raising their future income and taking them out of poverty later. The size of the CCT is not enough in itself to raise a recipient out of poverty now — it simply adds to current income and compensates parents for sending children to school rather than to work or begging.

Minimum Wages

Key concept: EquityThe purpose of minimum wages (Chapter 22) is to “protect workers against unduly low pay and ensure an equitable share in the fruits of progress to all and a minimum living wage to all who are employed and in need of such protection.” When minimum wages exist but do not provide a “living wage” (one that ensures a worker can meet basic needs), a development strategy is to raise the minimum wage.

Minimum wages exist in more than 90% of the 187 member countries of the International Labour Organization (ILO), including most developing countries. While credited with a positive impact on reducing poverty, it is argued they have only a moderate effect and cannot be the major tool to combat poverty, for several reasons:

Why minimum wages have limited impact on poverty
  • The informal sector. An ILO report (April 2018) found around 61% of the world’s employed population works in the informal sector (85.8% in Africa, 68.2% in Asia and the Pacific, 40% in the Arab States). Informal workers have no contract, no social protection, no guaranteed working conditions and no protection under the rule of law, and so receive no minimum wage — any minimum wage policy will not reduce poverty among informal workers. This drives the ILO’s emphasis on tackling informality and promoting formalization of employment.
  • Compliance and enforcement. Even in the formal sector, firms may pay below minimum wage and escape punishment. The ILO promotes information/awareness campaigns, better policy design, empowering workers to claim rights through collective action, effective disincentive punishments, and targeted labour inspections within global supply chains.
  • Unintended job losses. A (higher) minimum wage may cause some employers to lay off workers. If those workers are in low-income households, poverty may increase — especially if the worker was the sole earner. Laid-off formal-sector workers may seek informal-sector jobs, putting downward pressure on wages there.

Minimum wages must not be discounted as a poverty-reduction tool, but their effectiveness is limited by the size of the informal economy and the extent of non-compliance. SDG 8 recognizes this, setting targets to “encourage the formalization and growth of … enterprises” and “protect labour rights” for all workers.

Provision of Merit Goods (Health and Education)

Key concept: EquityMerit goods are underprovided by the market. Health services and education provide tremendous private and external benefits, but developing countries face enormous challenges in providing them.

Access to quality health care and education has the potential to raise the human capital of a country’s population. Significant challenges exist in identifying needs, choosing appropriate services, financing programmes and ensuring universal access — challenges identified throughout many of the Sustainable Development Goals. Supporting development also requires extensive investment in infrastructure — clean energy, transport, telecommunications, clean water and sanitation.

The increased supply of merit goods may be provided by many means: domestic government investment, foreign investment, microfinance, social enterprise, or international cooperation through foreign aid.

The Multidimensional Poverty Index — no one-size-fits-allIt would be inappropriate to adopt a single approach to poverty reduction everywhere, since the nature of poverty differs by country. Peru and Tajikistan show similar overall levels of multidimensional poverty, but children in Peru experience much greater educational deprivation (schooling contributes 18% to the MPI in Peru vs only 1% in Tajikistan), whereas in Tajikistan nutrition contributes 35% to the MPI (vs 18% in Peru). This shows why country-specific poverty-reduction strategies are needed.

Foreign Aid

Key concept: InterdependenceForeign aid is assistance given to a country that would not have been provided through normal market forces. It is provided to help disaster/war victims, help achieve development, fill the savings gap, improve human resources, strengthen institutions, improve technology, fund specific projects, build aid-for-trade capacity, or help meet the SDGs.

Types of Aid

Humanitarian aidAid given to save lives and alleviate suffering in response to emergencies — natural disasters (earthquakes, monsoons, prolonged drought) or human-made crises (wars, refugee crises), or medical crises such as an Ebola outbreak. Essentially short-term, but may be prolonged if the host government cannot take control of the crisis.
Development aidAid given by governments, multilateral organisations and NGOs to alleviate systemic poverty and promote economic, social, environmental or political development. Unlike humanitarian aid (short-term emergency response), development aid is long-term assistance responding to systematic problems.
Official Development Assistance (ODA)Defined by the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) as “government aid that promotes and specifically targets the economic development and welfare of developing countries”. Given either directly by a government (bilateral aid) or through a recognized multilateral institution such as the UN and its agencies, the World Bank or the IMF (multilateral aid). SDG Target 17.1 calls for developed countries to implement fully their ODA commitments, including the target of contributing 0.7% of GNI, as measured by the OECD DAC.

To count as ODA, aid must be provided by official agencies and must be “concessional” — either a grant or a soft loan (interest-free or below-market-rate, usually repaid over a longer period than commercial loans) — and must have the promotion of economic development and welfare of developing countries as its main objective.

What ODA excludes: military aidODA does not include military aid or aid given to pursue the donor’s own security interests. Example: a UK project to strengthen the Lebanese Armed Forces’ Land Border Regiment was deemed non-ODA eligible as it built the defence capacity of the partner’s armed forces; a Portuguese project providing health care to partner-country military personnel was also non-eligible. By contrast, an Austrian project to strengthen “Transnational Crime Units” fighting drug trafficking and organized crime in five West African countries was deemed ODA-eligible, since it helped partner-country police prevent and address crime for the benefit of the population as a whole.

Concerns about Aid

Concerns about the nature, disbursement and use of aid
  • If a recipient government does not have the welfare of the majority at heart, aid may go to a small sector of the population or economy that does not need it.
  • Aid is sometimes given for political rather than need-based reasons — developed countries tend to favour countries of political/economic interest, meaning the poorest people often receive less aid than people in middle-income countries.
  • Aid is often linked to the political views of the donor government; if these change (e.g. a change of government), it can have serious consequences for recipients.
  • Tied aid — bilateral aid given on condition that the recipient country spends the money on goods/services from the donor country. This means the recipient overpays compared to an open market, may reduce trade between developing countries, and calls into question the aid’s real motivation (it can act as a subsidy to donor-country industries). Tied aid has declined and was even made illegal in the UK in 2002.
  • Short-term food aid may be essential, but long-term provision of large quantities of food can depress domestic prices and hurt domestic farmers.
  • Aid can create a culture of dependency that limits long-term development, since the recipient government may have little incentive to implement its own strategies — particularly damaging if the donor country later reduces aid.
  • Aid is often focused on the modern industrial sector, potentially widening the gap between it and the traditional agricultural sector.
  • Aid is often conditional on adopting certain (usually free-market: liberalization, deregulation, privatization) economic policies, which some argue serve the interests of developed countries and their MNCs more than the recipient.
  • Even concessional loans must eventually be repaid, and repayments can lead to serious indebtedness problems.

Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs)

Key conceptsNGOs illustrate Equity (direct targeting of the poor), Sustainability and Economic Wellbeing.

NGOs are incredibly diverse in size, purpose, outlook, nationality, income and success, but their priority is generally to promote economic development, humanitarian ideals and sustainable development. Some receive government funding but claim to operate independently of government influence. Examples: Oxfam, CARE, Cafod, Greenpeace, Amnesty International, Global 2000, Doctors Without Borders (Médecins Sans Frontières).

NGOs carry out two main types of activity: operational activities (planning/implementing targeted projects) and advocacy activities (influencing public policy on poverty reduction, workers’ rights, human rights, the environment); some do both. Advocacy can create public pressure that affects official aid, and can influence consumer buying patterns towards better working conditions and sustainability. Working directly in the field, NGOs often develop a deeper understanding of the poor than official donors, can reach areas official aid cannot, and focus heavily on human capital — literacy programmes, health education, AIDS prevention, agricultural support, micro-credit, immunization and vocational training. Many focus particularly on women.

Concerns and criticisms of NGOs
  • NGOs rely on funding from private individuals, corporations, foundations and governments; what they can/can’t do depends on the source, so government funding can compromise independence. Example: in 2018 the US government banned aid organizations receiving US government money from providing (or even informing about) abortion services — expected to raise maternal deaths from unsafe illegal abortions.
  • Many NGOs operating in one country can produce uncoordinated, wasteful activity — e.g. drawing health workers away from other areas or from local government projects, undermining local government’s ability to manage issues itself.
  • NGOs may have a political or religious bias that shapes their activities and influences the communities they work in.
  • Unlike governments, NGOs are unaccountable — they are not elected by the people they represent.
  • NGOs are sometimes accused of spending disproportionately on advertising/self-promotion rather than on the actual projects.

Multilateral Development Assistance

Key concept: InterdependenceThe most well-known providers of multilateral assistance are the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

The World Bank

The World Bank comprises two closely linked organisations: the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) and the International Development Agency (IDA). Mission: “to end extreme poverty and promote shared prosperity in a sustainable way.”

  • IBRD: established after WWII to fund rebuilding of war-torn West European countries; now lends to middle-income, creditworthy developing countries. Funds are raised by issuing World Bank bonds on global capital markets, guaranteed by member states and the borrowing government; because the bonds are very safe, the IBRD can borrow (and thus lend) at relatively low interest rates.
  • IDA: founded 1960, works with the world’s poorest countries, providing grants and low/zero-interest loans — the poorer the country, the more favourable the loan conditions.

Historically the World Bank funded large-scale infrastructure (electricity generation, transportation); today it focuses more on smaller-scale projects directly targeting and benefiting the poorest people.

Did you know? The wider World Bank GroupBeyond the IBRD and IDA, three further organisations make up the World Bank Group: the International Finance Corporation (IFC) (1956) — promotes private sector investment by lending to private customers and giving technical assistance; the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA) (1988) — promotes FDI by selling political risk insurance to MNCs covering war, terrorism, asset confiscation and freezing of funds; and the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID) (1966) — facilitates settlement of investment disputes between member countries and individual international investors (over 150 members).

The International Monetary Fund (IMF)

Proposed at the 1944 Bretton Woods Agreements alongside the IBRD. Mission: “an organization of 189 countries, working to foster global monetary cooperation, secure financial stability, facilitate international trade, promote high employment and sustainable economic growth and reduce poverty around the world.” Its primary purpose is to ensure the stability of the international monetary system. Its mandate (updated 2012) covers all macroeconomic and financial-sector issues bearing on global stability.

Responsibilities set out in its Articles of Agreement: promoting international monetary cooperation; facilitating expansion and balanced growth of international trade; promoting exchange stability; assisting in establishing a multilateral system of payments; and making resources available (under safeguards) to members experiencing balance of payments difficulties.

Three IMF practices
  • Surveillance — annual in-depth reviews of each member’s economic performance, discussed with government, usually published to encourage transparency.
  • Technical assistance and training — usually free, in fiscal/monetary policy, exchange rate policy, banking, finance and statistics.
  • Financial assistance — loans to members with balance of payments problems, funded through a system of member “quotas” sized to each country’s economic size. Loans are conditional on the country implementing an agreed policy programme, with support continuing only if the policies are carried out. The IMF currently has approximately $1 trillion available for lending.

Working with the World Bank, the IMF provides concessional (zero-interest) lending through the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust and debt relief under the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative. In 2015 it created the Catastrophe Containment and Relief Trust, providing grants for debt-service relief to poor countries hit by natural disasters or public health emergencies — e.g. Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone received about $100 million in Ebola-related relief in early 2015.

Concerns about the World Bank and IMF
  • Both are headquartered in Washington D.C. and have been accused of adopting free-market, business-friendly policies that mainly benefit developed-country companies and high-income people, despite their stated poverty-reduction goals.
  • By tradition, the head of the World Bank is appointed by the American president, raising concern that policy is directed in US interests.
  • The IMF’s image is particularly damaged by conditions attached to its loans. Following the “Third World Debt Crisis” of the early 1980s, the IMF lent on condition that countries adopt free-market reforms known as Structural Adjustment Policies (SAP), reflecting the “Washington Consensus”:

Countries following SAPs may have been able to repay loans by pursuing free-market growth, but the consequences were widely seen as damaging to the poor and a failure in terms of economic development. The IMF’s approach has changed since the SAP era, but concerns remain.

Debt Relief

The IMF on why debt relief matters
  • Frees up resources for social spending — part of a larger effort (alongside aid flows) to address development needs while maintaining debt sustainability; for debt reduction to reduce poverty, the freed money must actually be spent on pro-poor programmes.
  • Boosts social spending — before the HIPC Initiative, eligible countries spent slightly more on debt service than on health and education combined; now such spending is about five times debt-service payments.
  • Reduces debt service — across 36 countries receiving debt relief, debt service paid fell about 1.5 percentage points of GDP between 2001 and 2015.
  • Improves public debt management — debt relief has improved debt positions of post-completion-point countries, though many remain vulnerable to export shocks, requiring cautious borrowing and stronger debt management going forward.
Debt is rising againDespite improvements, indebtedness appears to be rising again. A Jubilee Debt Campaign study of 126 developing countries found that, on average, countries were spending more than 10% of revenues on interest payments — the highest level since before the G7’s 2005 agreement to cancel the debts of the world’s poorest countries. There are calls for greater accountability from private lenders to developing countries to prevent renewed debt accumulation.

Intervention versus Market-Oriented Approaches: Synthesis

Strategies to achieve growth and development can be grouped into two broad categories: interventionist strategies and market-oriented strategies. All are employed to achieve growth and development, but economic growth can come at the cost of economic development, and both can come at the cost of sustainability. The overall conclusion is that for any country, regardless of national income, a complementary package of both types of policy should be adopted when approaching any economic issue, in order to achieve inclusive, sustainable economic development.
