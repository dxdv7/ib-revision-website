What is price elasticity of supply (PES)?

If the price of a product rises, producers will want to increase the quantity they supply to raise profits. How much they are actually able to increase quantity supplied depends on the price elasticity of supply for that product.

Key concept: ChangeDefinition: price elasticity of supply is a measure of how much the supply of a product changes when there is a change in the price of the product.
PES = Percentage change in quantity supplied of the product ÷ Percentage change in price of the product

To calculate PES, first find the percentage change in quantity supplied, using (change in quantity supplied ÷ original quantity supplied) × 100, and the percentage change in price, using (change in price ÷ original price) × 100. PES is then the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. Because price and quantity supplied normally move in the same direction, PES for a normal supply curve comes out as a positive number.

Worked example – magazine publisherA publishing firm realises it can now sell its monthly magazine for $5.50 instead of $5.00. In response it increases supply from 200,000 to 230,000 magazines per month.
  1. Percentage change in price = (+50 ÷ 500) × 100 = +10%
  2. Percentage change in quantity supplied = (+30,000 ÷ 200,000) × 100 = +15%
  3. PES = 15% ÷ 10% = 1.5

The range of values of price elasticity of supply

PES values range from zero to infinity (unlike PED, examples of both extremes are common in economics).

Figure 6.1: A perfectly inelastic supply curve (PES = 0)Vertical axis: price of product ($), horizontal axis: quantity supplied. The supply curve S is a perfectly vertical line at quantity Q. No matter whether price is P1, P2, or any other price, quantity supplied remains fixed at Q — supply is completely unresponsive to price. Since zero divided by any percentage change in price is zero, PES = 0.

This is typical of the very short run (“the immediate period”), where it is impossible for firms to increase supply at all, no matter what happens to price, until additional factors of production can be employed. Example: the number of tickets available for a sporting/entertainment venue with a fixed maximum capacity — e.g. Manchester United’s home stadium, Old Trafford, currently seats 74,944, so supply of tickets is perfectly inelastic at a quantity of 74,944; no price change can affect that quantity.

Figure 6.2: A perfectly elastic supply curve (PES = infinity)Vertical axis: price of product ($), horizontal axis: quantity supplied. The supply curve S is a perfectly horizontal line at price P1. At price P1, the supply curve extends indefinitely — quantity supplied is infinite. If price falls below P1, even by the smallest amount, quantity supplied falls to zero. Since any percentage change in price divided into an infinite change in quantity supplied yields infinity, PES = infinity.

In international trade, it is often assumed that the supply of a commodity (e.g. wheat) available for import to a single small country is infinite, because that country’s consumers can buy as much as they want as long as they pay the current world market price. The market facing that one country is therefore perfectly elastic at the current world price.

Categories of PES for normal products

Normal products have PES values between zero and infinity, split into three categories:

  • Inelastic supply: PES is greater than zero but less than one. A change in price leads to a smaller (less than proportionate) percentage change in quantity supplied.
  • Elastic supply: PES is greater than one (up to infinity). A change in price leads to a greater than proportionate percentage change in quantity supplied.
  • Unit elastic supply: PES is exactly equal to one. A change in price leads to a proportionate (equal percentage) change in quantity supplied.
Figure 6.3: Supply curves with different PES valuesVertical axis: price of product; horizontal axis: quantity supplied. Several straight-line supply curves are drawn from different starting points:
  • Curves S1 and S2 both pass through the origin — any straight-line supply curve through the origin has PES = 1 (unit elastic) along its entire length, because the percentage change in price always exactly equals the percentage change in quantity supplied.
  • Curve S3 starts from a point on the x-axis (quantity axis) — any straight-line supply curve starting from the x-axis has PES < 1 (inelastic) along its entire length, because the percentage change in price is always greater than the percentage change in quantity supplied.
  • Curve S4 starts from a point on the y-axis (price axis) — any straight-line supply curve starting from the y-axis has PES > 1 (elastic) along its entire length, because the percentage change in quantity supplied is always greater than the percentage change in price.

The determinants of price elasticity of supply

Key concept: EfficiencyDifferent products have different PES values — e.g. cans of soft drink might have PES ≈ 2 (elastic), while electricity might have PES ≈ 0.5 (inelastic). Several factors determine the actual PES value for a product.

1. How much costs rise as output is increased

If total costs rise significantly as a producer attempts to increase supply, the producer will be reluctant to raise supply much — supply will be relatively inelastic, since it would take large price rises to make increasing supply worthwhile. If total costs do not rise significantly, the producer will raise quantity supplied readily to take advantage of low-cost profit opportunities — supply will be more elastic.

Two factors help prevent a significant rise in costs:

  • The existence of unused capacity — if a firm has significant spare productive capacity, it can increase output easily and without a great rise in costs, so PES will be relatively elastic. If a firm is already producing at (or near) capacity, increasing supply requires a costly expansion of productive resources, so PES will be relatively inelastic.
  • The mobility of factors of production — if factors of production can easily be moved from one productive use to another, PES will be relatively elastic. Example: it is easy to shift a factory’s production from one-litre to two-litre plastic bottles, so if the price of two-litre bottles rises, the producer can quickly and cheaply increase the quantity supplied of the larger bottles.

2. The time period considered

The longer the time period considered, the more elastic supply will tend to be.

  • In the immediate period, firms cannot really increase supply at all, if at all, since they cannot immediately increase the number of factors of production they employ — PES is very inelastic.
  • In the short run, firms may be able to increase some factors (e.g. raw materials and labour) but not all factors (e.g. the number of machines or the size of the factory) — PES is more elastic than in the immediate period.
  • In the long run, firms can increase the quantity of all factors employed — PES is much more elastic.

3. The ability to store stock

If a firm can hold high levels of stock (inventories) of its product, it can react swiftly to price increases by releasing stock onto the market — PES will be relatively elastic.

Higher Level

PES for primary commodities vs manufactured goods

Key concept: SustainabilityPrimary commodities (raw materials) tend to have both inelastic demand (few or no substitutes, seen as necessities — covered in Chapter 4) and inelastic supply.

Commodities tend to have inelastic PES because a change in price cannot be met with a proportionate change in quantity supplied. Example: if demand for cocoa rises, raising its price, producers cannot proportionately increase quantity supplied quickly — it takes time to grow more cocoa, and it might not even be possible or desirable to reallocate more resources into cocoa production. Similarly, if the price of cocoa fell, quantity supplied would not adjust down accordingly if the crop has already been harvested.

Manufactured goods tend to have more elastic supply, since it is easier to increase or decrease the quantity supplied in response to a price change. This connects directly to the determinants above: manufacturing industries are more likely to have unused capacity, more mobile factors of production, and the ability to store high levels of stock — any or all of which make supply relatively elastic.
