What is equilibrium to an economist?

Key concept: InterdependenceConsumers and producers cannot make their choices in isolation. Bringing demand and supply together in one model illustrates their interdependence.
Definition: EquilibriumA state of rest, self-perpetuating in the absence of any outside disturbance. Example: a book lying on a desk is in equilibrium — unless someone moves it (an “outside disturbance”), it stays at rest. If someone does move it, it is in disequilibrium until it settles somewhere else, at which point it is in a new equilibrium.

Economists study how equilibria change and why, using this information to predict changes that particular actions may cause, and to help formulate economic policy.

Figure 7.1: The market for coffeeVertical axis: price of coffee ($); horizontal axis: quantity of coffee (kg). A downward-sloping demand curve D and upward-sloping supply curve S intersect at a single point, giving equilibrium price P1 and equilibrium quantity Q1. At P1, the quantity demanded exactly equals the quantity supplied. P1 is called the market-clearing price because everything produced is sold. The market stays at this point, in every time period, until there is an “outside disturbance”.

Excess supply, excess demand, and the self-righting market

Figure 7.2: The market for coffee — disequilibrium and adjustmentTwo diagrams, each with price of coffee ($) on the vertical axis and quantity of coffee (kg) on the horizontal axis, both showing the same D and S curves crossing at equilibrium (Pe, Qe).
(a) Producers raise price above equilibrium, to P1: at P1, quantity demanded falls to Qd while quantity supplied rises to Qs. There is now excess supply of Qs − Qd (more is supplied than demanded at that price). To eliminate the surplus, producers must lower the price; as they do, quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls until the two are equal again at the original equilibrium price. The market is self-righting.
(b) Producers lower price below equilibrium, to P2: at P2, quantity demanded rises to Qd while quantity supplied falls to Qs. There is now excess demand of Qd − Qs (a shortage). To eliminate the shortage, producers must raise the price; as they do, quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises until they are equal again at the original equilibrium.

In both cases, if price is pushed away from equilibrium for no external (“outside disturbance”) reason, the market’s own forces push it back to the original equilibrium price — this is what it means to say the equilibrium is “self-righting”.

Real-world illustrations mentioned: a fall in diamond prices linked to producers facing too much supply (excess supply pushing price down); and concerns about whether metals supply can keep up with rising electric-vehicle demand (a potential source of excess demand and rising prices).

How the equilibrium changes when there are shifts in demand and supply

Key concept: ChangeThe equilibrium can be moved by any “outside disturbance” — that is, a change in any determinant of demand or supply other than the price of the product itself, which shifts one or both curves.

Example: an increase in income for consumers of a normal good, foreign holidays. Rising income increases demand for holidays, shifting the demand curve right, ceteris paribus.

Figure 7.3: The market for foreign holidaysVertical axis: price per holiday ($); horizontal axis: quantity of holidays (days). Initially D and S intersect at (Pe, Qe). Demand shifts right from D to D1 (due to the rise in income). Price initially remains at Pe, where Qe continues to be supplied but quantity demanded has risen to Q1 — this creates excess demand of Q1 − Qe at the old price. To eliminate this excess demand, price must rise; it keeps rising until it reaches the new equilibrium price Pe1, where the new equilibrium quantity Qe1 is both demanded and supplied along D1 and S.

General rule: whenever there is a shift of the demand or supply curve, the market, if left to act alone, will adjust to a new equilibrium, market-clearing price.

The price mechanism and its functions

The forces of supply and demand moving markets to equilibrium are together known as the “price mechanism”. Price performs three significant functions in helping to allocate scarce resources:

Key concept: ScarcityPrices help ration the world’s scarce resources among competing uses.
Key concept: ChoicePrices act as signals and incentives that guide the choices consumers and producers make in the market.
  1. The signalling function: the price of a good is an important piece of information for both consumers and producers. Prices are set by the actions of consumers and producers in a market, and so they reflect changing circumstances in that market, acting as a signal to guide how people should act.
  2. The rationing function: resources are scarce and need to be allocated through some mechanism. If demand for a good significantly exceeds supply, price rations the limited supply to those consumers who are prepared to pay the (relatively high) price.
  3. The incentive function: prices act as an incentive for both consumers and producers. Lower prices give consumers an incentive to buy more of a good, since they receive more utility (satisfaction) relative to the money spent; higher prices act as a disincentive, since utility relative to money spent falls. For producers, a rise in price (e.g. due to a rise in demand) signals that consumers want more of the good; since producers are assumed rational profit-maximizers, the higher price gives them an incentive to supply more, because they can make more profit doing so.

Resources are allocated, and re-allocated, in response to changes in price. There is no central planning agency directly telling producers to produce more of a good — it is the rise in price itself that signals to producers and creates the incentive for them to produce more. This is often referred to, following Adam Smith, as an “invisible hand” moving the factors of production around the economy to produce the goods and services that buyers want.

The assumptions behind the model

Assumptions and their limitsEconomic models rest on assumptions. Behind the theory of demand is the assumption that consumers act rationally, seeking to maximize their own utility, making all decisions with perfect information. Behind the theory of supply is the assumption that producers act rationally, seeking to maximize their profits. If these assumptions are challenged (e.g. consumers do not have perfect information, or firms do not simply maximize profit), then conclusions drawn from the standard demand-and-supply model may have only limited validity. This is a theme to which the subject returns repeatedly when evaluating market outcomes.

Market efficiency: consumer and producer surplus

Key concept: EfficiencyConsider a market for an imaginary product, “thingies”.
Figure 7.4: The market for thingiesVertical axis: price per thingy ($), from 0 to 20; horizontal axis: quantity of thingies per week. Downward-sloping demand curve D and upward-sloping supply curve S intersect at equilibrium price $10 and equilibrium quantity 10 thingies per week.

At the equilibrium point, some consumers would have been prepared to pay a higher price for their thingies than $10. For example, at a price of $15 there would still be five thingies demanded, and at $17 there would be demand for three. However, consumers do not have to pay $15 or $17 — they only pay the equilibrium price of $10. This means every consumer who buys one of the first nine thingies (i.e. all units except the very last, marginal one) has made a gain: they paid a price below the one they were prepared to pay.

Definition: Consumer surplusThe extra satisfaction (or utility) gained by consumers from paying a price that is lower than the price they were prepared to pay. One consumer might have been willing to pay as much as $19 for a thingy but only has to pay $10, so gains $9 of surplus on that unit. Consumer surplus is shown graphically as the area under the demand curve and above the equilibrium price.
Figure 7.5: Consumer surplus in the market for thingiesSame axes and curves as Figure 7.4. The consumer surplus is the shaded triangular area labelled abc: bounded above by the demand curve D, below by the horizontal equilibrium price line at $10, and on the right by the vertical line up to the equilibrium quantity (10). This triangle sits above the price line and below the demand curve, between the price axis and the equilibrium quantity.

Symmetrically, at the equilibrium point some production of thingies would have taken place even at a price lower than $10. For example, at a price of $5 there would still be five thingies supplied, and at $3 there would be three thingies supplied. But the producer does not have to sell at $5 or $3 — she sells at the equilibrium price of $10 for every unit, so she gains on each of the first nine thingies (all but the last, marginal unit), receiving a price higher than the one she was prepared to accept.

Definition: Producer surplusThe excess of actual earnings a producer makes from a given quantity of output, over and above the amount the producer would have been prepared to accept for that output. The producer might be willing to supply a thingy for as little as $1, but receives $10 — a gain of $9 on that unit. Producer surplus is shown graphically as the area under (below) the equilibrium price and above the supply curve.
Figure 7.6: Producer surplus in the market for thingiesSame axes and curves as Figure 7.4. The producer surplus is the shaded triangular area labelled bcd: bounded below by the supply curve S, above by the horizontal equilibrium price line at $10, and on the right by the vertical line up to the equilibrium quantity (10). This triangle sits below the price line and above the supply curve.

Community (social) surplus and allocative efficiency

Key concept: EfficiencyWhen a market is in equilibrium, with no external influences and no external effects, it is said to be socially efficient or in a state of allocative efficiency: resources are allocated in the way that is most efficient from society’s point of view.
Allocative efficiency vs productive efficiencyThis meaning of “efficiency” differs from the everyday meaning. In everyday language, someone or something operating efficiently produces the maximum output with a given level of input — in economics this kind of efficiency is called productive efficiency (addressed later, at Higher Level). Allocative efficiency is a different, society-wide concept about whether resources are directed to their most valuable uses.
Figure 7.7: Community surplusVertical axis: price ($); horizontal axis: quantity (’000s). Demand curve D is relabelled as MSB (marginal social benefit) = marginal utility, and supply curve S is relabelled as MSC (marginal social cost). They intersect at equilibrium price P and quantity Q. The consumer surplus is the shaded triangle above price P and below D/MSB; the producer surplus is the shaded triangle below price P and above S/MSC. Together, consumer surplus + producer surplus = community (social) surplus — the total benefit to society, maximized exactly at this equilibrium point where MSB = MSC. This is the point of allocative efficiency: given this demand and supply, no other combination of price and quantity on the diagram could produce a greater community surplus — consumers might prefer lower prices and producers might prefer higher prices, but the actual equilibrium price and quantity are the allocatively efficient ones, because they maximize community surplus.

More precise language: the supply curve for an industry is largely determined by the industry’s costs of production. When we assume these costs of production to the firm are equal to the costs to society as a whole, the supply curve represents the marginal social cost curve (MSC). The demand curve is determined by the utility, or benefits, that consumption brings to consumers; when we assume the benefits in the market are equivalent to the benefits to society, the demand curve represents the marginal social benefit curve (MSB).

Conclusion: allocative efficiencyA free market leads to allocative efficiency. Community surplus is maximized, giving the optimum allocation of resources from society’s point of view. This occurs precisely where demand equals supply, i.e. where marginal social benefit (MSB) equals marginal social cost (MSC).
Higher Level

How to calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus

In examinations you may be asked to calculate the area of consumer surplus and/or producer surplus from a demand and supply diagram. This is done in three steps.

Figure 7.8: Calculating consumer and producer surplusVertical axis: price ($), from 0 to 5; horizontal axis: quantity, from 0 to 2,000 (marked at 500, 1000, 1500, 2000). Downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve intersect at equilibrium price $3 and equilibrium quantity 900 units. Consumer surplus is the shaded triangle above the $3 price line and below the demand curve; producer surplus is the shaded triangle below the $3 price line and above the supply curve.
  1. Identify the equilibrium price and quantity demanded and supplied — where the demand and supply curves intersect. In Figure 7.8, this is a quantity of 900 units supplied and demanded at a price of $3.
  2. Calculate the consumer surplus. This is the area of the triangle above the equilibrium price and below the demand curve. The area of a right-angled triangle = ½ × base × height. Here, the base of the triangle is 900 units and the height is $2. So area = (½ × 900) × $2 = $900. This is the total amount consumers were willing to pay but did not have to.
  3. Calculate the producer surplus. This is the area of the triangle below the equilibrium price and above the supply curve — a right-angled triangle turned upside down but with the same base (900 units) and height ($2). Area = (½ × 900) × $2 = $900. This is the extra earnings producers received over and above the minimum they would have accepted.

In this particular example, consumer surplus and producer surplus happen to be equal, but this is not always the case — the relative sizes of consumer and producer surplus depend on the relative price elasticities of demand and supply for the product (a market with relatively more inelastic demand than supply, for example, will tend to show a larger consumer surplus relative to producer surplus, and vice versa).

A special note about commodity prices and price volatility

Commodities (raw materials) tend to have both low price elasticity of demand and low price elasticity of supply. This combination of relatively inelastic demand and inelastic supply for commodities means that any changes in the demand or supply of a commodity will result in large swings in price — this phenomenon is referred to as “price volatility”.

Worked illustration – copper: in April 2010, the price of copper reached around $8,000 per tonne, its highest level since August 2008. The previous year, the price had fallen as low as around $3,000 per tonne. Thus, within about two years, there were price fluctuations of more than 150%. Part of the reason for such large fluctuations lies in the low elasticities of both supply and demand.

Figure 7.9: The market for copperVertical axis: price of copper ($ per tonne), horizontal axis: quantity of copper (thousands of tonnes). The supply curve S is drawn steep (relatively inelastic). A rise in demand (largely driven by demand from China) shifts the demand curve right, from D to D1. Because supply is inelastic (steep), this rightward shift in demand causes a very large increase in price (from around $3,000 to around $8,000 per tonne) but only a relatively small increase in quantity — illustrating how inelastic supply amplifies price volatility when demand shifts.

The textbook also reproduces a historical chart of copper prices (in US$ per pound) from 2009 to 2018, showing sharp rises and falls over the period — a real-world illustration of the same price-volatility phenomenon over a longer run of years.

Winners and losers from volatilityFor copper producers and copper-producing countries, upswings in price can generate very large revenue gains, but the same producers are correspondingly vulnerable to the inevitable downswings in price. The volatility of commodity prices, and the vulnerability of commodity producers to it, is a recurring theme returned to later in the course (e.g. in the context of developing countries dependent on commodity exports).
