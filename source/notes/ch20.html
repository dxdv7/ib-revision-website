1. What is inflation, and what does it cost us?

DefinitionInflation is a persistent increase in the average price level, usually measured through a consumer price index (CPI). A single, one-off price rise for one good is not inflation.

Costs of high inflation

  • Loss of purchasing power — hits people on fixed incomes or weak bargaining power hardest.
  • Saving — if inflation exceeds the nominal interest rate, the real rate turns negative and savers lose purchasing power.
  • Growth — a “flight to assets” (houses, gold) leaves fewer savings for firms to borrow and invest.
  • Interest rates — banks raise nominal rates to protect real returns, raising borrowing costs economy-wide.
  • Competitiveness — higher relative inflation makes exports pricier and imports cheaper, risking the trade balance.
  • Uncertainty — firms may delay investment.
  • Labour unrest — disputes when wages don't keep pace.
Real interest rate = Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate

The nominal interest rate is the rate actually quoted by a bank or lender, before adjusting for inflation, while the inflation rate is the percentage rise in the general price level over the same period. Subtracting the inflation rate from the nominal rate gives the real interest rate — the true change in purchasing power a saver or lender gains, or a borrower gives up, once the erosion of money’s value by inflation is taken into account.

Worked example

A bank advertises a savings account paying a nominal interest rate of 6% per year, and inflation over that year turns out to be 4%. Real interest rate = 6% − 4% = 2%. So although the saver’s account balance grows by 6% in money terms, their actual purchasing power — what they can buy with that money — rises by only about 2%.

2. How is inflation measured?

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) tracks the price of a representative “basket” of goods and services, with each category given a weight reflecting its share of typical household spending (e.g. Housing & household goods ≈29.8%, Transport ≈12.4%).

Inflation rate = [(IndexX+1 − IndexX) ÷ IndexX] × 100

IndexX is the value of the price index (e.g. the CPI) in an earlier period, and IndexX+1 is its value one period later (e.g. a year later). Subtracting the earlier index value from the later one gives the change in the index; dividing by the earlier value converts this into a proportion of the starting price level; and multiplying by 100 expresses it as a percentage — the inflation rate for that period.

Worked example

Suppose a country’s CPI stood at 108 in January 2023 (IndexX) and had risen to 112.5 by January 2024 (IndexX+1). Inflation rate = [(112.5 − 108) ÷ 108] × 100 = [4.5 ÷ 108] × 100 ≈ 4.17%. This means the average level of prices facing consumers rose by about 4.17% over that year.

Problems with the CPI

  • Not everyone has the same “basket” — a family with children spends very differently to a retired couple.
  • Regional variation is hidden by the national average.
  • Sampling and data-collection errors, since it's impossible to survey every household.
  • Quality changes (an upgraded product isn't really “inflation”).
  • International comparisons are difficult — different countries use different baskets and methods.
  • Volatile swings (seasonal food, oil shocks) distort the headline rate — “core inflation” strips these out.

3. What causes inflation? Demand-pull vs. cost-push

Demand-pull: a rise in aggregate demand “pulls up” the price level along SRAS — both price and output rise.

Figure 20.1: Demand-pull inflationAn increase in aggregate demand from AD1 to AD2 moves the economy up along the SRAS curve. The average price level rises from P1 to P2 and real output rises from Y1 to Y2.
The monetarist viewInflation is always ultimately caused by excessive growth of the money supply — more money chasing the same amount of goods.

Cost-push: rising production costs shift SRAS left — price rises but output falls. Causes: wage-push inflation, rising domestic raw material costs, import-push inflation (including from a weaker exchange rate, which makes everything imported more expensive).

Figure 20.2: Cost-push inflationHigher costs of production shift short-run aggregate supply left from SRAS1 to SRAS2. Against the unchanged AD curve the price level rises from P1 to P2 while real output falls from Y1 to Y2.

Inflationary spiral: rising AD raises prices (demand-pull) → workers negotiate higher wages to compensate, shifting SRAS left and pushing prices up further (cost-push) → if workers then spend their higher wages, AD rises again — inflation perpetuates itself.

Figure 20.3: Demand-pull inflationThe first round of a possible inflationary spiral: aggregate demand rises from AD1 to AD2 along SRAS1, lifting the price level from P1 to P2 and output from Y1 to Y2.
Figure 20.4: An inflationary spiralMovement (1) is demand-pull: AD rises from AD1 to AD2, taking the price level from P1 to P2 and output from Y1 to Y2. Tight labour markets push wages up, so (2) SRAS shifts left to SRAS2, raising the price level to P3 and pushing output back to Y1. Higher wages are spent, so (3) AD rises to AD3, taking prices to P4 and output to Y2 again. The wage-price cycle can keep repeating.

4. How can governments reduce inflation?

Demand-pull: contractionary fiscal or monetary policy. Cost-push: also usually contractionary policy, since supply-side policy works too slowly to help in the short run.

Problems: politically unpopular; fiscal policy has long time lags; using contractionary policy against cost-push inflation can push unemployment even higher on top of falling real GDP — risking stagflation.

Central bank independence & inflation targetingAn independent central bank with an explicit inflation target can make politically painful decisions (like raising rates) without worrying about re-election. A credible target also anchors inflationary expectations, which itself helps keep cost-push pressure down.
The monetarist prescriptionKeep money supply growth in line with real output growth (e.g. output growing 3% → money supply should grow about 3%). Hard for central banks to control precisely in practice.
The recurring policy trade-offFighting inflation by cutting AD may raise unemployment. Fighting unemployment (or boosting growth) by raising AD may fuel inflation.

5. Deflation — the “good” and the “bad”

Deflation is a persistent fall in the price level — don't confuse it with disinflation, where prices are still rising, just more slowly each year.

Figure 20.6: Changing rates of inflation and deflationHypothetical inflation rates: 1.8% (1999), 3.1% (2000), 2.4%, 1.5%, 0.6% (2003), then −0.7% (2004) and −0.2% (2005). From 2001 to 2003 the rate is falling but still positive, which is disinflation; in 2004 and 2005 it is below zero, which is deflation (the price level itself is falling).
“Good” deflation“Bad” deflation
From the supply side: better technology/resources shift LRAS right, so output rises and prices fall — genuine growth.From the demand side: a fall in AD means output falls, prices fall, and unemployment rises.
Figure 20.5: “Good” deflationBetter or more plentiful factors of production shift long-run aggregate supply right from LRAS1 to LRAS2. Against the fixed AD curve, real output rises from Y1 to Y2 while the price level falls from P1 to P2: deflation with growth rather than recession.

Costs of “bad” deflation: unemployment from falling AD; deferred consumption (buyers wait for lower prices, deepening the spiral); falling confidence; less investment; the real value of debt rises, making loans harder to repay; the “zero lower bound” leaves little room to cut interest rates further.

7. The Phillips curve

The original Phillips curve (1958) found an inverse relationship between the rate of change of money wages and the unemployment rate (UK data, 1861–1913).

Figure 20.7: The original Phillips curvePhillips’s original relationship: the rate of change of money wages falls as the unemployment rate rises. The convex curve is steep at low unemployment (wages rise quickly) and flattens as unemployment grows, crossing zero wage growth at a moderate rate of unemployment.

The short-run Phillips curve (SRPC) substitutes the inflation rate for wage growth, showing a genuine trade-off: lower unemployment comes with higher inflation, and vice versa.

Figure 20.8: The Phillips curve as it is usually drawnThe short-run Phillips curve trades unemployment against inflation. Moving up the curve from A (6% unemployment, 3% inflation) to B (4% unemployment, 7% inflation) lowers unemployment by 2 percentage points but raises inflation by 4 points; moving back down reverses the trade-off.
Figure 20.9: Phillips curve relationship through AD/AS analysisThe same trade-off seen in AD/AS terms: a rise in aggregate demand from AD1 to AD2 raises real output from Y1 to Y2 (so unemployment falls) and the price level from P1 to P2 (so inflation rises), a movement up the short-run Phillips curve.
Stagflation broke the modelThe SRPC trade-off held up until the 1970s. After that, many economies suffered high inflation and high unemployment at once — something a simple downward-sloping trade-off can't explain.

Milton Friedman's long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment: no permanent trade-off. Using expansionary policy to push unemployment below the natural rate only works temporarily.

Figure 20.10: The long-run Phillips curveStart at A on SRPC1: unemployment is at the natural rate of 5% and inflation is 3%. Expansionary policy moves the economy along SRPC1 to B (3% unemployment, 7% inflation). When workers see through the money illusion and expect higher inflation, the curve shifts up to SRPC2 and unemployment returns to 5% at C (7% inflation). Repeating the policy gives D (3%, 11%) and then E (5%, 11%) on SRPC3. Joining A, C and E gives the vertical long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) at the natural rate: no long-run trade-off.
Money illusionWorkers initially take new jobs for higher nominal wages without realizing their real wages haven't actually risen (prices rose too). Once they realize, they demand pay rises in line with the new, higher inflation — unemployment drifts back to the natural rate, but at higher inflation. Repeating the trick just raises inflation further with no lasting employment gain.
Figure 20.11: Supply-side policies can reduce the NRUEffective supply-side policies, such as better education and training or more flexible labour markets, shift the long-run Phillips curve left from LRPC1 to LRPC2. The natural rate of unemployment falls from NRU1 to NRU2 at any given rate of inflation.
Key takeawayDemand management can reduce cyclical unemployment in the short run, but cannot reduce the natural rate (structural + frictional + seasonal). Only supply-side policy can shift the LRPC itself to the left.
