What causes short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) to shift?

Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) shows the total output that producers in an economy are willing to supply at each average price level in the short run (the period in which at least one factor of production, such as the wage rate, is fixed/sticky). Any change in the costs of production across the whole economy will shift the SRAS curve. These economy-wide changes in production costs are often called supply-side shocks. A fall in costs shifts SRAS to the right (an increase in short-run aggregate supply); a rise in costs shifts SRAS to the left (a decrease).

Figure 15.1: The curved SRAS curveOne common way of drawing the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve is as a curve that gets progressively steeper: at low levels of output there is plenty of spare capacity, so extra output can be produced without pushing up costs and prices much, but as output rises towards full capacity, producers must bid for increasingly scarce factors of production, so each further increase in output requires a bigger rise in the price level.
Figure 15.2: The straight-line SRAS curveFor simplicity, textbooks and exam diagrams often draw SRAS instead as a straight upward-sloping line: a rise in real output (Y1 to Y2) is always accompanied by a proportional rise in the average price level (P1 to P2). This is the version of SRAS used in most of the diagrams in this chapter.
Figure 15.3: Shifts in SRASAxes: average price level (vertical) against real output, Y (horizontal). The SRAS curve is upward sloping. An increase in SRAS is shown as a rightward shift, SRAS1 → SRAS2 (more output supplied at every price level, caused by falling production costs). A decrease in SRAS is shown as a leftward shift, SRAS1 → SRAS3 (less output supplied at every price level, caused by rising production costs).

Typical causes of changes in the costs of production (and therefore of SRAS shifts) include:

  • A change in wage rates: higher wages raise firms’ costs of production and shift SRAS left. For example, a government-imposed rise in the legal minimum wage increases labour costs; strong trade unions negotiating higher wages (particularly in manufacturing) would have the same effect.
  • A change in the cost of raw materials: a rise in the price of a raw material used narrowly (e.g. rubber) may have little effect on SRAS overall, but a rise in the price of a widely-used input such as oil affects almost all industries and shifts SRAS noticeably.
  • A change in the price of imports: if capital or raw materials are imported, a rise in import prices raises costs of production. This can happen because of exchange rate changes – for example, if the euro depreciates, imported raw materials and capital become relatively more expensive for European producers, raising their costs.
  • A change in government indirect taxes or subsidies: an increase in indirect tax raises firms’ costs of production and shifts SRAS left; a fall in indirect tax shifts SRAS right. Conversely, an increase in government subsidies reduces firms’ costs and shifts SRAS right, while a decrease in subsidies shifts SRAS left. Because indirect taxes apply to the majority of goods and services, a change in the general rate of indirect taxation has a clear, economy-wide effect on SRAS. In contrast, a subsidy granted to a single product will have minimal impact on SRAS unless that product is essential and very widely used (e.g. fuel).

What happens when AD meets SRAS in the short run?

The economy operates where aggregate demand (AD) equals aggregate supply (AS). At the resulting average price level, all the output produced is fully consumed – there is no incentive for producers to raise or lower output or prices.

Figure 15.4: Short-run macroeconomic equilibriumAxes: average price level (vertical) against real output, Y (horizontal). An upward-sloping SRAS curve and a downward-sloping AD curve intersect at one point. This intersection determines the equilibrium price level (PL) and equilibrium real output (Y). At this point aggregate demand exactly equals aggregate supply, so there is no upward or downward pressure on prices or output.

The long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) debate

There is considerable disagreement among economists about the shape of the long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve. Two broad “schools of thought” exist: the new classical (or monetarist) LRAS, and a rival model developed from the ideas of John Maynard Keynes, known as the Keynesian AS. The different shapes underpin real disagreements about what policies governments should use.

What does new classical LRAS look like?

New classical economists include several related groups – monetarists, “supply-side economists”, and economists of the Austrian school. They share a belief in the efficiency of market forces and the view that government intervention in the economy should be kept to an absolute minimum.

The new classical LRAS curveIn this view the LRAS curve is perfectly inelastic (vertical) at the “full-employment level of output”, denoted Yf. This is the maximum potential output the economy can produce if operating at full capacity. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment (this is explained later in the unemployment topic). Potential output depends entirely on the quantity and quality (productivity) of the factors of production, not on the price level – so LRAS is completely independent of the price level.
Figure 15.5: New classical LRAS curveAxes: average price level (vertical) against real output, Y (horizontal). LRAS is drawn as a single vertical straight line at output level Yf. The price level can rise from P1 to P2 (shown as dashed horizontal lines), but the level of real output does not change – it stays fixed at Yf regardless of the price level.

What does Keynesian AS look like?

The Keynesian AS curve has a distinctive shape with three phases, and does not sharply distinguish between the short run and the long run the way the new classical model does.

Figure 15.6: Keynesian AS curve (three phases)Axes: average price level (vertical) against real output, Y (horizontal). The curve is flat (perfectly elastic) at low output, then curves upward, then becomes vertical at high output – an elongated “J”/hockey-stick shape read left to right.
  • Phase (1) – perfectly elastic: at low levels of economic activity, AS is horizontal. Producers can raise output without incurring higher average costs, because there is “spare capacity” in the economy – high levels of unemployed labour and underutilized capital that can be brought into use at constant average cost.
  • Phase (2) – upward sloping: as the economy approaches its potential output (Yf) the spare capacity is gradually “used up”. Available factors of production become increasingly scarce, so as producers try to raise output further they must bid up prices to attract increasingly scarce factors. Higher factor prices mean higher costs of production, so the price level must rise to compensate producers – giving the upward-sloping portion of AS.
  • Phase (3) – perfectly inelastic (vertical): once the economy reaches full capacity (Yf), it is impossible to raise output further because all factors of production are fully employed. AS becomes vertical. This vertical segment corresponds exactly to the new classical LRAS.

Shifts in AS/LRAS and supply-side growth

A country’s factors of production are constantly changing, so we would normally expect steady increases in a country’s AS/LRAS over time. An outward shift of AS/LRAS represents an illustration of potential economic growth – the economy’s productive potential has increased. This is directly analogous to an outward shift of the economy’s production possibilities curve (PPC).

Figure 15.7: A shift in the LRAS curve(a) Keynesian perspective: the whole hockey-stick-shaped AS curve shifts to the right, from AS1 to AS2 (axes: average price level vertical, real output horizontal). (b) New classical perspective: the vertical LRAS line shifts rightward, from LRAS1 to LRAS2 (same axes). In both views, a rightward shift means the economy can now produce more output at every price level – its productive potential (full-employment output) has increased.
Figure 15.8: An increase in productive potential and the PPCAxes: consumer goods and services (vertical) against capital goods (horizontal). The production possibilities curve shifts outward from PPC1 to PPC2, showing that the economy can now produce more of both types of goods than before. This outward shift of the PPC is equivalent to a rightward shift of the AS/LRAS curve – both represent an increase in the economy’s productive capacity.

An increase in AS/LRAS occurs when there is an improvement in the quality of the factors of production (an increase in productivity – output per unit of input) or an increase in the quantity of factors of production. Technological advances are especially important on the supply side, since they can improve both the quantity and quality of resources.

Many of these supply-side improvements arise naturally through market forces and the profit motive: students study engineering, science or entrepreneurship because of the incentive of higher wages; immigrants are attracted to a country by the incentive of a higher standard of living; businesses invest in research and development because of the desire to earn higher profits (and raw-material producers are incentivized to develop improved resource-extraction technologies for the same reason).

Key concept: InterventionRegardless of how much market forces alone contribute to increases in the quantity/quality of factors of production, governments also play an important role. Policies a government uses to increase the quantity or improve the quality of factors of production are known as supply-side policies, divided into interventionist policies and market-based policies (covered in full in Chapter 18).
