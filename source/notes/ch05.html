What is supply?

Definition: SupplySupply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to supply at different prices in a given time period. For example, firms may be willing and able to produce 4,000 frozen pizzas per week at a price of $3 each — so their supply of frozen pizzas at $3 is 4,000 units per week.

The key phrase is “willingness and ability”, exactly as with demand. It is not enough for producers to be willing to produce a good or service; they must also have the financial means to supply it — the ability to supply. This is known as effective supply, and it is effective supply that is shown on a supply curve.

The Law of Supply and the supply curve

The Law of SupplyAs the price of a product rises, the quantity supplied of the product will usually increase, ceteris paribus. This is sometimes expressed more simply as “the supply curve normally slopes upwards”.

The Law of Supply can be illustrated with a supply schedule or a supply curve.

The same information plotted graphically is a supply curve. The supply schedule and supply curve show the sum of all individual producers’ supply, and are known as market supply. A supply curve shows the relationship between the price of a product (vertical axis) and the quantity of that same product supplied over time (horizontal axis).

Figure 5.1: A supply curve for bicyclesThe supply curve S for bicycles slopes upward. At $150 producers supply 1,000 bicycles a month; when the price rises to $250 they supply 2,000. The curve gets steeper at higher prices, although economists usually draw supply curves as straight lines for simplicity.

A change in the price of the product itself leads to a change in the quantity supplied — this is shown as a movement along the existing supply curve, not a shift of it. In Figure 5.1, a rise in price from $2.50 to $3.00 causes a movement along S, with quantity supplied rising from 3,500 to 4,000 pizzas per week. The phrase “change in quantity supplied” is important because it distinguishes the effect of a price change from the effect of a change in any of the other (non-price) determinants of supply, which cause a shift of the whole curve.

In the neoclassical model, producers are assumed to be rational “maximizers” — their only goal is to maximize profits. Since rising prices can translate into higher profits, producers wish to increase supply to take advantage of higher potential profits. This assumption underlies the whole Law of Supply.

Key concept: ChoiceThe supply curve is drawn on the assumption that rational producers choose to maximize profits. In reality, not all firms have profit maximization as their sole goal — some owners might pursue other goals (e.g. growth, market share, social objectives), which would affect how supply responds to price.

Movement along a supply curve vs a shift of the supply curve

As with demand, it is essential to distinguish a movement along the supply curve from a shift of the whole curve.

  • A change in the price of the good itself leads to a movement along the existing supply curve, because price is on one of the axes.
  • A change in any other determinant of supply always leads to a shift of the supply curve to the left or right.
Figure 5.5: The supply of tennis racketsA fall in the price of tennis rackets from P to P1 causes a movement down along the supply curve S. Quantity supplied contracts from Q to Q1, and the curve itself does not move.
Figure 5.6: The supply of laptopsA rise in the rent for factory space raises the costs of a large laptop maker, so at the existing price P it supplies less (Q1 instead of Q). The whole curve shifts left from S to S1; a leftward shift means less is supplied at every price, and a rightward shift means more.

The non-price determinants of supply

A number of factors shift the supply curve left or right. Whenever a determinant changes, we assume ceteris paribus (all else unchanged) to isolate its effect — otherwise the analysis becomes too complicated to identify the effect of any single change.

1. The cost of factors of production

If the cost of a factor of production rises (e.g. a wage increase in a labour-intensive textile firm), the firm’s costs rise, so it can supply less at every price — the supply curve shifts left. A fall in the cost of factors of production shifts the supply curve right.

Figure 5.2: The supply of sneakersA rise in wages raises the cost of making sneakers, so supply falls: the curve shifts left from S to S1. At the same price P, producers now supply only Q1 instead of Q.

2. The price of related goods – competitive and joint supply

Competitive supply: producers often have a choice about what to produce with the factors of production they control, because those factors could be used to make more than one product. For example, a producer of roller skates could switch some production to skateboards with minimal changes to production facilities. If the price of skateboards rises, the producer is attracted by higher potential profits and supplies more skateboards and fewer roller skates — skateboards and roller skates are said to be in competitive supply (they compete for the same factors of production).

This causes a movement along the supply curve of skateboards (in response to skateboards’ own price rising) and a shift left of the supply curve of roller skates (even though the price of roller skates itself has not changed).

Figure 5.3: The supply of kayaks and paddleboardsLeft: when the price of kayaks rises from P to P1, there is a movement along the supply curve S and quantity supplied rises from Q to Q1. Right: as boat-builders move their materials and workers into kayaks, the supply of paddleboards shifts left from S to S1, so less is supplied at the same price P.

Joint supply: sometimes producing one good automatically produces another good at the same time — the goods are in joint supply, and one is often called a “by-product” of the other. Example: when sugar is refined, molasses (a black treacle-like substance) is produced as a by-product — sugar and molasses are in joint supply. Another example: refining crude oil to produce petrol also inevitably produces diesel and other goods. If demand for one good in joint supply increases, the supply of the other good(s) will also increase.

Figure 5.4: Joint supply (lamb and wool)Lamb and wool are in joint supply. Left: an increase in demand for lamb shifts D to D1, raising the price from P to P1 and the quantity from Q to Q1. Right: producing more lamb automatically produces more wool, so the supply of wool shifts right from S to S1, which lowers the price of wool from P to P1 and raises the quantity from Q to Q1.

3. Government intervention – indirect taxes and subsidies

Key concept: InterventionGovernments intervene in many markets, most commonly through indirect taxes and subsidies, both of which alter the position of the supply curve.

Indirect taxes (expenditure taxes) are taxes on goods and services that are added to the price of a product. Producers must pay these to the government, so taxes effectively raise firms’ costs of production. This shifts the supply curve vertically upwards by the amount of the tax — less will be supplied at every price.

Subsidies are payments made by the government to firms, which effectively reduce their costs of production. This shifts the supply curve downwards by the amount of the subsidy — more will be supplied at every price. (Indirect taxes and subsidies are covered in much more depth in Chapter 8.)

4. Expectations about future prices

Producers make supply decisions based on expectations of future prices, but the effect can vary. Producers who expect demand (and hence price) to rise in future may, if the product can be stored, withhold product from the market now in order to have more available to sell later at the higher price. Alternatively, they might increase supply now to be ready to meet higher future demand. Similarly, if producers expect future demand to fall, they are likely to reduce current supply. Producer confidence and expectations about the future exert a strong effect on production decisions.

5. Changes in technology

Improvements in technology should increase supply and shift the supply curve right, since the firm can now produce more efficiently. In the unlikely event of a backward step in technology, the supply curve would shift left. Although rare, natural disasters such as hurricanes or earthquakes may effectively move technology (productive capacity) backwards in an area or country.

6. Weather or natural disasters

In markets vulnerable to weather conditions, especially agricultural markets, weather has a major impact on supply. Extremely favourable weather can produce “bumper crops” and increased supply, while poor weather (e.g. drought) can cause significant cuts in supply. Scientists predict climate change will bring significant challenges to agricultural output.

Real-world illustration: a news item cited in the book (“Beer supply threatened by future weather extremes”) illustrates how climate-related weather extremes are projected to threaten the future supply (and raise the cost) of beer via effects on barley harvests.

Higher Level

How economists explain the Law of Supply

1. The short run and the long run

Economists distinguish two time periods for production, defined by whether factors of production can be varied:

  • The short run is the period of time in which at least one factor of production is fixed. All actual production takes place in the short run.
  • The long run is the period of time in which all factors of production are variable, but the state of technology is fixed. All planning takes place in the long run.

Because we are explaining actual production (and hence the Law of Supply), the analysis focuses on the short run, where some factor (often capital, land, or a specialist type of labour) is fixed and cannot be increased quickly even if the firm wishes to produce more. The firm can only increase output in the short run by applying more units of its variable factor(s) to its fixed factor(s).

The length of the short run varies by industry, depending on how long it takes to increase the fixed factor. Example: a small gardening firm whose fixed factor is its number of lawn mowers, which take a week to order and deliver — its short run is one week. A national electricity provider, constrained by the number of power stations it has, might take up to two years (or longer, if a nuclear plant is being built) to add capacity — its short run is much longer.

2. The law of diminishing returns

In the short run, if a firm adds more and more units of a variable factor to its fixed factor(s), it is logical that the output added by each successive unit will eventually fall. To understand this, we need three concepts:

Total product (TP) = the total output produced by a firm, using its fixed and variable factors, in a given time period.

TP is not calculated from other variables — it is simply the actual quantity of output the firm produces in the period, read directly off its production records once you know how many units of the variable factor (e.g. workers) were employed alongside the fixed factors.

Worked example

A small bicycle-assembly workshop has a fixed number of workbenches and tools. On a day when it employs 4 assembly workers, it completes 52 bicycles. That day’s total product is TP = 52 bicycles — the actual output produced by combining the fixed capital (workbenches, tools) with 4 units of labour.

Average product (AP) = TP ÷ V, where V is the number of units of the variable factor employed.

AP tells you how much output, on average, is associated with each unit of the variable factor. To calculate it, take the total product for a given quantity of the variable factor and divide by that quantity.

Worked example

Continuing the bicycle workshop: with V = 4 workers producing TP = 52 bicycles, AP = TP ÷ V = 52 ÷ 4 = 13 bicycles per worker — on average, each of the four assembly workers is associated with 13 completed bicycles that day.

Marginal product (MP) = ΔTP ÷ ΔV, where ΔTP is the change in total output and ΔV is the change in the number of units of the variable factor employed — i.e. the extra output produced by using one extra unit of the variable factor.
Worked example

Using Table 5.3 below: as the quantity of labour rises from 3 to 4 units, total product rises from 45 to 70, so ΔTP = 70 − 45 = 25 and ΔV = 4 − 3 = 1, giving MP = 25 ÷ 1 = 25 units. This means the fourth unit of the variable factor added 25 extra units of output on its own, on top of what the first three units were already producing.

Worked example – Ben’s hamburger standBen sets up a small hamburger stand (a shop, a refrigerator, a grill, countertops — all fixed factors). Working alone, Ben makes and sells 20 burgers per hour. Demand is high and he cannot keep up, so he hires Caroline: together they divide tasks and produce 50 burgers per hour (Caroline’s marginal product = 50 − 20 = 30 burgers). They hire Nick: with specialization, output rises to 90 per hour (Nick’s marginal product = 90 − 50 = 40 burgers). They hire Niki: total output rises to 124 per hour (Niki’s marginal product = 124 − 90 = 34 burgers) — note that Niki’s marginal product is lower than Nick’s. This is not because Niki is inefficient, but because the fixed factors (shop space, countertops, the grill) are limited: as more workers are added, they increasingly get in each other’s way, so each additional worker cannot add output by as much as the previous one did. Whether measured by the extra amount added by the variable factor (marginal product) or the amount added per unit of the variable factor (average product), logic tells us inefficiency must eventually begin — this is the law of diminishing returns in action.
Two related hypotheses
  • The hypothesis of eventually diminishing marginal returns: as extra units of a variable factor are added to a given quantity of a fixed factor, the output from each additional unit of the variable factor will eventually diminish.
  • The hypothesis of eventually diminishing average returns: as extra units of a variable factor are added to a given quantity of a fixed factor, the output per unit of the variable factor will eventually diminish.
These look at the same relationship from different angles — both are really just common sense.

3. Increasing marginal costs

Marginal cost (MC) = ΔTC ÷ Δq, where ΔTC is the change in total cost and Δq is the change in the level of output.
Worked example

Using the table below: when output rises from 45 to 70 units, total cost rises from $1,000 to $1,200, so ΔTC = $1,200 − $1,000 = $200 and Δq = 70 − 45 = 25, giving MC = $200 ÷ 25 = $8. This means producing each of those extra 25 units cost the firm $8 on average at that point on its cost curve.

Increasing marginal cost is directly related to diminishing returns. If the output produced by each additional worker (MP) begins to fall while each worker costs the firm the same wage, then the cost of producing each extra unit (MC) must begin to rise. As output increases, marginal costs eventually rise — so firms will only be willing to supply more if the price they receive for their product also rises, to cover the higher marginal cost of extra output. This is precisely why the supply curve slopes upward.

Worked example: the effect of removing an indirect tax

Illustrative worked example (Washington D.C. removing the sales tax on babies’ diapers to help families save money): removing an indirect tax is a determinant of supply. Removing the sales tax on diapers reduces producers’ costs, so it increases the supply of diapers, shifting the supply curve to the right (from S1 with the tax, to S2 without the tax), ceteris paribus.
