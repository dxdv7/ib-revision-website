What is economic integration?

Economic integration describes a process whereby economies coordinate and link their economic policies. As the degree of economic integration between countries increases, the trade barriers between them decrease and their fiscal and monetary policies become more closely harmonized.

Key concept – InterdependenceEconomic integration is fundamentally about interdependence: as countries integrate, their economic fortunes, policies and cycles become increasingly linked to one another.
  • Bilateral trade agreement: an agreement relating to trade between two countries. It usually aims to reduce or remove tariffs and/or quotas that have been placed on items traded between the two countries.
  • Multilateral trade agreement: an agreement relating to trade between multiple countries. It usually aims to reduce or remove tariffs and/or quotas that have been placed on traded items, but the agreement in this case applies to all of the multiple countries involved.

Trading blocs and the six stages of economic integration

A trading bloc is a group of countries that join together in some form of agreement in order to increase trade between them and/or to gain economic benefits from cooperation on some level. This coming together is called economic integration. The Hungarian economist Béla Balassa identified six stages (levels) of economic integration, each one representing a deeper level of integration than the last.

1. Preferential trading area (PTA)

A PTA is a trading bloc that gives preferential access to certain products from certain countries. This is usually carried out by reducing, but not eliminating, tariffs.

Example – EU/ACPThe PTA between the EU and the African, Caribbean and Pacific Group of States (ACP) is an agreement between the EU and 79 countries in the ACP, many of which are former colonies of EU members. It lets the EU guarantee regular supplies of raw materials and lets ACP countries gain tariff preferences and access to special funds used to try to achieve price stability in agricultural and mining markets. Since 2008 the arrangement has been a “reciprocal trade agreement”: the EU provides duty-free access to its markets for exports from ACP countries and also receives duty-free access for its own exports to the ACP. Not all ACP countries have to open up to EU exports – least-developed ACP countries may opt for other arrangements.

Economics in action: the Cotonou Partnership Agreement (signed 2000, a 20-year framework due to expire 29 February 2020) is the legal agreement governing this EU–ACP relationship, and at the time of writing new negotiations were underway to replace it.

2. Free-trade area

A free-trade area is an agreement between countries where the countries agree to trade freely among themselves, but each remains free to trade with countries outside the free-trade area in whatever way it sees fit (i.e. there is no common external tariff).

Figure 25.1: A free-trade areaCountries A, B and C trade freely with one another, but each sets its own policy towards outsider D: A bans trade with D completely (embargo), B taxes D’s goods with tariffs, and C trades freely with D. There is no common external policy.

Example – NAFTA: the North American Free Trade Area (USA, Canada, Mexico) was established in January 1994 and, following a final tariff reduction between Canada and Mexico in January 2003, virtually all trade in the NAFTA region became tariff-free. Over 70% of Canadian total exports now go to the USA, and Mexico’s share of the US import market grew from approximately 7% in the pre-NAFTA period to approximately 13.5% today.

Other examples: the European Free Trade Association (Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland), and the South Asia Free Trade Agreement (Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka).

Economics in action: in September 2018, after the US demanded that NAFTA be renegotiated or scrapped, the USA, Mexico and Canada reached a new agreement (worth over $1 trillion in regional trade) which, at the time of writing, had not yet been ratified by the respective parliaments – if it fails to be ratified and the US pulls out of NAFTA, the three countries would revert to pre-1994 trade rules.

3. Customs union

A customs union is an agreement between countries where the countries agree to trade freely among themselves, and they also agree to adopt common external barriers (a common external tariff) against any country attempting to import into the customs union.

Figure 25.2: A customs unionCountries A, B and C trade freely inside the union, and all three apply the same common external tariff (red boundary) to goods from D, whichever member they enter through.

Examples: the EU has a customs union; the Switzerland–Liechtenstein customs union; the East African Community (EAC) – Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, South Sudan, Tanzania and Uganda; Mercosur – Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay and Venezuela (although Venezuela’s membership is currently suspended).

4. Common market

A common market is a customs union with common policies on product regulation, and free movement of goods, services, capital and labour.

The best-known example is the EU Single Market. Another example is the CARICOM Single Market and Economy (CSME), expected to be fully implemented with harmonization of economic policy and, possibly, a single currency. Current CSME members include Barbados, Belize, Guyana, Jamaica, Suriname, Trinidad and Tobago, Antigua and Barbuda, Dominica, Grenada, St Kitts and Nevis, St Lucia, St Vincent and the Grenadines, and Montserrat.

5. Economic and monetary union

An economic and monetary union is a common market with a common currency and a common central bank.

The best example is the Eurozone, made up of the EU member countries that have adopted the euro as their currency and use the European Central Bank (ECB) as their central bank. As of 2019, members were: Austria, Belgium, Cyprus, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia and Spain.

6. Complete economic integration

This is the final stage of economic integration, at which point individual countries would have no control over economic policy: full monetary union and complete harmonization of fiscal policy. This is the direction in which the Eurozone is moving.

Higher Level

What are the advantages of membership of a monetary union?

Key concept – InterdependenceA monetary union is the deepest form of economic interdependence covered here: members give up their own currency and, with it, independent control of monetary policy.
  • No exchange-rate fluctuation/uncertainty: exchange-rate fluctuations between member countries disappear with a common currency, eliminating exchange-rate uncertainty between the countries involved – this should increase cross-border investment and trade.
  • Reduced speculation: a currency with the enhanced credibility of being used in a large currency zone should be more stable against speculation than the individual currencies were.
  • Improved business confidence: confidence in member countries tends to improve as there is less perceived risk in trading among the members, leading to both internal growth and trade growth.
  • Lower transaction costs: transaction costs are eliminated within the monetary union – normally there is a charge when currencies are exchanged, but this disappears with a single currency.
  • Price transparency: a common currency makes price differences between countries more obvious and should, over time, lead to prices equalizing across borders.

Higher Level What are the disadvantages of membership of a monetary union?

  • Loss of independent monetary policy: when countries enter a monetary union, interest rates are set by the common central bank. Individual countries can no longer set their own interest rates, so monetary policy is no longer available to them to influence inflation, unemployment or growth. This is especially damaging if one member is experiencing an economic situation (e.g. strong consumer demand and high inflation) not shared by others – it might want higher interest rates, but the shared central bank won’t oblige, so other (less effective) measures must be found.
  • Fiscal integration concerns: while a common central bank is seen as a prerequisite of monetary union, many argue that without fiscal integration – a common treasury, harmonized tax rates and a common budget – a monetary union will be weak and vulnerable, since some members will be more fiscally responsible than others, threatening the stability of the union.
  • Loss of exchange-rate policy: individual countries can no longer alter their own exchange rates to affect the international competitiveness of their exports or the cost of their imports.
  • High conversion costs: the initial costs of converting individual currencies into one currency are very large – taking old currencies off the market, printing/distributing the new currency, converting databases and software, rewriting price lists and invoices, re-pricing all goods and services, and recalibrating machinery that takes coins and notes (parking meters, vending machines).
Weighing it upIt is almost impossible to generalize about whether monetary union membership is beneficial – it depends heavily on context. Where there were previously large exchange-rate fluctuations between the countries involved, where the union creates a currency significant in world forex markets, and where business confidence would be strongly boosted, membership is more likely to be beneficial. Where exchange-rate fluctuations were minimal, the common currency would not be significant on world markets, and business confidence was already high, the advantages of joining would be fewer and the disadvantages may be greater.

What are the advantages of membership of a trading bloc (general)?

Key conceptsInterdependence, Change, Efficiency, Choice and Economic Wellbeing are all illustrated by the benefits of trading-bloc membership.

In purely economic terms, the benefits of being a member of a trading bloc are similar to those of free trade generally. These include:

  • A greater size of market with the potential for larger export markets and economies of scale for producers as they increase their scale of production. The consequences may not be even, since some domestic producers gain from the larger market while others may find themselves unable to compete.
  • Increased competition leading to greater efficiency, more choice and lower prices for consumers.
  • A further stimulus for investment due to the larger market size, and foreign investment might be attracted from outside the bloc as a way of getting a foot in the door of the larger market.
  • If the trading bloc includes free movement of labour, there may be greater employment opportunities for individuals in member countries.
  • There is an argument that, alongside economic gains, a trading bloc will foster greater political stability and cooperation.
  • It is possible that trade negotiations may be easier in a world made up of a number of large trading blocs, rather than among 195 sovereign states.

Higher Level Trade creation

If the trading bloc is a customs union, there may be trade creation. Trade creation occurs when the entry of a country into a customs union leads to the production of a good or service transferring from a high-cost producer to a low-cost producer – an advantage of greater economic integration.

Figure 25.3: Trade creation (worked example: ceramic tiles, Poland and Spain)Hypothetical example: Poland imports ceramic tiles from lower-cost Spain. With a tariff the price is Pw+t = €20: home firms make Q2 = 80 million m², buyers take Q3 = 100 million and 20 million are imported. Inside a customs union the tariff goes and the price falls to Pw = €12: home output falls to Q1 = 40 million, purchases rise to Q4 = 140 million and imports rise to 100 million. The shaded triangles are the regained world efficiency and consumer surplus.

It should be remembered this ought to be a two-way process: with free trade it is highly likely that there will also be French products (e.g. wine) that the UK now buys more of, because France has the comparative advantage there.

What are the disadvantages of membership of a trading bloc (general)?

Key concept – EquityTrading blocs can create winners and losers both between and within countries – raising equity concerns, especially for smaller or poorer non-member economies.
  • By their very nature, trading blocs favour increased trade among members but enact discriminatory policies against non-members, which can damage the achievements of multilateral trade negotiations at the WTO. Concern exists that a breakdown in WTO talks (e.g. in 2006) and inability to reach agreement will lead to more individual trade negotiations, undermining international trade rules and limiting potential gains achievable with more liberalized world trade. This may be less of a problem for large economies than for small or poor economies with little bargaining power.
  • As integration becomes greater, countries lose some degree of economic and political sovereignty. In a customs union, individual governments lose the power to make decisions on how to deal with trade with countries outside the union. In a common market, there are common policies on product regulation and free movement of goods, services, capital and labour, so governments lose even more sovereignty. If a monetary union is joined, individual governments lose all of the above plus the sovereignty and ability to manage their interest rates and exchange rates independently. In many countries, this potential loss of sovereignty is a strong deterrent to membership of more advanced forms of trading blocs.

Higher Level Trade diversion

If the trading bloc is a customs union, there may be trade diversion. Trade diversion occurs when the entry of a country into a customs union leads to the production of a good or service transferring from a low-cost producer outside the union to a higher-cost producer inside the union – a disadvantage of greater economic integration.

Figure 25.4: Trade diversion (worked example: footwear, Chile, Vietnam and Brazil)Hypothetical example: Chile imports footwear from Vietnam, the lowest-cost source, at PV = US$10, buying Q4-equivalent quantities (home output 30 million pairs, purchases 130 million). After joining a customs union with higher-cost Brazil, the tariff on Vietnamese footwear lifts it to PV+t = $22, so imports switch to Brazil at PB = $16: home output rises to Q2 = 60 million, purchases fall to Q3 = 100 million, and the shaded areas show the lost world efficiency, the extra cost of imports and the lost consumer surplus.

The World Trade Organization (WTO)

Key conceptsInterdependence, Efficiency and Equity are all central to how the WTO is meant to function.

The WTO is an international organization that sets the rules for global trading and resolves disputes between its member countries. Established on 1 January 1995, by April 2019 it had 164 members and 23 observer countries, most of whom were seeking membership. It replaced the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), set up after WWII. The WTO, along with GATT before it, is largely credited with average world tariffs on manufactured goods falling from approximately 40% (1947) to 4% (today).

All WTO members are required to grant “most favoured nation” status to one another, meaning that trade concessions granted by one WTO country to another must generally be granted to all WTO members.

Aims and principles of the WTO

The WTO describes itself as: “a place for governments to negotiate trade agreements... a forum for them to settle trade disputes... [and it] operates a system of trade rules.” It aims to increase international trade by lowering trade barriers and providing a forum for negotiation. Its stated principles include:

  • Non-discrimination – countries should not discriminate between their own products/nationals and those of foreign countries, nor between their different trading partners.
  • More open trade – lowering trade barriers (tariffs, import bans, quotas) through negotiation is one of the most obvious ways of increasing trade.
  • Predictability and transparency – economic stakeholders (governments, firms, investors) should be confident that trade barriers will not be raised, giving confidence to invest, create jobs, and increase consumer choice and lower prices.
  • Encouraging “fair” competition – discouraging “unfair” practices such as export subsidies and dumping of products in foreign markets to gain market share.
  • More beneficial for developing countries – giving them extra time to adjust to WTO provisions, greater flexibility, and special privileges where appropriate. Over 75% of WTO members are developing countries.
  • Protection of the environment – member countries may legislate to protect the environment, public, animal and plant health, but such laws must apply equally to domestic and foreign firms and must not be disguised protectionism.

Functions of the WTO

  • Administer WTO trade agreements
  • Be a forum for trade negotiations
  • Handle trade disputes among member countries
  • Monitor national trade policies
  • Provide technical assistance and training for developing countries on trade issues
  • Cooperate with other international organizations

The Doha round

The WTO operates through a system of trade negotiations, or “rounds”. The first rounds, held under GATT, dealt mainly with reducing tariffs; later negotiations covered other areas such as anti-dumping legislation and non-tariff barriers.

The current round is the Doha round (Doha Development Agenda), named after the site of the meeting where negotiations started in November 2001. It covers agricultural tariffs, non-agricultural tariffs, trade and environment, anti-dumping, subsidies, competition policy, transparency in government procurement, and intellectual property.

Negotiations have been very contentious and, at the time of writing, no agreement has been reached, despite ministerial conferences in Cancun (2003), Hong Kong (2005), Geneva (2009), Bali (2013), Nairobi (2015) and Buenos Aires (2017). In July 2006, Doha round negotiations broke down and were ultimately suspended over an inability to agree on fundamental issues: the EU and USA were being urged to reduce their agricultural subsidies to improve market access for developing countries’ exports, while more-developed larger countries such as Brazil and India were asked to lower their own barriers to manufactured imports. Despite a widespread view that such measures would raise growth in all countries, no compromise has been reached. The election of US President Donald Trump in 2017 (an “America First” policy, seen as anti-multilateralist) further complicated matters, with the US administration refusing to approve new members of the WTO’s dispute-settlement body and unilaterally applying tariffs on steel and aluminium.

What factors limit the effectiveness of the WTO in achieving its objectives?

  • Unequal bargaining power of member countries – even though all countries officially have an equal vote, large economies such as the USA and EU are thought to have too much power in negotiations, meaning the views of (by definition smaller) developing countries can be unheard or ignored.
  • Trade rules that are unfair for developing countries – many developed countries historically grew with the help of tariff protection, but WTO rules do not allow this for developing countries today, potentially preventing them from protecting “infant industries” and diversifying their economies.
  • The growing number of trade deals negotiated outside the WTO – such as the Transatlantic Trade and Investment Partnership (TTIP) negotiations between the USA and EU. Such deals exclude other countries and diminish the WTO’s importance by taking place outside its jurisdiction.
