Why do countries trade?

International trade is the exchange of goods and services between countries. Prices may be lower in some countries than others because of access to natural resources, differences in the quality of labour forces, or differences in the quality of capital and levels of technology. The cause of these lower prices is mainly determined by the concept of comparative advantage.

International tradeThe exchange of goods and services between countries, driven by differences in resource endowments, technology, and costs of production.

The seven gains from international trade

  • Lower prices: the main gain from trade – consumers buy goods and services more cheaply than the domestic price, and producers buy cheaper raw materials and semi-manufactured goods. This is the main reason for trade.
  • Greater choice: consumers gain access not just to domestically produced goods but also to goods from many different countries. (Key concept: Economic wellbeing.)
  • Differences in resources: some countries lack resources entirely (e.g. many countries have no copper, diamonds or oil) but need them to produce other goods, so must import them – and must export something else to earn the foreign currency to pay for these imports. Example: Singapore has almost no natural resources (must import even water) but exports high levels of manufactured goods and services to fund its imports. (Key concept: Scarcity.)
  • Economies of scale: producing for an international market as well as a domestic one increases the size of the market and thus demand, raising the level of production and the size of production units. Larger production units achieve economies of scale, greater specialization (e.g. accounting manager vs marketing manager), a finer division of labour (breaking production into simple, repetitive tasks), and “moving down the learning curve” (the long-run average cost curve) as firms gain experience and expertise in producing a specific commodity, lowering costs.
  • Increased competition: domestic firms compete with foreign firms, which should increase efficiency, lower prices, increase the quality and variety of goods for consumers, and increase competitiveness.
  • More efficient allocation of resources: when trade is free of government interference, countries that are best at producing certain goods (at lowest cost) will produce them. If this happens across all trading countries, the world's resources are being used most efficiently. (Key concept: Efficiency.)
  • Source of foreign exchange: exporting earns a country foreign currency, which it can then use to buy essential imports. This matters especially for countries without a freely convertible currency (one that can be freely exchanged for other currencies on the world market) – e.g. Ghana sells gold and cocoa to the Netherlands for euros, which it then uses to buy machinery or petroleum. Without exporting first, such a country cannot obtain the foreign exchange to import.

Overall, international trade can make a major contribution to a country's economic growth.

Higher Level

How does comparative advantage theory work?

There are many advantages to international trade, but which goods should a country produce for export, and which should it import? The answer lies in the concept of comparative advantage.

Absolute advantage

Absolute advantageA country has an absolute advantage in the production of a good if it can produce it using fewer resources than another country.

Table 23.1 shows the production outcomes where two countries, Australia and China, use the same quantities of resources to produce lamb and cloth.

CountryKilos of lambMetres of cloth
Australia61
China43
Total without trade104

Table 23.1 Absolute advantage

Australia has an absolute advantage in producing lamb (6 > 4) and China has an absolute advantage in producing cloth (3 > 1). Australia should specialize in lamb and China in cloth; total output of both goods is maximized when both specialize and then trade. If Australia doubled its resources, and assuming constant returns to scale, total lamb output from those resources would rise to 12 kilos (an increase of 2 kilos versus the pre-specialization total of 10). Similarly, if China doubled its resources with constant returns to scale, cloth output would rise to 6 metres (an increase of 2 metres versus the pre-specialization total of 4). So total output of both goods rises following specialization.

Reciprocal absolute advantageThe situation where each country has an absolute advantage in the production of one product (as with Australia and China above) is known as reciprocal absolute advantage.

Comparative advantage

The whole concept of absolute advantage seems obvious – but what happens if there is no reciprocal absolute advantage, i.e. one country has an absolute advantage in producing all goods? In the early nineteenth century, David Ricardo was the first economist to prove mathematically that trade could still be beneficial to both countries even when one has an absolute advantage in producing everything. Ricardo considered the opportunity cost of production to explain this.

Comparative advantageA country has a comparative advantage in the production of a good if it can produce that good at a lower opportunity cost than another country – i.e. country A has to give up fewer units of other goods to produce the good in question than does country B.

Table 23.2 shows the production outcomes where France and Poland use the same quantities of resources to produce wine and cheese.

CountryLitres of wineOpportunity cost of 1 litre of wineKilos of cheeseOpportunity cost of 1 kilo of cheese
France34⁄3 kilos of cheese43⁄4 litre of wine
Poland13 kilos of cheese31⁄3 litre of wine

Table 23.2 Comparative advantage

Opportunity cost of 1 unit of Good X ÷ Maximum output of Good Y possible with same resources ÷ Maximum output of Good X possible with same resources (i.e. opportunity cost of X in terms of Y = Max output of Y ÷ Max output of X)

To find the opportunity cost of one unit of Good X in terms of Good Y, divide the maximum amount of Good Y that could be produced with a given set of resources by the maximum amount of Good X that could be produced with that exact same set of resources. The answer tells you how many units of Y must be given up to produce one extra unit of X.

Worked example

Suppose Egypt and Kenya each have identical resources, and if fully devoted to one good could produce: Egypt, 8 tonnes of tea or 4 tonnes of cotton; Kenya, 5 tonnes of tea or 5 tonnes of cotton. Egypt's opportunity cost of 1 tonne of tea = Max cotton ÷ Max tea = 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5 tonnes of cotton, compared with Kenya's opportunity cost of 1 tonne of tea = 5 ÷ 5 = 1 tonne of cotton; since 0.5 is lower than 1, Egypt has the comparative advantage in tea. Conversely, Egypt's opportunity cost of 1 tonne of cotton = Max tea ÷ Max cotton = 8 ÷ 4 = 2 tonnes of tea, while Kenya's is 5 ÷ 5 = 1 tonne of tea; since 1 is lower than 2, Kenya has the comparative advantage in cotton. Egypt should therefore specialize in tea and Kenya in cotton, and both countries can gain by trading with each other.

France has an absolute advantage in both goods (3 > 1 wine; 4 > 3 cheese). However, in terms of comparative advantage: France only has to give up 4⁄3 kilos of cheese to produce a litre of wine, whereas Poland has to give up 3 kilos; so France has the comparative advantage (lower opportunity cost) in wine. Poland only has to give up 1⁄3 litre of wine to produce a kilo of cheese, whereas France has to give up 3⁄4 litre of wine; so Poland has the comparative advantage in cheese. The theory of comparative advantage tells us that France should specialize in wine and Poland should specialize in cheese. France then consumes the wine it wants and exchanges any extra wine for cheese; Poland consumes the cheese it wants and exchanges any extra for wine.

Figure 23.1: Production possibility curves to show comparative advantageTwo straight-line (constant opportunity cost) PPCs are drawn to the same scale on axes of Wine (litres) on the vertical axis and Cheese (kilos) on the horizontal axis. France's PPC lies further out along the wine axis (absolute advantage in wine) and Poland's lies closer to the origin. When a country has an absolute advantage in producing both goods (as France does), the comparative advantage of the better producer (France) lies in whichever good shows the greatest distance between the two production possibilities on that axis – labelled (a), here the wine axis, where France's frontier extends much further than Poland's. The comparative advantage of the less efficient producer (Poland) lies in whichever good shows the least distance between the two frontiers – labelled (b), here the cheese axis, where the gap between the two countries' maximum output is smallest. This is not a mathematical proof, just a visual shortcut for reading comparative advantage off two PPCs drawn to the same scale; the real justification is the relative slope of the two lines, since the slope of a straight-line PPC represents the (constant) opportunity cost.

Does the theory of comparative advantage always work?

Comparative advantage theory only generates gains from trade when the two countries face different opportunity costs. If both countries face the same opportunity cost (shown by parallel PPCs), there is no point in trade taking place, since neither country is relatively better at producing either good.

His famous case study of wine and cloth between England and Portugal showed that both would benefit from specializing and trading freely, making him an early advocate of free trade.
Higher Level

What gives a country a comparative advantage?

To a large extent, comparative advantage is based on a country's factor endowments:

  • A country "endowed" with abundant arable land may develop a comparative advantage in agricultural products.
  • A country with abundant unskilled labour can develop a comparative advantage in labour-intensive, low-skilled manufactured goods.
  • A country with abundant well-educated labour may develop a comparative advantage in financial services.
  • A country with beautiful beaches and a favourable climate may develop a comparative advantage in tourist services – illustrating that "climate" can itself act as a factor of production.

The abundance of a particular factor makes the price of that factor relatively lower than in other countries, which lowers the opportunity cost of goods/services that use that factor intensively, compared with other countries.

Limitations of the theory of comparative advantage

Comparative advantage theory is based on a number of simplifying assumptions, which limit its application in the real world:

  • As in perfect competition, it assumes producers and consumers have perfect knowledge and know where the least expensive goods may be purchased.
  • It usually assumes there are no transport costs. In reality, transport costs exist and may erode a country's comparative advantage, eliminating its competitiveness and making trade not worthwhile.
  • Basic theories assume only two economies producing two goods. This is not a major problem in practice – the theory can be applied to more countries and more products, and computer simulations make multi-country/multi-product analysis easier to conduct.
  • It usually assumes constant costs and constant returns to scale (i.e. no economies or diseconomies of scale). In reality, economies of scale would likely increase a country's comparative advantage further, as relative costs of production fall even more.
  • It assumes the goods being traded are identical (e.g. barley, cotton, bananas). Problems arise with differentiated consumer durables – e.g. it is hard to say a Toshiba TV and a Phillips TV are "the same good", so it is harder to prove that Japan has a comparative advantage in producing televisions.
  • It assumes factors of production remain within the country, rather than moving between countries. In reality, developed countries may invest capital in developing countries (rather than exporting finished goods), and labour may migrate from low-wage to high-wage countries.
  • It assumes perfectly free trade between countries, but in reality governments frequently impose trade barriers in many industries.

In spite of these limitations, comparative advantage theory remains at the core of international trade theory and goes a long way toward explaining real-world patterns of trade: countries that specialize in producing goods in which they have a lower opportunity cost than other countries can capture the gains from trade discussed earlier in the chapter.
