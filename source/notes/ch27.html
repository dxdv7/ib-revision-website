What is the balance of payments account?

DefinitionThe balance of payments account is a record of the value of all the transactions between the residents of one country and the residents of all other countries in the world over a given period of time (usually one year, though monthly accounts are also produced). It has three main parts: the current account, the capital account and the financial account.

Any transaction that leads to money entering the country from abroad is a credit item in the balance of payments and is given a positive value. Any transaction that leads to money leaving the country to go abroad is a debit item and is given a negative value.

Naming varies by countryDifferent countries (and even the same country over time) use different names for the sub-parts of the balance of payments. To avoid confusion, the IB curriculum lays down a fixed structure and set of terms, which is what is used throughout this chapter and should be used in IB assessment.

The elements of the current account

The current account measures the flow of funds from trade in goods and services, plus other income flows. It is sub-divided into four parts.

  • 1. The balance of trade in goods — also called the visible trade balance, the merchandise account balance, or simply the balance of trade. It measures the revenue received from exports of tangible (physical) goods minus expenditure on imports of tangible goods over a given period. It includes trade in all tangible goods, from airplanes to chickens.
    • Exports occur when an international transaction relating to goods or services leads to an inflow of money into the country.
    • Imports occur when an international transaction relating to goods or services leads to an outflow of money from the country.
    • When export revenue > import expenditure, there is a surplus on the balance of trade in goods. When import expenditure > export revenue, there is a deficit.
  • 2. The balance of trade in services — also known as the invisible balance, the services balance or net services. It measures the revenue received from exports of services minus expenditure on imports of services over a given period. It includes trade in services such as banking, insurance and tourism. For example, an Italian tourist on holiday in Vienna is spending money that represents an invisible export to the Austrian economy (money coming in) and so an invisible import to the Italian economy (money going out).
  • 3. Income — also known as net investment income (net factor income from abroad). It measures the net monetary movement of profit, interest and dividends moving into and out of the country over a given period, as a result of financial investment abroad.
    • Domestic firms may have branches abroad; profits repatriated count as a positive item. Profits sent out of the country by foreign-owned firms based domestically count as a negative item.
    • Residents may hold deposits in foreign banks/financial institutions; interest received counts as a positive item. Interest paid to foreign investors leaving the country counts as a negative item.
    • Residents may hold shares in foreign companies; dividends received count as positive. Dividends paid by domestic firms to foreign shareholders count as negative.
  • 4. Current transfers — a measurement of the net transfers of money, often known as net unilateral transfers from abroad. These are payments between countries when no goods or services change hands. At government level this includes things like foreign aid and grants. At an individual level it includes foreign workers sending money back to their families in their home country (remittances) or private gifts sent from a person in one country to a person in another.
Current account balance = Balance of trade in goods + Balance of trade in services + Net income flows + Net transfers

To calculate the current account balance, add together the balance of trade in goods, the balance of trade in services, net income flows and net current transfers for the same time period, giving a negative sign to any sub-account that is in deficit and a positive sign to any sub-account that is in surplus. The result is a single net figure showing whether more money flowed into the country from current international transactions than flowed out (a surplus) or vice versa (a deficit).

Worked example

Suppose the small economy of Solaria records the following over one year: a balance of trade in goods of −$3.2 billion (imports of goods exceed exports), a balance of trade in services of +$1.4 billion (it is a popular tourist destination), net income flows of +$0.6 billion (profits and interest earned abroad exceed payments to foreign investors), and net current transfers of −$0.3 billion (remittances and aid sent abroad exceed those received). Current account balance = (−3.2) + (+1.4) + (+0.6) + (−0.3) = −$1.5 billion. Solaria therefore has a current account deficit of $1.5 billion for the year, meaning more money left the country through current international transactions than entered it, and this deficit will need to be financed by an offsetting surplus on the capital and financial accounts.

Each component can differ in directionAny of the four sub-accounts might be in surplus or deficit at any given time independently of the others — e.g. a deficit on trade in goods, a surplus on trade in services, a deficit on net income flows, and a surplus on net transfers. The current account balance is the overall/net balance of all four, and it may end up in deficit or surplus regardless of the individual components.
Remittances — a fact to knowRemittances (funds sent home by migrants working abroad) have grown dramatically, from about US$18 billion in 1980 to over US$440 billion by 2015, and are now the second-largest source of external finance for developing countries after foreign direct investment (FDI), both in absolute terms and as a share of GDP. Unlike other capital flows, remittances tend to stay relatively stable even during economic downturns and crises, since migrants often send more home to help family during hard times.

The capital account

The capital account is a relatively small part of the balance of payments accounts and does not have a significant effect on the overall balance. It has two components.

  • 1. Capital transfers — a measure of the net monetary movements gained or lost through actions such as the transfers of goods and financial assets by migrants entering or leaving the country, debt forgiveness, transfers relating to the sale of fixed assets (tangible assets that firms own and use in production that have a useful life of at least one year), gift taxes, inheritance taxes and death duties.
  • 2. Transactions in non-produced, non-financial assets — the net international sales and purchases of non-produced assets, such as land or the rights to natural resources, and the net international sales and purchases of intangible assets, such as patents, copyrights, brand names or franchises.

The financial account

Financial account — definitionThe financial account measures the net change in foreign ownership of domestic financial assets. If foreign ownership of domestic financial assets increases more quickly than domestic ownership of foreign financial assets, there is more money coming into the country than going out, so there is a financial account surplus. If domestic ownership of foreign financial assets increases more quickly than foreign ownership of domestic assets, there is more money going out than coming in, so there is a financial account deficit.

The financial account has three components.

  • 1. Direct investment — a measure of the purchase of long-term assets, where the purchaser is aiming to gain a lasting interest in a company in another economy. It includes things such as buying property, the outright purchasing of a business, or purchasing stocks or shares in a business. In all cases the asset is expected to give a positive return in future, by making profits or by increasing in value over time. The investment does not have to be paid back and there is no guarantee it will provide a positive return — the buyer of the asset is taking a risk. Much of this activity is foreign direct investment (FDI) — investment by multinational corporations in another economy. IMF guidelines state that an investment in a firm is FDI if it accounts for at least 10% of the ownership of the firm, though many countries set a higher threshold.
  • 2. Portfolio investment — a measure of stock and bond purchases, which are not direct investment since they do not lead to a lasting interest in a company. This also includes the buying and selling of things such as treasury bills and government bonds, and (in the IB model) savings account deposits, although in many countries these come under "other investment". The investor puts forward money to purchase the asset in the expectation that interest will be paid and that the money will be repaid at a given point in time — these assets are simply borrowing and lending on the international market.
  • 3. Reserve assets — the reserves of gold and foreign currencies that all countries hold, itemized in the official reserve account. Movements into and out of this account help ensure the balance of payments balances to zero. If there is a surplus on all of the other accounts combined, the official reserve account total will increase; if there is a deficit on all of the other accounts combined, the official reserve account total will decrease. It is net changes in the official reserve account, over the period considered, that balances the accounts.

Does the balance of payments really balance?

In theory, the value of the current account = the value of the financial account + the capital account + net errors and omissions. In other words:

  • If the value of the current account is negative (current account deficit), this will be balanced by a positive value (surplus) on the financial account plus the capital account.
  • If the value of the current account is positive (current account surplus), this will be balanced by a negative value (deficit) on the financial account plus the capital account.
  • Adjustments are made via the "errors and omissions" (or "statistical discrepancy") item.
Figure 27.1: The balancing balance of paymentsShown as a balancing equation rather than an x–y graph: Current account = Capital account + Financial account + Net errors and omissions. Whatever its sign, the current account balance is always exactly offset by the combined capital and financial account balance, with any measurement gaps absorbed by net errors and omissions, so the overall balance of payments sums to zero by construction.

In reality, the balance of payments accounts will not actually balance perfectly on first calculation. This is because there are too many individual transactions taking place for the measurement to be exact — there will always be some transactions not yet recorded when the figures are put together. To resolve this, a balancing item called "net errors and omissions" (or "statistical discrepancy") is inserted to ensure the accounts do, in fact, balance. As time goes by and trading accounts are revised with more data, this discrepancy invariably gets smaller.

Higher Level What is the relationship between the current account and the exchange rate?

Key concept: CHANGEThis relationship illustrates the IB key concept of change — imbalances in the current account create pressure that pushes exchange rates to adjust (or forces policy change if the exchange rate is fixed).

A deficit in the current account of the balance of payments may result in downward pressure on the exchange rate of the currency. This is more of a problem in a fixed exchange rate system than a floating exchange rate system.

  • Fixed exchange rate system: a persistent deficit implies the exchange rate has been set too high. In the short run the deficit may be covered by increases in the capital and financial accounts, or by the government using reserve assets to balance the accounts. However, this cannot go on indefinitely since reserve assets will eventually run out, and in the end, the exchange rate will have to be devalued.
  • Floating exchange rate system: a deficit implies there is an excess supply of the currency on the foreign exchange market. This may be because the demand for exports has fallen, the demand for the currency has fallen, or the demand for imports has increased, leading to more demand for foreign currencies (and hence a greater supply of the domestic currency on the foreign exchange markets). In a freely floating system, the exchange rate should fall (depreciate), which should improve the competitiveness of the country's exports and increase the domestic price of imports — a corrective, self-adjusting mechanism.

In the same way, a surplus in the current account may result in upward pressure on the exchange rate.

  • Fixed exchange rate system: implies the exchange rate has been set too low. In the short run this may be offset by deficits on the capital and financial accounts or by increases in the reserve assets. In the long run, however, other countries will likely be unhappy with the artificially low exchange rate and will demand higher rates, or threaten protectionist measures against the country's exports. This may result in a revaluation of the currency.
  • Floating exchange rate system: implies there is excess demand for the currency — because export demand has risen, demand for the currency has risen, or import demand has fallen (leading to less demand for foreign currencies and a lower supply of the domestic currency). In a freely floating system, the exchange rate should rise (appreciate), decreasing the competitiveness of the country's exports and lowering the domestic price of imports.

Real-world illustration: a news report referenced in the textbook ("Canadian dollar nears 8-month high after surprise swing to trade surplus") illustrates a currency strengthening on the foreign exchange market following an unexpected move of the trade balance into surplus — consistent with the floating-system logic above.

Higher Level What are the consequences of current account imbalances?

Key concept: INTERDEPENDENCEThe existence of a deficit or surplus in the current account is bound to have economic consequences that affect the domestic economy and other economies — illustrating the interconnectedness of national economies.

Consequences of a current account deficit

We know that if the current account is in deficit, the capital and financial accounts must be in surplus to balance it out. This means one of three things is happening, plus further possible effects:

  1. Use of foreign exchange reserves: reserves may be run down to fund a deficit (a positive/credit entry when reserves fall). However, no country, no matter how rich or powerful, can fund long-term current account deficits from its reserves indefinitely — eventually the reserves would run out.
  2. Sale of domestic assets to foreigners: a high level of foreign buying of assets for ownership may be financing the deficit — e.g. foreign investors purchasing property, businesses, stocks or shares. This inflow funds the deficit but is not necessarily harmful, since it is based on foreign confidence in the domestic economy. However, there are sometimes fears that if foreign ownership of domestic assets becomes too great, this may threaten economic sovereignty. Moreover, if confidence drops, foreign investors might shift their assets to other countries; selling those assets would increase the supply of the currency and cause it to fall in value.
  3. Financing by high levels of foreign borrowing: if this is the case, high interest rates will have to be paid, which is a short-term drain on the economy and will further increase the current account deficit in future years (via higher income/debt-service payments), leaving the country in significant debt. There is also always a danger that lenders may, at some point, withdraw their money and place it elsewhere, which could lead to massive selling of the currency and a very sharp fall in the exchange rate.
  4. Credit rating downgrades: if a country has growing levels of economic debt, it may get a lower credit rating from credit rating agencies. The three main agencies are Fitch, Moody's and Standard & Poor's (S&P). These international agencies advise investors about the safest countries in which to invest funds. The lowest-risk (safest) rating is AA1, also known as "triple A" or "prime". Ratings range down to the highest-risk ratings, C ratings (substantial risks/extremely speculative) and D ratings (in default). If a country's credit rating is reduced, it becomes more difficult to borrow and, because the perceived risk is greater, interest rate charges on loans will be higher. (Example: in September 2017 the UK's credit rating was downgraded from AA1 to AA2 due to Brexit-related uncertainty; in April 2019, Fitch and S&P warned of a further possible downgrade as Brexit continued to progress slowly.)
  5. Loss of monetary policy independence: one response to a current account deficit is for the central bank to lower interest rates in order to lower the exchange rate. If the central bank is using interest rate changes to control the exchange rate, this takes away its ability to use interest rates for domestic demand management (monetary policy) for other purposes. Lowering the interest rate also acts as an expansionary demand-side policy, which can cause inflationary pressure in the economy.
  6. Negative impact on aggregate demand: a current account deficit is a negative component of aggregate demand (AD, since net exports X−M falls), and as such may shift the AD curve to the left, reducing economic growth and having a negative effect on employment.

Consequences of a current account surplus

First, consider why a country might run significant, persistent current account surpluses. In 2016, according to the World Bank, the ten countries with the largest current account surpluses were Germany, China, Japan, South Korea, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Singapore, Italy, Thailand and Russia. There are several possible factors, some structural (long-run) and some cyclical (short-run).

Structural factors

  • A country may have built up long-run competitive advantage in producing certain products, enabling relatively low prices and thus high export demand (e.g. currently South Korea and electronics).
  • Households in a country may have high savings ratios, so expenditure on consumption in general, and imports in particular, may be lower than in countries with lower personal savings ratios. (Example: Germany, which has continuous current account surpluses, has a personal savings ratio from disposable income of around 11%.)
  • A significant increase in the prices of a country's main exports on world markets (e.g. oil) can lead to higher export revenues when demand for the product is price inelastic.
  • A country may have achieved significant increases in productivity and research and development funding (e.g. Singapore).

Cyclical (short-term) factors

  • A depreciation of a country's currency increasing competitiveness in world markets.
  • An increase in foreign consumer demand in a country's main export markets, such as increased global demand for electronic products.
  • Cyclical improvements in the global economy leading to increased demand and higher prices for a country's exports.
  • An increase in net income flows and current transfers — for example, increases in profits earned abroad and remittances received from citizens working abroad.

If the current account is in surplus, there may be both positive and negative consequences.

  1. A current account surplus allows a country to run a deficit on its capital and financial account — by building up its official reserve account or by purchasing assets abroad. In many surplus countries, reserves have been transferred into Sovereign Wealth Funds (SWFs) that manage national savings for international investment. (Some countries have multiple SWFs — e.g. China has four: the China Investment Corporation (CIC), the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE), the National Social Security Fund (NCSSF), and the China-Africa Development Fund (CADF).)

  2. In the short term, a current account surplus implies a shift of AD to the right (as X−M increases), with an increase in real national output, increased employment and an increase in inflationary pressure.
  3. In the long term, a persistent current account surplus may cause an appreciation of the currency on the foreign exchange market, as it implies an increase in demand for the currency. This will make imports cheaper (reducing inflationary pressures) but will also make exports more expensive, which harms exporters. This may threaten employment in export industries and industries that compete with imports.
  4. Persistent current account surpluses may lead to a decrease in domestic consumption and domestic investment. In effect, if the extra revenue received from exports is not spent on imports but is instead used to purchase assets abroad and bolster reserves, it is like the government saving. Like any saving, this reduces potential consumption and will also reduce potential domestic investment, since the funds are not available for domestic use.
  5. Any current account surplus must be someone else's deficit. Thus, if a country is persistently achieving surpluses, other countries must be experiencing deficits. This may lead to retaliation in other countries, in the form of increased protectionism, as they attempt to "protect" their economies from the adverse consequences of trade deficits.
Key concept: INTERDEPENDENCE (again)Point 5 above is a direct illustration of interdependence: one country's persistent surplus is mathematically another's deficit, so policy responses (protectionism) in deficit countries are a direct consequence of surplus countries' external position.

How big is a "big" current account deficit or surplus?

There are two ways to interpret the size of a country's current account deficit or surplus.

  • Absolute value of the total — e.g. the current account surplus in Germany was US$287 billion in 2017, while the current account deficit in the US was US$488.5 billion.
  • Relative size, as a percentage of GDP — this is easier to interpret meaningfully. This is similar to understanding how much a person is in debt: a billionaire who owes US$1,000 is in a very different situation to an unemployed student who owes US$1,000.
The burden depends on ability to payThe burden of a deficit depends on the ability to pay. This is not much of a concern when a country has a current account surplus, although there are possible problems arising from the appreciation of the currency; but it is a problem when current account deficits reach a certain percentage of GDP. In the case of the US, the current account deficit was approximately 2.4% of GDP in 2017; for Germany, the surplus was approximately 7.8% of GDP.

Higher Level How might a government correct a persistent current account deficit?

Key concept: INTERVENTIONFor the most part, governments try to ensure a favourable current account balance by making sure the supply side of the economy is efficient and productive, keeping the country's exports competitive internationally and ensuring domestic producers can compete with imports. In this sense, supply-side policies are valuable in preventing a large current account deficit from arising in the first place. However, if a country is already running a large and persistent current account deficit, the government may have to turn to two further types of policy: expenditure-switching and expenditure-reducing policies.

Expenditure-switching policies

DefinitionExpenditure-switching policies are any policies implemented by the government that attempt to switch the expenditure of domestic consumers away from imports towards domestically produced goods and services. If successful, expenditure on imports will fall and so the current account deficit should improve.
  • Government policies to depreciate or devalue the value of the currency: if the government adopts policies that reduce the exchange rate, exports should become less expensive (to foreigners) and imports should become more expensive (to domestic buyers). Depending on how responsive domestic consumers and foreign consumers are to these price changes (i.e. price elasticity of demand), this should improve the current account as export revenue rises and import expenditure falls.
  • Protectionist measures: the government may attempt to restrict imports either by reducing their availability (using embargoes, quotas, voluntary export restraints, and administrative, health and safety, and environmental barriers) or by increasing their prices using tariffs. If domestic consumers switch expenditure from imports to domestic products as a result, the current account should improve. However, governments are often reluctant or unable to use such measures because they tend to lead to retaliation and are often against WTO agreements. Also, protecting domestic industries reduces competition, which may encourage inefficiency. Therefore, protectionism is not considered a good long-run solution.

Expenditure-reducing policies

DefinitionExpenditure-reducing policies are any policies implemented by the government that attempt to reduce overall expenditure in the economy, shifting AD to the left. If this occurs, expenditure on all goods and services should fall, and since this includes expenditure on imports, the current account deficit should improve. The size of the fall in imports will depend upon the level of the marginal propensity to import.
Conflict between internal and external objectivesThere is a direct conflict here between internal and external policy objectives. Deflating the economy may reduce the current account deficit, but the policy is likely to lead to a fall in domestic employment and a fall in the rate of economic growth. This makes it a difficult trade-off decision for a government.
  • Deflationary fiscal policies: increasing direct tax rates and/or reducing government expenditure. Clearly these would be politically unpopular and a government might be reluctant to use such a policy.
  • Deflationary monetary policies: increasing the rate of interest and/or reducing the money supply. Interestingly, higher interest rates should also increase capital flows from abroad, as foreigners put money into financial institutions attracted by the higher rates — this would lead to an inflow on the financial account, which helps to offset the current account deficit. This type of policy would also be politically unpopular, as higher interest rates increase people's mortgage, loan and credit card payments. Moreover, the higher cost of borrowing as a result of higher interest rates may act as a disincentive to domestic investment and limit potential growth.

The economic costs of reducing a large current account deficit suggest why it is important to prevent it from occurring in the first place. To avoid these costs, many governments actively pursue export promotion policies, which may include government-run trade missions, hoping to develop new markets, and government-sponsored advertising campaigns.

Higher Level What is the Marshall–Lerner condition?

Key concept: CHANGETheoretically, if a country's currency depreciates or is devalued, this should lead to an increase in exports (they become less expensive in foreign markets) and a decrease in imports (they become more expensive domestically). This should improve a country's current account — but this is not necessarily the case.

The effect of a price change on spending or revenues depends on price elasticity of demand (PED). The price of exports might fall because of depreciation of the currency, and according to the law of demand the quantity demanded will increase — but whether or not this leads to an increase in export revenues depends on foreigners' PED for exports. Similarly, the price of imported goods will rise if a currency falls in value, and according to the law of demand the quantity demanded will fall — but whether or not this leads to a fall in expenditure on imports depends on the PED for imports.

Marshall–Lerner conditionThe Marshall–Lerner condition is a rule that tells us how successful a depreciation or devaluation of a currency's exchange rate will be as a means to improve a current account deficit in the balance of payments. The condition states that reducing the value of the exchange rate will only be successful if the total value of the price elasticity of demand for exports and the price elasticity of demand for imports is greater than one.
A fall in the exchange rate will reduce a current account deficit if:
PEDexports + PEDimports > 1

To apply the condition, take the price elasticity of demand for exports and the price elasticity of demand for imports (both treated as positive numbers) and add them together. If the sum is greater than 1, a depreciation or devaluation of the currency will improve the current account balance; if the sum is 1 or less, the depreciation will fail to improve the current account, or may even make it worse.

Worked example

Using the estimated short-run trade elasticities for the United States, PED for exports is about 0.5 and PED for imports is about 0.6. Adding these together gives PEDexports + PEDimports = 0.5 + 0.6 = 1.1, which is greater than 1. The Marshall–Lerner condition is therefore satisfied, so in the short run a depreciation of the US dollar would be expected to improve, not worsen, the US current account balance (see the fuller country comparison below).

This is a straightforward application of the concept of elasticity of demand.

  • If demand for exports was price inelastic and price fell (as a result of a fall in the exchange rate), the proportionate increase in the quantity of exports demanded would be less than the proportionate decrease in the price of exports, and so export revenue would fall.
  • In the same way, if demand for imports was price-inelastic and price rose following a fall in the exchange rate, the proportionate fall in the demand for imports would be less than the proportionate increase in the price of imports, and import expenditure would actually increase.

In either of these inelastic cases, the current account deficit would become worse, not better — hence the need for the combined elasticities to exceed 1 for a depreciation/devaluation to work.

Time and elasticityOne of the determinants of elasticity of demand is the time period under consideration. Demand becomes more elastic over a longer period of time. This applies to the elasticity of demand for exports and imports too: PED values for exports and imports tend to be lower in the short run than in the long run.

Higher Level What is the J-curve?

Key concept: CHANGEIf a government is facing a current account deficit, it may reduce the exchange rate of its currency in order to make exports relatively less expensive and imports relatively more expensive. If this happens and the Marshall–Lerner condition is satisfied — i.e. PEDexports + PEDimports > 1 — then we would expect an improvement in the current account deficit.

However, in the short run this is not the case, and the current account deficit actually gets worse before it gets better. This is known as the J-curve effect. It shows what happens to a current account deficit over time when the exchange rate is devalued or depreciated.

Figure 27.2: The J-curveTime runs along the horizontal axis and the current account balance along the vertical, with a zero line separating surplus (above) from deficit (below). After a depreciation the current account starts in deficit at X, worsens further to its lowest point at Y as existing contracts take time to adjust, then improves – crossing back above zero and on to an improved position at Z – once the Marshall–Lerner condition starts to bite as elasticities rise with time, tracing the shape of a “J”.

Explanation of the mechanism (assume the deficit starts at point X and the government lowers the exchange rate):

  • The price of exports falls immediately, but communication is not perfect and it takes time for foreign buyers to realize prices have fallen. Also, buyers will have entered into contracts for goods and services that cannot be broken quickly, so they cannot change suppliers immediately. This means that, in the short run, PED for exports is inelastic and export revenue actually falls (as prices have fallen proportionately more than demand has risen). This increases the current account deficit and starts the movement from X to Y on the J-curve.
  • At the same time, the price of imports rises, but purchasers of imports will take time to find new suppliers; they may also be tied into contracts and have to wait for them to expire before they can move to other suppliers. This means that, in the short run, PED for imports is also inelastic, so import expenditure actually increases (prices have risen proportionately more than demand has fallen). This further increases the current account deficit and adds to the movement from X to Y.
  • As shown by the elasticities data (Table above), PED for exports and imports increases with time. By the time the current account deficit reaches point Y, the combined values of PED for exports and imports have risen to the point where, added together, they exceed one — i.e. the Marshall–Lerner condition is now satisfied. From this point onwards, the now-cheaper exports and the now-more-expensive imports lead to increased export revenue and decreased import expenditure, and therefore an improvement in the current account balance, shown by the movement from Y towards Z on the J-curve.
