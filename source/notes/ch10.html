1. Classifying costs: economic cost, explicit and implicit costs

Neoclassical economic theory of the firm assumes that producers behave “rationally” by always attempting to maximize the profits that they make. This is called profit maximization. Profits are earned when a producer’s revenues are higher than its costs, so to understand how producers maximize profits we must first understand the relevant concepts of cost and revenue.

Economic costEconomists use the term economic cost to describe the opportunity cost of producing a good — the opportunity cost of the factors of production (resources) used in the firm’s production. Economic cost is split into two categories.
  • Explicit costs: costs relating to factors of production purchased from outside the firm, that is, not already owned by the firm. Explicit costs involve the direct payment of money. Example: if a firm hires a worker for $1,000 a week, the opportunity cost of that worker is the cost of the worker and the other things on which the $1,000 could have been spent.
  • Implicit costs: costs relating to factors of production that the firm already owns. The firm does not pay money to use them, but there is still an opportunity cost involved in their use, because they could have been hired out, sold, or used elsewhere. Implicit costs are the earnings a firm could have had if it had employed its factors in another use, or if it had hired out or sold them to another firm.

Worked examples of implicit costs:

  • An owner who could earn $100,000 per year in her next best alternative job (e.g. as a tax accountant) has an implicit cost of $100,000 — the firm’s existence depends on covering this cost, since if it is not met the entrepreneur would presumably close down the firm and take the outside job.
  • A firm that owns its buildings, which could be rented out to other firms for $15,000 per month, has an implicit cost of $15,000 per month — the rent foregone and the things that could have been purchased with that money.
Accountants vs economistsIf economists count implicit costs (as well as explicit costs) and accountants do not, then economists and accountants will report different profit levels for the same firm. An accountant only subtracts explicit costs from revenue; an economist subtracts economic cost (explicit + implicit) from revenue. This means a firm can look profitable to an accountant while an economist judges it as only just breaking even (making normal profit) once the opportunity cost of the owner’s time and capital is included.

Costs are separated into two groups: total costs and average/marginal costs.

Total costs

  • Total fixed cost (TFC): the total cost of the fixed assets used by a firm in a given period. TFC is, by definition, fixed — it is the same whether the firm produces one unit or one hundred units. TFC = number of fixed assets × cost of each fixed asset. In the example, TFC is a constant $400 per week (four machines costing $100 each) at every level of output.
  • Total variable cost (TVC): the total cost of the variable assets used by a firm in a given period. TVC increases as the firm uses more of the variable factor. TVC = number of variable factors × cost of each variable factor. TVC is $200 with one worker and $1,200 with six workers.
  • Total cost (TC): the total cost of all fixed and variable factors used to produce a certain output. TC = TFC + TVC. E.g. at 105 units of output per week, TC = $1,600 (the fixed cost of $400 plus the variable cost of $1,200).

TVC starts at the origin and rises slowly at first, then more steeply as diminishing marginal returns set in. TC is the vertical sum of the two, so it starts at $200 and stays exactly $200 above TVC at every output (for example TVC is $224 and TC is $424 at 8 units).

Average costs

Average costs are costs per unit of output.

  • Average fixed cost (AFC): the fixed cost per unit of output.
  • Average variable cost (AVC): the variable cost per unit of output.
  • Average total cost (ATC): the total cost per unit of output. ATC = AFC + AVC.
AFC = TFC ÷ q

To find AFC, take the total fixed cost for the period and divide it by however many units of output (q) were produced in that same period.

Worked example

A bakery has weekly fixed costs (rent and equipment lease) of $2,000 and bakes 800 loaves that week. AFC = TFC ÷ q = $2,000 ÷ 800 = $2.50 per loaf — each loaf carries $2.50 of fixed cost once the rent and lease are spread across that week’s output.

AVC = TVC ÷ q

To find AVC, take the total variable cost for the period (spending on things like flour and workers’ wages that change with output) and divide it by the number of units of output produced.

Worked example

The same bakery spends $3,200 that week on flour and wages (its total variable cost) to bake the 800 loaves. AVC = TVC ÷ q = $3,200 ÷ 800 = $4.00 per loaf — each loaf carries $4.00 of variable cost.

ATC = TC ÷ q

To find ATC, take total cost (fixed plus variable) for the period and divide it by the number of units of output produced; equivalently, add AFC and AVC together.

Worked example

The bakery’s total cost that week is TC = TFC + TVC = $2,000 + $3,200 = $5,200 for 800 loaves. ATC = TC ÷ q = $5,200 ÷ 800 = $6.50 per loaf (matching AFC $2.50 + AVC $4.00) — this is the full cost, on average, of producing each loaf that week.

where q is the level of output.

Because TFC is a constant, AFC always falls continuously as output increases (a fixed number spread over more and more units) — in the example AFC is $40 per unit at output 10 and falls to $3.33 per unit at output 120. AFC never rises.

AVC tends to fall as output increases, then starts to rise again as output continues to increase. This is explained by the hypothesis of eventually diminishing average returns (met in Chapter 5): as more of the variable factor is applied to the fixed factors, the output per unit of the variable factor eventually falls, so the cost per unit of output eventually rises. In the example, AVC is $20 per unit at output 10, falls to $11.11 per unit at output 90, then rises to $13.33 at output 120.

ATC (like AVC) tends to fall as output increases and then rises again as output continues to increase. In the example, ATC is $60 per unit at output 10, falls to $15.24 at output 105, then rises to $16.67 at output 120.

Marginal cost

Marginal cost (MC)MC is the increase in total cost of producing one extra unit of output.
MC = ΔTC ÷ Δq

where ΔTC is the change in total cost and Δq is the change in the level of output.

Worked example

Continuing the bakery example: output rises from 800 to 850 loaves in a week when total cost rises from $5,200 to $5,450. ΔTC = $5,450 − $5,200 = $250 and Δq = 850 − 800 = 50, so MC = $250 ÷ 50 = $5.00 — producing each of those extra 50 loaves cost the bakery $5.00 on average.

MC tends to fall as output increases, and then starts to rise again as output continues to increase. This is explained by the hypothesis of eventually diminishing marginal returns: as more of the variable factor is applied to the fixed factors, the extra output from each additional unit of the variable factor eventually falls, so the extra cost per unit of output eventually begins to rise. In the example, MC falls from $20 as output rises from 0 to 10 units, falls to $8.00 as output rises from 45 to 70, then rises again to $40.00 as output rises from 115 to 120.

Figure 10.2: Short-run AFC, AVC, ATC and MC curvesAFC (= TFC/q, with TFC = $98) falls continuously. AVC, ATC and MC are U-shaped. MC has the lowest minimum (at 4 units) and cuts AVC exactly at the bottom of AVC (6 units, $24) and ATC exactly at the bottom of ATC (7 units, $39). ATC lies above AVC by the amount of AFC, so the gap narrows as output rises.
MC cuts AC and AVC at their minimum pointsThis is a mathematical relationship, not a coincidence: whenever the marginal value (MC) is below the average value (AC or AVC), it pulls the average down; whenever the marginal value is above the average, it pulls the average up. Therefore the MC curve must cross the AVC curve exactly at AVC’s lowest point, and must cross the ATC curve exactly at ATC’s lowest point. AFC continuously falls as output rises, and since the vertical gap between ATC and AVC is AFC, that gap gets smaller and smaller as output grows (AVC and ATC curves converge, getting closer together at higher output).

MC reaches its minimum first, then rises and cuts AVC at the lowest point of AVC and ATC at the lowest point of ATC (marked with dots). ATC lies above AVC and the gap between them (AFC) narrows as output rises.

3. Measuring revenue

Revenue is the income a firm receives from selling its products over a given time period. Revenue is measured in three ways.

Total revenue (TR)

TR = p × q

where p is the price the good sells for and q is the quantity sold in the period.

Worked example

If a firm sells 400 pizzas per week at $6 per pizza, TR = $6 × 400 = $2,400 per week — this is the total money the firm takes in from pizza sales that week, before any costs are subtracted.

Average revenue (AR)

AR = TR ÷ q = (p × q) ÷ q = p

Since TR is (p × q), q cancels from top and bottom, so AR always equals price, p.

Worked example

Continuing the pizza example, AR = TR ÷ q = $2,400 ÷ 400 = $6 per pizza — exactly the selling price, confirming that average revenue always equals price for a single-price seller.

Marginal revenue (MR)

Marginal revenue (MR)MR is the extra revenue a firm gains when it sells one more unit of a product in a given period.
MR = ΔTR ÷ Δq

where Δ means “the change in”.

Worked example

If the pizza firm lowers its price to $5 and weekly sales rise to 500 pizzas, TR rises from $2,400 to $2,500, so MR = ($2,500 − $2,400) ÷ 100 = $1. The extra revenue from selling an extra unit is $1 — much less than the $5 price, because part of the gain from higher sales is offset by receiving a lower price on every pizza sold, not just the extra ones.

What happens to revenue when output increases?

Two different situations must be considered.

1. Revenue when price does not change with output (perfectly elastic demand)

If a firm does not have to lower price as output increases, it faces a perfectly elastic demand curve. This happens only in theory, but it is very useful when economists build models of how markets work, starting with the theoretical market form of perfect competition (Chapter 12/11). This assumes the firm is very small relative to the whole industry, so it cannot affect total industry supply, and thus price, in any significant way — the firm can sell all it produces at the same price.

AR equals price and falls as output increases, since price has to be lowered to sell more. MR also falls as output increases, but at a greater rate than AR: MR is twice as steeply sloping as AR, and MR falls below the x-axis (goes negative) while AR is still positive. MR is below AR because in order to sell more units the firm must lower the price on all units sold, not just the extra one — it loses revenue on the units that could have been sold at the higher price.

Worked example (price fall, TR rising region): When price drops from $40 to $35, quantity demanded increases from 4 to 6. Before the price drop, TR = $40 × 4 = $160. After the drop, TR = $35 × 6 = $210. MR = ΔTR ÷ Δq = $50 ÷ 2 = $25. Two things affect TR: two extra units are sold at $35 each, adding $70; but the price of the 4 units that could have sold for $40 is dropped to $35, losing $20 (4 × $5) of revenue. Net effect: +$70 − $20 = +$50.

Worked example (price fall, TR falling region): When price drops from $15 to $10, quantity demanded increases from 14 to 16. Before: TR = $15 × 14 = $210. After: TR = $10 × 16 = $160. MR = −$50 ÷ 2 = −$25. The negative MR means TR falls: two extra units sell at $10 each (+$20), but the price on the 14 units that could have sold at $15 drops to $10, losing $70 (14 × $5). Net effect: +$20 − $70 = −$50.

For a normal downward-sloping demand curve, TR rises at first (extra revenue from extra sales outweighs the loss from lower price on existing sales) but eventually starts to fall (the loss from selling everything at a lower price outweighs the gain from the extra units sold).

Pricing rules for revenue-seeking firmsKnowing whether demand is elastic or inelastic tells a firm what pricing policy will raise revenue.
  • If the firm raises price and demand is inelastic, total revenue will increase, because the increase in price causes a relatively smaller fall in quantity demanded.
  • If the firm raises price and demand is elastic, total revenue will decrease, because the increase in price causes a relatively larger fall in quantity demanded.

The basic rules, summarized:

  1. When PED is elastic, any firm wishing to increase revenue should lower its price.
  2. When PED is inelastic, any firm wishing to increase revenue should raise its price.
  3. When PED is unity (equal to 1), any firm wishing to increase revenue should leave the price unchanged, since revenue is already maximized.

4. Measuring profits: normal profit, abnormal profit and losses

An illustrative dialogue: an economist and an accountant look at the same accounts for a firm making $80,000 profit (by the accountant’s reckoning). The accountant says the owner, Nermin, will be very happy. The economist says she will be satisfied, but only just — because the economist subtracts economic cost (which includes the implicit opportunity cost of the entrepreneur running the firm herself, rather than working elsewhere) whereas the accountant subtracts only explicit costs. If Nermin could earn $80,000 per year in her next best alternative occupation, then a $80,000 accounting profit represents exactly zero economic profit — she is no better and no worse off than her next best alternative.

The opportunity cost of the entrepreneurThe opportunity cost of the firm’s owner is the most important cost a firm faces, because it is the difference between survival and non-survival. If an owner does not cover their opportunity cost in the long run, they will close the firm and move to their next best alternative occupation.
Total profit = Total revenue − Economic cost (explicit costs + implicit costs)

To calculate total profit, add up everything the firm earns from sales (total revenue) and subtract its economic cost — that is, its explicit costs (money actually paid out, e.g. wages, rent, materials) plus its implicit costs (the value of opportunities given up, e.g. the salary an owner-entrepreneur could have earned elsewhere).

Worked example

A graphic-design studio earns total revenue of $150,000 in a year. Its explicit costs (staff salaries, software, office rent) come to $90,000, and its owner could have earned $50,000 a year working for another firm, so the implicit cost is $50,000. Economic cost = $90,000 + $50,000 = $140,000. Total profit = $150,000 − $140,000 = $10,000. Because this is greater than zero, the studio is making an abnormal (economic) profit of $10,000 — it is earning $10,000 more than enough to cover the owner’s opportunity cost of running the business.

From this point on, “total cost” is understood to include both explicit and implicit costs. Three profit situations are identified:

  • Normal profit (zero economic profit): total revenue = total cost (equivalently, average revenue = average cost). The firm exactly covers all its economic costs, including the entrepreneur’s opportunity cost.
  • Abnormal profit (also called economic profit): total revenue > total cost. The firm earns more than enough to cover all economic costs.
  • Losses (negative economic profit): total revenue < total cost. The firm does not cover all economic costs.

The entrepreneur will eventually close the firm and move to their next best occupation.

For the remainder of the chapter, “abnormal profit”, “normal profit” and “losses” are used, but remember these are the same as economic profit, zero economic profit, and negative economic profit respectively.

5. How firms maximize profits

Economists usually assume the main aim of a firm is to maximize profits. If at the firm’s present output the cost of producing another unit (MC) is less than the revenue that unit would bring in (MR), the firm could increase its profits by producing more — wherever MC < MR, the firm should increase production.

Between them MC < MR, so every extra unit adds to profit (blue). Below q0 and beyond q1 MC > MR (pink), so extra units reduce profit. Profit is therefore maximised at q1, where MC = MR with MC rising.
The MC = MR profit-maximizing ruleBecause the leftmost intersection of MC and MR (at q0) is actually the point of loss/profit minimization, to avoid confusion this left-hand part of the MC curve is normally omitted from diagrams, and only the profit-maximizing output q1 is shown. General rule: if a firm wishes to maximize its profits, it should produce at the level of output where Marginal Cost (MC) cuts Marginal Revenue (MR) from below (i.e. where MC is rising through MR).

6. Do businesses always behave rationally? Alternative objectives

In reality, the assumption that producers behave “rationally” by only attempting to maximize profits is often not true. Firms may pursue other aims.

1. Corporate social responsibility (CSR)

Corporate social responsibilityCSR is where a business includes the “public interest” in its decision making. It adopts an ethical code that accepts responsibility for the impact of its activities on areas such as the workforce, consumers, the local community and the environment.

Different businesses adopt different approaches to CSR: some concentrate on encouraging development in the workforce and local community through educational projects and fair trade; some focus on reducing negative environmental effects (cutting emissions, using sustainable resources); some prioritize aid to less developed communities. Some combine several approaches.

Key concept: EQUITYCSR is flagged in the textbook as an illustration of the key concept of Equity.
Key concept: ECONOMIC WELLBEINGCSR is also flagged as illustrating the key concept of Economic Wellbeing.
Key concept: SUSTAINABILITYThe rise of CSR is also flagged as illustrating the key concept of Sustainability.

Advantages of CSR: attracting and keeping a better workforce, building reputation, developing brand loyalty for being an ethical business. Appropriate CSR policies can reduce the need for government intervention in business activities. There has been a solid increase in “ethical consumerism” and CSR since the mid-1980s, as consumers become more aware of global and environmental issues and increasingly favour businesses that promote ethical considerations.

However, some economists argue companies may adopt CSR to gain a good reputation and distract attention from their main product (especially suggested for cigarette and alcohol producers).

Economics in action: the book highlights H&M, Ikea, Gap, Adidas and Nike as global brands leading on sustainable cotton sourcing, illustrating real-world corporate sustainability initiatives.

2. Satisficing

SatisficingSome economic theories doubt whether entrepreneurs ever, in reality, attempt to maximize profits. They claim entrepreneurs actually “satisfice” — aiming to perform satisfactorily rather than to a maximum level, in order to be able to pursue other goals.

Example: if people own their own firms, they will work hard enough to make a reasonable living (covering their opportunity costs), but will not push themselves further, preferring to pursue other goals such as leisure. In many cases firms are run by people who do not actually own them (e.g. a firm owned by shareholders not involved in running the company, managed instead by employed non-owner managers). In this case the managers have little to gain personally if the firm makes maximum profits — they are likely to make enough profit to keep the owners happy and keep their jobs, but no more — they “satisfice”.

Key concept: CHOICESatisficing is flagged as illustrating the key concept of Choice.

3. Growth maximization (increasing market share)

Growth maximizationCompanies may set their target to achieve growth in the short run, rather than profits, in order to gain a large market share and dominate the market in the long run. Growth may be measured by quantity of sales, sales revenue, employment, or the percentage of market share.

4. Revenue maximization

Revenue maximizationEntrepreneurs sometimes measure success by the amount of revenue made rather than profit. If so, firms attempt to maximize sales revenue by producing where marginal revenue is zero (the output where TR is at a maximum — see Figure 10.8/10.5). This output level is above (larger than) the profit-maximizing level of output.
