5.9 — Management information systems

Key terms

  • Management information system (MIS) — tools used to support business processes, operations, intelligence, and technology; MIS move data and manage information so data-driven reports can help businesses make the right decisions at the right time.
  • Executive information system (EIS) — used by senior management to make decisions affecting the entire organization, providing high-level data with the ability to drill into detail.
  • Marketing information system (MKIS) — used by a marketing team to report on the effectiveness of past and current campaigns and plan future ones.
  • Business intelligence system (BIS) — used by operations to make decisions based on collecting, integrating, and analyzing data; similar to an EIS but used by both lower-level managers and executives.
  • Customer relationship management system (CRM) — stores key customer information, including previous sales, contact details, and sales opportunities, used by marketing, sales, and customer service teams.
  • Sales force automation system (SFA) — a specialized CRM component that automates sales team tasks such as contact management, lead tracking, and order management.
  • Transaction processing system (TPS) — completes a sale and manages related details, e.g. a point-of-sale (POS) system, allowing reporting on usage trends and sales over time.
  • Knowledge management system (KMS) — used by customer service departments to answer questions and troubleshoot problems.
  • Financial accounting system (FAS) — an MIS specific to finance and accounting departments, such as accounts payable and receivable.
  • Human resource management system (HRMS) — tracks employee performance records and payroll data.
  • Supply chain management system (SCM) — used by manufacturing companies to track the flow of resources, materials, and services from purchase until final products are shipped.
  • Data analytics — the process of analyzing raw data to draw out meaningful, actionable insights that inform and drive business decisions.
  • General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) — the toughest privacy and security law in the world; passed by the EU but applies to any organization that targets or collects data on people in the EU, with harsh fines for violations.
  • Database — a collection of logically related information organized so it can be easily accessed, managed, and updated, generally controlled by a database management system.
  • Database administrator — the individual responsible for managing a business's databases, including security, access control, backup, and disaster recovery.
  • Cybercrime — the digital-world equivalent of analog crimes, such as theft of property or identity, hostage/ransom holding, and attacks on critical infrastructure, carried out through computer systems.
  • Hacktivists — cybercriminals who disrupt business as usual, breaching computer systems of governments or corporations to call out a perceived wrong or increase transparency.
  • Artificial neural network (ANN) — a computational model that attempts to replicate the brain's network of neurons so a computer can learn and make judgments in a human-like way, used to identify hidden patterns in data and make predictions.
  • Data center — a facility of networked computers, storage systems, and computing infrastructure that organizations use to assemble, process, store, and disseminate large amounts of data.
  • Cloud computing — the delivery of IT resources over the internet, often pay-as-you-go, so businesses can access storage and databases from providers without owning physical data centers.
  • Virtual reality (VR) — a computer-generated virtual world using 3D imagery and interactivity to create a realistic, immersive environment, often via a headset and controllers.
  • Internet of things (IoT) — the network of physical objects embedded with sensors, software, and other technologies that connect and exchange data with other devices and systems over the internet.
  • Artificial intelligence (AI) — technology used in business across automation, data analytics, and natural language processing to improve decisions, sales, finance, marketing, and HR processes.
  • Natural language processing (NLP) — an AI domain that helps search engines be more intuitive and makes chatbots more insightful and customer-centric.
  • Big data — large, diverse data sets that, when analyzed computationally, reveal patterns, trends, and associations, especially relating to human behavior, that can be used to inform decisions.
  • Volume, velocity, variety, veracity — the four dimensions of big data: there is a lot of it (volume), it needs analyzing quickly (velocity), it comes in many formats (variety), and it can be difficult to understand (veracity).
  • Customer loyalty program — a scheme that rewards repeat customers, used because retaining existing customers costs less than acquiring new ones.
  • Points-based loyalty program — customers build up points with every purchase (or through engagement, e.g. social media, reviews), redeemable for gifts, cashback, or perks.
  • Gamification — adding typical elements of game playing, such as point scoring and competition, to a company's systems and services to engage and stimulate users.
  • Tiered loyalty program — gives customers access to different benefits depending on their rank, usually based on spend or frequency of use, with higher tiers earning better rewards.
  • Paid loyalty program — gives customers immediate and ongoing benefits in exchange for a one-off or recurring participation fee.
  • Value-based loyalty program — connects with customers on a deeper level by donating a percentage of purchase costs to charity or welfare programs.
  • Digital Taylorism — the use of digital tools to oversee workers and business operations by monitoring and collecting as much data as possible to inform decisions on goals and productivity.
  • Data mining — the use of algorithms to convert large amounts of raw data stored in databases into meaningful information and new knowledge to inform decision-making.

Key theories & models

  • MIS categories model — a framework classifying the range of specialized management information systems a business can use (EIS, MKIS, BIS, CRM, SFA, TPS, KMS, FAS, HRMS, SCM), each supporting a different business function or level of decision-making.
  • Four dimensions of big data (volume, velocity, variety, veracity) — a model used to characterize and evaluate big data sets and the challenges of analyzing them for business decision-making.
  • Digital Taylorism — extends Taylor's scientific management principle of close monitoring into the digital age, using sensors, algorithms, and continuous data streams to control and evaluate workers, which research (e.g. a Cornell University study on logistics) links to reduced worker autonomy, sleep deprivation, and unsafe behavior such as speeding.
  • Data mining as a decision-making model — a process/theory of extracting useful information from large databases that traditional analysis cannot access, used to improve customer experience, increase profitability, reduce risk, and detect fraud, but requiring large databases, specialist training, and raising privacy concerns.
