2.2 — Organizational structure

Key terms

  • Organizational structure — how employees are coordinated and communicated with in a business, shaped by factors such as the number and type of employees, working practices, the business environment, and the stage of business development.
  • Formal organizational structure — a structure with clearly defined positions, duties, responsibilities, and authorities, operating within a set of rules and procedures that give stability and remove ambiguity over who is responsible for what.
  • Informal organization — member-developed networks of interpersonal relationships that develop within a formal structure as people interact, with no written rules or clearly defined roles.
  • Delegation — when a manager empowers another employee to make a particular decision or lead an initiative, while the manager retains overall responsibility for the outcome.
  • Span of control — the number of people for whom an individual has direct authority in a business.
  • Levels of hierarchy — the number of levels of authority within an organization, typically ranging from senior management and executives down to general employees.
  • Chain of command — the formal route through which instructions, communication, and decisions travel down through an organization.
  • Bureaucracy — the range of rules, regulations, and procedures that must be followed within an organization.
  • Centralization — a set-up in which decision-making powers are concentrated in a few leaders at the top of the organizational structure.
  • Decentralization — the transfer of control of an activity to several managers rather than being held by one person at the top.
  • Delayering — the process of reducing the number of levels in the hierarchy of an organization.
  • Flat organizational structure — a structure with limited levels of hierarchy, wider spans of control, short chains of command, frequent delegation, and more decentralized decision-making.
  • Tall organizational structure — a structure with more levels of hierarchy, limited delegation, narrow spans of control, longer chains of command, and a more centralized approach to decision-making.
  • Functional structure — an organization divided into departments by area of specialty, such as finance, marketing, IT, and operations, each led by a functional head.
  • Product-based structure — an organization divided into business units for each major product, with each product division containing its own supporting functional departments (e.g. sales, marketing, manufacturing).
  • Geographic (regional) structure — an organization divided by location (e.g. Asia, Americas, EMEA), with each region having its own management, operations, marketing, sales, and product teams operating under overall direction from central headquarters.
  • Matrix structure — a structure based on vertical relationships (e.g. function, project, product, or region) and horizontal, project-based relationships, in which an employee reports to two superiors, such as a functional manager and a project manager.
  • Project-based structure — a structure in which specialized teams are drawn from different departments to work on a specific project, reporting only to a single project manager rather than to more than one boss.

Key theories & models

  • Charles Handy's shamrock organization — a model, from his 1989 book The Age of Unreason, describing an organization as three "leaves": the professional core (essential senior/highly trained staff with in-demand skills), the contractual fringe (external specialists and organizations paid for results, not time), and the flexible labor force (part-time or intermittent workers); it is often used to explain a move toward outsourcing non-core functions.
