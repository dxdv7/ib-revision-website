2.4 — Motivation and demotivation

Key terms

  • Motivation — the ways a business can encourage its employees to work; a motivated workforce cares about the success of the business and helps it achieve its goals and mission.
  • Intrinsic motivation — motivation that comes from completing a task that is personally rewarding, with an absence of external threats or rewards.
  • Extrinsic motivation — motivation driven by an external factor, such as a reward like pay or the threat of a negative outcome.
  • Labor turnover — the rate at which employees leave employment, calculated as (number of employees leaving in a year ÷ average number of people employed) × 100.
  • Appraisal — a review of an employee's performance by a senior member of staff to verify their contribution against the roles and responsibilities in their job description.
  • Formative appraisal — appraisal taking place at the beginning, middle, and end of a training placement, or at regular points through a contract, used to assess progress and inform improvement.
  • Summative appraisal — a written account of an employee's performance over the year, documenting achievements against predetermined targets set at the start of the year.
  • 360-degree feedback — a system of anonymous feedback about an employee gathered from managers, subordinates, peers, and a self-evaluation, giving a well-rounded view of the individual.
  • Self-appraisal — employees assess their own performance against predetermined criteria, often compared with their line manager's assessment as a discussion starting point.
  • Horn effect — an appraisal bias where an appraiser focuses only on an employee's negative attributes and ignores their positive qualities.
  • Halo effect — an appraisal bias, the opposite of the horn effect, where an employee's positive qualities overshadow their negative attributes or behavior.
  • Internal recruitment — filling vacancies by drawing on talent already within the organization, such as transfers, promotions, and re-employing ex-employees.
  • External recruitment — filling vacancies by searching outside the organization, e.g. advertisements, employment agencies, educational institutions, headhunting, and employee recommendations.
  • Headhunting — a business approaches individuals already working elsewhere because they are highly sought-after for their experience, skill set, or expertise.
  • Salary — a yearly fixed financial payment to employees, paid monthly, with no extra pay for hours worked beyond the contracted amount.
  • Wages — payment, usually weekly, based on hours worked (time-based) or output produced (piece rate).
  • Minimum wage — the lowest remuneration that employers can legally pay their employees.
  • Commission — pay that rewards employees with a set percentage of the value of each sale, often combined with a minimal base salary.
  • Performance-related pay — pay awarded to employees for meeting or exceeding performance targets, e.g. cash bonuses, salary increases, or gratuity pay.
  • Profit-related pay — remuneration giving employees a percentage of the business's annual profits, on top of regular pay, when profit targets are met.
  • Employee share ownership scheme — a financial reward giving employees company shares (free, as a reward, or at a discounted purchase price), which dilutes ownership as shareholder numbers rise.
  • Fringe benefits (perks) — non-cash financial rewards provided in addition to basic pay, such as medical insurance, a company car, or gym membership, which raise production costs.
  • Non-financial rewards — cost-efficient rewards that do not require a financial outlay and instead focus on recognizing the employee, helping with retention.
  • Job enrichment — involving employees in a wider variety of more challenging tasks and responsibilities that require new skills, building confidence and competence.
  • Job rotation — employees switch between jobs over time to upskill them and spread skills throughout the organization, guarding against boredom.
  • Job enlargement — a horizontal restructuring method that increases the number of tasks in a specific job, increasing workforce flexibility and reducing boredom.
  • Empowerment — delegating power, autonomy, and authority to workers so they have responsibility and freedom to carry out tasks in their own way, increasing morale and trust.
  • Purpose — an intrinsic motivator associated with making a personal, professional, or social difference through work, common in fields like healthcare, education, and social work.
  • Teamwork — collaborating with colleagues toward a common goal, which can increase job satisfaction, productivity, and innovative ideas, though it can carry a financial cost.
  • Training — a learning and development process through which employees acquire the skills, abilities, and knowledge needed to improve performance in their current job.
  • Induction training — a workplace orientation program that familiarizes new employees with an organization's rules, culture, and working environment.
  • On-the-job training — instruction that takes place at work, such as job shadowing, so employees can eventually perform tasks without supervision.
  • Off-the-job training — training that takes place away from the workplace, e.g. courses run by outside experts, allowing focus without workplace distractions.

Key theories & models

  • Taylor — scientific management — proposed that work should be scientifically analyzed to find the 'one best way' of doing a job, workers scientifically selected and trained to be 'first-class', and management and workers should cooperate, with a clear division between planning (management) and doing (workers).
  • Maslow — hierarchy of needs — a five-level pyramid of human needs (physiological, safety, love/belonging, esteem, self-actualization); only unsatisfied needs motivate, and as one level is met the next level becomes the motivator, with self-actualization stimulating a desire for 'peak experiences'.
  • Herzberg — two-factor theory — job satisfaction and dissatisfaction are driven by separate factors: motivators (found within the job itself, cause employees to work harder) and hygiene factors (surround the job; their absence causes employees to work less hard).
  • McClelland — acquired needs theory — individuals have three needs acquired over time and specific to them — the need for power (to control and influence others), the need for affiliation (to belong and be liked), and the need for achievement (to set and accomplish challenging, calculated-risk goals).
  • Deci and Ryan — self-determination theory — people become self-determined when their needs for competence, connection/relatedness, and autonomy are met; self-determined people take responsibility for failure and do not rely on external rewards.
  • Adams — equity theory — motivation depends on employees perceiving a fair balance between their 'inputs' (effort, skill, loyalty) and 'outputs' (pay, recognition, benefits); perceiving inputs as greater than outputs leads to demotivation and disruptive behavior.
  • Vroom — expectancy theory (VIE theory) — individuals rationally weigh valence (how much they value an outcome), instrumentality (the link between performance and reward), and expectancy (the belief that effort leads to the desired outcome) before deciding how hard to work.
  • McGregor — Theory X and Theory Y — a named theory contrasting an assumption that workers are lazy and must be controlled (Theory X) with an assumption that workers are self-motivated and enjoy responsibility (Theory Y).
  • Ouchi — Theory Z — a named theory of management emphasizing employee involvement, long-term employment, and collective decision-making.
  • Mayo — the Hawthorne effect — a named theory/finding that employees improve or modify their behavior in response to being observed or given attention, highlighting the importance of social factors at work.
  • Pink — three elements of intrinsic motivation — a named theory of intrinsic motivation built around autonomy, mastery, and purpose; referenced in this course in relation to employee empowerment and autonomy.
