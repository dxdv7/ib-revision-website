1.5 — Growth and evolution

Key terms

  • Average cost — the ratio of the total cost of production to the number of items produced.
  • Economies of scale — a decrease in average cost as a business grows and output increases, allowing it to charge lower prices than competitors.
  • Diseconomies of scale — an increase in average cost due to internal or external inefficiencies as a business grows, making it less competitive.
  • Internal economies of scale — cost savings that arise from a business's own growth and greater efficiency.
  • Managerial economies — cost savings from employing better-qualified, better-trained managers who make quicker, more profitable and innovative decisions.
  • Financial economies — larger firms enjoy greater creditworthiness, allowing them to borrow funds at lower interest rates and raise equity capital more easily.
  • By-products — waste from higher output that a large business can manufacture into saleable goods or sell to other businesses to generate extra revenue.
  • Technical economies — savings from large firms being able to use complex machinery more efficiently, increasing productivity and reducing average costs.
  • Economies of inventory management — a larger company's greater ability to adjust input and finished-product inventories, reducing discrepancies between production and sales.
  • Marketing economies — cost savings from bulk purchasing and bulk selling, and the stronger bargaining power that comes with size.
  • Advertising economies — a larger advertising budget lets a firm use the most effective media efficiently, lowering its per-unit advertising costs.
  • Risk economies — a large, diversified company can lower its average risk, as profits from some product lines can offset losses from others.
  • Internal diseconomies of scale — an increased average cost of production caused by problems arising from a business's own growth.
  • Organizational diseconomies — communication, control, and motivation problems in a growing workforce (e.g. staff feeling isolated or unappreciated) that raise average cost.
  • Technical diseconomies — inefficiencies in the production process caused by growth, such as overcrowding, lack of equipment, or unfamiliarity with new facilities.
  • External economies of scale — cost savings for all firms in an industry caused by factors outside any single company's control, such as a skilled local workforce, good infrastructure, or favorable business regulations.
  • External diseconomies of scale — rising average costs affecting all firms in an industry due to external factors, such as competition for scarce resources or traffic congestion.
  • Internal (organic) growth — growth achieved through a business's own operations, e.g. launching new products, entering new markets, opening new stores, e-commerce, or outsourcing; relatively low-risk but slow.
  • E-commerce — the sale of goods and services online, allowing a company to reach new customers and markets and increase market share.
  • Outsourcing — using workers or businesses outside the company to carry out a task, allowing it to grow capacity quickly and cheaply, though with less control and possible quality/reputation risk.
  • External (inorganic) growth — growth achieved by buying, combining with, or partnering with other companies rather than through a business's own operations; faster than internal growth but often costlier and riskier.
  • Merger — a financial transaction in which two businesses combine into one new business with the consent of both boards of directors.
  • Acquisition — a financial transaction in which an acquiring company (the bidder) purchases a controlling stake in a target company.
  • Friendly takeover — an acquisition carried out with the approval of the target company's management and shareholders.
  • Hostile takeover — an acquisition carried out without the consent of the target company's management.
  • Takeover — when one business acquires control of another organization by purchasing the majority of its shares, typically a larger company pursuing a smaller one.
  • Dawn attack — a hostile takeover tactic where the acquirer purchases a sizable stake in the target as soon as markets open, gaining control before the target is aware.
  • Poison pill — a defense tactic allowing a target company's existing shareholders to buy additional shares at a reduced price, diluting the acquirer's stake and raising the cost of a takeover.
  • Joint venture — two or more companies investing together in a business activity for a finite time, agreeing to share revenue, expenses, and assets; useful for low-risk entry to new markets.
  • Strategic alliance — a formal agreement between two or more organizations to work together toward shared goals for a defined period, sharing resources and knowledge while remaining independent, unlike M&As.
  • Equity alliance — a strategic alliance in which separate businesses join forces to create a new company that is co-owned by the participating partners; a joint venture is the most typical example.
  • Non-equity alliance — a strategic alliance established through a contract rather than shared ownership, such as a licensing agreement.
  • Licensing — a non-equity alliance in which one firm sells goods, services, or intellectual property to another company in return for a fee.
  • Franchising — a method of external growth where a franchisor sells another business (the franchisee) the right to use its brand name and sell its products, in exchange for a fee and royalties.
  • Franchisor — the business that sells the right to use its established brand name and products to a franchisee.
  • Franchisee — a business or individual that purchases a franchise, gaining use of an established brand, training, and shared marketing, in exchange for setup costs and royalties.
  • Royalties — ongoing payments, usually a share of profits or sales, made by a franchisee to a franchisor.
