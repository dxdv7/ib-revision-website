2.3 — Leadership and management

Key terms

  • Management — a process, engaged in by all managers regardless of aptitude or skill, of achieving an organization's goals through the inter-related functions of planning, organizing, leading, and controlling.
  • Leadership — the process of motivating people to understand and believe in a company's vision and to work on achieving its goals; leaders have followers, whereas managers simply have people who work for them.
  • Planning and decision-making — the management function of looking to the future, predicting trends, setting an organization's goals, and deciding how best to achieve them.
  • Organizing — the management function that enables plans to happen by coordinating the activities and resources needed to reach the goals within a plan.
  • Leading — the management function of influencing or prompting stakeholders to work together, creating a positive attitude toward the work and goals among employees, managers, and other stakeholders.
  • Controlling — the management function of monitoring and evaluating progress toward goals, measuring and adapting tasks and actions to enable the plan to be accomplished.
  • Autocratic leadership — also known as authoritarian leadership; a top-down style in which little or no input is requested from employees, and senior management or a central leader has the final say on all decisions.
  • Paternalistic leadership — a style in which a dominant authority figure acts like a parent or caregiver, treating employees as members of an extended family and expecting loyalty and trust in return.
  • Democratic leadership — also known as participative or shared leadership; leaders and employees are brought into every decision, with equal participation in discussion and debate, although the final word still belongs to the leader.
  • Laissez-faire leadership — from the French for 'let it be'; leaders give subordinates the freedom to complete their responsibilities in a manner they choose, without strict policies or procedures.
  • Situational leadership — an adaptive leadership style in which a leader changes their approach based on the individual, the task, and the context, rather than relying on one fixed style.
  • Intuitive thinking/management — decision-making that relies on instinctive understanding, gut feeling, and experience rather than conscious reasoning, often used to make fast decisions.
  • Scientific thinking/management — decision-making that applies logic and relevant data, such as data mining, software logic, predictive models, and forecasts, to reduce uncertainty and risk.
  • Micromanagement — a detail-oriented, controlling approach in which a leader closely oversees subordinates' work; whether it is viewed negatively can depend on the leader's competence and the employee's own experience.

Key theories & models

  • Blanchard and Hersey's situational leadership theory — a framework, from their book Management of Organizational Behavior, in which the right leadership style depends on an employee's development stage: the enthusiastic beginner (high commitment, low competence, needs highly directive leadership), the disillusioned learner (needs both highly directive and highly supportive leadership), the capable-but-cautious performer (needs less direction while support remains high), and the self-reliant achiever (needs delegation rather than direction or close support).
