4.5 — The seven Ps of the marketing mix

Key terms

  • Marketing mix — the seven Ps (product, price, promotion, place, people, process, physical evidence) that a business combines in a strategic, coordinated way to market its products effectively.
  • Product — a good or service that a business sells to its customers.
  • Price — the amount a business charges its customers in exchange for the product.
  • Promotion — a business informing people about products and attempting to persuade them to buy.
  • Place — the process of getting products from the business to its customers (distribution), ensuring goods and services are available where and when required.
  • People (marketing mix) — the skills, attitudes, knowledge, and appearance of employees, and the interaction and relationships between key stakeholders such as customers and employees.
  • Process (marketing mix) — the way a service is ordered, received, and paid for by the customer; also called 'the ease of doing business'.
  • Physical evidence — the tangible aspects or physical environment of a business in which customers and employees interact; acts as 'proof' of service quality since a service cannot be experienced before delivery.
  • Introduction stage — the first product life cycle stage: slow sales growth, high spending, negative or zero profit, and poor (negative) cash flow as the product becomes known.
  • Growth stage — the product life cycle stage where sales rapidly increase, profits rise, and cash flow improves as the product becomes better known.
  • Maturity stage — the product life cycle stage where sales growth slows after reaching most potential customers; profits stabilize or fall and cash flow remains strong but begins to weaken.
  • Decline stage — the final product life cycle stage: sales and profits fall, cash flow worsens, and the business typically phases the product out.
  • Product portfolio — a business's range of products; ideally spread across multiple life cycle stages to balance investment needs and cash flow.
  • Extension strategies — actions used to prolong a product's life cycle, e.g. cosmetic changes, new product variants, new advertising, lowering price, new distribution channels, or finding new market segments.
  • Brand — an identifying name, symbol, image, or trademark that enables a product to be easily recognized and distinguished from competitors.
  • Brand awareness — the extent to which a brand is recognized and correctly associated with a particular product; usually built during the introduction stage through informative promotion.
  • Brand loyalty — the extent of customer faithfulness to a brand even when alternatives exist; usually built during the growth and maturity stages to encourage repeat purchases.
  • Brand development — the process of building brand awareness over time, involving reviewing strategy, identifying the target market, market research, developing a USP, and adjusting the marketing mix.
  • Brand value — an estimate of how much a brand is worth in terms of income, potential income, reputation, prestige, and market value; important when one business acquires another.
  • Goodwill — the difference between the purchase price an acquiring company pays and the net assets of the target company, reflecting the value of its brand.
  • Unique selling point (USP) — a feature of a product or business that differentiates it from competitors.
  • Cost-plus (mark-up) pricing — setting price by adding a percentage mark-up to the total cost of production per unit; simple and flexible, but relies on being able to accurately allocate costs.
  • Contribution pricing (HL) — setting price based only on the direct (variable) cost per unit plus a mark-up, where the mark-up represents how much the product contributes toward fixed (overhead) costs.
  • Penetration pricing — setting a low price to build awareness and market share when launching a product, with the intention of raising the price once the brand is established.
  • Loss leader pricing — selling a product below its unit cost (at a loss) to attract customers who then buy other, more profitable products; common in supermarkets.
  • Predatory pricing — setting a very low price to eliminate existing competitors or deter new ones from entering the market; often illegal and difficult to prove.
  • Premium pricing — deliberately setting a high price to create the impression of higher quality or value, aiming for high profit margins from a luxury market segment.
  • Dynamic pricing (HL) — changing the price of a good or service according to demand, e.g. surge pricing or time-based pricing; used by airlines, ride-sharing firms, and utility providers.
  • Competitive pricing (HL) — basing price on what competitors charge for similar products, by charging less, the same, or more than the competition.
  • Price elasticity of demand (PED) (HL) — a measure of how much quantity demanded responds to a change in price, calculated as (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price).
  • Inelastic demand (HL) — when PED is between 0 and 1; quantity demanded does not change much when price changes, allowing a business to charge a higher price.
  • Elastic demand (HL) — when PED is greater than 1; quantity demanded changes proportionately more than price, so a business should charge a lower price to raise total revenue.
  • Unit elastic (HL) — when PED equals 1; in theory the optimal, revenue-maximizing price point, where a change in price causes a proportional change in demand.
  • Above-the-line (ATL) promotion — untargeted mass promotion, such as television, radio, and print advertising; costly, hard to measure, and best used early in the product life cycle to build awareness.
  • Below-the-line (BTL) promotion — targeted promotion aimed at specific segments, e.g. flyers, emails, text messages, and in-store promotions; usually cheaper and best used later in the life cycle to build loyalty.
  • Through-the-line (TTL) promotion — an integrated marketing approach combining ATL and BTL elements, often through digital marketing, to balance the benefits of both.
  • Social media marketing (SMM) — using digital platforms (e.g. Facebook, Instagram, TikTok) to build a brand, increase sales, connect with customers, and gather data analytics; also called digital marketing.
  • Customer — a stakeholder who purchases a product from a business; can be an individual or another business, and may or may not be the final user of the product.
  • Consumer — the ultimate user of a product, who may or may not be the same person as the customer who purchased it.
  • Intermediary — a business (agent, wholesaler, or retailer) that helps move a product from the producer to the final consumer.
  • Agent (broker) — an intermediary who facilitates a sale by bringing buyers and sellers together, usually in exchange for a commission.
  • Wholesaler — an intermediary who makes bulk purchases from producers and sells the product on in smaller quantities, often to retailers.
  • Retailer — an intermediary who sells single items directly to consumers, sometimes purchasing stock from wholesalers.

Key theories & models

  • Product life cycle — a model charting a product's sales over its lifetime through four stages (introduction, growth, maturity, decline), used to guide marketing objectives, pricing, promotion, and distribution strategies at each stage.
  • Boston Consulting Group (BCG) matrix — classifies a business's products by relative market share and market growth rate into four categories — stars (high share, high growth), question marks (low share, high growth), cash cows (high share, low growth), and dogs (low share, low growth) — to evaluate a product portfolio and guide investment decisions.
