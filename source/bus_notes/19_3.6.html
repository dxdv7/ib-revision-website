3.6 — Efficiency ratio analysis

Key terms

  • Efficiency ratio — a ratio that assesses how effectively the directors of a business manage its current assets and current liabilities, such as stock, debtors, and creditors.
  • Stock turnover (number of times) — cost of sales ÷ average stock; measures how many times a business sells and replenishes the value of its stock over a period, with a higher figure generally preferred.
  • Stock turnover (number of days) — average stock ÷ cost of sales × 365; measures the average number of days stock is held before being sold, with a lower figure generally preferred.
  • Average stock — (opening stock + closing stock) ÷ 2; used in stock turnover calculations because the balance sheet only shows the closing stock value, and opening stock equals the previous year's closing stock.
  • Debtor days — debtors ÷ total sales revenue × 365; measures the average number of days customers take to pay for goods and services bought on credit; a lower figure is generally preferred, but the ratio should be consistent with the firm's credit policy (often 30–60 days).
  • Creditor days — creditors ÷ cost of sales × 365; measures the average number of days a business takes to pay its own suppliers for goods and services bought on credit; a higher figure is generally preferred, provided it stays consistent with supplier terms.
  • Gearing ratio — non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100; measures the proportion of a business that is funded through long-term debt/loan capital rather than equity, and is used by investors and lenders to assess financial risk.
  • Capital employed — non-current liabilities + equity; the total long-term capital invested in a business, used as the denominator of the gearing ratio.
  • Highly geared business — a business with a gearing ratio above roughly 50 per cent; it relies heavily on loan capital and is more exposed to financial risk from rising interest rates.
  • Low geared business — a business with a gearing ratio below roughly 25 per cent; it relies little on loan capital, which may indicate unused room for future borrowing to fund expansion.
  • Insolvency — when a business is unable to pay debts that become due; it can occur even if total assets exceed total liabilities (e.g. assets cannot be converted to cash in time), and it can be temporary and reversed.
  • Bankruptcy — when a business is judged by a court of law to be unable to pay its debts because its liabilities exceed its assets; it usually results in the business ceasing to exist in its current form, with assets distributed to creditors and lenders.
  • Just-in-time stock control — a stock management technique in which a business acquires stock only when it is needed, reducing storage costs but risking an inability to respond to a sudden surge in demand.
  • Trade credit — an arrangement allowing a business to receive goods or services now and pay for them at a later date; it underlies both the debtor days ratio (credit given to customers) and the creditor days ratio (credit received from suppliers).
