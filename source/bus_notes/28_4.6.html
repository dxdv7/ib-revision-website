4.6 — International marketing

Key terms

  • Exporting — making a product in the home country and selling it abroad, either managing the marketing directly (e.g. e-commerce) or hiring an intermediary/agent abroad.
  • Direct investment — a business sets up and establishes production plants and distribution channels abroad under its own direct control and ownership.
  • Franchising (international) — a business sells the right to use its brand and related products or services to franchisees abroad.
  • Licensing (international) — a business in another country purchases the right to produce a multinational company's product; often used for physical goods, while franchising is more common in services.
  • Joint venture (international) — businesses from two different countries join to form a third company, sharing risk and combining the comparative advantages of both original companies.
  • Mergers and acquisitions (international) — two businesses with similar aims legally consolidate into one company to share resources across borders.
  • Larger market size — an advantage of operating internationally: access to many more potential customers, increasing market share and the chance of becoming a market leader.
  • Greater security — an advantage of operating internationally: spreading risk across countries, so a downturn in one country's economy can be offset by gains in another, reducing the risk of bankruptcy or takeover.
  • Economies of scale (international) — expanding internationally can lower per-unit production costs through economies of scale in management, marketing, or purchasing.
  • Brand development (international) — international marketing lets a business build its brand and communicate with customers globally, gaining prestige and differentiating itself from domestic-only competitors.
  • Social factors (STEEPLE) — variations in demographics, lifestyles, tastes, and cultures that make entering and operating in international markets challenging.
  • Technological factors (STEEPLE) — differences in technological infrastructure and the use of innovative technologies across localities; e-commerce has increased global competition.
  • Economic factors (STEEPLE) — differences in employment rates, inflation, income levels, cost of living, purchasing power, and exchange rates that make it hard to replicate a business model across borders.
  • Environmental and ethical factors (STEEPLE) — challenges of sharing prosperity and minimizing environmental impact; multinationals risk damaging a host country's cultural identity or their own reputation at home.
  • Political and legal factors (STEEPLE) — threats such as political instability, varying regulation, anti-competition legislation, trade agreements, tax rates, health and safety laws, consumer protection, employment laws, and intellectual property rights.
  • Pan-global approach — a business applies the same marketing mix and brand worldwide without adapting it to local markets (standardization).
  • Global localization — a business changes its marketing mix to adapt to the local language, culture, and customs of each market (adaptation); Netflix's regionally adapted content is a cited example.

Key theories & models

  • STEEPLE analysis — a framework used to analyze the external Social, Technological, Economic, Environmental/ethical, and Political/legal factors affecting an organization, applied here to assess the threats of entering and operating internationally.
