3.2 — Sources of finance

Key terms

  • Internal sources of finance — funds generated from within the business itself, such as personal funds, retained profit, and sale of assets; the main advantage is that owners keep full control with no interest or borrowing costs, but the amount available is often limited.
  • External sources of finance — funds obtained from outside organizations or individuals, such as share capital, loan capital, overdrafts, trade credit, crowdfunding, leasing, microfinance, and business angels.
  • Personal funds — money invested by an entrepreneur from their own savings, typically a short-term source used in the early stages of a sole trader's business.
  • Retained profit — profit kept by a business from previous periods, after dividends and taxes have been paid, and reinvested for future expansion rather than distributed.
  • Sale of assets — raising cash by selling business assets, such as unused buildings or machinery, that are no longer needed; more common for older, established businesses.
  • Share capital (equity capital) — funds raised by selling shares in a company, giving investors part ownership; a permanent source of finance that never needs repaying and carries no interest, but dividends may be paid and control/ownership becomes diluted.
  • Loan capital (debt capital) — funds borrowed from a financial institution and repaid with interest through installments over an agreed period; owners retain control, but lenders often require collateral and a detailed business plan.
  • Overdraft — a short-term facility from a financial institution allowing a business to withdraw more than it holds in its account, up to an agreed limit, to cover cash deficits; flexible but often carries high interest rates.
  • Trade credit — a short-term arrangement allowing a business to receive goods or services now and pay the supplier later (commonly within 30–90 days), improving short-term cash flow but risking supplier relationships if payment is delayed.
  • Crowdfunding — raising finance by attracting a large number of people, usually via websites or social media, to each contribute a small amount; types include donation-based, rewards-based, profit/revenue-sharing, and debt (peer-to-peer) crowdfunding.
  • Leasing — obtaining the use of an asset by making periodic payments to its owner (the lessor) without ever owning it, as the lessee; the asset is not recorded as a fixed asset on the balance sheet, so it cannot be used as collateral.
  • Microfinance providers — organizations that provide small amounts of capital to entrepreneurs and small businesses, often in developing countries, who might otherwise lack access to finance due to a lack of collateral.
  • Business angels — wealthy individuals or groups who invest in small or medium-sized privately-owned businesses in exchange for partial ownership, often also offering business skills and expertise; unlike loans, no monthly repayments or interest are required.

Key theories & models

  • Factors influencing choice of source of finance — a framework for recommending an appropriate source of finance, considering the type and size of the business (for example, personal funds and microfinance suit sole traders, while share capital suits companies), the type and amount of expenditure (short-term revenue versus long-term capital expenditure), the amount of risk involved, and how quickly the funds are needed.
