1.1 — What is a business?

Key terms

  • Business (as an activity) — any activity that provides goods and services to consumers in order to make a profit.
  • Business (as an organization) — an entity that produces goods and services, adding value to resources to create products that meet the needs and wants of consumers.
  • Needs — human requirements that must be met for survival, and which are limited; examples include food, warmth, shelter, clothing, and healthcare.
  • Wants — human desires that are not essential for survival and are unlimited; examples include entertainment, travel, and gym memberships.
  • Resources / inputs (factors of production) — the four major types of resource used to produce goods and services: capital, land, labor, and enterprise/entrepreneurship.
  • Capital — manufactured goods needed to operate a business, such as machines, buildings, vehicles, and equipment; distinct from consumer goods, which are sold to the public.
  • Land — the raw materials and natural resources needed to produce goods and services.
  • Labor — the effort or work done by people, the quality of which depends on their skills, knowledge, and experience.
  • Enterprise / entrepreneurship — making a profit by identifying an opportunity, starting a company, arranging business deals and taking risks, and efficiently using capital, land, and labor.
  • Products — the output of business activity, divided into goods and services.
  • Goods — physical products; their production can require large, capital-intensive facilities, and their quality can be easily measured.
  • Services — intangible products, or tasks performed by labor; providing them usually requires smaller, more labor-intensive facilities with higher customer contact, and their quality is difficult to measure.
  • Manufacturing organization — a business that produces goods.
  • Service organization — a business that provides services; a business offering both goods and services is categorized as both a manufacturing and a service organization.
  • Business process — any activity performed to add value to basic resources, converting inputs into goods and services to generate profit for the business.
  • Human resources (HR) function — manages the labor and workforce, including recruitment, compensation, training, and motivation of employees.
  • Finance and accounts function — manages the organization’s capital and assets, identifying, recording, and communicating economic transactions, and ensuring sufficient funds exist to produce goods or services.
  • Marketing function — researches consumers’ needs and wants, including where and how they will buy a product and how much they are willing to pay, so the business produces what consumers want.
  • Operations function — manages the activities involved in production, overseeing the quality of materials, production methods, and stock control to keep production uninterrupted.
  • Business sectors — the four categories businesses are grouped into depending on their main activities: primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary.
  • Primary sector — businesses that extract and use natural resources to produce raw materials, such as farming, fishing, and mining; these products typically have lower value added.
  • Secondary sector — businesses that manufacture goods using natural resources and raw materials from the primary sector, such as a factory transforming oranges into bottled juice; these products have more value added.
  • Tertiary sector — businesses that sell services to consumers or other businesses, such as banks, transportation companies, and insurance companies; more significant in developed economies, where it employs a larger share of the workforce.
  • Quaternary sector — businesses that provide knowledge-based services such as consultancy, research and development, information services, or technology services, requiring a highly educated workforce.
  • Value added — the difference between the cost of extracting or producing a good and the price at which it is sold.
  • Entrepreneur — a risk taker who invests capital and time to create goods or services that fulfill consumer wants and needs; entrepreneurs are proactive, understand their market, and are committed and motivated.
  • Ethical business — a business that tries to minimize any negative impact on the environment, its workforce, and society, which can improve its image and customer loyalty.
  • Financial challenges (start-up) — entrepreneurs need money for the initial investment (machinery, premises, materials, registration) and for ongoing expenses such as wages; loans from banks are hard to get without a track record and often require collateral.
  • Marketing challenges (start-up) — a new business may offer a product that does not meet consumer needs, is priced uncompetitively, suffers from low brand awareness, or is not available in the target consumer’s area.
  • Human resources challenges (start-up) — a new business may struggle to hire the right people due to a lack of suitable or skilled candidates, or because its working conditions and salary are not attractive enough.
  • Production balance (start-up challenge) — deciding the right level of production; producing too little risks a supply shortage that damages the business’s image, while producing too much creates excess supply and an inefficient use of resources.
  • External (exogenous) factors — factors outside a business’s control that affect its success, such as natural disasters, the economic situation of a country, or a pandemic.
  • Gap in the market — an opportunity that arises when a business identifies a good or service that consumers want but that is not currently available.
  • Niche market — a relatively small market with few competitors; new businesses can succeed here if the entrepreneur has specific knowledge of the goods and services sold.
  • Innovation (as an opportunity) — bringing new or improved products or services to the market, whether by innovating on existing products or creating entirely new ones, to ensure a business’s future success.
