5.7 — Crisis management and contingency planning

Key terms

  • Crisis management — the response to and management of an emergency when it occurs, involving all members of an organization working to limit damage and restore normal operations as quickly as possible.
  • Contingency planning — the process of preparing for potential emergencies before they occur, including envisioning possible scenarios and setting the policies and procedures that will guide the organization's crisis management function.
  • Crisis — an emergency event affecting a business, which can take many forms such as natural disasters, environmental issues, cybersecurity threats, product recalls, employee misconduct, or conflict with interest groups.
  • Simulated exercises — practice scenarios used during contingency planning that place a crisis management team in a real-world-style situation to test decision-making and identify weaknesses in procedures.
  • Transparency — being open and honest in communications during a crisis, including proactively admitting fault when necessary, which helps maintain brand integrity and speeds recovery.
  • Speed — acting quickly during a crisis to minimize damage; effective only when paired with clear direction, as unplanned "knee-jerk" reactions can worsen a crisis.
  • Control — taking charge of a crisis situation and its associated communication to prevent further damage or the spread of inaccurate news, often requiring a more autocratic style of leadership.
  • Cost (of contingency planning) — the expense of developing a plan, including senior management time and consultant fees, plus the ongoing opportunity cost of regularly reviewing and updating it and of insurance; usually far smaller than the cost of an actual crisis.
  • Time (in contingency planning) — the time-consuming process of drafting and regularly revisiting contingency plans, as well as the speed of response required once an actual crisis occurs.
  • Risk (in contingency planning) — the level and range of potential dangers that must be assessed, which vary depending on the industry and can be minimized through effective contingency planning.
  • Safety (in contingency planning) — the health and safety implications and legislation that must be considered, since people may behave differently in an actual crisis, such as a fire, than they do during drills.

Key theories & models

  • Ready-Respond-Recover-Restore model — a framework describing the role of strategic communication across four phases of crisis management: Ready (groundwork laid during contingency planning), Respond (timely, accurate information provided as the crisis strikes), Recover (clarity provided to stakeholders as the organization stabilizes), and Restore (communication realigned to the organization's purpose, mission, and values after the crisis).
