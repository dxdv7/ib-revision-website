5.4 — Location

Key terms

  • Location decision — the choice of where to set up or relocate a business's operations, driven by factors such as cost, suppliers, infrastructure, competition, government, workforce, and potential for growth.
  • Bulk-gaining production — a process where the finished items are larger than their components, making it economical to locate close to the market or point of use, e.g. a narrowboat maker locating next to the canals.
  • Bulk-reducing production — a process where the finished items are smaller than the raw materials used, making it economical to locate close to the source of raw materials to minimize transport costs, e.g. a sawmill locating near timber.
  • Linked industries / external economies of concentration — the benefits firms gain from clustering close to similar industries, such as shared infrastructure, supply networks, and easy access to a pool of skilled labor, though this can raise the cost of locating there.
  • Just-in-time (JIT) and suppliers — being close to good local suppliers helps a business using JIT production reduce lead times on the production of final products.
  • Infrastructure — all aspects of transport and communication networks in an area, plus supporting facilities such as healthcare, education, utilities, housing, and policing, which affect a location's accessibility and appeal.
  • Subsidies — payments made by government to firms that can be offset against the costs of production, sometimes used to incentivize businesses to locate in deprived or high-unemployment areas.
  • Outsourcing — the process of permanently allocating whole departments or jobs to an external firm, often to reduce labor costs and let internal staff focus on core tasks.
  • Subcontracting — hiring an individual or company to complete a specific, usually specialized and temporary task that current in-house employees cannot complete; unlike outsourcing, it is not a permanent arrangement.
  • Offshoring — relocating production facilities from higher-cost countries to lower-cost countries to take advantage of cheaper labor, then selling the goods back in the developed country at a discount while maintaining profit.
  • Insourcing — performing work in-house that would otherwise have been contracted out, often by bringing in specialists temporarily or training existing staff to take on the work.
  • Reshoring — the opposite of offshoring; bringing back offshored manufacturing or other jobs to a company's home country, often due to rising overseas costs or delayed manufacture-to-market times.
