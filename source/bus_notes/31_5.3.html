5.3 — Lean production and quality management

Key terms

  • Lean production — an approach to manufacturing focused on eliminating waste from all processes to reduce costs and improve efficiency.
  • Waste — any activity that does not add value from the customer’s perspective; research cited in the text suggests around 60 per cent of production activities in a typical manufacturing operation are waste.
  • Inventory (waste) — storing excessive stock, which wastes both space and money.
  • Motion (waste) — wasted physical movement by workers (excessive reaching, bending, stretching) or by machines (using oversized machines for small jobs).
  • Over-processing (waste) — making too much, too soon, often resulting in excessive inventory due to miscommunication or supply chain breakdowns.
  • Overproduction (waste) — producing more than is immediately needed; also called just-in-case (JIC) production.
  • Waiting (waste) — time lost when one department must wait on another for a part, price, or process, disrupting production flow.
  • Transport (waste) — time and resources wasted by moving products or materials inefficiently.
  • Defects (waste) — time, money, and effort wasted when a batch of products fails to meet specifications.
  • Non-utilized talent (waste) — failing to make effective use of employees’ skills, efficiency, and ideas.
  • Just-in-time (JIT) production — producing exactly the amount needed exactly when customers need it, minimizing the stock held and the capital tied up in it.
  • Just-in-case (JIC) production — overproduction that creates long lead times and high storage costs; the opposite approach to JIT.
  • Kaizen (continuous improvement) — a Japanese term meaning ‘good change’ or ‘improvement’; an approach based on the idea that small, ongoing positive changes can produce significant results over time.
  • The Toyota Way — Toyota’s kaizen-based management philosophy, developed to lower defects, eliminate waste, boost productivity, and encourage worker purpose and accountability.
  • Cradle to cradle (C2C) design and manufacturing — a sustainable design approach in which products are designed to be reused as materials for new products or to harmlessly biodegrade.
  • Circular economy — an economic model based on the continual, safe circulation of materials and nutrients, aiming to eliminate the concept of waste.
  • Biological cycle — in C2C design, the cycle followed by consumption products (for example, natural fibers and biodegradable packaging) that safely biodegrade and become ‘nutrients’ for new products.
  • Technical cycle — in C2C design, the cycle followed by service products (for example, electronics) whose components are disassembled, sorted, and reintroduced into production after use.
  • Quality — the degree to which a product or service meets the specific requirements of customers.
  • Quality assurance — a preventive, process-oriented approach that aims to prevent defects and mistakes before they occur, and is the responsibility of everyone involved in developing the product or service.
  • Quality control — a corrective, product-oriented technique that identifies and fixes defects after they occur, typically the responsibility of a dedicated testing team.
  • Statistical Quality Control (SQC) — statistical tools and techniques applied to end products, forming part of quality assurance.
  • Statistical Process Control (SPC) — statistical tools and techniques applied to processes, forming part of quality control.
  • Quality circle — a voluntary group of around eight to ten employees from a specific work area who meet regularly to solve problems and improve quality standards.
  • Benchmarking — measuring an organization’s products, services, and processes against those of organizations known to be leaders, in order to identify and close performance gaps.
  • Benchmark gap — the difference between an organization’s current performance and that of the market leader (industry best practice).
  • Total quality management (TQM) — a management framework based on the principle that an organization builds long-term success by having all members, from workers to senior executives, focus on continuously improving quality.
  • International Organization for Standardization (ISO) — an independent, non-governmental international organization that develops and publishes quality, product, and service standards to guide, coordinate, and standardize practice across countries.
  • ISO 9000 — a quality management standard whose goal is to embed a quality management system within an organization, increasing productivity, reducing unnecessary costs, and ensuring the quality of processes and products.

Key theories & models

  • Eight wastes of lean production — a framework identifying the eight forms of waste an organization should minimize: inventory, motion, over-processing, overproduction, waiting, transport, defects, and non-utilized talent.
  • Ten principles of kaizen — a set of guiding principles for continuous improvement, including letting go of assumptions and perfectionism, being proactive, asking ‘why’ five times to find root causes, empowering everyone to contribute, and never stopping improving.
  • The kaizen cycle — a seven-step cycle for continuous improvement: get employees involved, find problems, create a solution, test the solution, analyze the results, adopt the solution if successful, and repeat.
  • Principles of TQM — a framework of seven principles for achieving total quality management: customer first, employee ownership, process-based, system integration, communication, data-driven decision-making, and constant improvement.
