2.1 — Introduction to human resource management

Key terms

  • Human resource management (HRM) — the process of employing and training people, compensating them, developing policies relating to them, and developing strategies to retain them.
  • Workforce planning — the process of analyzing, forecasting, and planning workforce supply and demand, assessing gaps, and determining talent management interventions so the organization has the right people with the right skills in the right places at the right time.
  • Recruitment and selection — the process of identifying a vacancy, analyzing and describing the job, attracting applicants, screening and shortlisting them, interviewing and testing candidates, and making a job offer.
  • Job analysis and evaluation — the process the HR department undertakes to formulate the job description and person specification once a vacancy has been identified.
  • Job description — a document outlining the duties and responsibilities of a role, shared with potential applicants.
  • Person specification — a profile of the skills, qualifications, and qualities required of the ideal candidate for a role, shared with potential applicants.
  • Aptitude test — a task-oriented activity used during selection to assess a candidate’s ability to perform job-relevant tasks.
  • Psychometric test — a personality questionnaire used during selection to assess a candidate’s traits and suitability for a role.
  • Induction — a program that helps new employees orientate themselves with the workplace and the expectations of their job role after they are hired.
  • Performance management — activities that establish objectives through which individuals and teams see their part in the organization’s mission, aiming to improve performance and hold employees accountable by linking it to reward, career progression, and continued employment.
  • Appraisal system — a framework, often reviewed annually, used to set targets at the start of a cycle and monitor progress against them, which can flow both ways between employees and managers.
  • Formative appraisal — documented appraisal sessions held at regular intervals during an employee’s contract to assess what is going well and how performance can improve.
  • Summative appraisal — a concluding appraisal at the end of a set period that summarizes an employee’s performance against their targets.
  • 360-degree feedback — an appraisal method that draws on feedback from colleagues and/or customers, gathered through a questionnaire.
  • Self-appraisal — an appraisal method in which employees assess their own performance against their key performance indicators.
  • Compensation and benefits management — establishing salary ranges and placing employees in the relevant salary band based on qualifications, experience, and achievements, benchmarked against the market rate, and overseeing the wider benefits package such as leave and healthcare.
  • Professional development and training — the HR function that oversees the organization and provision of training and development for employees.
  • On-the-job training — training delivered while an individual performs tasks related to their occupation, typically used to broaden skills and increase productivity.
  • Off-the-job training — training in which employees learn about their job or the latest advancements in their field at a location away from their workplace.
  • Behavioral training — training that develops interpersonal, regulatory, and task-related skills and abilities to ensure good performance in professional settings.
  • Cognitive training — training that aims to improve the skills the brain uses to think, read, learn, remember, reason, and pay attention.
  • Employee relations — the HR function of maintaining positive relationships with employees to create a pleasant work environment and increase productivity.
  • Worker health and safety — a critical HR function including creating safety guidelines, providing employee wellness programs, and conducting emergency planning.
  • Dismissal — the termination of an employee’s contract by the employer due to the employee’s actions; the HR department must ensure it is fair, or it can be challenged at a tribunal.
  • Redundancy — a type of dismissal that occurs when a job role is no longer needed.
  • Internal factors influencing HR planning — factors arising from within the business, including changes in business organization (such as mergers), business strategy, business profitability, and labor relations (such as unionization).
  • Demographic change / immigration — falling birth rates, an aging population, or rising net migration levels, which can affect the number of potential employees available in the labor pool.
  • Technological change (HR) — improvements in technology, such as video conferencing and remote working tools, that change how and where employees work and communicate.
  • Government regulations (HR) — legislation relating to health and safety, working hours, retirement age, pensions, and equality that can impact HR planning.
  • Socio-cultural trends (HR) — changes in society, such as changing family structures and gender roles or increased emphasis on employee well-being and flexible working, that impact HR planning.
  • State of the economy (HR) — fluctuations in the business cycle that affect labor supply; recession raises unemployment and lowers wage pressure, while growth increases demand for labor and pushes wages up.
  • Labor mobility — the ease with which people can change jobs (occupational mobility) or locations (geographic mobility), which affects the labor pool available to a country.
  • Emerging industries — industries with above-average growth that must hire staff rapidly, such as artificial intelligence, virtual reality, renewable energy, big data analytics, cyber security, and cloud computing.
  • Gig economy — an economy involving the exchange of labor for money between individuals or companies via digital platforms, on a short-term, payment-by-task basis.
  • Resistance to change — loss of job security — employees resist change that they fear will eliminate or reduce their role, especially if they were not involved in the decision.
  • Resistance to change — prior change experience — negative past experiences of change, or poorly managed change, make employees more likely to resist future change.
  • Resistance to change — poor reward systems — employees resist change if they are unclear how it benefits them or are not rewarded for supporting it.
  • Resistance to change — fear of the unknown — a lack of transparent, timely communication about a change and its impact makes employees more fearful and resistant.
  • Resistance to change — loss of control — change can disrupt familiar routines, leaving employees feeling confused and out of control.
  • Resistance to change — fear of failure — employees may doubt their ability to perform under radically changed job requirements and fear being unable to adapt.
  • Resistance to change — not understanding the need for change — employees resist when they do not understand or agree with the reason behind a change, especially if the old way of working seemed stable.
  • Resistance to change — mistrust — change instigated by a leader who has not yet built trust with employees is more likely to be met with resistance.
  • HR strategy — communicating and educating — providing clear communication and education about a change before it begins, so employees can rationalize it.
  • HR strategy — participative strategy — involving relevant stakeholders in the design of a change so employees are less likely to resist something they helped create.
  • HR strategy — support — offering employees support throughout the change process to help them develop new skills and avoid change burnout.
  • HR strategy — incentives — offering rewards such as extra pay, improved benefits, or structured career plans to encourage employees to accept change.
  • HR strategy — co-opting — bringing the employees most resistant to change into central roles in implementing it; this can win their favor but may also give them a platform to inspire wider resistance.
  • HR strategy — coercion — using the threat of disciplinary action to force compliance when speed of change is critical; it does not remove resistance and may create a destructive atmosphere later.

Key theories & models

  • Kotter’s eight-step change model — a model introduced by John P Kotter in his book Leading Change (1996), based on research into 100 organizations, describing eight steps for driving lasting change across three phases: creating a climate for change, engaging and enabling the organization, and implementing and sustaining the change.
