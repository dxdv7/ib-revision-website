1.4 — Stakeholders

Key terms

  • Stakeholder — any individual, social group, or other business that is directly impacted, financially or physically, by the operations and decisions of a business, and therefore has an interest in how it is run.
  • Internal stakeholder — a stakeholder who has direct involvement in the workings of the business, such as employees, managers, and shareholders.
  • External stakeholder — a stakeholder who is not employed by the business and has no direct involvement in it, but still has an interest in its operations, such as customers, creditors, government, competitors, the local community, and suppliers.
  • Employees — individuals hired to carry out the business's operations; their key interests are job security, a good salary, opportunities for growth, and better working conditions.
  • Managers — executive management and the board of directors; their key interests are increased financial performance, meeting strategic and tactical goals, satisfying shareholders, and smooth day-to-day functioning.
  • Shareholders — individuals or organizations that invest capital in a business by buying shares; their key interests are growth of profits, a clear long-term vision, efficient workflow, and liquidity.
  • Capital gain — the profit a shareholder makes when the value of a share rises above its purchase price and is sold.
  • Dividends — the portion of a company's profits distributed to shareholders in proportion to their shareholding.
  • Customers — buyers of a business's products; their key interests are the quality, quantity, distribution, and pricing of goods and services.
  • Creditors — external stakeholders, such as banks or financing companies, who provide capital to a business in the form of loans; their key interest is repayment of the money loaned plus any interest due.
  • Government — a stakeholder interested in a business's tax contributions, compliance with regulation and legislation, ethical practices, and wider impact on society.
  • Competitors — other businesses affected by a firm's decisions, who monitor it in order to adjust and improve their own strategies.
  • Local community — residents and groups interested in a business's environmental impact and the job opportunities it creates, positively or negatively affected by its activities.
  • Suppliers — businesses that provide materials to a firm; their key interests are profits and increased demand for their own goods, which depend on the performance and purchasing practices of the business they supply.
  • Stakeholder conflict — a situation in which the goals of different stakeholder groups do not align, for example shareholders wanting higher profits through increased production versus the local community wanting reduced environmental harm; such conflict can affect business performance and public image.
