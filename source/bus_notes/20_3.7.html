3.7 — Cash flow

Key terms

  • Cash flow — the movement of money (inflows and outflows) into and out of a business, as opposed to profit; think of it as money moving through the business's bank account or "wallet".
  • Profit versus cash flow — profit (revenue minus expenses) is recorded using the accrual method on the date it is earned or incurred, while cash flow only changes when cash is actually received or paid; the two differ whenever sales or purchases are made on credit.
  • Net cash flow — total cash inflows minus total cash outflows for a given period.
  • Working capital — total current assets minus total current liabilities; a measure of liquidity showing whether a business can meet its daily obligations such as wages and raw materials.
  • Insolvency — the state of being unable to meet short-term debts, which can result from having insufficient working capital even if the business is profitable.
  • Cash flow forecast — a projection of future cash inflows and outflows (often monthly) for a business, showing the opening balance, cash inflows, cash outflows, net cash flow, and closing balance.
  • Cash flow statement — a historical financial statement showing actual past cash flows, published alongside the profit and loss account and balance sheet; unlike a forecast, it is not a projection.
  • Opening balance — the amount of cash a business holds at the start of a time period; equal to the closing balance of the previous period.
  • Cash inflow — money received by a business during a period, such as cash sales revenue or a tax refund.
  • Cash outflow — money paid out by a business during a period, such as rent, wages, cost of sales, or delivery costs.
  • Closing balance — opening balance plus net cash flow for the period; becomes the opening balance of the following period. A negative closing balance signals a cash shortfall.
  • Debt factoring — a financial service in which a third party buys a business's debts for a percentage of their total value, giving the business an immediate cash advance while the third party collects the full amount owed from debtors.
  • Debtor days / creditor days — the average time taken to collect payment from customers (debtors) or to pay suppliers (creditors); reducing debtor days and/or increasing creditor days improves cash flow but can strain stakeholder relationships.
  • Just-in-time (JIT) — a stock control technique that minimizes stock held in storage, reducing cash tied up in inventory, though it can leave a business unable to react to a sudden surge in demand.
  • Overdraft — a short-term financing facility that helps a business cover cash flow problems, such as those caused by seasonal sales, although it can carry high interest rates.

Key theories & models

  • Investment–profit–cash flow cycle — a conceptual model describing how an initial investment increases sales and eventually profit, while cash flow improves later (lagging behind profit) once payments are received and working capital needs fall; the resulting cash can then fund further investment, restarting the cycle.
