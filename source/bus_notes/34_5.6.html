5.6 — Production planning

Key terms

  • Production planning — the process of deciding how a product or service will be manufactured before actual production commences.
  • Supply chain — the network of all individuals, organizations, resources, activities, and technology involved in creating and selling a product, from raw material delivery to final delivery to the consumer.
  • Local sourcing — sourcing products or materials from suppliers within the home country; often more expensive but easier and faster, useful when a quick turnaround is needed.
  • Global sourcing — sourcing raw materials, components, goods, and services from suppliers across geopolitical boundaries; can save money by finding cost-effective production locations.
  • Regenerative supply chain — a supply chain that first accounts for its negative impacts, such as carbon, waste, and resource use, then seeks to restore, replenish, and regenerate more resources than it requires, benefiting people and the planet.
  • Just-in-time (JIT) — a stock control approach in which a business orders stock to mirror actual customer demand, minimizing waste and storage costs, but requiring an issue-free supply chain and accurate demand forecasting.
  • Just-in-case (JIC) — a more traditional stock control approach in which a business holds a buffer or safety stock so it can respond quickly to unexpected demand surges or supply chain disruption.
  • Stock control chart — a visual representation of a firm’s inventory levels, used to monitor and analyze the rate stock is used, when it needs replenishing, and delivery expectations.
  • Maximum stock level — the maximum amount of stock a business wants to hold at any point, usually based on storage capacity.
  • Minimum stock level — the minimum amount of stock a business aims to always maintain.
  • Buffer stock — stock kept available to use in case of an unexpected increase in demand or a supply shock causing late deliveries.
  • Reorder level — the level of inventory that, once reached, triggers a new stock order.
  • Reorder quantity — the amount of new stock ordered for production once the reorder level is reached.
  • Lead time — the time between placing a stock order and receiving the delivery at the place of business.
  • Usage rate — the speed at which stock is used up in the production process.
  • Capacity utilization rate — (actual output ÷ potential output) × 100; measures the extent to which a business is realizing its potential output capacity, with 90 per cent considered a sensible target.
  • Excess (spare) capacity — when a firm’s output is below its maximum possible output, representing a waste of resources as it still pays for unused fixed assets.
  • Capacity shortage — when a firm’s capacity is not large enough to meet demand for its products, which can lead to disappointed customers, delays, and lost sales to competitors.
  • Defect rate — (number of defective units ÷ output tested) × 100; measures the proportion of production that is defective, helping a business assess financial losses and product quality.
  • Productivity — a measure of how well an organization converts inputs, such as labor, materials, machinery, and capital, into outputs of goods and services.
  • Labor productivity — output per period (units) ÷ number of employees at work; measures how much output is produced per unit of labor input.
  • Capital productivity — total output ÷ capital input; a higher figure shows physical capital is being used efficiently, a lower figure shows the opposite.
  • Productivity rate — total output ÷ total input; measures the amount of output generated from each unit of input.
  • Operating leverage — [quantity × (price − variable cost per unit)] ÷ [quantity × (price − variable cost per unit) − fixed costs]; measures a company’s fixed costs as a proportion of total costs and is used to evaluate the break-even point and profit sensitivity.
  • High operating leverage — a situation where a large proportion of costs are fixed, so a firm earns a large profit on each sale once fixed costs are covered, but earnings are more sensitive to changes in sales volume (for example, a software company).
  • Low operating leverage — a situation where a large proportion of costs are variable, so a firm earns a smaller profit per sale but needs less sales volume to cover its lower fixed costs (for example, a supermarket).
  • Cost to buy (CTB) — price × quantity; the total cost of purchasing a product or component from an outside supplier rather than making it.
  • Cost to make (CTM) — fixed costs + (average variable cost per unit × quantity); the total cost of manufacturing a product or component in-house rather than buying it.
