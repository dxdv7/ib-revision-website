2.7 — Industrial/employee relations

Key terms

  • Resistance to change — a source of workplace conflict where employees show fear of the unknown and resist new management or working practices, causing confusion and denial.
  • Poor work habits — a source of workplace conflict arising from individual habits harmful to workplace harmony, such as arriving late or creating a messy environment.
  • Unclear job expectations — a source of workplace conflict where employees are not made aware of their roles and responsibilities, making it difficult to understand how to do their job effectively.
  • Poor communication — a source of workplace conflict where employees do not have access to important information they need.
  • Personality differences — a source of workplace conflict arising when individual differences in background, culture, and temperament become so great that mutual respect between co-workers breaks down.
  • Toxic work culture, abusive conduct, and bullying — a source of workplace conflict leading to unhappiness and negativity, which can have serious repercussions if left unchecked.
  • Misunderstandings related to policies and practices — a source of workplace conflict where policies or practices are too numerous or complex for employees to remember and follow correctly.
  • Collective bargaining — the official process by which trade union representatives negotiate with employers on behalf of members over terms and conditions of employment, such as pay and working conditions.
  • Work-to-rule — a form of employee protest in which workers do exactly what is stated in their contracts and nothing more, slowing productivity and signalling the end of goodwill.
  • Goodwill (employment) — the gap between the minimum an employee needs to do to keep their job and the maximum they will do if genuinely enthusiastic about the work.
  • Strike action — a work stoppage caused by the mass refusal of employees to work, usually a last resort during collective bargaining, which may include picketing to dissuade others from working.
  • Redundancy — a form of dismissal that happens when an employer no longer needs a particular job role, potentially entitling the employee to redundancy pay.
  • Changes of contract — an employer response to conflict involving altering the terms and conditions of employee contracts, which normally requires employee consent and consultation under employment law.
  • Lockout — a work stoppage or denial of employment through a temporary closure of the workplace, initiated by management during a labor dispute to hinder union organization or gain leverage.
  • Closure (business) — the permanent shutting down of a business itself, rather than a temporary stoppage, seen as the most severe employer reaction to workplace conflict.
  • Conciliation — a conflict resolution process using an independent mediator to help both parties reach a mutually beneficial, non-legally-binding agreement, often requiring compromise.
  • Arbitration — a conflict resolution process, used if conciliation fails, in which an independent arbitrator reviews both parties' demands and makes a legally binding decision.
  • Employee participation — giving workers responsibilities and autonomy to do their jobs, providing a sense of ownership and accountability that helps improve staff morale.
  • Industrial democracy — an arrangement in which workers make decisions and share responsibility and authority in the workplace, treated as responsible partners in the enterprise.
  • No-strike agreement — a provision in a collective bargaining contract in which a union promises employees will not strike, slow down, or take other job action during the contract, usually in exchange for employer concessions.
  • Single-union agreement — an arrangement in which a company agrees to discuss pay, working conditions, and other matters with only one main trade union, increasing clarity and efficiency in negotiations.

Key theories & models
