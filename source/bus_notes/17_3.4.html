3.4 — Final accounts

Key terms

  • Final accounts — the financial statements produced from a business’s accounting records; publicly held companies must publish theirs, while sole traders and partnerships are not required to prepare them.
  • Statement of profit or loss (profit and loss account / income statement) — a financial statement showing an organization’s income and expenses over a defined period, such as a year.
  • Statement of financial position (balance sheet) — a financial statement showing an organization’s assets, liabilities, and equity at one specific point in time.
  • Sales revenue — the total income generated from the sale of goods or services associated with a business’s main operations.
  • Cost of sales (cost of goods sold) — the money spent on raw materials, manufacturing, and/or acquiring the goods a business sells.
  • Gross profit — sales revenue minus cost of sales.
  • Expenses (overheads) — indirect costs subtracted from gross profit to calculate profit before interest and tax.
  • Profit before interest and tax — gross profit minus expenses; used to calculate net profit margin and return on capital employed.
  • Profit before tax — profit before interest and tax minus interest expense.
  • Profit for the period (net profit) — profit before tax minus tax; allocated as dividends or retained profit.
  • Dividends — payments made to shareholders out of the profit for the period.
  • Retained profit — profit kept in the business, rather than paid out as dividends, to fund future growth; carried over to the balance sheet as retained earnings.
  • Surplus and deficit — the terms non-profit entities use instead of profit and loss, since they have no owners or shareholders to pay dividends to.
  • Retained surplus — for a non-profit entity, the surplus for the period reinvested back into the organization.
  • Non-current assets (fixed assets) — assets that will be used by the business for more than a year, such as buildings, land, and machines.
  • Accumulated depreciation — the total depreciation expense deducted from a non-current asset’s original cost to reflect its current value.
  • Current assets — assets that will be used up or converted into cash within a year, such as cash, debtors, and stock.
  • Debtors (accounts receivable) — money owed to the business by customers who purchased goods on trade credit.
  • Stock (inventory) — the value of raw materials, unfinished goods, and finished goods held by the business.
  • Total assets — non-current assets plus current assets.
  • Current liabilities — debts or payables due within one year, such as bank overdrafts, trade creditors, and other short-term loans.
  • Trade creditors — money owed to suppliers for purchases made on trade credit.
  • Non-current liabilities (long-term liabilities) — debts or payables not due until after one year, such as long-term bank loans.
  • Total liabilities — non-current liabilities plus current liabilities.
  • Net assets — total assets minus total liabilities.
  • Equity — the value of the business belonging to its shareholders; calculated as total assets minus total liabilities and must equal net assets.
  • Share capital — funds raised by a company through the sale of shares.
  • Retained earnings — the accumulated retained profits from all previous profit and loss accounts, shown on the balance sheet.
  • Intangible assets — long-term assets that lack physical substance but still add value to the business, usually included within non-current assets.
  • Patent — a government-issued license giving an individual or business sole rights to make or sell a new invention or product.
  • Copyright — legal protection typically used for art and literature, such as books, plays, movies, or music.
  • Trademark — legal protection for a company’s name, short slogan, or logo.
  • Brand value — an estimate of how much a brand is worth in terms of income, potential income, reputation, prestige, and market value.
  • Goodwill — the difference between the price paid to acquire a business and the value of its net assets, recorded as an intangible asset.
  • Depreciation — the fall in value of a non-current asset over time due to wear and tear, recorded as an expense in the profit and loss account and accumulated on the balance sheet.
  • Residual value — the estimated value of an asset at the end of its useful life.
  • Book value — the original cost of an asset minus its accumulated depreciation.

Key theories & models

  • Straight line depreciation method — divides the total depreciation (original cost minus residual value) equally over the asset’s expected useful life, giving a constant annual depreciation expense; simple to calculate, but may not reflect fluctuating asset use.
  • Units of production depreciation method — calculates depreciation based on actual usage: (original cost minus residual value) divided by the estimated total units the asset can produce, multiplied by the actual units produced each year; more accurate when use fluctuates, but harder to track and not always accepted for tax purposes.
