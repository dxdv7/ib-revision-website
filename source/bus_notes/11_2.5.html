2.5 — Organizational (corporate) culture

Key terms

  • Organizational (corporate) culture — the shared beliefs, meanings, and values collectively held by the members of an organization.
  • Influences on organizational culture — factors shaping a firm's culture, including its size, its traditions and values, the attitudes of senior managers, and the societal and cultural norms of the country or region it operates in.
  • Cultural clash — conflict that arises when groups or organizations with different cultures interact, often triggered by growth, external factors, or a change of leadership.
  • Cultural clash from mergers and acquisitions (M&As) — M&As are usually driven by revenue gain and rarely account for cultural differences between the merging firms, increasing the risk of a clash.
  • Cultural clash from multinational expansion — expanding across borders through mergers, acquisitions, joint ventures, or subsidiaries does not automatically create a standardized culture, since hierarchies and ways of motivating staff vary between countries.
  • Emotional quotient (EQ) — a measure of a person's ability to understand and manage emotions; managers need a mix of EQ and IQ to recognize and shape organizational culture effectively.

Key theories & models

  • Handy's Gods of Management — Charles Handy's model classifying organizational culture into four types, each represented by a Greek god: Power (Zeus), Role (Apollo), Task (Athena), and Person (Dionysus).
  • Power culture (Zeus) — decision-making centers on an individual or small group of senior staff (often the founder); few rules and fast decisions, but can turn toxic without a strong moral compass at the top.
  • Role culture (Apollo) — underpinned by clear policies and hierarchical structures with clearly defined employee roles; common in large, bureaucratic organizations, offering stability but slow to react to change.
  • Task culture (Athena) — teams are formed around specific problems or projects, with power based on individual expertise; effective in dynamic, creative businesses such as matrix organizations, but can be costly to maintain.
  • Person culture (Dionysus) — power is held by individuals who view their own skills as more important than the organization itself, such as barristers; requires generous rewards to recruit and retain staff.
  • Quinn and Cameron's Competing Values Framework — a model exploring organizational effectiveness along two polarities, internal versus external focus and flexibility versus stability, producing four culture types: Clan, Adhocracy, Market, and Hierarchy.
  • Clan culture (collaborate culture) — a people-focused, family-like culture emphasizing mentorship, teamwork, and horizontal leadership; common in start-ups but hard to maintain as a company grows.
  • Adhocracy culture (create culture) — a culture rooted in innovation, risk-taking, and being first to market, seen in companies like Google and Apple; can drive high profits but carries the risk of failed ventures.
  • Market culture (compete culture) — a results-oriented culture focused on profitability, competition, and meeting targets; can deliver strong results but risks employee burnout.
  • Hierarchy culture (control culture) — a traditional, stable culture with a clear chain of command and set procedures; offers clear direction but can be slow to adapt to change.
  • Organizational culture iceberg model — a model illustrating that organizations have both visible (surface) and invisible (hidden) cultural elements; overlooking the invisible elements during a merger or acquisition increases the risk of a cultural clash.
