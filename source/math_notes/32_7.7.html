Standardizing: the z-score

For \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the z-score is \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\), where \(\mu\) is the mean, \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation, and \(x\) is a raw value of \(X\). Once standardized, \(P(X < x) = P(Z < z)\), using the standard normal distribution \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\).
Worked exampleA factory fills cereal boxes with mass \(X \sim N(750, 8^2)\) (mean 750 g, standard deviation 8 g). Find the probability that a randomly selected box has a mass less than 738 g.
\[z = \frac{738-750}{8} = -1.5\]\[\boxed{P(X < 738) = P(Z < -1.5) = 1 - P(Z < 1.5) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668 \ (4 \text{ sf})}\]

Finding probabilities between two values

For \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\), \(P(a < X < b) = P(X < b) - P(X < a)\). This is calculated directly with the normal cumulative distribution function on a GDC, or by standardizing both \(a\) and \(b\) to z-values and using the standard normal distribution.
Worked exampleThe heights of a species of plant are normally distributed, \(H \sim N(40, 5^2)\) (mean 40 cm, standard deviation 5 cm). Find \(P(36 < H < 48)\).
\[z_1 = \frac{36-40}{5} = -0.8, \quad z_2 = \frac{48-40}{5} = 1.6\]\[\boxed{P(36 < H < 48) = P(Z < 1.6) - P(Z < -0.8) = 0.9452 - 0.2119 = 0.733 \ (3 \text{ sf})}\]

Complementary events and symmetry

\(P(X > a) = 1 - P(X < a)\). By the symmetry of the normal curve about its mean \(x = \mu\), \(P(X < \mu - k) = P(X > \mu + k)\) for any \(k > 0\).
Worked exampleThe mass of a packet of biscuits is \(W \sim N(250, 4^2)\) (mean 250 g, standard deviation 4 g). Find \(P(W > 256)\), and hence state \(P(W < 244)\).
\[z = \frac{256-250}{4} = 1.5\]\[P(W > 256) = 1 - P(Z < 1.5) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668 \ (4 \text{ sf})\]Since 244 is 6 g below the mean and 256 is 6 g above the mean, by symmetry:
\[\boxed{P(W < 244) = P(W > 256) = 0.0668}\]

The 68-95-99.7 empirical rule

For \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)\): \(P(\mu - \sigma < X < \mu + \sigma) \approx 0.68\), \(P(\mu - 2\sigma < X < \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 0.95\), \(P(\mu - 3\sigma < X < \mu + 3\sigma) \approx 0.997\).
Worked exampleBattery lifetimes are normally distributed with mean 500 hours and standard deviation 20 hours. Estimate the percentage of batteries lasting between 460 and 540 hours, and predict how many of a batch of 2000 batteries will last in this range.
\[460 = 500 - 2(20), \quad 540 = 500 + 2(20)\]so this is the interval within 2 standard deviations of the mean.
\[P(460 < X < 540) \approx 0.95\]so approximately 95% of batteries last between 460 and 540 hours.
\[\boxed{\text{Expected number} = 2000 \times 0.95 = 1900 \text{ batteries}}\]

Inverse normal: finding a boundary value

If \(P(X < a) = p\) is known, then \(a = \mu + \sigma \times z\), where \(z\) is the z-value satisfying \(P(Z < z) = p\). This value of \(z\) is found using the inverse normal function on a GDC.
Worked exampleThe weights of apples from an orchard are normally distributed, \(X \sim N(180, 15^2)\) (mean 180 g, standard deviation 15 g). The heaviest 10% of apples are classified as "premium". Find the minimum weight for an apple to be classified as premium.
\[P(X > a) = 0.10 \implies P(X < a) = 0.90\]\[\text{Inverse normal: } z = 1.2816 \ (P(Z < z) = 0.90)\]\[\boxed{a = 180 + 15 \times 1.2816 = 199 \text{ g} \ (3 \text{ sf})}\]

Expected frequency in a sample

Expected number of occurrences in a sample \(= n \times P(\text{event})\), where \(n\) is the sample size and \(P(\text{event})\) is the probability of the event for a single trial.
Worked exampleCommute times are normally distributed, \(T \sim N(32, 6^2)\) (mean 32 minutes, standard deviation 6 minutes). Find the probability that a commute takes more than 38 minutes, and predict how many of 250 working days will have a commute longer than 38 minutes.
\[z = \frac{38-32}{6} = 1\]\[P(T > 38) = 1 - P(Z < 1) = 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587\]\[\boxed{\text{Expected number} = 250 \times 0.1587 = 39.7 \approx 40 \text{ days}}\]

Conditional probability with the normal distribution

\(P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\). For a normal variable \(X\) and thresholds \(a < b\), the event \(\{X > b\}\) is a subset of \(\{X > a\}\), so \(P(X > b \mid X > a) = \dfrac{P(X > b)}{P(X > a)}\).
Worked exampleThe lengths of fish in a lake are normally distributed, \(L \sim N(30, 5^2)\) (mean 30 cm, standard deviation 5 cm). Find \(P(L > 38 \mid L > 33)\).
\[P(L > 38): \ z = \frac{38-30}{5} = 1.6 \implies P(L > 38) = 1 - 0.9452 = 0.0548\]\[P(L > 33): \ z = \frac{33-30}{5} = 0.6 \implies P(L > 33) = 1 - 0.7257 = 0.2743\]\[\boxed{P(L > 38 \mid L > 33) = \frac{0.0548}{0.2743} = 0.200 \ (3 \text{ sf})}\]
