Building a linear model from two data points

A linear model has the form f(x) = mx + c, where m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept. Given two data points (x1, y1) and (x2, y2), the gradient is m = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1), and c is found by substituting one point into y = mx + c.
Worked exampleA shipping company charges $40 to ship a 5 kg package and $100 to ship a 20 kg package. Let C be the cost in dollars and w the weight in kg.
m = (100 − 40) / (20 − 5) = 60 / 15 = 4
Substitute (5, 40): 40 = 4(5) + c, so c = 40 − 20 = 20
Model: C = 4w + 20
Estimated cost to ship a 12 kg package: C = 4(12) + 20 = $68

Direct variation models

When two quantities are in direct proportion (variation), the model has the form y = mx, with no fixed constant term (the line passes through the origin), where m is the constant rate of change.
Worked exampleThe exchange rate is UK£1 = AUD1.73. Let p be the amount in UK£ and a(p) the equivalent amount in AUD.
Model: a(p) = 1.73p
Amount equivalent to UK£70: a(70) = 1.73 × 70 = AUD121.10

Using a model within its domain: interpolation and extrapolation

Interpolation means using a model to estimate a value within the domain (range of x-values) of the data used to build it; this is generally reliable. Extrapolation means estimating a value outside that domain; this can be unreliable, since the linear pattern may not continue.
Worked exampleA leaking water tank is measured at t = 2 minutes (w = 1900 litres) and t = 4 minutes (w = 1800 litres). Using these two points:
m = (1800 − 1900) / (4 − 2) = −100 / 2 = −50
Substitute (2, 1900): 1900 = −50(2) + c, so c = 2000
Model: w = −50t + 2000, with domain based on the data, roughly 2 ≤ t ≤ 9
Interpolation at t = 5 (within the domain): w = −50(5) + 2000 = 1750 litres — a reliable estimate.
Extrapolation at t = 45 (far outside the domain): w = −50(45) + 2000 = −250 litres — a negative volume is impossible, showing that extrapolating far beyond the data is unreliable.
