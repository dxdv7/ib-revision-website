The addition rule (general)

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B), where P(A and B) is subtracted so outcomes belonging to both events are not counted twice.
Worked exampleP(A) = 0.55, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.15. Find P(A or B).
P(A or B) = 0.55 + 0.4 − 0.15 = 0.8

The addition rule for mutually exclusive events

If A and B are mutually exclusive (they cannot both occur), then P(A and B) = 0, so P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
Worked exampleA fair six-sided die is rolled. A = "throw a 2", B = "throw a 5". A and B are mutually exclusive. Find P(A or B).
P(A) = 1/6, P(B) = 1/6
P(A or B) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6 = 0.333 (3 sf)

The multiplication rule for independent events

If A and B are independent (the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other), then P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
Worked exampleAt a cafe, P(a customer buys coffee) = 0.6 and, independently, P(the same customer buys a pastry) = 0.45. Find P(buys coffee and buys a pastry).
P(A and B) = 0.6 × 0.45 = 0.27

General multiplication rule (rearranging conditional probability)

P(A and B) = P(A|B) × P(B), where P(A|B) is the probability of A given that B has occurred. This holds for any two events, not only independent ones.
Worked exampleP(A|B) = 0.8 and P(B) = 0.25. Find P(A and B).
P(A and B) = 0.8 × 0.25 = 0.2

Conditional probability

P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B), the probability that A occurs given that B has already occurred (B becomes the new, reduced sample space).
Worked exampleP(A and B) = 0.18 and P(B) = 0.4. Find P(A|B).
P(A|B) = 0.18 / 0.4 = 0.45

Testing for independence

A and B are independent if and only if P(A|B) = P(A), or equivalently P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). If neither equality holds, A and B are dependent.
Worked exampleP(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.5, P(A and B) = 0.2. Determine whether A and B are independent.
P(A) × P(B) = 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15
Since 0.15 ≠ 0.2 = P(A and B), A and B are not independent (they are dependent).
