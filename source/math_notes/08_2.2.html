Area of a quadrilateral by splitting into two triangles

To find the area of a quadrilateral ABCD, draw a diagonal (for example AC) to split it into two triangles. Find the area of each triangle using
\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{side}) \times (\text{side}) \times \sin(\text{included angle})\]then add the two areas together:
\[\text{Area of ABCD} = \text{Area of triangle ABC} + \text{Area of triangle ACD}\]
Worked exampleQuadrilateral ABCD has diagonal \(AC = 20\) m. In triangle ABC, \(AB = 15\) m and angle \(BAC = 40^{\circ}\). In triangle ACD, \(AD = 17\) m and angle \(CAD = 32^{\circ}\). Find the total area of ABCD.
\[\text{Area of ABC} = \frac{1}{2} \times 15 \times 20 \times \sin 40^{\circ} = 150 \times 0.6428 = 96.4 \text{ m}^2\]\[\text{Area of ACD} = \frac{1}{2} \times 17 \times 20 \times \sin 32^{\circ} = 170 \times 0.5299 = 90.1 \text{ m}^2\]\[\text{Total area} = 96.4 + 90.1 = 186.5\]\[\boxed{\text{Total area} \approx 187 \text{ m}^2 \text{ (3 s.f.)}}\]

Area of a regular polygon using triangles from the centre

A regular polygon with \(n\) sides, inscribed in a circle of radius \(r\), can be split into \(n\) congruent isosceles triangles from the centre, each with two sides of length \(r\) and an included central angle of \(\frac{360^{\circ}}{n}\).
\[\text{Total area} = n \times \frac{1}{2} \times r \times r \times \sin\left(\frac{360^{\circ}}{n}\right)\]
Worked exampleA regular hexagon is inscribed in a circle of radius 8 cm. Find the area of the hexagon.
The hexagon splits into 6 congruent triangles, each with central angle \(\frac{360^{\circ}}{6} = 60^{\circ}\).
\[\text{Area of one triangle} = \frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 8 \times \sin 60^{\circ} = 32 \times 0.8660 = 27.7 \text{ cm}^2\]\[\text{Total area} = 6 \times 27.7 = 166\]\[\boxed{\text{Total area} \approx 166 \text{ cm}^2 \text{ (3 s.f.)}}\]

Composite area: sine rule to find a side, then the area formula

When two angles and a side of a triangle are known (AAS), first use the sine rule to find the length of a second side, then substitute both sides and the angle between them into
\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{side}) \times (\text{side}) \times \sin(\text{included angle})\]
Worked exampleAn isosceles roof truss forms triangle PQR, where \(PQ = RQ\), angle \(P = 58^{\circ}\) and \(PR = 14\) m. Find the area of triangle PQR.
Since \(PQ = RQ\), the angles opposite them are equal: angle \(R = \) angle \(P = 58^{\circ}\).
\[\text{Angle } Q = 180^{\circ} - 58^{\circ} - 58^{\circ} = 64^{\circ}\]Use the sine rule to find PQ:
\[\frac{PQ}{\sin R} = \frac{PR}{\sin Q}\]\[PQ = \frac{14 \times \sin 58^{\circ}}{\sin 64^{\circ}} = \frac{14 \times 0.8480}{0.8988} = 13.2 \text{ m}\]\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times PR \times PQ \times \sin P = 0.5 \times 14 \times 13.2 \times \sin 58^{\circ}\]\[\text{Area} = 92.4 \times 0.8480\]\[\boxed{\text{Area} \approx 78.4 \text{ m}^2 \text{ (3 s.f.)}}\]

Composite area: cosine rule to find an angle, then the area formula

When all three sides of a triangle are known (SSS), first use the cosine rule to find one angle, then substitute the two adjacent sides and that angle into
\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{side}) \times (\text{side}) \times \sin(\text{included angle})\]
Worked exampleA triangular plot has sides \(AB = 18\) m, \(AC = 9\) m and \(BC = 14\) m. Find its area.
Use the cosine rule to find angle C (between sides AC and BC):
\[\cos C = \frac{AC^2 + BC^2 - AB^2}{2 \times AC \times BC}\]\[\cos C = \frac{9^2 + 14^2 - 18^2}{2 \times 9 \times 14} = \frac{-47}{252} = -0.1865\]\[C = \cos^{-1}(-0.1865) = 100.7^{\circ}\]\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times AC \times BC \times \sin C = 0.5 \times 9 \times 14 \times \sin 100.7^{\circ}\]\[\text{Area} = 63 \times 0.9825\]\[\boxed{\text{Area} \approx 61.9 \text{ m}^2 \text{ (3 s.f.)}}\]

Composite area applied to a real-world quantity

In an applied problem, first find the triangle’s area (using the cosine or sine rule as needed, followed by the area formula), then use that area as one step in a further calculation, such as a proportion or rate.
Worked exampleA gardener has a triangular plot with \(AB = 22\) m, \(AC = 16\) m and \(BC = 27\) m. Seeds cost 0.5 kg per 150 m² of coverage. Find the mass of seeds needed to cover the whole plot.
Use the cosine rule to find angle A:
\[\cos A = \frac{AB^2 + AC^2 - BC^2}{2 \times AB \times AC}\]\[\cos A = \frac{22^2 + 16^2 - 27^2}{2 \times 22 \times 16} = \frac{11}{704} = 0.01563\]\[A = \cos^{-1}(0.01563) = 89.1^{\circ}\]\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times AB \times AC \times \sin A = 0.5 \times 22 \times 16 \times \sin 89.1^{\circ} = 176 \times 0.9999\]\[\text{Area} \approx 176 \text{ m}^2 \text{ (3 s.f.)}\]\[\boxed{\text{Mass of seeds} = \frac{176}{150} \times 0.5 = 0.587 \text{ kg (3 s.f.)}}\]
