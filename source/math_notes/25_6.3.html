Coefficient of determination, r-squared

r2 = (r)2, where r is Pearson's product moment correlation coefficient. Multiplying r2 by 100 gives the percentage of the variation in y that can be explained by the linear relationship with x (the remaining percentage is due to other factors or random variation).
Worked exampleA study of study hours (x) and exam score (y) for a group of students gives Pearson's correlation coefficient r = 0.88. Find r2 and interpret it.
r2 = 0.882 = 0.7744
0.7744 × 100 = 77.44%
Interpretation: approximately 77.4% of the variation in exam scores can be explained by the variation in study hours; the remaining 22.6% is due to other factors.

Finding r from r-squared

r = √(r2), with the sign of r taken to match the direction of the correlation (positive if y increases with x, negative if y decreases as x increases).
Worked exampleA regression analysis shows r2 = 0.49 for two variables that are negatively correlated. Find r.
r = √0.49 = 0.7
Since the correlation is negative, r = −0.7

Interpreting the gradient of the regression line

For a regression line y = mx + c, the gradient m represents the change in y for each one-unit increase in x, stated in the context and units of the two variables.
Worked exampleA survey of students found a linear correlation between weekly hours spent on social media (x) and weekly hours of sleep (y), with regression line y = 50 − 2x. Interpret the gradient.
Gradient = −2
Interpretation: for each extra hour per week spent on social media, a student's weekly sleep is predicted to decrease by 2 hours, on average.

Interpreting the y-intercept of the regression line

For a regression line y = mx + c, the y-intercept c is the predicted value of y when x = 0. This value is only meaningful in context if x = 0 lies within (or very close to) the range of the collected data; otherwise interpreting it requires extrapolation and it may not be meaningful.
Worked exampleA group of boys aged 5 to 8 years had their hand lengths measured, giving regression line y = 1.7x + 1.5, where x is age in years and y is hand length in cm. Interpret the y-intercept.
y-intercept = 1.5, the predicted hand length at age 0.
Since the data was only collected for ages 5 to 8, x = 0 lies far outside this range, so this value is an extrapolation and is not a meaningful interpretation in context.
Compare: a car's value against age, y = −250x + 9000, has y-intercept 9000. Here x = 0 (time of purchase) lies at the very start of the data range, so 9000 meaningfully represents the car's value when new.

Interpolation and extrapolation

Interpolation: using the regression line to estimate y for an x-value that lies within the range of the original data (generally reliable, since the linear trend is known to hold there). Extrapolation: using the regression line to estimate y for an x-value outside the range of the original data (generally unreliable, since the linear trend may not continue).
Worked exampleData on advertising spend x (in $1000s, ranging from 2 to 12) and sales y (in $10 000s) gives the regression line y = 5 + 2x.
Estimate y when x = 8: y = 5 + 2(8) = 5 + 16 = 21. Since x = 8 lies within the data range [2, 12], this is interpolation and the estimate is reliable.
Estimate y when x = 20: y = 5 + 2(20) = 5 + 40 = 45. Since x = 20 lies outside the data range [2, 12], this is extrapolation and the estimate is unreliable, as the linear trend may not continue beyond the observed data.
