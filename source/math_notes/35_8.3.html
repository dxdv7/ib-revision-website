Chi-squared goodness of fit test: hypotheses and expected frequencies

A goodness of fit test checks whether observed data for a single categorical variable matches a claimed distribution. \(H_0\): the data follows the claimed distribution. \(H_1\): the data does not follow the claimed distribution. The expected frequency for category \(i\) is \(E_i = n \times p_i\), where \(n\) is the total number of observations and \(p_i\) is the claimed probability of category \(i\).
Worked exampleA standard six-sided die is rolled \(n = 120\) times to test whether it is fair.
\(H_0\): the die is fair (each face has probability \(\frac{1}{6}\))
\(H_1\): the die is not fair
Under \(H_0\), expected frequency for each face:
\[\boxed{E = 120 \times \frac{1}{6} = 20}\]

Chi-squared test statistic and degrees of freedom

\(\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\), where \(O_i\) is the observed frequency and \(E_i\) is the expected frequency of category \(i\), summed over all categories. Degrees of freedom \(v = k - 1\), where \(k\) is the number of categories (not \((\text{rows} - 1)(\text{columns} - 1)\), which applies only to a two-way test for independence).
Worked exampleContinuing the die example, the observed frequencies for the six faces are 18, 15, 24, 20, 25, 18 (\(n = 120\)), with expected frequency 20 for each face.
\[\chi^2 = \frac{(18-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(15-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(24-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(20-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(25-20)^2}{20} + \frac{(18-20)^2}{20}\]\[\chi^2 = 0.2 + 1.25 + 0.8 + 0 + 1.25 + 0.2\]\[\boxed{\chi^2 = 3.7}\]Degrees of freedom:
\[\boxed{v = 6 - 1 = 5}\]

Decision rule: critical value and p-value

If \(\chi^2_{\text{calc}} > \text{critical value}\) (for the given significance level and degrees of freedom), reject \(H_0\). If \(\chi^2_{\text{calc}} \le \text{critical value}\), do not reject \(H_0\). Equivalently, reject \(H_0\) if the p-value is less than the significance level.
Worked exampleContinuing the die example, at the 5% significance level with \(v = 5\), the critical value is 11.070.
\(\chi^2_{\text{calc}} = 3.7\), and \(3.7 < 11.070\), so do not reject \(H_0\).
Conclusion: there is insufficient evidence to suggest the die is unfair.

Goodness of fit test for a claimed binomial distribution

The same test applies to any claimed distribution (uniform, binomial, normal, etc.) — only the calculation of the expected frequencies \(E_i = n \times p_i\) changes, using the probabilities \(p_i\) from the claimed distribution.
Worked exampleHagar tosses three fair coins \(n = 200\) times and records the number of heads (0, 1, 2 or 3) each time. She tests, at the 5% significance level, whether the results follow a binomial distribution \(B(3, 0.5)\).
Under \(B(3, 0.5)\): \(P(0) = 0.125\), \(P(1) = 0.375\), \(P(2) = 0.375\), \(P(3) = 0.125\)
Expected frequencies: \(E_0 = 25\), \(E_1 = 75\), \(E_2 = 75\), \(E_3 = 25\)
Observed frequencies: \(O_0 = 28\), \(O_1 = 67\), \(O_2 = 83\), \(O_3 = 22\)
\[\chi^2 = \frac{(28-25)^2}{25} + \frac{(67-75)^2}{75} + \frac{(83-75)^2}{75} + \frac{(22-25)^2}{25}\]\[\chi^2 = 0.36 + 0.8533... + 0.8533... + 0.36\]\[\boxed{\chi^2 = 2.4267 \text{ (4 dp)}}\]Degrees of freedom \[\boxed{v = 4 - 1 = 3}\], critical value at 5% is \(7.815\)
Since \(2.4267 < 7.815\), do not reject \(H_0\): the data is consistent with a \(B(3, 0.5)\) distribution
