The fundamental counting principle

If one stage of a combined event can happen in \(n_1\) ways, a second stage in \(n_2\) ways, and so on, then the total number of possible outcomes for the combined event is \(n_1 \times n_2 \times \ldots \times n_k\). Listing outcomes in a systematic order (for example, a table) keeps the sample space complete (nothing missing), concise (no repeats) and consistent (one clear order).
Worked exampleA person has 4 shirts and 3 pairs of trousers. If they also choose from 2 pairs of shoes, how many different complete outfits (shirt, trousers, shoes) are possible?
\[\text{Outfits (shirt and trousers only)} = 4 \times 3 = 12\]\[\text{Complete outfits (shirt, trousers and shoes)} = 4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24\]\[\boxed{24 \text{ possible outfits}}\]

Venn diagrams for combined and conditional probability

For two events \(A\) and \(B\) in a sample space \(U\), the Venn diagram splits \(U\) into four mutually exclusive regions: \(P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B') + P(A' \cap B) + P(A' \cap B') = 1\). This gives a complete and consistent representation from which every simple, combined and conditional probability can be read.
Worked exampleIn a class of 15 students, 3 study art (A), 6 study biology (B), and of these, 1 student studies both art and biology. A student is chosen at random. Find the probability that the student studies neither art nor biology.
\[P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{15}\]\[P(A \text{ only}) = P(A \cap B') = \frac{3-1}{15} = \frac{2}{15}\]\[P(B \text{ only}) = P(A' \cap B) = \frac{6-1}{15} = \frac{5}{15}\]\[P(\text{neither}) = P(A' \cap B') = 1 - \left(\frac{1}{15}+\frac{2}{15}+\frac{5}{15}\right) = 1 - \frac{8}{15} = \frac{7}{15}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{neither art nor biology}) = \frac{7}{15} \approx 0.467}\]

Tree diagrams and the multiplication law (with replacement)

Along any path of a tree diagram, the probability of a combined event is the product of the probabilities on each branch: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)\). When trials are independent (for example, sampling with replacement), \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\), so the same probabilities are used on every set of branches.
Worked exampleA box contains 50 red, 30 blue and 20 green dice (100 in total). A die is chosen at random, its colour noted, then it is replaced and a second die is chosen. Find the probability that both dice are the same colour.
\[P(\text{red, red}) = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\]\[P(\text{blue, blue}) = 0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09\]\[P(\text{green, green}) = 0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.04\]These three outcomes are mutually exclusive, so add them:
\[\boxed{P(\text{same colour}) = 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04 = 0.38}\]

Tree diagrams without replacement

When sampling without replacement, the sample space shrinks by 1 after each draw, so the probabilities on the second set of branches are conditional on the first outcome: for \(n\) items of one colour out of a total \(T\), \(P(\text{second same colour} \mid \text{first that colour}) = \dfrac{n-1}{T-1}\).
Worked exampleThe same box of 50 red, 30 blue and 20 green dice (100 in total) is used, but the first die chosen is not replaced before the second is chosen. Find the probability that both dice are the same colour.
\[P(\text{red, red}) = \frac{50}{100} \times \frac{49}{99} = \frac{2450}{9900} \approx 0.2475\]\[P(\text{blue, blue}) = \frac{30}{100} \times \frac{29}{99} = \frac{870}{9900} \approx 0.0879\]\[P(\text{green, green}) = \frac{20}{100} \times \frac{19}{99} = \frac{380}{9900} \approx 0.0384\]\[\boxed{P(\text{same colour}) = 0.2475 + 0.0879 + 0.0384 = \frac{37}{99} \approx 0.374 \ (3 \text{ sf})}\]This is lower than the 0.38 found with replacement.

The complementary probability law

\(P(A) = 1 - P(A')\). This is useful when the complement of an event is quicker to calculate directly than the event itself, for example when an event is described as “at least one” or “different”.
Worked exampleA box contains 5 red, 6 blue and 7 orange batteries (18 in total). Two batteries are chosen at random without replacement. Find the probability that the two batteries are different colours.
\[\text{Total ways to choose 2 from 18: } \binom{18}{2} = 153\]\[\text{Ways to choose 2 of the same colour: } \binom{5}{2} + \binom{6}{2} + \binom{7}{2} = 10 + 15 + 21 = 46\]\[P(\text{same colour}) = \frac{46}{153}\]\[P(\text{different colours}) = 1 - \frac{46}{153} = \frac{107}{153}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{different colours}) \approx 0.699 \ (3 \text{ sf})}\]

Total probability and conditional probability from a weighted tree

If \(A\) and \(A'\) partition the sample space, the law of total probability gives \(P(B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A) + P(A') \times P(B \mid A')\). Conditional probability can then be found with \(P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\).
Worked exampleMark buys 73% of his t-shirts from supplier 2aT and the rest from Netshirts. 16% of his 2aT t-shirts are grey, and 80% of his Netshirts t-shirts are grey. Find the probability that a t-shirt chosen at random is grey, and find the probability it came from Netshirts given that it is grey.
\[P(\text{grey}) = P(2aT) \times P(\text{grey} \mid 2aT) + P(\text{Netshirts}) \times P(\text{grey} \mid \text{Netshirts})\]\[P(\text{grey}) = 0.73 \times 0.16 + 0.27 \times 0.80 = 0.1168 + 0.216 = 0.3328\]\[P(\text{Netshirts} \mid \text{grey}) = \frac{P(\text{Netshirts} \cap \text{grey})}{P(\text{grey})} = \frac{0.216}{0.3328}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{Netshirts} \mid \text{grey}) \approx 0.649 \ (3 \text{ sf})}\]
