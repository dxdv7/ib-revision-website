Hypotheses for the t-test

The t-test compares the means, \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\), of two data sets measuring the same quantity. The null hypothesis is always \(H_0\): \(\mu_1 = \mu_2\). For a two-tailed test, \(H_1\): \(\mu_1 \ne \mu_2\). For a one-tailed test, \(H_1\): \(\mu_1 > \mu_2\) or \(H_1\): \(\mu_1 < \mu_2\).
Worked exampleMaya wants to test whether a new fertiliser increases the mean height of tomato plants compared with the standard fertiliser. Let \(\mu_1\) be the mean height using the new fertiliser and \(\mu_2\) the mean height using the standard fertiliser. Write down the null and alternative hypotheses.
Because the claim is directional (“increases”), this is a one-tailed test.
\[\boxed{H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2}\]\[\boxed{H_1: \mu_1 > \mu_2}\]

Degrees of freedom

For a two-sample t-test (pooled), the degrees of freedom are \(v = n_1 + n_2 - 2\), where \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are the sizes of the two samples.
Worked exampleA t-test compares a sample of 14 students from Class A with a sample of 12 students from Class B. Find the degrees of freedom.
\[v = n_1 + n_2 - 2\]\[v = 14 + 12 - 2\]\[\boxed{v = 24}\]
The test has 24 degrees of freedom.

The t-test statistic

The pooled two-sample t-statistic is \(t = \dfrac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}\), where the pooled variance is \(s_p^2 = \dfrac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\). In practice this is found directly using a GDC.
Worked exampleSample A: \(n_1 = 8\), mean \(\bar{x}_1 = 72.5\), standard deviation \(s_1 = 6.2\). Sample B: \(n_2 = 10\), mean \(\bar{x}_2 = 68.1\), standard deviation \(s_2 = 7.4\). Find the t-statistic.
\[s_p^2 = \frac{(7)(6.2^2) + (9)(7.4^2)}{16}\]\[s_p^2 = \frac{7(38.44) + 9(54.76)}{16}\]\[s_p^2 = \frac{269.08 + 492.84}{16}\]\[s_p^2 = \frac{761.92}{16} = 47.62\]\[s_p = \sqrt{47.62} \approx 6.90\]\[t = \frac{72.5 - 68.1}{6.90 \times \sqrt{\frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{10}}}\]\[t = \frac{4.4}{6.90 \times 0.4743}\]\[t = \frac{4.4}{3.273}\]\[\boxed{t \approx 1.34}\]Degrees of freedom:
\[\boxed{v = 8 + 10 - 2 = 16}\]

Using the p-value to reach a conclusion

Compare the p-value given by the GDC with the significance level (commonly 1%, 5% or 10%). If \(p > \text{significance level}\), accept (do not reject) \(H_0\). If \(p \le \text{significance level}\), reject \(H_0\) in favour of \(H_1\).
Worked exampleA t-test at the 5% significance level gives a p-value of 0.0392. State the conclusion.
\(0.0392 < 0.05\), so the null hypothesis is rejected.
Conclusion: there is significant evidence that the two population means are different.
