Reading conditional probability from a Venn diagram

On a Venn diagram, the conditional probability \(P(A|B)\) can be found by treating the region for \(B\) as the new sample space, and comparing it to the overlap \(A \cap B\):
\[P(A|B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)} = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\]where \(n(\cdot)\) denotes the number of outcomes in a region.
Worked exampleIn a class of \(30\) students, \(18\) study French (\(F\)) and \(12\) study Spanish (\(S\)); \(6\) students study both. Find \(P(S|F)\).
\[n(F) = 18, \quad n(F \cap S) = 6\]\[P(S|F) = \frac{n(F \cap S)}{n(F)} = \frac{6}{18}\]\[\boxed{P(S|F) = \frac{1}{3}}\]

Constructing a tree diagram

A tree diagram organizes a sequence of events as branches, where each branch is labelled with the conditional probability of that outcome given everything that happened earlier on its path. To find the probability of a complete path, multiply the probabilities along its branches; to find the probability of an outcome that can happen via more than one path, add the probabilities of those paths:
\[P(\text{path}) = P(\text{branch}_1) \times P(\text{branch}_2) \times \cdots\]
Worked exampleA bag contains \(4\) red and \(6\) blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn at random without replacement. Using a tree diagram, find the probability that exactly one red marble is drawn.
\[P(R_1) = \frac{4}{10}, \quad P(B_1) = \frac{6}{10}\]\[P(B_2|R_1) = \frac{6}{9}, \quad P(R_2|B_1) = \frac{4}{9}\]\[P(R_1 \cap B_2) = \frac{4}{10} \times \frac{6}{9} = \frac{4}{15}\]\[P(B_1 \cap R_2) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{4}{15}\]\[P(\text{exactly one red}) = \frac{4}{15} + \frac{4}{15}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{exactly one red}) = \frac{8}{15}}\]

Finding conditional probabilities from a tree diagram

Once a tree diagram is complete, a conditional probability such as \(P(A|B)\) can be found by applying the definition of conditional probability directly to the path probabilities: identify every path consistent with \(B\), then find what fraction of their total probability also satisfies \(A\).
Worked exampleUsing the tree diagram from the previous example (drawing two marbles without replacement from \(4\) red and \(6\) blue), find the probability that the first marble was red, given that exactly one red marble was drawn.
\[P(R_1|\text{exactly one red}) = \frac{P(R_1 \cap B_2)}{P(\text{exactly one red})}\]\[P(R_1|\text{exactly one red}) = \frac{4/15}{8/15}\]\[\boxed{P(R_1|\text{exactly one red}) = \frac{1}{2}}\]

Two-way tables

A two-way (contingency) table organizes outcomes according to two categorical variables at once. Each interior cell gives a joint frequency or probability, row and column totals give marginal frequencies or probabilities, and a conditional probability is found by dividing a cell by its row or column total:
\[P(\text{column category}|\text{row category}) = \frac{\text{cell value}}{\text{row total}}\]
Worked exampleA survey of \(150\) students classifies them by gender and usual transport to school: Male: \(40\) bus, \(35\) walk (total \(75\)). Female: \(30\) bus, \(45\) walk (total \(75\)). Column totals: \(70\) bus, \(80\) walk.
Find \(P(\text{walk}|\text{female})\).
\[P(\text{walk}|\text{female}) = \frac{45}{75}\]\[\boxed{P(\text{walk}|\text{female}) = \frac{3}{5}}\]

Testing independence from a two-way table

Two categorical variables shown in a table are independent if, for every cell, the joint probability equals the product of the corresponding row and column marginal probabilities:
\[P(\text{row} \cap \text{column}) = P(\text{row}) \times P(\text{column})\]It is enough to find one cell for which this fails to show the variables are not independent.
Worked exampleUsing the table from the previous example, test whether gender and transport are independent.
\[P(\text{female}) = \frac{75}{150} = \frac{1}{2}, \quad P(\text{walk}) = \frac{80}{150} = \frac{8}{15}\]\[P(\text{female}) \times P(\text{walk}) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{8}{15} = \frac{4}{15}\]\[P(\text{female} \cap \text{walk}) = \frac{45}{150} = \frac{3}{10}\]Since \(\dfrac{4}{15} \ne \dfrac{3}{10}\):
\[\boxed{\text{gender and transport are not independent}}\]

Combining the tools to solve problems

Venn diagrams are best suited to two or three overlapping qualitative events and their unions, intersections and complements. Tree diagrams are best suited to a sequence of events, since probabilities are multiplied along branches and added across paths. Two-way tables are best suited to data already organized by two categorical variables, since probabilities can be read directly from cells and totals.
Worked exampleMachine \(A\) produces \(60\%\) of the items in a factory and Machine \(B\) produces the remaining \(40\%\). \(5\%\) of the items from \(A\) are defective and \(8\%\) of the items from \(B\) are defective. Using a tree diagram, find the probability that a randomly selected item is defective.
\[P(\text{defective}) = P(A) \times P(\text{defective}|A) + P(B) \times P(\text{defective}|B)\]\[P(\text{defective}) = (0.6 \times 0.05) + (0.4 \times 0.08)\]\[P(\text{defective}) = 0.03 + 0.032\]\[\boxed{P(\text{defective}) = 0.062}\]
