Constructing a cumulative frequency table

The cumulative frequency of a class is the total number of data items up to and including that class, found by adding each class's frequency to the running total of all previous classes. The cumulative frequency at the last class is always equal to \(\sum f\), the total frequency \(n\).
Worked exampleThe times, in minutes, taken by 20 runners were grouped into the frequency table:
\[20 \le t < 26: f=3, \quad 26 \le t < 32: f=3, \quad 32 \le t < 38: f=3\]\[38 \le t < 44: f=5, \quad 44 \le t < 50: f=3, \quad 50 \le t < 56: f=2, \quad 56 \le t < 62: f=1\]Build the cumulative frequency table.
\[20 \le t < 26: \quad \text{cf} = 3\]\[26 \le t < 32: \quad \text{cf} = 3 + 3 = 6\]\[32 \le t < 38: \quad \text{cf} = 6 + 3 = 9\]\[38 \le t < 44: \quad \text{cf} = 9 + 5 = 14\]\[44 \le t < 50: \quad \text{cf} = 14 + 3 = 17\]\[50 \le t < 56: \quad \text{cf} = 17 + 2 = 19\]\[56 \le t < 62: \quad \text{cf} = 19 + 1 = 20\]\[\boxed{\text{Final cumulative frequency} = 20 = n}\]

The cumulative frequency curve (ogive)

A cumulative frequency curve, or ogive, is drawn by plotting each point \((\text{upper class boundary}, \text{cumulative frequency})\), starting from \((\text{lower boundary of the first class}, 0)\), and joining the points with a smooth curve. Because cumulative frequency never decreases, the curve always rises (or stays level) from left to right, reaching a final height of \(n\), the total frequency.
Worked exampleFor the runner times, the ogive passes through \((20,0)\), \((26,3)\), \((32,6)\), \((38,9)\), \((44,14)\), \((50,17)\), \((56,19)\), \((62,20)\). Use the curve to estimate the median.
Reading across from a cumulative frequency of \(\frac{n}{2} = 10\) on the vertical axis to the curve, then down to the horizontal axis, gives an approximate reading of:
\[\boxed{\text{median} \approx 39 \text{ minutes}}\]This graphical estimate is confirmed algebraically using linear interpolation in the next concept.

Estimating the median by linear interpolation

The median class is the first class whose cumulative frequency is at least \(\frac{n}{2}\). Within that class, the median is estimated by linear interpolation, assuming the data is evenly spread across the class:
\[\text{median} = L + \left(\frac{\frac{n}{2} - F}{f}\right) \times h\]where \(L\) is the lower boundary of the median class, \(F\) is the cumulative frequency before the median class, \(f\) is the frequency of the median class, and \(h\) is the class width.
Worked exampleUse linear interpolation to estimate the median of the runner times (\(n = 20\)).
\[\frac{n}{2} = 10\]The cumulative frequency reaches 9 at the end of \(32 \le t < 38\) and 14 at the end of \(38 \le t < 44\), so the median class is \(38 \le t < 44\), with \(L = 38\), \(F = 9\), \(f = 5\), \(h = 6\).
\[\text{median} = 38 + \left(\frac{10 - 9}{5}\right) \times 6\]\[\text{median} = 38 + (0.2)(6)\]\[\boxed{\text{median} = 39.2 \text{ minutes}}\]

Estimating quartiles by linear interpolation

The lower and upper quartiles are estimated from a grouped frequency table in the same way as the median, but using the classes containing the \(\frac{n}{4}\)th and \(\frac{3n}{4}\)th positions:
\[Q_1 = L + \left(\frac{\frac{n}{4} - F}{f}\right) \times h, \qquad Q_3 = L + \left(\frac{\frac{3n}{4} - F}{f}\right) \times h\]where \(L\), \(F\), \(f\), and \(h\) refer to the class containing each position, as for the median.
Worked exampleEstimate \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) for the runner times (\(n = 20\)).
\[\frac{n}{4} = 5\]The cumulative frequency reaches 5 inside \(26 \le t < 32\) (cumulative frequency 3 before it, rising to 6), so \(L = 26\), \(F = 3\), \(f = 3\), \(h = 6\).
\[Q_1 = 26 + \left(\frac{5 - 3}{3}\right) \times 6\]\[Q_1 = 26 + \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)(6)\]\[Q_1 = 26 + 4\]\[\boxed{Q_1 = 30 \text{ minutes}}\]\[\frac{3n}{4} = 15\]The cumulative frequency reaches 15 inside \(44 \le t < 50\) (cumulative frequency 14 before it, rising to 17), so \(L = 44\), \(F = 14\), \(f = 3\), \(h = 6\).
\[Q_3 = 44 + \left(\frac{15 - 14}{3}\right) \times 6\]\[Q_3 = 44 + \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)(6)\]\[Q_3 = 44 + 2\]\[\boxed{Q_3 = 46 \text{ minutes}}\]

The interquartile range from grouped data

As with discrete data, the interquartile range of grouped data is the difference between the upper and lower quartiles:
\[IQR = Q_3 - Q_1\]It measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data and, unlike the range, is not affected by extreme values.
Worked exampleUsing \(Q_1 = 30\) and \(Q_3 = 46\) found above, find the interquartile range of the runner times.
\[IQR = 46 - 30\]\[\boxed{IQR = 16 \text{ minutes}}\]This means the middle 50% of runners finished within a 16-minute window.
