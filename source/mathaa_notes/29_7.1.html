Populations, samples and censuses

A population is the entire collection of individuals or items that a study is concerned with. A sample is a subset of the population that is actually examined, and is used to draw conclusions about the whole population. A census collects data from every member of the population, while a sample survey collects data from only part of it. Samples are used instead of a census because they are cheaper, faster, and often the only practical option when the population is very large.
Worked exampleA school has \(1200\) students. A researcher wants to know the average time students spend on homework each week, and surveys \(50\) randomly chosen students. Identify the population and the sample.
The population is all \(1200\) students at the school.
The sample is the \(50\) students who were surveyed.

Reliability and validity

A measurement process is reliable if repeating it under the same conditions gives consistent (the same or very similar) results. A measurement process is valid if it actually measures what it is intended to measure. Reliability and validity are independent of each other: a measurement can be reliable without being valid, valid without being perfectly reliable, both, or neither.
Worked exampleClassify each situation as reliable, valid, both, or neither.
(a) A bathroom scale is wrongly calibrated so that it always reads \(5\text{ kg}\) light, but gives the same reading every time the same person steps on it twice in a row.
This scale is reliable (consistent) but not valid (it does not give the correct weight).
(b) A classroom typing-speed test is used to estimate a student's mathematical ability.
This test may be reliable, but it is not valid, since typing speed does not measure mathematical ability.

Simple random sampling

In simple random sampling, every member of the population is numbered, usually from \(1\) to \(N\), and a sample of size \(n\) is chosen using a random number generator so that every possible sample of size \(n\) has an equal chance of being selected. This is the most basic random sampling technique, and every unit of the population has an equal probability of being chosen.
Worked exampleA company has \(400\) employees. Describe how to select a simple random sample of \(30\) employees.
Number each of the \(400\) employees from \(1\) to \(400\). Use a random number generator to produce \(30\) distinct numbers between \(1\) and \(400\). The employees corresponding to these numbers form the sample.

Systematic sampling

In systematic sampling, every \(k\)th member of the population is selected to give a sample of size \(n\) from a population of size \(N\), where the sampling interval is:
\[k = \frac{N}{n}\]If \(k\) is not a whole number, it is rounded to the nearest integer. A starting point is chosen at random between \(1\) and \(k\), and then every \(k\)th member after that is included.
Worked exampleA factory produces \(840\) items per day. A quality inspector wants a systematic sample of \(60\) items. Find the sampling interval \(k\), and describe how the sample is chosen if the random starting point is \(5\).
\[k = \frac{N}{n} = \frac{840}{60}\]\[\boxed{k = 14}\]Starting at item \(5\), every \(14\)th item is selected: items \(5, 19, 33, 47, \ldots\)

Stratified sampling

In stratified sampling, the population is divided into non-overlapping subgroups called strata, based on a shared characteristic. A simple random sample is then taken from each stratum, usually in proportion to its size, so that a stratum containing a fraction \(\frac{N_i}{N}\) of the population contributes the same fraction of the overall sample:
\[n_i = \frac{N_i}{N} \times n\]where \(N_i\) is the size of stratum \(i\), \(N\) is the population size, and \(n\) is the total sample size.
Worked exampleA school has \(320\) boys and \(480\) girls. A stratified sample of \(50\) students is required, proportional to each stratum. Find the number of boys and girls to be sampled.
\[N = 320 + 480 = 800\]\[n_{\text{boys}} = \frac{320}{800} \times 50\]\[\boxed{n_{\text{boys}} = 20}\]\[n_{\text{girls}} = \frac{480}{800} \times 50\]\[\boxed{n_{\text{girls}} = 30}\]

Convenience sampling, quota sampling and bias

Convenience sampling selects whichever members of the population are easiest to reach, such as passers-by in a particular location. Quota sampling sets a fixed number (a quota) to be interviewed from each subgroup, using a non-random method to fill each quota. Neither technique gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection, so both can introduce bias: a systematic tendency for the sample to misrepresent the population.
Worked exampleA researcher stands outside one entrance of a shopping mall between 9 a.m. and 11 a.m. and interviews the first \(100\) shoppers who pass by, to estimate the opinions of all adults in the city on a new transport policy.
This is convenience sampling, since the shoppers are selected simply because they are readily available at that place and time. The sample is likely to be biased, since it excludes people who do not visit that mall, work during the morning, or use other entrances, so it may not represent the whole adult population of the city.
