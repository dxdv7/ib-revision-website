Trigonometric ratios in a right-angled triangle

For an acute angle \(\theta\) in a right-angled triangle:
\[\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\]\[\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\]\[\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\]where "opposite" and "adjacent" refer to the sides relative to angle \(\theta\), and the hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle.
Worked exampleTriangle \(ABC\) has a right angle at \(C\), angle \(A = 40^{\circ}\) and hypotenuse \(c = 12\text{ cm}\). Find the lengths of the other two sides, correct to 3 significant figures.
\[a = c\sin A\]\[a = 12\sin 40^{\circ}\]\[\boxed{a \approx 7.71 \text{ cm}}\]\[b = c\cos A\]\[b = 12\cos 40^{\circ}\]\[\boxed{b \approx 9.19 \text{ cm}}\]

Exact trigonometric values for common angles

The sine, cosine and tangent of \(30^{\circ}\), \(45^{\circ}\) and \(60^{\circ}\) can be found exactly, without a calculator, using the side ratios of a 30-60-90 triangle or a 45-45-90 triangle:
\[\sin 30^{\circ} = \cos 60^{\circ} = \frac{1}{2} \qquad \sin 60^{\circ} = \cos 30^{\circ} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \qquad \tan 30^{\circ} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \qquad \tan 60^{\circ} = \sqrt{3}\]\[\sin 45^{\circ} = \cos 45^{\circ} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \qquad \tan 45^{\circ} = 1\]
Worked exampleA right-angled triangle has hypotenuse \(10\text{ cm}\) and one angle of \(30^{\circ}\). Find the exact lengths of the other two sides.
\[\text{opposite} = 10\sin 30^{\circ} = 10 \times \frac{1}{2}\]\[\boxed{\text{opposite} = 5 \text{ cm}}\]\[\text{adjacent} = 10\cos 30^{\circ} = 10 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\]\[\boxed{\text{adjacent} = 5\sqrt{3} \text{ cm}}\]

Finding an unknown angle using inverse trigonometric ratios

When two sides of a right-angled triangle are known, an unknown acute angle \(\theta\) can be found using the inverse sine, cosine or tangent function:
\[\theta = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\right)\]\[\theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\right)\]\[\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\right)\]The notation \(\tan^{-1}\) denotes the inverse tangent function, not the reciprocal of tangent.
Worked exampleA right-angled triangle has an opposite side of \(7\text{ cm}\) and a hypotenuse of \(11\text{ cm}\). Find the angle \(\theta\) opposite the side of length \(7\text{ cm}\), correct to 3 significant figures.
\[\theta = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{7}{11}\right)\]\[\boxed{\theta \approx 39.5^{\circ}}\]

Angles of elevation and depression

The angle of elevation of a point \(P\) above an observer at \(O\) is the acute angle between the horizontal through \(O\) and the line of sight \(OP\). The angle of depression of a point \(P\) below an observer at \(O\) is the acute angle between the horizontal through \(O\) and the line of sight \(OP\). The angle of elevation from \(O\) to \(P\) is always equal to the angle of depression from \(P\) to \(O\), since the two horizontal lines are parallel.
Worked exampleA flagpole casts a shadow \(15\text{ m}\) long on horizontal ground when the angle of elevation of the sun is \(38^{\circ}\). Find the height of the flagpole, correct to 3 significant figures.
\[\tan 38^{\circ} = \frac{h}{15}\]\[h = 15\tan 38^{\circ}\]\[\boxed{h \approx 11.7 \text{ m}}\]

The angle between two lines meeting at a common point

If two line segments meet at a common point and each makes a known angle with the same horizontal (or other reference) line, the angle between the two segments is found by subtracting the smaller angle from the larger one. Extracting the two individual right-angled triangles, each containing the reference line, allows each angle to be found separately using right-angled trigonometry before subtracting.
Worked exampleTwo straight support wires are attached to the top of a vertical pole of height \(12\text{ m}\). One wire is anchored on level ground \(5\text{ m}\) from the base of the pole, and the other is anchored \(9\text{ m}\) from the base, on the same side and in line with the first. Find the angle between the two wires, correct to 3 significant figures.
\[\theta_1 = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{12}{5}\right) \approx 67.4^{\circ}\]\[\theta_2 = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{12}{9}\right) \approx 53.1^{\circ}\]\[\theta = \theta_1 - \theta_2\]\[\theta \approx 67.4^{\circ} - 53.1^{\circ}\]\[\boxed{\theta \approx 14.3^{\circ}}\]

The angle between a line and a plane in a 3D solid

To find the angle between a line (such as an edge of a solid) and a plane (such as a face or base), first find the point where the line meets the plane, then identify the projection of the line onto the plane. The required angle is the angle between the original line and its projection, which can be extracted as a right-angled triangle: the line is the hypotenuse, the projection is one leg, and the perpendicular height is the other leg.
Worked exampleA right pyramid has a square base of side \(6\text{ cm}\) and height \(8\text{ cm}\), with apex \(A\) directly above the centre \(M\) of the base. Find the angle between a lateral edge (from \(A\) to a base vertex \(G\)) and the base, correct to 3 significant figures.
The distance from the centre \(M\) to a vertex \(G\) (half of the base diagonal) is:
\[MG = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{6^{2}+6^{2}} = 3\sqrt{2} \text{ cm}\]Triangle \(AMG\) is right-angled at \(M\), so the required angle is \(AGM\):
\[\tan(AGM) = \frac{AM}{MG} = \frac{8}{3\sqrt{2}}\]\[AGM = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{8}{3\sqrt{2}}\right)\]\[\boxed{AGM \approx 62.1^{\circ}}\]
