Statement of the chain rule

If \(y\) is a composite function such that \(y = f(u)\) where \(u = g(x)\), then \(y\) can be differentiated with respect to \(x\) using the chain rule:
\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}\]Equivalently, in function notation, if \(y = f(g(x))\):
\[\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\]
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y = (3x + 1)^5\) using the chain rule, letting \(u = 3x + 1\).
\[u = 3x + 1 \implies \frac{du}{dx} = 3\]\[y = u^5 \implies \frac{dy}{du} = 5u^4\]\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx} = 5u^4 \cdot 3\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = 15(3x + 1)^4}\]

Identifying the outside and inside functions

To differentiate a composite function with the chain rule, first identify the outside function \(f\) and the inside function \(g\). The derivative of the composite equals the derivative of the outside function, evaluated at the inside function, multiplied by the derivative of the inside function:
\[\frac{d}{dx}[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\]In words: derivative of composite = (derivative of outside function, with inside function unchanged) times (derivative of inside function).
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y = \sin(2x)\) by first identifying the outside and inside functions.
\[f(u) = \sin u, \quad g(x) = 2x\]\[f'(u) = \cos u, \quad g'(x) = 2\]\[\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = \cos(2x) \cdot 2\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = 2\cos(2x)}\]

The chain rule for powers of a function

For a function of the form \(y = [g(x)]^n\), the chain rule gives the generalized power rule:
\[\frac{dy}{dx} = n[g(x)]^{n-1} \cdot g'(x)\]
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y = (2x^2 - 5)^4\).
\[g(x) = 2x^2 - 5 \implies g'(x) = 4x\]\[\frac{dy}{dx} = 4(2x^2 - 5)^3 \cdot 4x\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = 16x(2x^2 - 5)^3}\]

The chain rule for trigonometric functions

If \(y = \sin(g(x))\), \(y = \cos(g(x))\), or \(y = \tan(g(x))\), the chain rule gives:
\[\frac{d}{dx}[\sin(g(x))] = \cos(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\]\[\frac{d}{dx}[\cos(g(x))] = -\sin(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\]\[\frac{d}{dx}[\tan(g(x))] = \sec^2(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\]
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y = \cos(x^3)\).
\[g(x) = x^3 \implies g'(x) = 3x^2\]\[\frac{dy}{dx} = -\sin(x^3) \cdot 3x^2\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = -3x^2\sin(x^3)}\]

The chain rule for exponential functions

If \(y = e^{g(x)}\), the chain rule gives:
\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[e^{g(x)}\right] = e^{g(x)} \cdot g'(x)\]
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y = e^{x^2 - 3x}\).
\[g(x) = x^2 - 3x \implies g'(x) = 2x - 3\]\[\frac{dy}{dx} = e^{x^2 - 3x} \cdot (2x - 3)\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = (2x - 3)e^{x^2 - 3x}}\]

The chain rule for logarithmic functions

If \(y = \ln(g(x))\), the chain rule gives:
\[\frac{d}{dx}[\ln(g(x))] = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\]
Worked exampleDifferentiate \(y = \ln(1 - 4x)\).
\[g(x) = 1 - 4x \implies g'(x) = -4\]\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-4}{1 - 4x}\]\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-4}{1 - 4x}}\]
