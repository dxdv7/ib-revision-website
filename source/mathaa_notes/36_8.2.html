Union and intersection of events

For two events \(A\) and \(B\) in the same sample space \(U\): the union \(A \cup B\) is the event "\(A\) occurs, or \(B\) occurs, or both", containing every outcome in \(A\), in \(B\), or in both. The intersection \(A \cap B\) is the event "\(A\) and \(B\) both occur", containing only the outcomes common to both events. On a Venn diagram, a rectangle represents \(U\), closed curves represent the events, and \(A \cap B\) is the overlap between the curves for \(A\) and \(B\).
Worked exampleA standard six-sided die is rolled. Let \(A\) = "an even number" \(= \{2, 4, 6\}\) and \(B\) = "a number greater than \(3\)" \(= \{4, 5, 6\}\). Find \(A \cup B\) and \(A \cap B\).
\[\boxed{A \cup B = \{2, 4, 5, 6\}}\]\[\boxed{A \cap B = \{4, 6\}}\]

The addition rule for combined events

For any two events \(A\) and \(B\) in the same sample space:
\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\]Subtracting \(P(A \cap B)\) corrects for the outcomes in the overlap, which would otherwise be counted twice when \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\) are added.
Worked exampleFor events \(A\) and \(B\), \(P(A) = 0.4\), \(P(B) = 0.5\), and \(P(A \cap B) = 0.2\). Find \(P(A \cup B)\).
\[P(A \cup B) = 0.4 + 0.5 - 0.2\]\[\boxed{P(A \cup B) = 0.7}\]

Mutually exclusive events

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive (or disjoint) if they cannot occur at the same time, so they share no outcomes:
\[A \cap B = \emptyset, \quad P(A \cap B) = 0\]On a Venn diagram, the curves for \(A\) and \(B\) do not overlap. Since there is nothing to subtract, the addition rule simplifies to:
\[P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\]
Worked exampleA card is drawn at random from a standard deck of \(52\) playing cards. Let \(A\) = "drawing a king" and \(B\) = "drawing a queen". Explain why \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, and find \(P(A \cup B)\).
A single card cannot be both a king and a queen, so \(A \cap B = \emptyset\) and \(A\), \(B\) are mutually exclusive.
\[P(A \cup B) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{4}{52}\]\[P(A \cup B) = \frac{8}{52}\]\[\boxed{P(A \cup B) = \frac{2}{13}}\]

Choosing the correct version of the addition rule

Before applying the addition rule, check whether \(A\) and \(B\) can occur together. If the events overlap, use the general rule \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\). If the events cannot both occur, then \(A \cap B = \emptyset\) and the simplified rule \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\) applies directly, with nothing to subtract.
Worked exampleA fair six-sided die is rolled. Let \(A\) = "rolling an even number", \(B\) = "rolling a \(5\)", and \(C\) = "rolling a number greater than \(2\)". (a) Are \(A\) and \(B\) mutually exclusive? Find \(P(A \cup B)\). (b) Are \(A\) and \(C\) mutually exclusive? Find \(P(A \cup C)\).
(a) \(A = \{2,4,6\}\), \(B = \{5\}\), so \(A \cap B = \emptyset\): mutually exclusive.
\[P(A \cup B) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6}\]\[\boxed{P(A \cup B) = \frac{2}{3}}\](b) \(C = \{3,4,5,6\}\), so \(A \cap C = \{4,6\} \ne \emptyset\): not mutually exclusive.
\[P(A \cup C) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{3}\]\[\boxed{P(A \cup C) = \frac{5}{6}}\]

The probability of neither event occurring

Combining the addition rule with the complement rule, the probability that neither \(A\) nor \(B\) occurs is the complement of their union:
\[P((A \cup B)') = 1 - P(A \cup B)\]
Worked exampleIn a class, \(P(\text{studies French}) = 0.6\), \(P(\text{studies Spanish}) = 0.3\), and \(P(\text{studies both}) = 0.15\). Find the probability that a randomly selected student studies neither French nor Spanish.
\[P(F \cup S) = 0.6 + 0.3 - 0.15\]\[P(F \cup S) = 0.75\]\[\boxed{P(\text{neither}) = 1 - 0.75 = 0.25}\]
